## Vorwort des Herausgebers. — Editor’s Preface

The essays announced on the title page were composed by *Bolzano* at very different times. Earliest of all are the *four academic speeches*, which treat “*of the abuse of religion*” and which, understandably, date from the time when he still held the chair of the science of religion in the philosophical faculty at Prague. He deliberately delivered them only towards the end of the academic year (1819), when the number of his hearers, through the departure of many for the holidays, had already dwindled to a small elite; before these few, for the most part the more talented young men, he believed, no doubt rightly, that he might broach subjects which, if delivered otherwise and before an all too mixed crowd, could easily through misunderstanding have occasioned the very abuse he meant to obstruct. The complaint about the abuse of religion, or (in Bolzano’s sense) of religious truths, has indeed been raised often and loudly enough; but nowhere yet — at least so far as I know — has anyone offered an effective remedy, nowhere yet set up the principle by whose earnest application one would be put in a position to prevent such abuse for ever and to secure oneself as far as possible against every harm that can otherwise arise even from such sacred convictions and, alas, has so often arisen already. — Insofar, then, as those four academic lectures aim at this, or even actually achieve it, they deserve without doubt to be published; indeed I do not say too much if I say that in the *interest of mankind* they deserve to be published and disseminated. My undertaking therefore needs no further justification on this side. That I have, however, [p. iv] appended the speeches to the philosophical treatises was done partly because they seemed to me to stand in some connection with the first treatise (see p. 17), partly also because I promise myself from this very circumstance a wider circulation for them, chiefly among the educated class. For although it is to be expected that the editors of Bolzano’s devotional addresses will likewise take them into their collection (Prague, published by Heß, 1848 — 1850), this has not yet happened, and if it does, it is much to be feared that there, among the many other remarkable addresses, they will easily be overlooked — and will not attain the deserved general attention which is possible in a separate edition. Bolzano himself set some store by them and more than once expressed to me the wish that they might one day appear apart from the rest, a wish I hereby intend at the same time to fulfil.

As regards the other essays, first of all the two first: “*On* *Hegel’s dictum: everything actual is rational and everything rational is actual*” and “*on his concept of history in general and of the* *history of philosophy in particular*”, these were written down as early as the thirties (about the year 1835) and would probably have been published by the author himself at that very time, had only the government of the day permitted it. For the decree issued to the censorship authority soon after his dismissal (1820), to reject everything whatsoever that Bolzano might submit to it, was still in full force; and to come forward abroad with a couple of treatises he did not think worth the trouble, and in part did not wish to do so for the very reason that he considered it wrong to circumvent a law of his authorities merely in order to achieve something from which he expected no especially great benefit. So the publication did not take place, and the two essays were kept in his desk. [p. v] When he later took them out again on occasion, he found, in keeping with his well-known peculiarity, a good deal in them to improve; indeed he even resolved to subject both, and quite especially the treatise on Hegel’s concept of history, to a revision. For this purpose the latter was furnished in the manuscript with a multitude of corrections, to the point of illegibility, and all kinds of marginal glosses; but to accomplish more was not granted him. For the rest, the loss that arises for the reader from this omission is not to be rated very high, since the intended changes relate almost solely to greater stylistic finish and a clearer exposition of the subject. Moreover, I have faithfully used everything that was at all legible for the improvement of the text and have incorporated the marginal glosses exactly into it, except for one which, because it seemed destined to serve as the basis for a more considerable expansion, I have taken the liberty of transferring to a note (p. 25).<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup> The matter would, of course, stand somewhat differently if Bolzano had been able to carry out what he later hinted at in passing, with regard to the second treatise, on a *supplementary sheet* of his manuscript (printed at the end of this treatise); but then not merely an improved, but an entirely new treatise would have come out of his essay.

But even though years have already passed since these essays were composed, let no one suppose that they are therefore largely obsolete and of merely historical value. The subjects discussed here, particularly in the first philosophical essay and in the academic lectures, are of lasting importance and anything but already decided and disposed of by our philosophers. If, then, every contribution to their elucidation must appear deserving of thanks, how much more a treatment of them by a man to whom the talent had been given of raising the most difficult and abstruse concepts to clarity and distinctness [p. vi] of consciousness and of *presenting them intelligibly* to every educated person. But to what purpose these and other recommendations of the present speeches and treatises? Bolzano’s name is sufficient guarantee that what is here offered to the reader is nothing commonplace.

The *Bolzano bibliography* in the appendix may not be unwelcome, inasmuch as it gives information about our author’s activity as a writer which is not available to everyone. It comprises not only his own works, not yet published hitherto, but also such writings of his pupils as concern themselves either with the person of Bolzano or with the further development of his concepts.

Budissin in Upper Lusatia, 15 February 1851. Dr. Fr. Prihonsky.

### I. Ueber Hegel’s berühmten Spruch: Alles Wirkliche ist vernünftig und alles Vernünftige ist wirklich. — I. On Hegel’s Famous Dictum: Everything Actual Is Rational and Everything Rational Is Actual

In erring reasons spite / One truth is clear: whatever is, is right.

Pope.

Much has already been disputed about *Hegel’s* famous proposition “*that everything actual is rational and everything* *rational in turn also actual*”; and that the philosopher’s friends would endeavour to remove what is offensive in this pronouncement by a special interpretation was to be expected. Most of their explanations aim to show that the proposition essentially means only that everything irrational, such as all moral evil, all pain, etc., has no *true* actuality, because none that endures, but only a *seeming* one, because a transient one. But since some may well have felt that even this interpretation still does not give a rational sense of the proposition, and hence, to speak by their own instruction, not its actual sense either, *Höndschker* recently assured us that according to Hegel’s own explanation the proposition means nothing other than this: “*whatever is rational bears within it the invisible power* *to break through in spite of all opposition and to establish itself; and* *whatever has attained to indestructible actuality must rest on a* *rational ground*.” — Neither of these would be untrue; but we may fairly ask how a truth to be understood only in this way should arrive at such an expression as the Hegelian one above? Höndschker thinks that to see this it is necessary “to take the concepts *rational* and *actual* in their *sharpest sense*.” — We believe, however, that exactly the reverse holds: only if one takes these words in a very improper and moreover fluctuating [p. 2] sense can that interpretation come about; taken in their sharp sense they state something quite different. But we do not wish to investigate further whether this or that was the sense Hegel attached to his proposition; nor do we wish to inquire for what purpose he put it forward, put it forward precisely in the preface to his *Philosophy of Right*, and applied it in many ways elsewhere, notably in his *Lectures on the* *Philosophy of History*. It is bad enough that even the man’s friends (e.g. *Chalybäus* in Fichte’s Zeitschrift für Philosophie u. specielle Theologie, vol. I, p. 318) admit that by the interpretations attempted people “merely wanted to *hush up the* *meaning of the notorious proposition*, and that before *Hegel’s* mind, when he lectured on that history and on other courses bearing on history, there stood an *actuality* to which he — did not deny *actuality*.” — We can leave all this undecided all the more willingly because the proposition itself, at least in very similar words, has actually been put forward occasionally by others too (e.g. by *Pope* in the motto above). Without concerning ourselves, then, with mere personalities, we wish in this essay to examine only the question whether the proposition can in fact be put forward and defended if all words retain the meaning long fixed by usage, or whether it cannot at least be *justified* with a restriction easily supplied in thought? And if this should be answered in the affirmative, we shall consider what *applications* the proposition admits, what may and what may not be inferred from it.

We call “*actual*”, by a usage fixed in the most definite way, only everything, but also everything, that is, or has *existence*, and produces *effects*; we call it so not only if it exists at *all* times, as God does, but also if it lasts only through a certain [p. 3] *finite* time, indeed, if need be, if it only *is* in a single indivisible instant. Or do we not also say of the child that was born yesterday and has today already ceased to live that it existed, *actually* existed? Indeed, do we not say even of a body in motion whose path leads through a given place that its presence at this place had *actuality*? although it is certain that it could only have been a single indivisible instant in which it was at this place. — To depart from this meaning of the actual, and to want to call *actual* only that which maintains its actuality through a long time, would be a most violent infringement of the most settled usage, and an infringement that could only occasion confusion; especially since it would not be precisely determined *how long* something must properly persist in order to acquire a claim to the name of an actuality no longer merely seeming but already *true*.

We take “*rational*” in a twofold sense: first, for something that is *endowed* with reason (as in the phrase: a rational being); secondly, for something that is *in accordance* with reason (namely with its precepts) (as in the phrase: a rational action). Now if we raise the question whether everything actual is rational and conversely, the first meaning cannot possibly be intended. For we regard mountains and valleys, rivers and seas as something actual too, although it does not occur to us to declare them on that account things endowed with reason. By the rational, therefore, is to be understood here solely *that* *which accords with reason*. But we ask whether something accords with reason only insofar as we imagine that it owes its existence, more proximately or more remotely, to the activity of the will of a thinking being (to speak more precisely, a [p. 4] being *endowed with reason*). For to accord with reason means only to be so constituted as reason demands that something *should* be constituted. But a *should*, precepts or demands, reason prescribes only to beings that are endowed with reason and possess a power of producing effects which they can arrange as these demands direct. It would not be reason but unreason if we wished nevertheless to make certain demands of an object which has no capacity whatever for producing effects, or which — because it lacks reason — cannot perceive any demands at all, or which in any case cannot direct its activity according to them; if, for example, we addressed to an odd number the demand to be an even one, or to a clock that goes unevenly the demand to go more evenly. The latter demand we can at most, if we imagine that there is a being endowed with reason who made this clock, address to that being itself. But if we speak of an object which we think of as something that has not come to be at all, or as something that has indeed come to be but came about without any being endowed with reason having had, through its will, a determining influence on its origin, then we never ask whether this object is also in accordance with reason; unless it were in the single case where we also know objects of a similar kind and wish to investigate how that object would have had to be arranged had it too arisen in such a way. Thus, for example, on seeing a figure in the sand, even if we know that it comes from a being without reason, we may nevertheless raise the question whether it is constituted exactly as a rational being would have arranged it had it intended thereby to give sensible form to a certain geometrical truth. Apart from this case, however, we shall certainly not apply the predicate of accordance with reason to any object of which we know [p. 5] that it owes its existence neither more proximately nor more remotely to the activity of the will of a rational being. Thus no one, surely, will seriously want to say that it is something rational that of two contradictory properties one, and only one, always belongs to every object; or again that the volume of a sphere amounts to exactly two thirds of the cylinder enclosing it. Let me not be misunderstood. The *propositions* that express these relations between things we do indeed call rational, i.e. propositions in accordance with truth; but no one will call these relations themselves in accordance with reason. And why not? Solely because we do not think of these relations as something that has arisen at all, still less as something that is so because a rational being has willed that it be so. If, then, with regard to anything actual, we ask whether it is rational, we do this, if we understand ourselves, always only insofar as we presuppose that this actual thing is grounded, immediately or mediately, in the power of will of a rational being, and by that question we seek precisely to learn whether this thinking being, in its relevant determination of will, acted in accordance with the demands of reason or not?

Since, however, one and the same object can sometimes also be brought about by the agency of *several* intelligent beings, one contributing to its origin more proximately, another more remotely, a third perhaps only in part, the case is conceivable that we declare the same object, with regard to one being, with full right something rational, and with regard to a second, with just as much right, something irrational. Thus a fit of anger into which a sick man is thrown by the deliberate provocation of his physician might be very irrational on his part, but very rational on the part of the physician, who needed this excitement as a remedy. Who does not think here of his own accord that in a similar way even what [p. 6] we human beings commit that is foolish or vicious may, in relation to God, who permits it for wise purposes, be called a very rational event? And indeed, if we wish to call not merely one thing or another but each and every actual thing in the whole universe *rational* in a certain respect, this can evidently, according to what has been shown so far, happen only insofar as we presuppose a thinking being which contains in itself the immediate ground of *all* these actualities. Without presupposing the existence of such a being, that is, of *God*, and indeed of a thinking God, or (as people nowadays like to say) of a *personal* God, the assertion that everything actual is rational has no sense whatever: a matter which most defenders of that proposition do not themselves seem to know. But if we believe, as we are in fact fully entitled to do, in the existence of a being that has no further ground of its existence outside itself and is for that very reason the unlimited sum of all perfection; if we believe in a being whose power of cognition is omniscient, whose will is holy and whose outwardly acting power is almighty; if we believe (as we must likewise do) that all other, limited beings exist only through the will and power of that One Infinite, and that likewise all changes they undergo, — hence everything whatsoever that is and happens in the world, is and happens only through God’s will: then it is true that one may in a certain sense make the assertion of everything actual (namely of everything *finite* that is actual) *that it* *is rational*; as also the converse, that everything rational at some time and somewhere attains to actuality. The first, namely, insofar as all finite actualities are brought about or permitted by God only then and there, when and where they serve the well-being of the living better than anything else whose bringing about in their place would have been possible; [p. 7] in which respect they therefore deserve the most complete approval of reason. The other, again, insofar as everything that is truly *rational*, i.e. that reason demands because it is possible in itself and conducive to the well-being of the whole, is known by the Omniscient, willed by the Holy One, and also brought about by the Almighty at the right time and in the right place.

Thus we should have found that the *Hegelian* proposition, that everything actual is rational and conversely, can in fact be justified according to a certain interpretation, namely only with regard to the share which God himself takes in everything whatsoever that is and happens in the world. It is only to be doubted whether this interpretation will be agreeable to many friends of the Hegelian philosophy; for in truth it would then have been something very old and familiar that was said with those strange-sounding words, and one would have spoken far more intelligibly by borrowing the old formula, *What God does is well done*, from the old schoolmaster Lichtknecht. But let us drop this, and examine further the<sup>2</sup> *application* that can be made of our proposition, if it may be understood only in this way.

<sup>2</sup> In Salzmann’s Schwarzmantel.

That we ought to strive, with regard to every fate, however adverse, that befalls us or others — once our conscience testifies that we have not brought it about by any wicked or even merely thoughtless action, and once we have moreover already tried everything that we were permitted by the laws of morality to try for its removal or mitigation — that we ought then, I say, to strive *to compose ourselves* about this fate *in such a way* that we keep before us the thought that *the best* *effects for ourselves and others* will one day still arise from it: [p. 8] this is surely a truth that must be evident to everyone, especially since this truth really holds quite independently of our proposition above. For once we have done everything that lies within our powers and is compatible with the laws of morality to avert an evil, we always do very well to ease as much as possible the bearing of what we cannot or may not avert from ourselves by considering how it will one day still bring the most blessed consequences in its train. An exception to this maxim would have to be made only in the case where we had occasioned the evil through our own fault and it were to be feared that we should merely become frivolous if we were to surrender too quickly to the thought of how even from this evil a preponderant good could and will soon spring forth. Understandably, however, even in the case where we do not know ourselves free of all guilt, a point of time may arrive at which it becomes expedient to moderate and shorten the excessive vehemence or duration of our remorse by the consoling idea just mentioned. If, now, the circumstances are really of the kind just described, so that comfort and encouragement may and should find a place, then the proposition of which we are speaking will serve us as *the most excellent means* *of furthering such a purpose*. For when we consider that everything that is and happens in the world is rational in the sense that it is as conducive to the well-being of all living things as is at all possible if an omniscient, holy and almighty being has created and governs everything, can we then still fear that any suffering that befalls us blamelessly, or only after we have repented of our fault, amended it and made good the wrong as far as possible, will not in the end resolve itself into our good?

It is true, indeed, and deserves to be expressly noted here: [p. 9] in a certain sense even he who lacks belief in a “personal God” can always say that *everything in the world is exactly as* *good as it is at all possible to be*; possible, that is, only under the presupposition he holds to be true, that because there is no God, everything happens only according to the laws of a blind natural necessity. But the great, the immeasurably great advantage of those who believe in a God consists precisely in this, that through God’s infinite power they can assume an infinite multitude of things and arrangements in the world which promote the well-being of living beings and accord with morality to be *possible*, and for that very reason also *actual*, whereas every ground for assuming them falls away as soon as the belief in this God is lacking. To be sure, once we have formed a *distinct* concept of what *omnipotence* means, we shall not imagine that God must be able to produce everything that may occur to us; rather we shall only assert that he can produce everything *that stands in contradiction with no pure conceptual truth*. Thus, for example, even with the belief in an almighty God we shall not demand that a heavenly body which has the shape of a sphere should nevertheless offer its inhabitants all those advantages that would be attainable only with the shape of a cube; for this contradicts a pure conceptual truth. Just as little shall we demand that the Almighty should make what has happened not to have happened, or turn vice into virtue, and the like, for all this contradicts itself already in its mere concept. Yet even with such a restriction, how much, indeed infinitely much, must be possible through an omnipotence that would be impossible without its existence! How greatly does the number of purposive arrangements you may assume to be possible in a state increase as soon as you enlarge the number of its intelligent and well-disposed citizens by even a single one? And the sum of wise [p. 10] arrangements promoting the well-being of the whole which you can assume to be possible in a world if it owes its existence to mere chance or to necessity, should it not be surpassed infinitely many times by the sum of purposive arrangements which can and must come about in this very world as soon as it is the work of an Almighty?

What gives us the greatest offence, however, when we consider the various events of this world, are without doubt the evil actions of which rational creatures make themselves guilty by virtue of the *freedom* granted to them. But as soon as we remember that this freedom is in itself no imperfection but rather an advantage, and that such an abuse of it as we meet with here on earth occurs only among creatures who still stand on a low level of spiritual cultivation, since all those whose power of knowledge is already more developed than ours never forget for a single moment that by every evil deed, as surely as there is a just God, they would make only themselves unhappy; and if we add to this the consideration that for a being clothed with omnipotence there is an infinite multitude of means by which now the very formation of the evil resolve is prevented, now its execution in the deed is — frustrated, now what was intended as evil is given a beneficial outcome through a peculiar concatenation of all the circumstances: then we shall know how to set our minds thoroughly at rest about all the evil that we see occur without our own doing; and it will become ever clearer to us that it is a truly inexhaustible abundance of consolation which springs from faith in God for a heart that is reconciled with him.

This, then, would be the right use of this faith, or (what amounts to the same) the blameless application of the proposition that everything actual is rational, and conversely. — But how are we to judge the case in which someone, concerning the evil and foolish things that he does not accomplish without [p. 11] an inner contradiction of his spirit, wishes to set his mind at rest with the idea that in the end it must after all be something rational and good, simply because God permits only what is most beneficial for the well-being of the whole? What are we to say when someone will at least never fear a punishment for his conduct, merely because he maintains that under the circumstances then prevailing, under which he determined himself to this deed, it would not have been in his power at all to make another choice?

Such a procedure, we must answer, is the most shameful abuse that can ever be made of faith in God and of the consequence arising from it, that everything actual is rational. We say frankly that anyone who hopes to remain unpunished in an evil way of life is caught in the most ruinous error; and this even on the supposition that it were entirely true that each of us cannot act otherwise than we do act, given the natural malice of our heart, under the influence of all the circumstances present. For precisely because your heart is still so malicious, your desire still so violent, your reason still so far from having gained mastery over your sensuality, for that very reason there exists the unavoidable necessity that strong sufferings that bring you to your senses, ills in which you recognise the punishments and effects of your perverse conduct hitherto, should break in upon you, so that you may at last learn to abhor evil and be able to desist from it. What is the only means to your amendment, what may also be necessary as a deterrent for many others, should you not *deserve* that in the fullest sense of the word? How then can you console yourself in your errant ways with the thought that *everything is good* *and rational* in the world, when it is good and rational only in relation to the share that God has in it, and not with regard to your own share? when precisely because everything is [p. 12] arranged well and rationally on God’s side it is required that every evil and foolish thing done on your side should meet with its punishment? You say, to be sure, that the wisdom of God will let many a beneficial thing for the whole come forth even from your evil and foolish deeds. That may indeed be true; but if God, through his wise guidance, is able to draw forth many a beneficial thing from your perverse behaviour, from actions which in the ordinary course of nature bring only ruinous consequences in their train: could not an incomparably greater and purer blessing for you and others have been developed from your good deeds? What, then, can be more mistaken than to think that through God’s infinitely wise and gracious government of the world it becomes a matter of indifference whether we act well or ill: when, quite the reverse, it is raised by that government to the most complete certainty that every good deed, even the least, not only does not remain unrewarded but is also used to bring about every blessing for all that is at all possible? Never, therefore, can the useful consequences that God’s wise omnipotence knows how to draw even from whatever evil or folly we commit, never can they serve us as an excuse, or grant us the hope that we shall remain unpunished.

But this abuse of the proposition that everything actual is rational is in fact so glaring that it can occur only rarely and only among people who wish to deceive themselves. Certainly; for our innermost feeling and everyday experience teach us, after all, that God by his government of the world does not abolish either the difference that exists between pleasure and pain, between agreeable and disagreeable sensations, or the inner connection that prevails among the various events in the world, a connection by virtue of which some are the cause of others and these in turn the effect of those. But from this it already follows that the difference between good and evil, prudent and foolish actions is not abolished either, but persists [p. 13] in its full actuality. An action which, according to the effects that naturally proceed from it and are foreseen by us, is conducive to the well-being of the whole, is and remains *good*; one which disturbs the well-being of the whole through its effects perceptible to us is and remains *evil*. An action that does not merely *seem* fit, but is in truth fit, to bring about the end we intend by it is and remains *prudent*; one of which the opposite holds is and remains *foolish*. Although, therefore, everything whatever that is and happens in the world can be called rational and good in relation to God, there are nevertheless not only good and prudent but also evil and foolish ways of acting; and it is not permissible to conclude that everything whatever that comes about, because it is good and rational with regard to the share that God has in it, can also be called good and rational with regard to us human beings or other rational beings through whose activity of will it has been brought about.

Nor (to pass at once to a new consequence) is it by any means permissible to infer, from the fact that everything actual, so long as it is actual, can in a certain respect be called good and rational, that it ought to be tolerated by us when we have the power to remove it, or even upheld if it threatens to collapse of itself. For everything that at any time attains actuality is to be called good and rational not in *this* sense, that because it once came about it would now deserve to continue into all the future; it is called good and rational only in the sense that it is the best of all that could come about under God’s guidance in this place and throughout the whole time during which we have so far seen it exist; and by this it is not in the least said that it will still remain the best later on as well, and therefore deserve to last longer and will last longer. —

[p. 14] “But if this inference does not hold for every thing that comes into actuality for some period of time, however short: then perhaps it does hold for such institutions as have maintained themselves for centuries, and maintained themselves in spite of all efforts to overthrow them and abolish them for ever; at least it will be permissible to declare an establishment a good thing, standing under God’s special protection, when we perceive that, already on the point of perishing, it has been saved again through an unexpected concurrence of the most unusual events? Must we not at least here recognise the finger of God, and not only call him a fool, but say that he rebels against God’s declared counsels, who stakes his strength on overthrowing such an establishment? Should not, if he succumbs, *the outcome itself* have proved that he attempted only what is displeasing to God, since otherwise his striving would not have been frustrated?” — To all this too, however plausible it may seem to many, I must object with complete decisiveness. So much only is true: that the long existence of such an institution may, under certain circumstances, awaken *a favourable prejudice* for it, one which places everyone who wishes to attempt its abolition under the obligation first to consider very carefully indeed whether this institution is in fact quite so dispensable and objectionable as it seems to him; and likewise, how it has come about that, for all its objectionableness, it has nevertheless maintained itself so long? But to wish to derive, from the long existence of a thing taken by itself alone, a conclusion as to its fitness for the past as well as for the future is altogether inadmissible and has the most dangerous consequences. The inadmissibility of such an inference betrays itself already in this, that no one is able to tell us *how long* this existence of a thing must really have lasted in order to be called sufficiently long? Who, moreover, [p. 15] does not know that even highly harmful and perverse institutions and customs can maintain themselves, and have maintained themselves, among us human beings for the longest time, when they were favoured by ignorance, error and passion? Can there be, to touch on only a single example, anything more abominable, anything more damnable, than the custom of human sacrifice? and yet has not this terrible custom prevailed among almost all the peoples of the earth for long centuries? But more still: how will you infer straightway from the long duration of an institution its goodness, and that it deserves to be retained further, when there are also institutions which for centuries were beneficial, indeed even necessary, and only now, owing to altered circumstances, have become dispensable, nay, a hindrance to the further progress of mankind? Can I cite a more remarkable and decisive example of this than the Mosaic religion? And so, if you wish to act rationally, O men, you may not, from the duration of an institution, nor from its wide diffusion, nor even from the number of defenders it still finds at this hour (how great was not the number of defenders of which heathendom could once boast over against Christianity?) — decide as to its worth and as to the important question whether you are to vote for its further retention or rather to summon up all your forces for its final abolition? On grounds of an altogether different kind, from the consideration of all the nearer as well as the remoter effects that an institution brings in its train, from regard to all the changes that would result from its abolition, from its inner nature as well as from all the external circumstances and relations that have an influence here, must you decide what is to be done in such a case. But once you have clearly recognised that, and in what way, an establishment is detrimental to the virtue and happiness of the human race, and [p. 16] which other is to be put in its place: then regard it as a duty not to be evaded by anything to work towards the displacement of the harmful by the decidedly better. And believe with confidence that this duty is not altered in the least, even when you see that many an attempt to introduce this improvement has already remained without success. Or what would you conclude from such experiences? That it did not happen without God’s will that those attempts at an overthrow were frustrated? That I grant you in advance, but only in just the sense in which you can say even of the most atrocious crime that it did not pass from the realm of possibility into that of actuality without God’s will, namely not without his *permissive* will. Now just as little as a crime ceases to be what it is merely because it could not come about without God’s permission: so little do perverse institutions cease to be what they are when God — who alone surveys the connection of the whole, lets centuries pass without bringing about the means that would suffice for their abolition. Nay, let even more happen, let the rarest events combine to help up again an institution that was already close to its overthrow: even this changes nothing in your duty to set yourselves against what you have clearly recognised as an institution detrimental to the well-being and morality of men. What, or would it perhaps accord with the laws of your reason, this guide given you by God himself, to recognise in the concurrence of such events the *signs* that he has taken the establishment in question under his protection, that he wishes to see it upheld, and upheld moreover through your co-operation? So you might well conclude if it were a question of an establishment which, by all that you are able to discern, presents itself as beneficial for mankind. But if the very opposite is the case, then you are [p. 17] never entitled to such a conclusion, even if you are utterly unable to explain for what other purpose those events are meant. For does not every single thing in the world have immeasurably many effects as well as causes? Cannot, then, the events that so astonish you also be grounded in a very necessary way in the connection of the whole, and likewise have their beneficial, though to you imperceptible, effects for the future? Nothing, therefore, compels us, indeed nothing even permits us, to say that they are there to be a sign to us that God wills us to do what, according to all our insight, appears to us as something harmful and manifestly evil. — You reply, to be sure, that we might after all be mistaken in judging whether an establishment really has such ruinous consequences. And this possibility I will again grant you; nevertheless it remains certain that we are free of all responsibility when we follow what, according to our best insight, presents itself to us as good and of common benefit; and equally certain that God, if he wishes to make his counsel known to us by signs, will not and cannot give ambiguous signs, signs resting on a mere perhaps. If, then, we see that an institution which after the most mature deliberation we can only declare ruinous is preserved by the concurrence of the most unusual occurrences, just when we hoped to have displaced it by something better: let us confess that we cannot, it is true, state with certainty why this came about, but that we do know with sufficient certainty that it did not happen in order to deter us from further attempts, but that it happened so that in future we should apply ever more deliberation and caution. We do not, therefore, fear that we have done what is displeasing to God when we have acted according to our best knowledge with the purest intention; we do not believe that the apparently favourable failure of our undertaking proves [p. 18] by the deed itself that we acted against God’s will, least of all against a will of God recognisable by us beforehand and *declared* to us; but after we have examined everything anew and found no reason to alter our views, let us proceed untiringly to new attempts; let us use the experiences we have gained only for what they can be used for, namely to fight in future still more cautiously and, if possible, with stronger weapons.

But yet another question arises, which we must discuss in a few words. Even if the proposition that everything actual is rational, and conversely, alters nothing in the difference that exists between the morally good and evil: will not at least the difference that, according to the common view, we make between events which we call *fortunate* or *gladdening* and others which we call *unfortunate* or *saddening* undergo a considerable change from the higher point of view that faith in God opens to us? And this question I must answer in the affirmative, but in such a way that I maintain: faith in God takes from the events we call *fortunate* not the least of their *gladdening quality* for us, but rather heightens it; while the *saddening* character that the *unfortunate* events have is indeed diminished by this faith, and diminished to a degree the more considerable, the more vividness and strength we are able to give to it. For that we *rejoice* in an event is certainly in no way forbidden or spoiled for us by our faith in God or by the proposition that everything actual is rational, and conversely; unless perhaps what gladdens us in an event were in contradiction with the laws of morality; in which case, however, every rightminded person would not wish to rejoice and would not rejoice, even if no faith in God forbade it. Apart from this case, we can rejoice all the more heartily in every event that we have hitherto been accustomed to call a *fortunate* one, I mean in every event that promises us agreeable things not [p. 19] only in its immediate but also in all the more remote consequences that our eye is able to reach, the less we have to fear that mere chance will destroy our happiness again, or even that a maliciously gloating being who directs our destinies gladdens us for a time only in order suddenly to wound us all the more deeply through disappointed hopes. So much concerning the events that bring happiness; as regards those, however, which we are accustomed to call unfortunate, that is, those which at least in their nearest effects are painful to our sensibility: it is surely natural that at their onset we *grieve*, and are unable altogether to suppress the wish that we might be spared them, or at least had already got through them; this is natural, I say, even if we possess faith in God and therefore do not in the least doubt that everything whatever that is and happens is rational, that even from the sufferings and pains we now experience predominantly beneficial consequences for ourselves and others will spring, and that not a single one of these bitter sensations could be spared us with greater advantage. This, I repeat, is natural, because painful sensations cannot be abolished and annihilated by the mere idea of a happiness that will only sprout from them in the future. Or how could we perhaps ward off the sadness that takes possession of our heart when we must look on as a devastating plague spreads through whole continents, and not only threatens our own life but also carries off thousands of our brothers by a dreadful death? how could we, how should we, not mourn, even if we have no reason to reproach ourselves that in the end we ourselves too had in some way a nearer or more remote share in its spread? But if faith in God cannot transform an event sad in itself into a gladdening one, or even [p. 20] into an altogether indifferent one: yet it is evident, and was already remarked above, that it can make the bearing of it uncommonly easier for us. The more *familiar* we make to ourselves the consoling thought, *what God does is well done*, the more *strength* and *vividness* we procure for it by making use of all the cases in which its truth can become intuitively clear to us; the more unshakeable the firmness we give to faith in God by resolving into their nothingness all doubts and objections opposed to it: the more calmly and composedly shall we go to meet every fate against which our sensuous nature rebels, and know how to bear it.

If this really is the only way it stands with the sense and interpretation of the proposition that *everything actual is rational, and conversely*: then let each judge for himself whether it is praise of a philosophy when it is said of it that “<sup>3</sup> *it has made* *peace with actuality; it wishes to create nothing, to reshape nothing;* *it wishes to be no wiser than the world spirit, that is, the reason of* *mankind, is just now*”? — No, truly! such a thing would be praise only if the proposition that everything actual is rational held in a sense in which it is the most palpable untruth, namely that all institutions that attain actuality among us human beings, and likewise all *concepts* and *opinions* making themselves known through such institutions, must be fit for their purpose and rational, at least when they enjoy great generality. We ourselves do indeed maintain, and shall never cease to maintain, that there is certainly one case in which we may conclude with much confidence that a doctrine is *rational* [p. 21] and *true* merely because we find it generally diffused. It is the case when the question at issue is one that can be brought to a decision by mere reason, combined if need be with experiences that are at everyone’s disposal, and when this decision runs in such a way that it does not flatter sensuality but rather impairs it. In such a case the concordant conviction of men of all times and lands is indeed an authority than which there is none greater. But where these conditions do not obtain, where we are speaking of a doctrine or institution in regard to which either an actual agreement of all is not present at all, because thousands among the wiser have made their disapproval known loudly enough, or where the correct judgement of the matter would require certain peculiar observations available to very few people, or where the following that a doctrine, and certain institutions resting upon it, find can very easily be explained by the many inconveniences that were at first bound up with changing it, or perhaps also by the danger that threatens everyone who thinks otherwise if he lets his opinion be heard: then indeed it is for no one more disgraceful and more culpable than for the philosopher not to wish to be wiser than the world spirit, that is, the great, the ruling multitude, *is just now*. How is mankind ever to become wiser than it is just now if even the philosopher will not presume to think otherwise on any point than the great multitude thinks just now? Who, then, is to dare to propose improvements, if not even he, the philosopher, will “*create*” anything, will “*reshape*” anything that has attained actuality? if even he seeks his glory in “*having made peace*” with *actuality*, that is, with everything once established? It was indeed a fault, to be sure, when philosophers, or better said, merely men who most unlawfully arrogated to themselves this venerable name, wished to overthrow every established doctrine [p. 22] and order merely because it was the established one, without first undertaking the most careful deliberation and without having found the most valid reasons for doing so. But must one always fall from one extreme into the other? Because some formerly went too far in their mania for improvement, may we now, in direct contrast, *make peace with* *everything established*? — That a certain philosophy has a great deal of suppleness, and can prove as true anything you like, we have long known; it was not necessary to tell us so openly its maxim of setting up as true only what it can hope will be heard and accepted not unwillingly.

<sup>3</sup> As, for example, Dr. Mayer recently said of the Hegelian philosophy in a work written in a very popular vein, in a *Schreiben an eine Dame über* *Philosophie* (Berlin 1837).

[p. 23]

### II. Ueber Hegel’s und seiner Anhänger Begriff von der Geschichte überhaupt und insbesondere von der Geschichte der Philosophie. — II. On Hegel’s and His Followers’ Concept of History in General and of the History of Philosophy in Particular

Posthac potius de dissensionibus tantis summorum virorum disseramus, de obscuritate naturae deque errore tot philosophorum, qui de bonis contrariisque rebus tantopere discrepant, ut cum plus uno verum esse non possit, jacere necesse sit tot tam nobiles disciplinas. [Hereafter let us rather discuss the great disagreements among the most eminent men, the obscurity of nature, and the error of so many philosophers, who differ so widely about good things and their contraries that, since no more than one of them can be true, so many noble schools must necessarily fall to the ground.]

Cicero, Academica, book IV.

Among the doctrines by which *Hegel* won attention and applause for his philosophical system even from a part of that learned public which otherwise did not take to his highly peculiar method of philosophising, the foremost place belongs to his distinctive view of history in general and of the history of philosophy in particular. Hegel, as is well known, put forward the assertion that a (philosophical) *history of mankind* has nothing else to narrate, or rather (by a peculiar confusion of history as the *narrator* with the *events* which it has to narrate) that the history of mankind is already in itself nothing other than the *development of the world spirit* completing itself in time, whereby that spirit gradually attains to its own self-consciousness, or to freedom. Every succeeding state of mankind is, he held, at bottom never anything other than a *progress*, issuing from all the earlier ones, in the rational and necessary course of the world spirit. In the same way the *history of philosophy* too is supposed to be nothing other than its own *development*. Here it is to be presupposed as something indubitable, indeed necessary, that every system appearing later in time is [p. 24] nothing other than a timely perfecting of the one immediately preceding it. More than that: every system, at least every philosophical system that attained a certain currency in its time, is really to be regarded as already *perfect*, perfect, that is, for the age in which it appeared and held sway; we are to regard it not merely as a preparatory labour towards the solution of the whole problem incumbent on philosophy, but as already the complete solution of that problem, though only for that time. More specifically, it is demanded that the whole history of philosophy be divisible into nothing but groups of three systems each, in the first of which (the earlier) something is posited, in the second something is opposed to it, and in the third both are united in a higher unity. For one of Hegel’s pupils, von *Cieskowski*, all this is not yet scientific<sup>4</sup> enough. According to him the trichotomous articulation is to find so thoroughgoing an application in history as well (not only of philosophy, but of all mankind) that no other may gain ground beside it. Therefore history too, when one takes it in its totality, that is, when one includes the future along with the past and the present, must fall into three periods, the first of which is the *thetic*, the second the *antithetic*, the last the *synthetic*. The first was that of antiquity, the second the Christian-Germanic world, and we are supposed to stand just now at the entrance of the third. In the first the *Beautiful* is supposed to have been dominant, in the second *Truth*; in the third the *Deed* is to attain dominion. Besides this organism of *succession* there is also supposed to be one of *juxtaposition* and finally one of *concentration*. By virtue of the second, all the peoples coexisting at one time mirror past as well as future; by virtue of the last, great men are the representatives of the [p. 25] universal historical idea as well as of the particular character of their own people; hence (according to C.) one has as a rule narrated the history of a whole people if one has only narrated that of its great men, of its rulers for example, etc. As regards *philosophy*, C. holds that it has reached its highest point in the present to such a degree that from now on it must consent to go beyond itself and to descend from the heights of theory into the fields of practice, or to be *applied*. Its next destiny, he says, will be to popularise itself, “*to become* *shallow in depth.*”

<sup>4</sup> Prolegomena zur Historiosophie. Berlin 1838.

It cannot be denied that there is something seductive in all these assertions. For, to dwell only on what is said about<sup>5</sup> philosophy: how pleasant it is to think that we attain so cheaply, namely merely by acquainting ourselves with the system that happens to be dominant in the present, to the possession of the philosophy most perfect for this time, indeed (according to C.) even of the absolutely perfect philosophy! Nor, truly, do they make it hard for us to find out, among the several systems set up here and there in our days, which one would deserve to be called κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν [par excellence] the “*philosophy of the present*.” For since we Europeans are indisputably the most cultivated people on the face of the earth, and in Europe no one really philosophises except in Germany, and in Germany, finally, only Berlin (and at a pinch Munich too) can without contradiction point to the most celebrated philosophers: it is surely (so they think) as clear as day that only the Hegelian (or at a pinch the Schellingian-Hegelian) [p. 26] philosophy alone can be called the philosophy of the present, while the other systems one and all must be regarded as not existing at all.<sup>6</sup>

<sup>5</sup> It is altogether most *reassuring* to hear, to hear from the mouths of celebrated philosophers, that whatever folly each individual, and especially the great ones of the earth, may undertake, whatever sins against mankind they may allow themselves to commit in their own persons: all of it must nevertheless be regarded only as a *rational* and *necessary* step forward!! —

<sup>6</sup> Since our author wrote this, much has indeed changed in this respect. The Editor.

But will the readers not first examine whether the premises on which these gratifying assertions rest are all correct as well?

That there is a gradual progress in perfection we too assume; indeed we assert that such progress takes place in every finite substance individually as well as in our human race as a whole, and we assert this on grounds of which we are very clearly aware, although we can only hint at them fleetingly here. In every finite *substance* (the word substance taken not in the vacillating sense of modern philosophy but in the strict sense, in which it designates an actual thing that has no adherence in another actual thing and is for that very reason simple, indestructible, and so on), in every finite substance, then, the *possibility* of a progress reaching into the infinite rests essentially on the circumstance that every such substance is *changeable* and possesses a *power of ideation*, by means of which it constantly receives new ideas through the influence of the others surrounding it, and therefore, at least in one respect which contains the condition for all the rest, namely in *the sum of its ideas*, increases unceasingly. A gradual<sup>7</sup> progress of *mankind as a whole* presents itself to us as *possible* in so far as the experiences that an earlier time makes can be used by a later one, to which they have been handed down, in [p. 27] order thereby to become ever wiser and more prudent, and hence also better and happier. But that this merely possible progress, after many temporary interruptions and regressions, will never fail altogether, at least not in mankind *as a* *whole*, as also in every individual substance once it has been brought so far as to will the good in earnest: this we expect partly on the ground that increasing knowledge joined with good will must gradually always result in an increase in every other perfection as well, partly because we believe that every good which is not in itself impossible will one day attain to actuality through God’s almighty governance of the whole. If these are the only grounds for which we expect a progress in every individual as well as in our race as a whole, then it is self-evident that, and why, this progress need not be an uninterrupted one. And this agrees too with experience, according to which it would be hard to demonstrate that, for example, every individual human being becomes uninterruptedly, and thus necessarily with every hour as well, somewhat more perfect; so that, for example, when he falls asleep in the evening tired from his day’s work, he would be and become more perfect during that very sleep than he was the day before in his waking state, and the next day again more perfect on waking than he had been in his sleep, etc. Stranger still is it to think that those among us who are so unfortunate as to sink into follies and vices would become more perfect even then, when all the world judges that they grow worse with every day. And are we perhaps to say even of the *madman* that he is now more perfect than he was before his misfortune? — Here, then, we should already have fallen into contradiction with the assertions of the modern school; for according to it every succeeding state of mankind would have to be regarded as a true *progress*, and thus presumably as a more perfect state than the one immediately preceding. It is true that they try to make [p. 28] this credible to us by the remark that every later state of the whole of *mankind* can be regarded as *the result* of all the preceding ones, and thus as the *development* arising out of them: and were this quite correct, then we ourselves too would not be disinclined to call the later state always a more perfect one. But they seem to have forgotten that the complete ground from which any later state of a thing emerges includes not merely the sum of all its earlier states but also the sum of all its relations and of all the forces acting upon it from without. In these relations, then, in these influences coming from without, it may lie that the development turns out one way in one case and otherwise in another, and does not every time deserve the name of a true *progress towards* *perfection*.

<sup>7</sup> For the fuller exposition of these important doctrines see B.’s Athanasia, pp. 85–91. The Editor.

According to the modern philosophers it would indeed be the *Absolute*, or God himself, who develops in the universe and attains to *self-consciousness* in *mankind* in particular; and then, to be sure, there would be no influences from without to be feared that might disturb the regularity of this course of development. But the assumption of such a God cannot be justified at all. Not only the originator of this whole newer manner of philosophising, *Schelling*, but even several of the most gifted of *Hegel’s* pupils, such as *C. H. Fichte*, *Weisse* and others, have long since renounced this pantheistic doctrine again, inasmuch as they acknowledge in the most unequivocal manner that there is a being to which real totality or absoluteness belongs; that this being is to be thought of not as one that first works its way up to self-consciousness in man, but as one endowed with self-consciousness from all eternity; that this thinking being is the ground of all other existence, or the creator of the world, and so on. —

However, no attack on your pantheism is needed in order to show you contradictions, or at least arbitrary assumptions, [p. 29] in your doctrine of the development of the world spirit in mankind as a whole. Is the human race perhaps the only kind of beings in the universe that are rational, that you continually say that here and here alone the world spirit comes to its self-consciousness? But if (as no one can deny) the human race is surrounded by a countless multitude of other beings, rational and irrational; and if (as is equally settled) these other beings too have an influence on the shaping of our destinies:<sup>8</sup> by what right do you presuppose that the history of mankind must follow so regular a course? By the very same right by which one would also have to be able to assert that the history of every single people, indeed of every family, of every society however arbitrarily assumed, of several persons as well as of every individual by himself, must present a regular whole. If you lack the courage to assert that the life of every *single* *human being*, a life which has often lasted only a few hours and has often been determined in its course by the most accidental external influences, represents a regular progress, a development of the world spirit: then neither may you assert with full confidence that the history of mankind as a whole presents such a development. And is it even a thought that will bear a bright light, this thought of yours that *one and the* *same spirit*, namely what you call the *world spirit*, *appears* in a plurality of individuals, namely in every single human being, and *attains to its consciousness in them*? One could speak so if the sensations, thoughts and actions of the individuals were at the same time sensations, thoughts and actions of this spirit appearing in them. Now I know, to be sure, that the sensations [p. 30] which my sore finger causes me may be accompanied, perhaps even (in part at least) produced, by certain *other* (possibly similar) sensations which my finger itself (the soul dwelling in it) has. And in a similar way you may, if you otherwise have grounds for it, imagine a spiritual being (the soul of the earth, say) which undergoes certain changes through everything that we human beings feel, think and do here; you may even assume that every sensation or idea which we human beings have produces a more or less similar sensation or idea in the world spirit as well. But what you can in no case assert without uttering the most manifest absurdity is that the sensations, ideas, etc. which we human beings have are themselves already sensations, ideas, etc. *in* this world spirit; or, what amounts to the same thing, that this world spirit attains to its self-consciousness not merely *through* us but — *in* us; that our thoughts are not certain similar thoughts merely *occasioned* in it, but are in themselves *its* thoughts.

<sup>8</sup> A slight change in the mixture of the elements can cause whole nations to perish.

Finally, even if we could grant you all this, so much at least remains certain: that your presupposition that all the destinies of our race are *mere developments of the world spirit* does not make either the *understanding* of these events or their *prediction* for the future in the least easier than did the view which you have sought to supplant with yours, namely that an almighty and an all-knowing being has created and governs all things. For do give us for once a definite concept of what this *development* of the world spirit is or is to become, and which changes in the universe you consider necessary as the *means* to it, so that we may be able to judge whether they are fit to determine every kind of development in advance, or at least to explain it wherever we meet it. We hear you say that the development of the world spirit has as its goal that it [p. 31] should attain to self-consciousness; and that this takes place in man, and most perfectly in him who (like the late *Hegel*) has worked his way up to the knowledge that knowing and being are one and the same. But this really sounds almost too absurd to need a serious refutation. For even if we leave Hegel wholly out of the game, how will you prove that *self-* *consciousness* is the highest consummation of spirit? Or, should you succeed in justifying yourselves through a special interpretation of the words in the manner you favour, what will you reply to the second question: what means are necessary to a development? If you cannot tell us this, then it is plain that nothing whatever can be done with your attempted explanation of the course of the world by means of the presupposition you have made. The most intelligible thing we have heard from you on this subject was put forward only recently by Carl Philipp *Fischer*. “In order that the power and truth of<sup>9</sup> spirit may prove itself and be known in all-round mediation,” (he said) “*all real possibilities* must *become actual*, and even the phenomena most contradictory to the idea of spirit must of necessity emerge. For just as *nature* already presents a system only through this, that in the course of its development *all the* *forms* of external existence *possible through its idea* emerge, so the *world of spirit* forms a determinate system only through this, that in the course of history the idea of mankind is realised in *all the forms* in which it can become actual.”

<sup>9</sup> In *Fichte’s* Zeitschrift für Philosophie und speculative Theologie, vol. 3, no. 1, pp. 153 ff.

On this view, then, the means to the development of the world spirit would be the production of all the forms of existence that are in any way possible, especially within the human race. But how will you establish this, how prove that the world spirit cannot develop otherwise than by human [p. 32] beings of every possible kind (not only good ones but bad ones of every kind as well) appearing in the world? To us such an assertion seems almost as if someone were to say that a scholar could not develop his mind otherwise than by writing works of every possible kind (not only good ones but the most wretched as well)! We must further draw attention here to the circumstance that the mere demand, “all forms of men (and of other beings) that are at all possible are gradually to come into existence,” does not yet in any way determine the order in which they are to appear, nor any more the destinies they are to undergo. From this it follows that this demand, even if it were correct, would still not express the *true, complete law* which the world obeys in its arrangements and in its course. This, then, you would still have to find; and so long as you have not told it to us, you may not boast of being able to explain the course of world history more completely, indeed to predetermine it, than we were able to do long before you, in possession of a law which you regard with contempt, the law of the *governance of God*, according to which God brings the greatest possible sum of good into actuality: a law which, even if it does not enable us to foretell the individual events of the world, nevertheless contains within itself their true and objective ground.

You do claim, it is true, to know a law which can be applied everywhere, and thus to the course of the world as well, and you promise us great things from this law. It is the favourite law of the *thesis*, of the *antithesis* issuing from the thesis, and of the *synthesis* which in turn sublates both within itself. But we think: if this law is in fact true and demonstrable, then we too shall be allowed to use it, and since we possess besides it another presupposition of the most fruitful consequences, namely the one just mentioned, of the governance of a personal God, we shall now be able to explain far [p. 33] more than you. But seen in the light of day, your highly praised law is neither demonstrable nor of any particular usefulness. Nowhere have you yet clearly proved that and why such a law must hold everywhere; you have not even communicated to us a clear definition of its meaning, but whenever one pressed for one, you have withdrawn behind all manner of vague expressions. This much, however, one gathers from everything: that you speak here neither of a *contradiction* or *opposition* that would be a contradiction or opposition in the proper sense, nor of a *sublation* of this contradiction or a *union* of the opposition that could be called a sublation or union in the proper sense. For a contradiction or opposition it is enough for you, whenever you happen to need one, that any *difference* whatever should obtain between the objects in question; hence you call, for example, the plant a refutation of the seed, the blossom a refutation of the leaves, and so on. Of the *sublating* of this contradiction, however, you yourselves declare that this word must be taken with such indeterminacy that it means both at once, an *annihilating* as well as a *preserving*. And so in the end your celebrated law says nothing other than that for every object some second one can be found which can in a certain sense be opposed to the first, and then for both perhaps a third which can in some way be regarded as the union of those two. But what consequences does one mean to derive from so indeterminate a law, even if it were perfectly true and proven? By what right may one boast that through this single proposition a wholly certain method of progression, *excluding all arbitrariness*, has been found for every scientific course of thought? —

[p. 34] In what way, to stay here with history alone, is that to follow from this principle which *Hegel*, and still more his pupils who go even further on some points, assert with regard to this science? How is it to follow from it, because every thing posited calls forth a kind of opposite and the two finally unite in a third, with incontrovertible necessity that every later state of our race must be a more perfect one, when (as has already been remarked) such a thing certainly cannot be asserted of every later state of an individual human being, or of any other being whatever, or of any aggregate of beings?

But you shall see how gladly we concede to you whatever is at all compatible with the truth. If you were to say, namely, that it is a bad habit of men, of the individual as well as of whole societies, immediately upon discovering certain merits or else defects in an object to exceed the right measure somewhat, in the first case in their esteem for it, in the second in their abhorrence and censure of it: then we would admit that you had put forward something very true, though also easily explained. If you were further to say that this exaggeration after some time — now by the same persons, now by others (in a very understandable way) — is noticed, and then (just as before) results in an exaggeration in the opposite sense, that is, in a neglect, now again going somewhat too far, of what was formerly overrated, and in a defence and exaltation, somewhat too venturesome, of what was formerly rejected: then we would gladly grant you this assertion as well. Now go further still and say that a large, indeed perhaps the largest part of the changes that have been brought about in human society by men’s own activity can be explained, now wholly, now in part, from this bad habit; add that through such fluctuations in their judgement men nevertheless gradually come [p. 35] nearer to the truth, especially when at last someone with enough level-headedness sets forth the truth lying in the middle clearly and distinctly before everyone’s eyes; build on this a ground for hoping that mankind really progresses; give this ground, if you like, the expression that the later insight, coming nearer to the truth or more perfect, has been won through the *union* of the formerly contradictory *extremes*: then you have indeed still saved a shadow of your principle, and we grudge you not this joy, provided only that you do not infer from it what cannot also be proved true in some other way.

As something indemonstrable without your presupposition we must already describe the assertion that the whole course of history, according to past, present and future, must necessarily be divisible into three periods related to one another as thesis, antithesis and synthesis. For it is self-evident that this assertion rests on no other ground than that untenable principle of dialectical trichotomy. It therefore stands without support once that principle falls. No better proven, too, is the assertion, repeated many times by Hegel himself, that *every people of the earth is destined* to realise the development of the world spirit *at a stage of its own*. How then is one to establish that the world spirit can develop in no other way, that is, that mankind can progress in no other way, than by there being for each stage of development or of progress precisely one *whole people* that finds itself at this stage? Could not someone else assert with equal right that it suffices if only a single human being reaches this stage? And yet another, with equal right, that it is required that all peoples, indeed all individual human beings, should at some time enter upon this stage? When we come to consider more closely what are passed off as these necessary stages of transition, we become aware of new arbitrary assumptions. Thus [p. 36] *Hegel* asserts that there have been, and had to be, peoples (the Orientals) in whom no one yet knew himself to be free, and others (the Greeks) in whom only some (the citizens) knew themselves to be free, and finally peoples (the Christian ones) in whom *all* know themselves to be free. Stranger still it sounds when *Cieskowski* assures us that in world history *all the* *spheres of being, thinking and doing* received their manifestation, so that not only the *logical* but also the *physical* and the *psychological* categories must find their application here; whence he further asserts that, for example, the Chinese spirit represents *mechanism*, that Athens corresponds to *dynamic* and Sparta to *static electricity*, that the two were united in the *electromagnetic* system of the Macedonian empire and were thus set against the *religion of light* of Persia, until at last, through the expansive and absorbing force of *heat*, which forms the *symbol of* *Rome*, they passed over into the latter; and so on. — We deliberately refrain from all remarks on this new play of images, and turn back to Hegel. How much that is arbitrary there is, too, in his assertion that *every people becomes world-historical* *only once*, which is supposed to follow from the fact that it can only once be the bearer of that spiritual principle which forms the stage currently present in the onward course of the world spirit! There is, after all, no such sharply defined boundary at all between what deserves to be called world-historical and what does not. And why, even supposing that history so far offered us no example of this kind, why should it not nevertheless be possible that a people which at a certain point of time exercised a really great influence on the whole of mankind, *perceptible* to us too, and was thus without any contradiction *world-historical*, should now sink into insignificance for centuries, and then nevertheless once again, perhaps in an entirely different domain than before, become influential [p. 37] and set the tone ? Still stronger objections, however, we must raise against what our new “historiosopher” has said *about the significance of great men*. Would it not, in the first place, already be unjust to be willing to grant the name of a *great man* only to one who has actually brought about a great and conspicuous change in his people, indeed in his whole age? And would it not be just as incorrect to count everyone who succeeded in something of the kind at once among the great men of mankind? For even a quite ordinary man, aided by favourable circumstances, can sometimes bring about a very far-reaching change; and conversely a man of the rarest powers and virtues can quite often fail in the execution of his great plan; indeed external circumstances can sometimes be of such a kind that they do not even permit him to attempt the great things of which he feels himself inwardly capable, and a particular strength of mind may show itself precisely in this, that he will not even attempt what is impossible in the present! — If this is so, with what truth can it be said “that whenever a great man is to step onto the world’s stage, the need for him is *felt* long beforehand; and when he leaves it, the fruits of his activity are still enjoyed long afterwards”? — But granted that for *history* only he is to be called great who has actually accomplished great things: how can it occur to you to say of every great man that he is “*the representative of his* *age*”? since it rather lies in the concept of a great man that he — though not free of all the defects and errors of his time — nevertheless towers above the ordinary measure of men, and is thus by no means fitted to give us an example of how people were in his time. — But enough of these specimens of modern wisdom in the treatment of *world history* in general. [p. 38] Let us now consider what has been taught about the history of *philosophy* in particular.

If it was already mistaken to assert that every later state of humanity as a whole is to be regarded as a result that has issued with necessity from all its earlier states, since there are, after all, many other things as well that exert a now greater, now lesser influence upon the state it is coming into: then it is all the more mistaken to say that every philosophical system which gained some currency at the time of its appearance developed with a kind of necessity out of those that preceded it. One may indeed doubt whether every author of a system that was received with great applause in its day had come to know all the systems that existed before, or even only all those that immediately preceded his own and were displaced by it, in all their doctrines and in the grounds of those doctrines, and had done so, moreover, before he became conscious of a single one of his own theorems. How many a great thinker had voiced the first original ideas, which we meet as essential parts of his philosophical system, already in earliest youth, at an age when not a single philosophical system was known to him even by name! How, further, can one deny that with every philosopher who has enriched the history of philosophy with a system of his own, besides the various doctrines he read in the writings of his predecessors, a good many other circumstances too, upbringing, temperament, health and sickness, outward position, the fortunes he lived through, and much else, had a considerable share in his philosophical system taking exactly this shape and no other; taking this shape not only in its practical parts and those connected with experience, but also in its most speculative portions? Does not the same thing happen in every other science? Or is it perhaps [p. 39] only the empirical sciences whose progress we daily see occasioned by the most fortuitous events (observations and experiments)? Does not the same hold of pure mathematics too? Is it not a mere accident that one mathematician fails to achieve the attempted integration of a differential expression, which another succeeds in fifty years later? Had Picard not measured a degree of the meridian and thereby corrected the size of the earth’s radius, which in Newton’s time was wrongly assumed: then, to judge by every ground of probability, the latter would never have written his *Principia mathematica* *philosophiae naturalis* [Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy]. — You say, to be sure, that such accidents change nothing in the essence; had it not been this man, you say, then another would have come forward with the same philosophical system, once the time had become ripe for it. But how do you prove this? It is true, no doubt, that in every science, philosophy included, there are certain steps which can hardly fail to be taken once certain others have been taken. Thus, after Newton had once shown that the law of attraction explains so many phenomena in the heavens and on earth, it was to be expected with confidence that people would gradually learn to explain a good many other things in this way too. But not every step is taken so easily and follows so necessarily from the preceding one. And can we deny that in every philosophical system some steps too have been taken which a later age recognised, or will yet recognise, as missteps? Shall we then really assert that these missteps too were all *necessary* missteps, that they were brought about not by the accidental outward circumstances and peculiarities of the thinker, but by the inner nature of the matter itself? — But to this question you have a twofold answer ready; for you either say that everything in a philosophical system that is incorrect does not [p. 40] belong to its *essential content*, but concerns only its garb; or you assure us that all the propositions which a later age drops not only counted as truth for the earlier one that held them, but also *were* truth, albeit only truth of a lower kind, which was already destined to be *sublated in a higher* one later on. — We must confess that neither the one nor the other of these two answers satisfies us. Not the first; for if you insist on regarding everything in a philosophical system that is incorrect in it not as belonging to its essential content but as an inessential appendage: then you will not be able to tell us what the essential content of a system is until you have come to agreement with yourselves, and have indeed been able to convince all of us, as to what in it is true or untrue. And is it not entirely contrary to all usage to want to distinguish content and garb, essence and accident in such a way? What would come of it if we attempted something similar in other sciences? Everywhere nothing but perfectly correct systems! Even our schoolboys, if they chose to imitate us in our wisdom, would all at once be skilful enough to produce all their arithmetic exercises free of error, since every error they committed in them would concern only something accidental and inessential. Whether humanity would gain anything by this progress, we must fairly doubt. — If each thing is to be called by its right name, then all those propositions in a philosophical system must be called *essential* which give the answer to questions that one wants answered precisely in philosophy, or which serve even only as premises for such answers, in such a way that if they were altered, these answers too would have to read differently. Thus the doctrine of the mortality or immortality of our souls is a supremely essential doctrine in every system of philosophy. If, then, in the one mortality is asserted, [p. 41] in the other immortality: then one of the two must necessarily be an erroneous system; erroneous because it errs not in its garb but in an essential point. — Worse still is it when you obscure the distinction between *true* and *false*, this so supremely important distinction, even within us, by trying to persuade us that even a pair of mutually contradictory propositions can be united and sublated in a higher truth. In order to be able to grant this in any way at all, we must first give the meanings of the words *true* and *false*, *contradiction* and *sublation* of this contradiction, such breadth and indeterminacy that all their usefulness ceases, and the confusion in speaking as well as in thinking becomes boundless. — Not a hair better is it when you put it that all the defects of a philosophical system always consist merely in its presenting the truth from only one side, that the various systems therefore complement one another, and that the most perfect would be the one which indicates the manner of their union. That this man and that man grasped the truth only one-sidedly, and that it is presented more completely only by uniting what both have said, is something we can properly say only in the case where both, speaking of one and the same object, noticed, the one one property of it, the other another, without denying the property noticed by the other. And even where this latter has happened, we shall be permitted, and that only where the precision of expressions is not at issue but indulgence may have its place, to declare, directing our attention solely to the affirmative part of the assertions on either side, that one need only unite what both said in order to have the complete truth. But when the difference between two systems consists precisely in this, that the one rejects what the other puts forward; when, as this case presents itself to us in the systems that have [p. 42] just now issued from the Hegelian school, the one defends from the same premises a personal God, personal immortality and the like, while the other refutes all this: by what euphemism are we to say in such a case that each presents the truth only one-sidedly, but that through the union of the doctrines of both it will dawn upon us in its full splendour?

To vindicate a kind of infallibility for philosophers will therefore by no means do. But if no single one, where he stands alone, is safe from error, may not perhaps he who is so fortunate as to win the assent of all or nearly all his contemporaries for his system give himself over to the confidence that he has produced a correct system? Or should it be possible that a system which has found general recognition (even if only for some years) should nevertheless be erroneous and deserve rejection? — Certainly we shall not be entitled to blame the adherents of a system received with general applause if they are strengthened in their trust by this applause; I mean, if in such a case they assume with greater confidence than in the opposite case that there lurks, in the proofs by which each single doctrine has been demonstrated to them, no fallacy or sophism that has escaped their attention; but no rational person will or may hold this to be *completely certain*. For experience has taught all too often that even systems which had enjoyed almost undivided applause through a long series of years later had to be rejected after all as misconceived and untenable, and that in proofs which thousands had held irrefutable fallacies were in the course of time discovered after all. Whom such experiences do not make cautious is no wise man; and whoever does not see for what reason it is in such purely speculative investigations, as [p. 43] the greater part of a philosophical system comprises, almost impossible to avoid all danger of error, is not even to be called very sensible. No, philosophers worthy of this name will of their own accord renounce the prerogative of infallibility for their doctrinal edifices, however loud the applause that meets them. The only question is whether we should not at least grant Hegel another of his apophthegms, namely that every system appearing later in time is always a more perfect one. If the talk here were solely of what is to be expected as *most probable* in *most cases*, and in particular where the one who comes later has come to know all the views of his predecessors: then it might indeed be said that more may be expected from a later thinker than from all his predecessors. And this would have to hold in the same way as of philosophy, of all the other human sciences and arts. But if we wish to pass the proposition off as something more than a merely probable expectation, in whose case it must always first be examined in some other way whether it is fulfilled or not, and Hegel’s expressions do in fact want the proposition to be regarded as a universally valid and necessary truth: then we likewise cannot agree. It is not always the case that he who comes later succeeds in improving what he alters in the system of his predecessor; often he departs from what needed no alteration because it was perfectly correct; often, too, he departs from something that is in itself incorrect, but puts in place of a not especially momentous error a whole web of errors that are far more dangerous and pernicious; his new system is to be called not an advance but a retrogression in our knowledge. But how, you ask, is it possible that his system should nevertheless be received with applause, and with preponderant applause? I answer that this can happen merely [p. 44] because a good many outward circumstances combine in its favour; perhaps because it is proclaimed from a chair which, erected in an eminent place, shines far and wide into men’s eyes and draws a large following from all sides; or because the personality of the teacher has a great deal that is attractive and commends him; or because his manner is at least of such a kind as to make youth believe that he, and he alone, has happily found the philosophers’ stone so long sought; or because a new language he has introduced offers everyone an easy means of giving himself, without thorough inquiry, merely by the use of certain turns of phrase, the air of a profound thinker; or because in the new system theorems occur which harmonise excellently with the newest and most favoured views in other sciences (in theology, jurisprudence, aesthetics and the like), or are even so well fitted to the newest observations in the field of the empirical sciences that it looks as though one could have derived the latter from the former in advance; or, worse still, theorems that bribe not merely the head but also the heart of men, and flatter their sensuality, their pride, their love of ease or other passions; or finally because it is the powerful of the earth themselves who, by the most diverse means at their command, promote the spread of such views. If even one or another of these favouring circumstances can contribute exceedingly much to the spread of a philosophical system: what must happen when several of them, perhaps even all, act in combination? Merely from the fact, then, that a new philosophical system has succeeded in displacing another that ruled before it, no thoughtful person can or may conclude that the former is more perfect than the latter; just as, by such a way of concluding, it would also be inexplicable, what history teaches us clearly enough, that philosophical systems and doctrines which had been displaced [p. 45] by a newer system were after a longer or shorter interval drawn forth again from their oblivion. And indeed, is not something similar happening right now before our eyes? Has not the critical philosophy, which through the achievements of Schelling and Hegel had already been pushed entirely into the background, again won a great many new adherents in the last few years?

What, then, are we still to say of the assertion that every advance in philosophical knowledge takes place according to the law of thesis, antithesis and synthesis? We already know how little reason there is to regard this law as a wholly universally valid one. That, however, the philosophical systems in particular, as they followed one another in time, by no means conform to this law, anyone can convince himself who will but question experience itself and receive its answer with an unprejudiced mind. For only through the most forced presentations of the philosophical systems handed down to us by history, or better said, through the most forced *distortions* of them, and through the most arbitrary banishment of all those that could by no mutilation be fitted to the bed of Procrustes from the register of the systems alone deemed worthy of the name and of attention, could one at a pinch produce but a faint appearance as if this law had been followed. A single circumstance brings it about that we do sometimes perceive among successive systems a relation somewhat reminiscent of that law, namely the bad habit of men already mentioned above (of which philosophical minds too have admittedly been guilty at times), *of readily passing from one extreme to the* *other*. Thus it came about that when the author of a system had erred on a certain point, his successor, who perceived the error, overstepped the right measure in his censure, and, rejecting along with the erroneous some of what was true, fell [p. 46] from one error into the opposite; whereupon after some time a third could come forward who, in the place of both systems, erring as they did in opposite directions through their exaggeration, set a new one that kept to the right mean, or (as was said in a highly improper manner) that comprehended the contradictions in a higher unity and sublated them. This holds in particular of *Hegel* himself, that he loved the exaggerated, or (to speak in his language) liked to push the matter to its extreme; but must every philosopher do this? and would it not be a gross insult to our human reason if we had to admit that all progress in philosophy can of necessity be brought about only through such exaggerations? Do mathematicians, say, proceed in this way to advance their science?

After all this we may leave it to everyone’s judgement whether it is so completely settled, as not indeed *Hegel* himself, but some of his admirers have ventured to assert, that philosophy has already reached its culmination in the present, so that its next fate will only be “*to become popular and to flatten out into the depths*.” We note only one thing. In making this prediction, it seems to be conceded that the Hegelian philosophy, as it now lies before us, still lacks a clear, distinct presentation. And how indeed could this be denied, since even the most intimate pupils of the master are at odds with one another over the sense of his doctrines, even the most important ones, and the one accuses the other of having wholly misunderstood the teacher? It must also be granted us that in the writings of this philosopher, so many as have so far come to light, nowhere are there to be found precise explanations of the sense of the words and turns of phrase he employs, so that one must laboriously guess what he roughly had in mind only from the context and on the presupposition that he meant to say something rational everywhere. It is just as undeniable [p. 47] that *Hegel* seldom states in clear words on what grounds he puts forward his assertions, still less whether what he has said contains all the grounds known to him, or whether he has still others, etc. The natural consequence of all this is that the cognitions which the reader draws from Hegel’s writings, whatever other virtue they may possess, in any case lack the necessary *distinctness*. But distinctness is precisely one of the most important properties of genuinely philosophical knowledge. For if we cannot give an account even to ourselves (let alone to others) of the concepts we connect with our own words and turns of phrase, and of the premises on which each of our judgements rests: how shall we be sure that we do not err in many ways, and that our own concepts and assertions do not, as it were, transform themselves in our own mouths while we are still speaking of them? — But the worst of it is<sup>10</sup> that this lack of distinctness in the Hegelian philosophy does not merely exist *de facto* [in fact], but that an essential change in its doctrines themselves would first have to be made if one wished to free it of this defect. For there can be no question at all in this philosophy of coming to an understanding about a concept by a definition stating whether it is simple or of what parts it is composed, since the master has declared the view that a concept is composed of others to be a *barbarous* view. Moreover *Hegel* assures us that *speculative truths* could never be completely expressed by simple propositions. — So long, [p. 48] then, as his pupils hold fast to these pronouncements and thus make no change in the doctrines, there is surely but little hope that their presentations will enjoy greater distinctness. But does not precisely this furnish a strong proof that this philosophical system is still fairly far removed from the summit of perfection? Several of its admirers have indeed actually recognised and admitted this. The same *Cieskowski*, for example, who assured us in one place that philosophy had reached its culminating point through Hegel, makes us in another (p. 105) the remarkable confession “that an end must at last be made of the contempt for the understanding, and that it would be easy to show that when one often believes oneself to *rise* above the understanding, one does not oneself understand what one wants,” and on p. 133 he says even: “those who find themselves at a considerable height of speculation are sometimes so seized with dizziness that they see clearly only what lies just at their own *level*, not what really remains lower and through which they themselves had to wind their way. But what really towers *above them* they displace, by an optical illusion, into a lower region, and with the firmest consciousness they hold it *bona fide* [in good faith] to be something they have long since passed beyond.” He therefore hopes “that no one will assert that the system of philosophy is already absolutely *constituted*,” and speaks “of *gaps*, indeed even of wrongly executed determinations” occurring in it. Others express themselves more strongly still; let it suffice us in conclusion merely to refer to *Chalybäus* (in Fichte’s journal already mentioned, vol. 3, no. 1, p. 189 ff.). After he, in agreement with several other adherents of Hegel, has complained that the chief point and pivot of the whole speculative philosophy, the principle of the identity of being and thinking, has been demonstrated neither in the *Logic* (where it is already required for understanding the very first proposition) nor in the *Phenomenology*- (to [p. 49] which Hegel referred) nor anywhere else, he censures the wavering in Hegel’s writings in the following words: “All such utterances in Hegel one is supposed to take now one way, now another; truth is supposed to be now the substantial indifference, now again the existential concretion . . . . One could set up a whole collection of turns of phrase in divided columns facing each other, of which one always states the opposite of the other, and which is chosen by the Hegelian school at will, according as now the one, now the other is to be maintained . . . . With joy would the reviewer return beneath Hegel’s banner, where *one philosophises so comfortably,* *and soon finds good friends and patronage*, if only he could free himself of these stumbling-blocks. He still seeks to keep his mind and receptiveness for the doctrine of the great master continually unprejudiced, but *the deeper he strives to penetrate,* *the more significantly and clearly does the one-eyedness of this system also stare back at him from the deepest ground*.” — Men who make so candid a confession surely deserve high respect, and we need not fear to offend them if we invite them to look about and see whether the outlines of a system that can afford more satisfaction than the Hegelian one are not perhaps already available.<sup>11</sup>

<sup>10</sup> And this latter, men of Hegel’s school have regarded not at all as an evil, but as something quite in order. Therein, they supposed, consists precisely the movement of thought; thereby it proves itself to be not a merely dead mechanism but a living organism; and many were not even content if a concept merely passes over gradually into one akin to it, but they rejoice when it turns round into its direct opposite!

### Zusätze zu der Abhandlung: Ueber Hegel’s Begriff von der Geschichte überhaupt und von der Geschichte der Philosophie insbesondere. — Addenda to the Treatise: On Hegel’s Concept of History in General and of the History of Philosophy in Particular

What is understood by the philosophy of an age? Does one<sup>12</sup> count as belonging to it every system of philosophy that was put forward or held fast in this age by any single individual? Must it have been printed? or is it enough if it was merely composed in manuscript, or merely carried about in someone’s head? And how far must it have been developed in order to be called a system? Must all the individual branches into which we have learned to divide philosophy, perhaps only in the most recent times, have been worked out, or is it enough if only the main principles have been put forward? — How many philosophical systems, differing greatly from one another, will one not have to count in the same age, according as one answers these questions this way or that?

<sup>12</sup> On the concept Bolzano connected with the word philosophy, see his valuable treatise: Was ist Philosophie? Vienna 1849, published by W. Braumüller. Ed.

Or does one understand by the philosophy of a certain age only such a philosophical system as was generally accepted in this age? Then there might well be no age that had a philosophy. — Or only one that enjoyed a very large number of [p. 51] adherents? Then we ask, how large must this number be? The relatively largest, perhaps? What if two or three systems exist which had an equal number of adherents? or if it cannot be ascertained which system had the larger number of adherents? perhaps because in one country everyone who has learned to write believes he must write a printed work, while in another scarcely one in hundreds resolves to send anything to the press? Or should regard perhaps also be had to the nation among which a philosopher lived? whether it was receptive to his philosophy or not? whether he lived among people who stood at a still very low level of education, so that they grasped nothing of what he occupied himself with, or so that they were just able to accept it by rote, or so that they were capable of judging it? In the first and the last case his philosophical system will count very few adherents, in the second very many. Shall we then still wish to assert that a philosophical system is the development of one preceding it, when its author did not know the preceding one at all, or came to know it only later, after he had already fixed his first and most important concepts or borrowed them from some other (already forgotten) system? —

Ch. H. *Weisse* asserts (Zeitschrift für Philos. und spec. Theol., ed. Fichte, vol. 1, no. 2, new series, p. 241 ff.) that “philosophy, expressly insofar as it is conceived as science, as a whole of cognition steadily developing and forming itself in the spirit of the human race, is and remains subject to *an* *unceasing organic transformation of all its parts*; that this science has the remarkable *peculiarity* that *in it every newly discovered* *result* reacts upon the preceding ones and *more or less thoroughly transforms them*.” — In a word, this means nothing other than: in philosophy not a single proposition has yet been correctly put forward and correctly proved. For if this were so, how and why would something in what has gone [p. 52] before have to be *altered* by every new result? — The same author says on p. 242: “No philosophical system that *deserves* this name and *counts* in history would be what it is *without the* *totality of those preceding it*. Each of them is rooted in the preceding ones, etc.” I ask, why is this the case? In the end you declare only that system to be *philosophical* and *one that counts* which issued from the preceding ones. But then you have asserted a tautology. — It goes on: “The later system takes on the *content* of the preceding ones, but not their *form*; which is illustrated by the image of an intensive magnitude, which does not arise through external increase, but with every quantum changes the *form* and constitution of the object as well” (of which I know nothing). Yet “this image” is also supposed “not to be adequate, because the concept of *truth* as such, as it is outside all human systems, falls as little under the category of intensive as of extensive magnitude.” — “The various systems (p. 243 f.) differ in turn not so much through the difference of the solution they give for the same problems as rather through the difference of the problems themselves. It is a peculiarity of philosophical cognition” (another one!) “that every solution of a given problem leads to new problems, and that of these new problems each in its own way again, like the earlier ones, embraces the whole of cognition, each therefore, within the standpoint from which it is raised, makes necessary a new shaping of philosophical science.”

### III. Ueber den Begriff des Organismus nebst einigen damit verwandten Begriffen. — III. On the Concept of the Organism, together with Some Related Concepts

Omnis organismus revera est mechanismus, sed exquisitior atque ut sic dicam, divinior. [Every organism is in truth a mechanism, but a more exquisite and, so to speak, a more divine one.] Leibnitius in respons. ad Stahl. observ. [Leibniz, in his replies to Stahl’s

observations.]

#### Vorerinnerung. — Preliminary Remark

The words *organic* and *organism* have, as is well known, become so fashionable nowadays that one cannot pick up a book without meeting them at least once on every page. In every human association, and in civil association (the state) in particular, people praise an organic coherence; they want to find organism in every language, and to demonstrate organism in every science precisely when its doctrines have been so badly connected that the first proposition receives its supposed proof only from the conclusion, and through what stands there as the last consequence drawn from it. This meaning, which is not only very broad and highly vague but also an entire abuse of the word, we shall here leave wholly aside. For we are now seeking only to determine precisely that meaning of the word organism which the *natural scientist* has in mind when he distinguishes *machine* and *organism* as a higher and a lower concept; when he raises the question whether there are also bodies, especially among the rigid ones, which are entirely without organism; when he counts animals and plants among the indisputably organic objects, but in the case of crystals already falls into some indecision as to whether he may rightly call them wholly inorganic; when, accordingly, he in any case recognises several *levels* of organisation, and, for example, grants to certain genera of animals a [p. 54] far more perfect organism than to several others, and to the entire animal kingdom a higher one than to the kingdom of plants. We now set ourselves the task of raising to clear consciousness in ourselves the concepts which he connects with the words just named, that is, of taking apart the more proximate components out of which he (perhaps without being aware of it himself) composes those concepts, which are certainly not simple. Since, however, this definition, at least as I believe it must be framed, cannot be properly understood and appreciated unless certain other concepts are set out beforehand, namely those of a merely *mechanical* and of a merely *chemical* change and force, I shall also try to determine these, together with a few others closely connected with them.

#### Abhandlung. — Exposition

§ 1. For reasons belonging to metaphysics we are compelled to assume that no two substances (indeed no two things at all) are completely alike, but that the differences which obtain between any two *bounded*, or — what amounts to the same — *dependent or created substances* can in the end always be reduced to a merely finite *difference of degree* (to a certain more or less); so that essentially the same *fundamental forces* are to be found in all these substances, only developed in different degrees, and that those forces which the less perfect substances may still lack, or at least seem to lack, will gradually appear at higher levels and begin to manifest themselves.

§ 2. We must, however, assume in every substance at least *two* kinds of forces (that is, of properties through which it is the cause of something actual): *immanent* ones (acting within) and *transient* ones (acting outwards). The idea of a *matter*, i.e. of an aggregate of substances whose individual simple parts, namely the substances themselves, had *no forces at all*, and thus [p. 55] *effected nothing and could effect nothing*, is in my view just as erroneous as the idea of mere *forces*, that is, of properties supposed to exist without a *substance* to which they belong as the predicate proper to this subject. Just as incorrect, in my view, is the claim that a single substance should have at most only a single force (fundamental force), or (as some have even wished in more recent times) that every simple real should have only a single property (*quality*).

§ 3. As *immanent* forces, that is, as forces whose proximate effect is still to be found in the substance itself to which they belong, one may, according to a division which I need not justify further here, since we shall not refer to it again, consider the following: 1) a force of *forming ideas*, 2) one of *sensing*, 3) one of *desiring* or *abhorring*, and 4) one of *willing*. It is only among the *transient* forces, that is, those through which one substance acts immediately upon another, that we are without doubt to look for those whose conceptual determination is to occupy us here, the *mechanical*, *chemical* and *organic* forces.

§ 4. It is to be well noted, however, that all the forces inherent in *dependent* substances act only in *time*, that is, bring about mere *changes*: either, like the immanent forces, changes that take place in the very substance in which they inhere, or, like the transient ones, changes that are called forth by them in another substance. The contradictions which people in recent times claimed to have found in the concept of change, and which were touched on already by the Sceptics, though hardly with serious intent or in the belief that they were insoluble, disappear of themselves, in my view, as soon as one has properly clarified for oneself the concepts of substance, property and time that occur here.

§ 5. It is further certain to me that every bounded substance acts upon every other so as to change it, and does so [p. 56] continually, so that each also continually suffers reciprocal influences (reactions) from all the rest.

§ 6. Every bounded substance, further, is at every moment of time at a determinate point of *space*; and it is precisely in these *spatial relations* of it that the ground lies which determines the *changes* that the substances, given their respective properties and forces, bring about and suffer, the one in the other, within a given time.

§ 7. But just as these *spatial relations* obtaining at any given moment belong, on the one hand, to the complete ground from which it comes about that the substances, by means of their forces, bring about precisely these changes, the one in the other, within a given time: so, conversely, these changes produced in their *interior* often bring about an alteration in their local relations. Thus, for example, a certain local relation between the two substances of which the one is the thinking being in myself and the other the thinking being in my friend — the circumstance, namely, that we are near enough to each other to recognise one another just now — can become the cause of the desire arising in both of us to speak with one another; and this change taking place in us can in turn become the cause of our altering the spatial relation between us still more considerably, namely of our stepping so close to each other that we can also begin a conversation with one another.

§ 8. Just as it is conceivable that in particular cases the change which given substances undergo within a certain time in their *spatial relations* to one another is quite insignificant, or even that none at all occurs, as, say, when one and the same substance is pulled with equal strength by the surrounding ones in two opposite directions: so it is also conceivable, and indeed the case is probably much more frequent, that the [p. 57] change which goes on in the *inner state* of a substance during a certain time is so *slight* that we are quite unable to perceive it; and this even while its spatial relations to many other substances, and to ourselves too, have altered to a very noticeable degree. Thus, for example, I perceive no change in this wall that I am just now looking at during this short time, and have no reason to believe either that any significant change is taking place in the individual substances of which it consists, although the spatial relations between it and myself, who am walking up and down here, and many other objects round about, are changing very noticeably; which, to be sure, cannot be wholly without any influence upon it.

§ 9. It is certain in any case that we can separate *in our* *thoughts* the alteration which the local relations of two or more (or, if one will, even of infinitely many) substances undergo from the change which these same substances suffer in their interior (whether they undergo it as cause or as effect of the former). It lies in the word that we may then call such a change a mere *change of place* or *motion*, or, with a Greek word, a merely *mechanical* phenomenon. And that which we think of as the *proximate ground* producing it we may call a merely *moving* or *mechanical* force. Thus we regard the collision of two balls (no matter whether hard, soft or elastic) as a merely *mechanical process*, insofar as we imagine that nothing but places are altered here. For the partial flattening of these balls at the point of collision, and the moving apart again of the parts that had approached one another, we regard as mere changes of place, without thinking of what may perhaps be going on in the interior of these substances meanwhile.

§ 10. The name *force* is admittedly given only in an improper sense to what we think of as the ground of such a *sum of mere changes of place*, since force in the proper sense designates [p. 58] only something *actual* which is the cause of something else that is actual, whereas the *places* of things, and hence also the *changes* in them, do not belong to actual things. Yet since closer consideration shows that in every change of place a force acting outwards must always have been active (as *primus motor* [prime mover]) in at least one of the substances present, this generally accepted usage may well always be excused.

§ 11. If we perceive a change that has taken place between given substances in which we do not wish to abstract from what goes on in their *interior*, but on the contrary take this into view: then I permit myself to call the process considered a *dynamic* one, and the proximate cause which we think of as the ground of this change a *dynamic force*. When, for example, we see oxygen and hydrogen, ignited by a spark, rapidly turn into water, something takes place which I call a dynamic process, inasmuch as I do not doubt that a change has occurred here not merely in the *places* but also in the *forces*, namely in the *outward*-acting forces of the substances involved, and that an activity not present before has set in. In my view, the process observed here consists roughly in the following. Through the ether streaming out of that spark (= caloric = that almost homogeneous stuff, still at the lowest level of formation, which is spread through the whole universe), the equilibrium was disturbed at first only between some of the distinctive atoms by which the two kinds of gas differ from each other (namely between some oxygen atoms and some hydrogen atoms), and these were thereby induced, each accompanied by a quantity of ether appropriate to it, to assemble into groups of their own and, by the strength of the [p. 59] attractive forces awakened in them, to move so close together that the volume of the little corpuscle which they now filled with their ether spheres (a little particle of water) amounted to only about a thousandth part of the volume they had occupied before. Now as the oxygen or hydrogen atoms, or even mere ether atoms, that happened not to be taken up in this formation of groups went on disturbing the equilibrium in the immediately adjacent portions of the two gases, and indeed disturbed it over an ever-widening extent, the change spread ever more rapidly, until both gases were converted into water. But however much the view of this process, here only indicated in rough outline, may differ from the views of others, everyone is nonetheless of the opinion that in this phenomenon peculiar forces of attraction or repulsion manifest themselves, which are awakened by their coming together in the so-called *smallest particles* or *molecules* of the bodies present here. And precisely therein lies the reason why the whole process is declared to be something more than a merely *mechanical* process.

§ 12. The somewhat unusual expression *a dynamic change*, and the expression *a dynamic force*, which on account of its pleonasm is admittedly still more objectionable, I permit myself only for want of another for the concept of that genus under which the two species of *chemical* and *organic* change and force fall, in a manner that will be shown directly. Everyone knows, however, that others too have already used these expressions, and indeed in roughly the same meaning, as for instance *Hildebrand* in the Anfangsgründe der dynamischen Naturlehre, § 1032.

§ 13. If a change, merely according to what we may now wish to take into view in it, is to extend as far as the *inner* *state* of the substances involved in it, then the least we can [p. 60] assume here is indisputably that the change occurs in one of their *forces acting outwards*. For the forces of a substance acting in its *interior* are certainly all to be regarded as higher and nobler. After all, most natural scientists, not to say all, still assume to this day that in certain substances, namely in those whose consideration they make their very particular object, in the so-called *material* stuffs, not even the slightest degree of *sensation or idea* is to be met with, and still less any *will*. However erroneous this may be, it is indisputable that only substances already standing at a higher level of development possess such *psychical forces* in a degree high enough to make their presence known to us through their effects. We are therefore in any case right if, for the least kind of action of one substance upon the interior of another, we require no more than an action upon its outward-acting force, and indeed upon that force which we have already come to know as the least, that is, upon its *mechanical* force. Indeed, in order to stay with the very least, we must restrict ourselves solely to that motive force which suffices to produce a change in the spatial relation *merely between two substances* (the one acting and the other suffering the action); in other words, we may require nothing more than an action upon the *force of attraction or repulsion* of the one substance towards the other. Now if a dynamic process satisfies this condition just described, that is, if we perceive in it a change that extends as far as the inner state of the substances involved, but no further than to their *forces of attraction or repulsion*, then I call this process a *merely chemical* one, and the *forces* by which it has been proximately brought about, and which are therefore capable of [p. 61] producing changes in the powers of attraction or repulsion of a substance, mere *chemical forces*. Of this kind, for example, is the phenomenon when iron comes into contact with water, to which we may have added some sulphuric acid to speed up the process, and we perceive that hydrogen gas is evolved, while the iron is converted into iron oxide and, when sulphuric acid is present, also, through combination with the latter, into green vitriol. For here it is the iron which, in its distinctive atoms, acts so as to change the distinctive atoms of the water by means of the attraction obtaining between the two, and which, by attracting the oxygen atoms of the latter, passes into iron oxide, while the hydrogen atoms thus set free let the ether adhering to them spread out into a larger space and so constitute hydrogen gas. The sulphuric acid accelerates this process in that, through its great attraction to the iron oxide, it forms green vitriol with it and so facilitates the decomposition of the water. In this process, then, forces indisputably manifest themselves which act upon the attraction between the substances present here; but of influences of a higher kind we find no trace; we therefore call the whole process a merely chemical process.

§ 14. If, in the opposite case, a certain change is of such a kind that the assumption of merely chemical forces does not suffice to explain it; if, in some one of the substances occurring here, or perhaps even in several, we must presuppose certain outward-acting forces capable of something more than merely acting so as to change the forces of attraction and repulsion of others: then I call the process an *organic* one; and the outward-acting forces of those substances which we must presuppose as the proximate cause of these alterations in the forces of attraction or repulsion I call *organic forces*. Thus I say [p. 62] of every plant that is still living (growing) that *organic* forces manifest themselves in it, because I perceive changes that I am unable to explain to myself by mere chemical forces, by mere alterations in the forces of attraction or repulsion of the substances present here.

§ 15. This definition of an organic process agrees entirely, I believe, with what hitherto usage in the mouths of natural scientists so called. Suppose an artist succeeded, by combining various bodies, partly solid and partly fluid, in producing an object that bore the most deceptive resemblance to a plant or (if one will) even to an animal, that gradually enlarged itself from within (grew), and was even able to produce its like, i.e. other bodies similar to it, and so on: no natural scientist would nevertheless call the body a truly *organic* one as soon as it was proved to him that, and how, all this was brought about by the mere application of mechanical and chemical forces.

§ 16. But nothing more than what has just been said is required for an organic process either. In particular, it is by no means required that *a cause acting according to purposes must* *make itself known in it*. For true as it is that in all the organisms we meet with on earth we perceive a host of arrangements which, on the presupposition of the existence of a rational author of them — God — may and must with full right be regarded as *purposes* that this author set himself in producing these bodies: still, this does not belong essentially to the *concept* of an organic change or of an organic body. For even if we were not in the least able to guess the purpose of a phenomenon before us, we should nevertheless be ready to declare it an organic one, if only we felt obliged, in order to explain it, to have recourse to certain outward-acting forces [p. 63] capable of producing something more than mere forces of attraction or of repulsion.

§ 17. There are, however, *organic processes of a higher and a* *lower kind*. If formations were put before us in which it was merely the way they enlarge themselves from within outwards (grow) that we are unable to explain from the mere presence of certain mechanical and chemical forces, we should presumably place them at the *lowest* level of organic products. They would appear to us at a somewhat higher level if the way in which *several others* gradually arise from one or a few such bodies, that is, the way they *propagate*, were also incomprehensible from mere mechanical and chemical forces: we should call them *plants*. If, moreover, we perceived *movements* in them for whose explanation we had to presuppose a force acting not merely outwards but within a simple substance, that is, a *psychical* force, say a force of sensation: then we should call them *living organisms* or *animals*.

§ 18. Of a body which does not now, but did at some earlier time, exhibit *organic* changes according to the definition given in § 14, we say that it has ceased to *live*, or that it is a *dead, deceased organism*. Since this expression is used even when the organism in question was a merely *plant-like* one, to which common opinion ascribes no sensation, one sees that a kind of *life* is granted even to these organisms, no doubt only because of their great resemblance to animal organisms.

§ 19. It is still to be asked in which case we declare a whole before us to be a *single* organism or several organisms that are here merely connected in some way or other. I think we do the former when we see before us a whole in which every [p. 64] organic part, that is, every part which itself still presents phenomena that we cannot explain by any merely mechanical or chemical forces, stands with the rest in a connection that is again *organic*, i.e. such that effects are produced in it which cannot be completely derived from merely mechanical or chemical forces. Of this kind, for example, is the connection of the mother with the embryo in her womb, which, as is well known, drives roots into the uterus and is nourished by it. If, on the other hand, the connection is merely mechanical or merely chemical, we distinguish *several* organisms. Thus, for example, the tooth that has been inserted into the gap in a jaw is a foreign organism, and moreover now a dead one; the pea that begins to sprout when laid in an issue is a foreign organism, and one still living. In the same way, the maggots and worms that are generated in a deceased organism are obviously foreign organisms, and we cannot therefore call it one that has come to life again.

§ 20. The definition of the concept of an organism set out here may well prompt some to ask: *What, then, may the forces* that we call *organic*, and designate here only by the general *mark* that they are certain outward-acting forces which in any case bring about something *more* than mere attraction and repulsion, *be in themselves? In what does the more that they are* *supposed to bring about properly consist?* — To this question, speaking generally, I know no other answer than that these forces *in themselves* are just that *which brings about those properties belonging exclusively to organisms*. If the definition of a concept is correct, it indicates, after all, only the *essence*, that is, what certain objects *are and must be in themselves, insofar as we* *wish to think of them as objects of this concept*.

[p. 65] § 21. Not to be confused with the question just discussed is the following one, which is admittedly related to it. Since by organic forces we understand only such outward-acting forces as can be regarded as *the proximate cause* of an organic change (among which the coming into being of an organism itself is also to be counted), we ourselves indicate that there may be, and indeed actually are, a number of more remote causes as well. *Which, then, are these?* To this I reply that these more remote causes must in turn be different according to the difference of the result itself that is to be brought about and according to the difference of the circumstances under which it is to come about. Thus, for example, every movement of our hands, even one that occurs quite without, indeed against, our will, say through a cramp or through a blow coming from outside, is to be regarded as an organic process, because in it certain changes certainly occur in our muscles, nerves and other parts which cannot be explained from mere mechanical or chemical forces; but already by the designation of such a movement, when we call it an *involuntary* one, we ourselves indicate that we count at least among its more remote causes some quite different ones from those in the case where we call it a *voluntary* one. For in the latter case we expressly designate the *resolution of the will* of our soul as that cause which (admittedly only through the mediation of our outward-acting forces) brought about this movement of the hand. It is still more evident that quite different *forces* must be active when a quite different *result* is to appear: when an organism already present is merely to be developed further, that is, merely to *grow*, or is first to be produced; when this is to happen through others of the same genus by way of *propagation* or *generation*, or in some other [p. 66] way, as in the case where the *first individuals* of a certain genus are to be produced, and so on. It might well be that, to explain the manner in which a certain organism came into being in this last-mentioned case, we find ourselves compelled to have recourse to the *highest psychical forces*, to the presupposition of a being who vastly surpasses us in wisdom and power; whereas the mere coming into being of an organism by way of propagation or generation presupposes little or no understanding, not even a noticeable *faculty of sensation*, and thus *life*.

§ 22. Finally, the question might be answered here whether, and in what sense, a distinction between *artificial* and *natural* organisms could also be justified. I reply: this may indeed be justified, provided that by *an artificial organism*, whether it be a *self-subsistent whole* or a mere *organic part*, one understands only an organism of such a kind as owes its *origin* or *modification* (further development) in *a considerable degree to* *the cooperation of human art*, that is, here, not to a deed performed merely by instinct, but to certain *actions resting on* *deliberation and learned knowledge*. Where this is not the case, where the origin and modification of a given organism came about through mere *nature*, that is, here, without the intervention of human activity, or at least through no human *art*, or where the share of the latter is in any case regarded as a very *inconsiderable* one, the object may be called a *natural or* *naturally originated organism*. A plant, on the other hand, which grows in a certain place merely because we happened to sow its seed there, does indeed exist through our cooperation; but since the share which human art has in its existence [p. 67] is so inconsiderable, we shall not be inclined to count it among the artificially produced organisms. Not even when it is owing to our diligent care (watering, pruning and the like) that it thrives very luxuriantly shall we presume to give it this name. Only when our art succeeds in bringing about *more considerable changes*, e.g. in the form of its blossom, in the taste of its fruits, etc., shall we call its present organism an artificially ennobled one. In some cases, as is readily understood from this definition, it may be disputed whether we ought to count a given body among the natural or among the artificially produced organisms. So with the cereal grains, if, as some believe, they are nowhere to be found wild and only gradually became, under the hand of man, what we now call oats, rye, wheat and the like. For however significant these differences may be, it would still properly have been undesigned doing rather than art proper that gradually perfected them to such a degree.

§ 23. The more trouble has been taken over the determination of some of the concepts defined here, especially that of the *organism*, and the more the definitions attempted differ partly from the one I have ventured to set up here, partly among themselves, the more decidedly it is incumbent upon me not to pass over those attempts of others entirely in silence, as though I held them unworthy of any attention. Rather, I assure the reader that I have carefully weighed all of them, as many as I have had occasion to become acquainted with; here, however, in order not to become too prolix, I shall indeed content myself with examining only a few which seem to me the most noteworthy.

§ 24. Here belongs without doubt the definition which *Leibniz* put forward in the Monadology, when, certainly with deep insight, he defined the *organism* as a machine of such a [p. 68] kind as is composed, down to its smallest parts, of other machines that contribute to the purpose of the whole. It is undeniable that one will not be able to boast of any machine built by human hand that, when taken apart down to its smallest parts, it presents machines so arranged that they correspond to the purpose of the whole as much as is at all possible, that is, correspond in a degree which could not have been better attained by any other arrangement of them. Iron, wood, ivory, or whatever other *materials* the human artificer may choose from the supply present in nature for the construction of his machine, will by no means have nothing but such properties and arrangements as would be the most desirable of all for him; rather, one material is perhaps too hard for him, another too soft, one too pliable, another too rigid, one too heavy, another too porous, a third not so transparent as he would wish, and so on. But does the mark which Leibniz here set up, taken in all strictness, belong even to organisms alone, even to the most perfect ones? Only of the *world as a whole* might it hold in the fullest sense of the word that all its parts and arrangements, down to the smallest and seemingly most insignificant, correspond *most perfectly* to the purpose which the Creator set himself, that is, as perfectly as is at all *possible* in itself; or (if this is to be expressed still more clearly) correspond so fully that the assumption of a still more fitting (more perfect) arrangement would contradict some purely theoretical conceptual truth. Of the *universe* alone does this hold, I say; of all other organisms, on the other hand, as of every single part of the world (be it organic or not), only this much can be said: that it corresponds to the purpose to be attained through the universe in such measure as an object of this kind, in such relations of time and space, is just able to. A great many of the organisms we meet with on earth, indeed [p. 69] properly all of them, suffer from certain imperfections, have certain arrangements which do not correspond so perfectly to the well-being of the creature inhabiting them as would be the case with other arrangements not impossible *in* *themselves*, which, however, the connection of the whole did not permit to be given them. But if, in order to remedy this fault in the definition, we were to declare, say, that for an organism to be present we require only a machine whose arrangements, down to the smallest parts, satisfy most perfectly not indeed the purpose which some human being assigns to it, but the one which *God himself* has with it: then the definition becomes too wide again. For we should then have to raise to the rank of an organism not merely plants and animals, but also every machine made by human hand and every object whatever; for each of them corresponds most perfectly to the purpose which God himself has with it, since there are no purposes at all which the Almighty, if he has really set them for himself, would not attain.

§ 25. A definition that is very widely current even in our own day is the one first devised by *Kant*, which, however, since natural science has become so hostile to the assumption of *purposes*, a German philosopher has only recently put into the following words: “Organism is present in a whole when *each part exists only through* the other, and *all* only through *the* *one force pervading the whole*.” — This definition, taken literally as it stands, contains a contradiction. No object *A* can exist through another *B*, and this in turn through the former. If *B* is the cause of *A*, then *A* cannot in turn be the cause of *B*. What, further, is that *force pervading the whole*, through which the whole exists, supposed to indicate here? Hardly anything other than a complete cause of the whole. But such a cause is had by *every whole*, indeed by every part of it as well; in general by every substance, indeed by everything actual — with [p. 70] the exception of the one *unconditionally actual being*, which, grounded in no other, is itself the ground of all the rest. This definition, then, has in fact entirely miscarried. Meanwhile it is not so incomprehensible how it could arise and be spread abroad at a time when clear thinking is so little loved. It was noticed (for what was easier to notice?) that animals and plants do not come into being in such a way that their individual members (which are themselves something organic), e.g. an eye, a lung, a leaf, arise *each by itself* and only then join together or are joined together; further, that these members stand in *reciprocal relation* to one another, that, e.g., the hands suit the feet, the heart the lung. This *fitting-for-one-* *another*, which admittedly often goes so far that the one part contributes to the preservation or growth of the other, and this in turn serves the former, — was described in the very imprecise words that *one part is grounded in the other*; whereas at most it can be said that each part has received certain properties because the rational author of the whole willed that they should fit together and work together in this way and that, and further that a good deal of *reciprocal* influence might take place in their combination. But this reciprocal influence is not, and can never be, of such a kind that the very force or property which was produced in the one by a certain force or property of the other should have produced that same force or property in this other; rather, it is always one thing that acts as cause in the one upon the other, and another thing that is produced in it by the reaction of the latter upon the former. In this respect, therefore, the parts of an *organism* fare no differently at all from a thousand other things which form anything but organisms; e.g. with the reciprocal influence of two boys upon one another, of whom the one teaches the other many a thing and in turn takes over [p. 71] many an other thing from him; or as with a teacher who years ago taught a pupil the rudiments of a science, but now lets himself be taught many a thing by him in return. If, then, that *reciprocal influence* which is supposed to constitute the essence of the organism were understood only in this way, it would have to be permissible to declare the most heterogeneous aggregates in the world to be organisms, and we should have no mark by which we could tell what is only a single organism and what two. Thus, for instance, I am at this moment putting these very thoughts down on paper, and these written characters produced by me render me in return the service of bringing those thoughts back before my mind at will: accordingly I and this paper together would — in the sense of that definition — form an organism; which is surely not the concept that the natural scientist connects, and has reason to connect, with this word when he wishes to divide bodies into organic and inorganic.

§ 26. *Whewell* too remarks this (Spuren der Gottheit, in Seubert’s adaptation, Stuttgart, 1846, p. 204 ff.), and therefore returns to the concept of a *purpose*, which Kant himself had already declared *unavoidable* (in his *Critique of Judgement* § 68, though in a somewhat strange manner), and says: “A whole is *organised* only when the effects taking place between the parts are *essential to our concept of the whole*; when the whole would be *no whole*, and the parts *no parts*, without the *appearance* of these effects; when these effects not only happen as *facts*, but are necessarily included in the *concept* of the object; when they are not only *seen* but *foreseen*, not only expected but *intended*; in short, when instead of causes and effects they are *means and* *ends*.” — It is certainly a gratifying phenomenon that the celebrated English scholar did not let himself be deterred by the [p. 72] authority of his revered countryman *Bacon*, and of so many other scholars who are so averse to the consideration of *final causes*, from speaking in their favour, and that he has defended the inference, in fact a very legitimate one, from the purposiveness of certain arrangements of the world to a purpose underlying them in the Author of the world . But I have<sup>13</sup> already indicated in § 16 why I do not believe that the mark of purposiveness can be regarded as a constituent of the *concept* of the organism. In everything whatever that is and happens in the world, in so far as it is not a mere *consequence of a pure* *conceptual truth* (that is, as one is wont to say, does not happen thus by necessity, as is the case, e.g., with the laws according to which the heavenly bodies move in their orbits), the greatest purposiveness doubtless prevails; we may therefore presuppose it everywhere, even where we are unable to demonstrate it, and in this respect there is no difference between the organised and the non-organised parts of the universe.

<sup>13</sup> I have set out in the Wissenschaftslehre, vol. III, § 386 no. 7, and in the Religionswissenschaft, part I, § 143, under what conditions, hitherto usually not clearly stated, such an inference is entirely legitimate.

§ 27. *Link* takes particular pains to determine the essence of the organism more precisely when, in the Propyläen der Naturkunde (in the first as well as in the second part), he seeks out various characteristics by which organic bodies are distinguished from inorganic ones. The principal ones are that organic bodies are composed of flexible parts, inorganic ones of rigid parts (part II, p. 50); that the former are supposed to have a symmetrical, the latter an asymmetrical structure (p. 55); that the former are bounded by curved, the latter by plane surfaces (p. 65 ff.); but above all that (p. 132) the changes [p. 73] of organic bodies are *periodic*: they arise out of an organic rest (?), develop up to a certain degree, gradually decline again, and then come to rest once more — they die. This is supposed to be (according to the synopsis, p. IX) “*the proper* *concept*” of them. — I, on the contrary, hold that every limited substance is continually changing; I readily grant, however, that these changes are far more rapid and perceptible to the senses in the (as I call them) distinguished or ruling substances in organic bodies, and all the more so the higher the stage on which they already stand. And though I also concede a temporary and partial sinking of certain forces (e.g. in falling asleep, dying and the like), I still cannot assume in any single substance a genuine cycle (a period) in which a state wholly resembling one that had already existed earlier would return.<sup>14</sup>

<sup>14</sup> Bolzano seems to have intended, in the event of a revision of this essay, to take a few other definitions into account as well, as may appear from a note attached to the manuscript, which reads as follows: Dr F. W. *Tittmann* (Senior Consistorial Councillor at Dresden) says in his book Ueber die Bestimmung des Gelehrten (Berlin: Reimer, 1833), p. 10: “The well-being of the *organic* is closedness into an individual which directs its activity upon itself, which has an inner being in which it accomplishes its changes, its creation.” — How far a resemblance to the *individual life* of the organic may be ascribed to the inorganic as well, I will leave undecided. —

§ 28. That many objections will be raised against the definition of the organism attempted here as well, I know beforehand; for the views from which I start are far too different from those to which the present age is still almost universally inclined. Since, however, I do not consider myself so infallible as certain philosophers who, even when they overturn their entire system, wish to persuade us that it was not erroneous [p. 74] but only truth from a lower standpoint, I most unreservedly retract as erroneous the definition put forward in the Athanasia (p. 307, 2nd ed.). There, namely, I said that a dynamic force (which I defined then just as I do now) is called by me *chemical* if in its operation it is bound to no definite *form* of matter; if it is so bound, *organic*. I understood this to mean that in the case of an organic force a definite form of matter must be present if it is to act; since, e.g., a seed-corn, when it is cut through, at once loses its germinative power. On closer consideration, however, it became clear to me that precisely this could also be said of all *chemical* forces. For if the properties which the chemical product to be obtained is to have were fixed more precisely — if, e.g., it were required that an efflorescence, a tree of Diana, or hoar-frost or frostflowers running along this or that line should appear — then a particular form of matter, in which the chemical forces are to exert their operation, would indeed be required as well; then provision would have to be made that the solution spread only in certain directions, rising perhaps only in certain narrow spaces acting in the manner of capillary tubes, or be distributed by electrical influences: and the forces active here would nevertheless, even then (in my view), be partly of a merely chemical, partly of a merely mechanical nature. With the alteration, now, that I have made here in the definition of chemical and organic forces, not only is this fault (as it seems to me) avoided, but their adoption might further recommend itself by the circumstance that we now have *four* most intimately *interconnected concepts*, so regularly constructed that not only can the second be derived from the first exactly as the fourth from the third, but also the constituents which we are to use for forming the first and the third, and thus properly [p. 75] for forming all four, offer themselves to us as if of their own accord. For what we have to divide here is a genus of *outwardly acting* (transient) forces; and indeed that species of them which comprises only the forces acting in time, and thus bringing about a mere *change*, since creative power does not belong to a limited being (one that is thus itself subject to change). Now the least change that can be produced in such beings is a change in their mere places, which in itself is not yet any change at all in their *properties* (although it may have such a change as its consequence or also as its ground). The concept of a force, then, which in the first instance *changes* *nothing more than mere places*, of a *moving* or *mechanical* force, presents itself here *first*. But it already points to a *second* concept as well, namely to that of a force which brings about *more* than mere changes in *places*, that is, changes in the *inner* state of substances, to which I have given the name of a *dynamic force*. If we now consider, in order to proceed here again by the same rule as in finding the first concept, in what the least change taking place in the interior of a substance could consist, it at once becomes apparent that it could concern only its outwardly directed activity, or, to speak more precisely, only its *power of attraction or repulsion*. We thus arrive at the concept of a force which in the first instance acts only upon the mere power of attraction or repulsion of other substances coming within its range, and which I call — certainly in no contradiction with previous usage — a chemical force. If we now proceed with this *third* concept exactly as with the first, we come to the concept of an outwardly acting force which we must presuppose as the proximate cause of such changes as no merely chemical forces, still less mere mechanical [p. 76] ones, suffice to explain. And it is this force which scientific usage, before the onset of the Babylonian confusion of tongues of most recent times, universally called an *organic* force.

§ 29. But in the manner, too, in which the so important concept of *life* (§ 17) was determined, many will perhaps take great offence that it thus depends on a mere more or less whether we declare an organic body *living* or *lifeless*. For in itself it surely seems that life is not something *merely relative*, and that between animate and inanimate there is a true opposition. And so it is, in a certain sense, in actual fact; for if we take the word life in its most proper sense, then only that has *life* which possesses *sensation*, or in general forces acting within its *interior*; thus only a *simple* substance. And such life, in the strictest sense of the word, is possessed, in infinitely many gradations, by *all* simple beings, and by them alone. Never to the composite as such, but only to its simple parts, can sensation and life be attributed. In a wider sense, however, we allow ourselves to grant *life*, and indeed a single *life*, to the composite too, even to a whole body extended in the three dimensions of space and composed of an infinite multitude of simple substances, in so far as we may presuppose in it the presence of a single substance raising this whole to an organic formation, which we call its soul. And this is in fact a matter about which there may be dispute in many, indeed in the most frequent cases, and which must sometimes be left entirely undecided. In the case of a human body we are, to be sure, only seldom in doubt whether we have still to count it among the living or already among the dead; but already with certain genera of animals that stand on low stages, e.g. the corals and several other kinds of polyps, it is often very [p. 77] doubtful how far the soul extends its dominion in the organic formation we have before our eyes, that is, whether we ought to ascribe this whole formation lying before us, or only a part of it, and which part, as the body belonging to this soul. Still more uncertain must we be in the case of plants, even supposing that we assume souls in them, as to whether there is in each case one soul ruling the whole plant, or whether, e.g., the soul that rules in this flower-bud or leafbud also organically rules some further formation or not. Now if, e.g., there were no substance dwelling in a given plant-formation that could in any way be regarded as ruler over the whole, then this plant would indeed contain many individual souls and thus much — indeed infinitely much — individual life; but we could hardly declare it to be a single living organism, any more than we may call, e.g., a whole army or any other aggregate of human beings a living being.

## Vier akademische Reden über Mißbrauch der Religion, vornehmlich über den Grundsatz, der festgehalten und richtig angewendet vor jedem Mißbrauche religiöser Wahrheiten für immer bewahrt. — Four Academic Speeches on the Abuse of Religion, Chiefly on the Principle Which, Held Fast and Rightly Applied, Preserves for Ever from Every Abuse of Religious Truths

Delivered at the Sunday service in one of the lecture halls of the Prague Faculty of Philosophy.

Religion too can be abused, like every law, every form of government, like reason and like civil liberty.

Ildephons Schwarz, Handb. d. Rel., vol. 1, p. 58.

[p. 81]

### I. V. Moses 13, 1–5. — I. Deuteronomy 13:1–5

#### Eingang. — Introduction

Religion, my friends, is the subject to which I have already for a number of years devoted all my powers and efforts, and to which I mean to devote them in the future as well. To restore to Christianity, if possible, the esteem it has lost among the higher classes is the end whose attainment I have made the task of my life. Other works of some extent that I undertake, even those among them which at first sight appear to have no connection whatever with religion, are undertaken by me only because I consider them not unsuitable means to that ultimate end. Nevertheless I cannot conceal from myself that religion has at all times been the object of a great deal of abuse, and will probably long remain so. I cannot conceal from myself that the teachings of Christianity, however beneficial they are when properly understood and interpreted, can nevertheless, under a false interpretation and a perverse application, do a great deal of evil, and, alas, have only too often already actually done it. Providence itself has let me, in the short span of my life so far, have many an experience through which the harmful influence and the ruinousness of a Christianity disfigured by misunderstanding became vivid enough to me. Yet all this cannot in the least shake my resolve. I mean to live, to my last breath, for the defence, the revival and the further spread of Christianity, and I wish for nothing more ardently than to be truly successful in this work through [p. 82] the blessing of God. Why I think so I will tell you at once, my friends. I believe that the harm which our holy religion does through foolish misunderstanding or through malicious abuse will turn out smaller and smaller the longer it goes on; I believe that, even if it should be that at some time this harm had outweighed the benefit, this is nevertheless no longer to be feared in future; I believe, rather, that I am in possession of such views of Christianity that, were they generally spread, it would not even be possible for the most beneficent religion on earth to produce ruinous effects. In these circumstances it goes without saying that I must hold it my duty not merely to promote the cause of Christianity in general, but also to spread my views of it. But through whom can I more fittingly bring this about than through you, my friends? Once I have succeeded in gaining acceptance among you for these better views, I may hope that you yourselves will work for their further spread among the rest of mankind — may it only ever be done with due caution! Much of what you need to know for this purpose I have already said on various occasions; but never yet have I spoken of this subject in greater detail, never yet developed the chief principle which everyone must adopt who wants to be entirely secure against his religious faith ever bringing him any harm. Today I intend to set forth this principle. The less it has so far been known and accepted, the more it deserves that I communicate it, yet not without the request that you not give it your assent until its correctness has become evident to you through your own reflection. In Holy Scripture — this too I must admit — it is not to be found; rather, we discover in it at most a hint which, if we [p. 83] reflect further upon it, can lead us to the discovery of this principle. More Scripture could not give, for the truth of which I speak here is itself exposed to the danger of being misunderstood by the great multitude of uneducated people. And if it is itself misunderstood, then it can be of no use either; then, far from preventing the misunderstanding of the teachings of religion, it might rather first give rise to very many and extremely dangerous misunderstandings of them. As clearly as one might point to this truth, so clearly does Scripture actually point to it, and that already in its oldest book. In that oldest religion which was revealed to men by God, they were already warned against every abuse of faith in divine revelations. Let us hear how the venerable lawgiver of the people of Israel declared himself on this point.

(Text as above.)

#### Abhandlung. — Exposition

It will hardly be necessary to remind you, my friends, that today I take the word religion in that narrower meaning in which it designates faith in God and in certain truths grounded only on this faith. We are now speaking only of the disadvantages which this faith in God can occasion among men, and a principle is to be found by holding fast to which one could prevent all these disadvantages and bring it about that this faith would in future have to produce none but beneficial effects. A hint which, through further reflection, can easily lead us to this principle lies in the text I have read out. Here Moses impresses upon the people of Israel that they are to follow no prophet who should seek to turn them away from faith in Jehovah, however many signs and wonders he might perform. “If a prophet,” he says, “arises among you, and he [p. 84] does signs and wonders which come to pass exactly as he foretold; but he says to you: Let us go after other gods, then do not heed his words.” Wonders alone, then, events however extraordinary, by no means decide whether a revelation is true or not true. What, then, must be added? The new prophet, says Moses, may not turn them away from the God whom Israel worships. And why this? By what did faith in this God itself prove to be a true faith? — By its beneficence — Moses seems to mean, for it was this faith to which Israel owed its rescue from the bondage of Egypt. “That prophet,” it says in our text, “deserves to die, because he dared to tempt you to fall away from the God who led you out of Mizraim and redeemed you from the land of slavery!” The blessed effects, then, which faith in Jehovah produced were, as it seems, regarded by Moses as the only true and only certain mark from which one could infer that this faith was true, and that one should continue to abide by it. If this is correct, my friends, it will follow conversely that every teaching of religion and every interpretation of such a teaching must, if in its effects it proves harmful to men, for that very reason be regarded and rejected as a false interpretation. But if we are not to go round in a circle in this inquiry, not to assume at the very beginning what is first to be proved, then it is evident that, in discussing the question whether a certain opinion has beneficial or harmful consequences for mankind, we must not yet presuppose any religious opinion as true. This, then, leads to the rule: One should declare nothing to be good and generally beneficial by means of the teachings of religion and on their account which cannot be shown to be so without them. And precisely this rule, my friends, is the principle which I promised to communicate to you today, and through which [p. 85] alone, I believe, every ruinous influence of religion upon mankind can be prevented. New, I admit, and almost the very opposite of what is usually said on this matter, is the assertion I here venture. What I here forbid men and declare to be something dangerous is permitted in countless cases, and people even imagine they are doing something meritorious in it. Daily and hourly people allow themselves to infer, from faith in God and in the revelation he has communicated to us, what one ought or ought not to do in this or that situation of life, what will have a beneficial or a ruinous outcome; and if one were not allowed such an application of faith, then, people think, it would be as good as superfluous to men. You hear me assert the very opposite. Hear, then, my reasons as well. — Never, I say, should we make use of faith in God in order to try to know from it alone what is good or not good, beneficial or not beneficial.

⁠1) This is impracticable, first of all, for the reason that closer reflection shows that all our concepts of God are only figurative, and for that very reason unfit for drawing definite conclusions from them. For however certain it remains, my friends, that there is a God, a being, I mean, which as the ultimate ground of all things itself needs no further ground, however certain it remains that this being must be the sum of all perfections: we nevertheless have so much cause to be mistrustful of ourselves when we try to derive the attributes which this infinitely exalted being possesses from our mere concept of it. You who are acquainted with the inferences that are made here know yourselves that one really infers from what is found in man to what must also be present in God, the Infinite. Is this anything other than inferring according to the rule of analogy? Can the theorems we set up by this mode of inference concerning the nature of the Godhead deserve [p. 86] more than the name of figurative teachings? Can anyone say that he forms for himself any other than a merely figurative concept of the manner in which the infinite understanding of God knows all, sees through and judges all, and in which his infinite will creates and preserves all and guides it to definite ends? of how he is in possession of the highest blessedness without needing any external object for this blessedness, or being disturbed by one in its enjoyment? Indeed, is it not already a figurative way of speaking that we assume in God three such powers, of thinking, of willing and of feeling, as we find united in ourselves? Can we use such merely figurative teachings about God in order to derive definite conclusions from them? Can we be sure that we are not mistaken if, merely from an attribute assumed in God, we try to derive the conclusion that a certain action is a duty for man, when we notice no benefit at all that the performance of this action would bring to mankind, when, on the contrary, as far as we can see, it has harmful consequences? But perhaps, my friends, you will object that in developing the divine attributes we need not keep to mere concepts alone; we could also consult experience on this matter, and experience, after all, confirms in the most gratifying way the concepts we have acquired through reflection concerning the wisdom, omnipotence and holiness of God. — I gladly grant this, and I even declare that I know of no more agreeable occupation than the contemplation of nature and of the innumerable traces of wisdom and purposiveness which it contains in all its parts and arrangements. Only I believe that this in no way overturns what I said before. For if it is true that we meet with an innumerable multitude of traces of wisdom and purposiveness in nature itself, it follows from this only that we can infer various truths, which are otherwise usually first inferred from faith in God, also without it, directly from the contemplation [p. 87] of nature itself. These truths, then, will remain unimpaired for men under the rule I have set up today. Thus one infers — to mention only the most important example of this mode of inference — one infers from God’s wisdom, omnipotence and holiness that he will let our soul continue to exist, and continue to exist with consciousness, even after the death of this body. What prevents this from being expected with great confidence under the principle I recommend today as well? Are not the wise arrangements that we have so far discovered in countless parts of creation, as far as our eye can reach, are they not a guarantee that the rest too, which we have not yet examined and have not been able to examine, will be wisely and purposively arranged? Can we not, then, infer whatever another infers from God’s wisdom directly from the observed wisdom of the divine works, that is, from nature? — But if someone goes further and infers from his concept of God things for which no grounds of probability whatever lie in the contemplation of nature, then he evidently exposes himself to the danger of erring, and asserts in his conclusion more than is contained in those premises from which alone, properly, the inference can be drawn. For these secure premises are not the figurative concepts of the attributes of God, but merely those traces of wisdom and purposiveness which experience itself meets with in the works of God.

⁠2) But even if, my friends, the concepts we form of God’s attributes were not merely figurative, I would still abide by my assertion above, for the second reason: because our concepts of God are in any case so general that nothing can be inferred from them which would not have at least probability without them as well. For among all the attributes which reason [p. 88] and revelation have made known to us of God, those which are still the most fruitful in their consequences are surely those of the omnipotence, wisdom and holiness of God. But even from these, given the generality of our concepts of them, nothing can be inferred that would not be at least probable without them as well. By virtue of the omnipotence we admire in God, we know, to be sure, that he can do everything that is not in itself impossible and not contrary to the well-being of the whole. But does so general a concept suffice for expecting any single event in the world on account of God’s omnipotence, when we have no other grounds to expect it? Can we know whether it is not in itself impossible, or at any rate not in contradiction with the well-being of the whole? — By virtue of the omniscience we ascribe to God, he knows even our most secret thoughts, and if we combine his justice with this attribute, it becomes evident to us that even the good or the evil we commit only in thought will not remain unrequited. But can this very thing not also be inferred directly from the contemplation of nature? Would not the purposiveness we meet with in all the arrangements of animate, animal creation as well as of inanimate creation be suddenly interrupted if a requital were not also to be found in the fortunes that man experiences? And how many examples that such a requital exists do history and daily experience not supply us with? By virtue of the holiness we revere in God, there is indeed no doubt that he makes every provision that serves to promote the virtue and happiness of his creatures. But we have already seen above that this circumstance cannot justify the prediction of even a single definite outcome. It is not to be forgotten, indeed, that this holiness of God also includes truthfulness; and from this one infers that he must fulfil everything he has ever promised us in his revelation. [p. 89] This — people think — lets us expect a sufficiently large number of future outcomes of which we would know nothing without faith in God. This inference too, my friends, is in my opinion a fallacy. For unless I am mistaken, there is a great difference between the rule of truthfulness to which God is bound in his statements and that which is incumbent on us men. We have no right whatever to demand of God that he depict for us everything about which he chooses to declare himself to us in his revelation exactly as it is constituted in itself: it must suffice us, indeed it must be welcome to us, if he depicts it in the way that, in his omniscience, he foresees will be most beneficial for us. From this, however, it follows that *we are to make no other use of any teaching we find in God’s revelation than such as remains purposive whether the teaching be true in* *itself or not true*, that is, that we make use of it merely as an encouragement to such actions as we can see, even without any faith in God, to be beneficial to the well-being of the whole. This becomes all the clearer when we look at those marks by which alone we can be assured whether the mouth of God has spoken to us or not. For only such a teaching as we recognise in advance will be beneficial for ourselves and others to accept and follow, only such can we regard as a revelation that has been granted to us by God. We therefore misinterpret divine revelation, we extend its teachings further than we are entitled to, we attribute to it opinions which lack the seal of divine testimony and which are our own inventions, as soon as we allow ourselves to derive from its theorems conclusions of which we cannot see, from mere grounds of reason, that it is beneficial to accept and follow them. Nothing that we cannot show to be good and generally beneficial without [p. 90] the teachings of religion may we acknowledge as such on their account.

That we, holding fast to this principle, have never to fear any disadvantage from any teaching of religion is, I think, clearly evident, my friends. But the question arises: “Whether all the benefit which religious faith is able to afford us is not also cancelled by this principle?” That this is by no means the case I must still show in a few words.

I believe, then, that a truly confident faith in our holy religion can everywhere do much good among us, even if we never make use of it for any actual extension of our knowledge. I believe this, first of all, because my own experience tells me so. For truly I would have to be ungrateful if I would not confess with a moved heart how much good I too owe to the holy religion of which I am called a teacher. I would be ungrateful if I wished to conceal that it has destroyed and rooted out much evil in me, that through its splendid institutions it has greatly helped me in laying aside many a bad habit, that every good quality which may perhaps be found in me has been developed and purified by it, that in the saddest moments I have lived through it has been my only source of comfort and reassurance, that it has done exceedingly much to make life pleasant for me, through those serene views which it and it alone spreads over time and eternity! All this, my friends, the Christian Catholic religion has done for me, without my ever having allowed myself to infer from its teachings what is not demonstrable without them as well. Shall I not, then, draw the conclusion from this that, with such a use, it can afford the very same advantages to others as well?

But this is evident to me also from the nature of the matter itself. For there are really only two advantages which faith in God and his revelation is destined to afford men, and which it can afford perfectly well even without the application [p. 91] against which I warn. First, it is to remind us of our duties, and, once we have seen that something is good and generally beneficial, to bring it about that we also do it. Second, it is to calm us about everything which it is not possible for us to change, and to fill our heart with good hope for the future and with gladdening ideas. How well the one as well as the other can come about without violating the rule which I set up today! How well one can make use of the teachings of our holy Church in order to be reminded of all one’s duties, without nevertheless ever holding anything to be a duty, or even merely meritorious, which, judged by mere grounds of reason, appears as something harmful! How well one can use faith in God, in his eternal justice, in the rewards as well as the punishments of the life to come, in that public judgement which awaits us, in order to do with zeal everything of which one has seen that it has generally beneficial consequences, without yet ever inferring from these teachings what would not remain true and demonstrable without them as well! How well, and very well, one can use the teachings of Christianity about God’s fatherly mind, about that redemption which the Son of God has wrought for us, about that readiness of the divine will to heed all our prayers, about those blessings which a proper use of the Catholic means of sanctification brings forth, and many other teachings besides, in order to find comfort in misfortune, to remain full of good hope even in the saddest prospects, to enjoy every joy of life in all its inwardness! How well, I say, one can attain all this without ever letting oneself be led, on account of these theorems, to expect events of which even the most unbelieving man would not have to admit that they are very probable, or that it is in any case harmless and even salutary to expect them! In this way, then, and in this way alone, I would wish that our holy religion — if this is not yet being done by everyone [p. 92] — were used and applied by all educated people, at least by you. Then no one could reproach us with becoming harmful to ourselves or others through our faith; then even the most sworn enemy of Christianity would have to fall silent and admit: If it is used in this way, then it is indeed an undeniable power to make good and blessed everyone who believes in it. To win this admission even from those who have so far shown themselves averse to Christianity is surely the finest victory we can win for the good cause of religion. May this victory be granted us through the blessing of that very God for whose glorification we wish to win it! Amen.

### II. I. Mos. 4, 1–5. — II. Genesis 4:1–5

#### Eingang. — Introduction

Clearly enough, my friends, Holy Scripture gives us to understand in the text just read what the occasion was of that misdeed of Cain’s which we cannot contemplate without a shudder, of that first horrible crime with which our race branded itself after the expulsion from Eden. Clearly enough we are given to understand that abuse of religion occasioned this crime. “Once” — so run the words of our text — “once Cain brought the Lord an offering of the fruits of his field, Abel, on the other hand, an offering of the firstlings of his flock and of the fat thereof. On Abel’s offering God looked with favour, but to Cain and to his offering he paid no heed. At this Cain grew wroth, and so much so that even his countenance fell.” Plainly, then, it was nothing but the belief in God and in certain signs by which he gave men to know his pleasure or displeasure that produced that first and deadly quarrel between brothers. Instead of using the discovery which Cain believed he had made, that God spurned his offering, for his own improvement; instead of getting quite clear with himself as to what it really was that brought him to the supposition that he was displeasing to God, and carefully avoiding this in future, he grew wroth rather against his innocent brother, and, despite the warning which God gave him, he did not master [p. 94] the raging of his unhappy passion until with horror he became aware that Abel lay dead at his feet! — Shall we be mistaken, my friends, if we suppose that Holy Scripture relates this sad event to us, among other reasons, for this one too: that we may see in it, as in an eternally memorable example, what pernicious effects even the holiest thing, even belief in God and his revelations, produces when it is abused? Or is a warning against such an abuse perhaps no longer necessary at all in the present day? It is indeed true that, particularly in the more educated classes, there are many people for whom religion can hardly become pernicious any longer, because they have almost no religion left at all. But by far the greater part of mankind still believes, thank Heaven! in God and revelation; it has not yet divested itself of the most glorious jewel that God has granted to men in this earthly vale, the jewel of religion; and precisely because it still possesses religion, it also needs a definite warning against every abuse of it. It was this, then, that moved me at our last assembly to state the truth which is the most comprehensive that can ever be laid down in this regard, to set forth the principle from which one can judge most surely when a use of religious doctrines deserves approval or disapproval. But however rich in consequences this principle is, much caution is nevertheless needed in its application. And for this reason alone it may be necessary that we also go into particulars, and *reflect specially on the most* *important abuses to which religion is exposed even in our own day*. Let us do this today at once, and in some of our next assemblies too. May God himself so bless these reflections that we not only never abuse our belief in him ourselves, but also prevent the abuse of this belief in many thousands of other people over whom our influence will one day extend!

[p. 95]

#### Abhandlung. — Exposition

Of such an abuse of religious doctrines as someone commits knowingly and out of mere malice I do not at all intend to speak, my friends. For I do not expect that anyone inclined to such an abuse is to be found in our assembly. I shall therefore direct my attention solely to abuses into which one often falls without being clearly aware of it oneself; but even of these I shall take only the most common and the most important into closer consideration.

⁠1) The *first* that I must mention is *the neglect of duties* *towards men in order to fulfil supposed duties towards God himself*. For although I gladly admit that this is an abuse of which our age is guilty to a lesser degree than all earlier centuries, we must not therefore believe that we are already entirely free of this fault. Not only does the great mass of uneducated people still very often offend in this respect, and in quite gross fashion; it also happens now and then even to us educated people that, in striving to fulfil a supposed duty towards God, we harm the general well-being rather than promote it. According to sound concepts, all of man’s duties towards God have absolutely no other ground and purpose than the promotion of the well-being of mankind itself. We are obliged to believe in God’s existence, to develop ever further our concepts of his eternal perfection, of his infinite power, wisdom and holiness, and to make them ever more vivid and familiar to ourselves, only for this reason; we are obliged to pray to God often, to thank him for benefits received, to ask him to grant us more, to vow to him as solemnly as possible the fulfilment [p. 96] of our duties, to do everything that we know to be his holy will and his command to us, to praise him not only in our hearts but also with our lips and publicly before others, to take part in every purposeful manner of worshipping God that we find established among men — we are obliged to all this, I say, only because, properly applied, it can become a means to our own happiness and that of our fellow men. It is not God but ourselves that we serve when we perform in the right way what goes by the name of divine service, or by the name of a duty towards God. But it by no means follows from this truth that actions of this kind lose all binding force for us as soon as we merely notice that performing them demands some sacrifice. No, my friends: so long as this sacrifice does not outweigh the benefit which the performance of the action affords, so long, to be sure, may we not release ourselves from the obligation to it; the duty towards God stands. Thus the duty of taking part in public worship certainly holds also for the man who believes that he could edify himself better in solitude than in the assembly of many. For what he sacrifices is surely not so much as what others gain; what he loses in his own edification or in time is not to be compared with the scandal others would take if he withdrew from all assemblies for worship. But let us suppose that someone is seized by an indisposition which makes attendance at the public house of prayer not impossible for him, yet dangerous; let us suppose that everyone can easily convince himself of the reality of this obstacle, that there is nothing present which could arouse the well-founded suspicion that he neglects his duty out of mere indifference: then he is indeed entirely released from this duty, and it would be superstition and a foolish abuse of religion if, in the belief that he was serving God, he were willing [p. 97] to expose his health and his life to danger. Let us imagine, on the other hand — to speak of a case in which many of us will yet find ourselves — that a dangerously sick man sends for the priest in order to reconcile himself with God through his mediation; let us imagine that either he himself or at least those around him fancy that it depends on the priest’s coming or staying away whether in death he hastens to eternal blessedness or to eternal damnation: will it not then be the priest’s duty to come, even if he should thereby expose himself to the greatest danger of falling ill himself? If he did not do so, what a scandal would he not give his congregation? Would not all believe they now had proof that he was nothing but a wretched hireling who, to save a soul entrusted to him from eternal death, will not even stake this so insignificant earthly life? — Exactly the opposite ought to happen if we ourselves were in the sick man’s place. We, that is, ought to see that the obligation our holy religion lays upon us to send for the priest is not unconditional; we ought to know that this obligation, where it cannot be fulfilled without plunging another into visible danger of death, is as good as cancelled; we ought therefore not to send for the priest, but only to testify to all around us that we cherish the confident trust in God the allmerciful that, if only we do everything that depends on ourselves, he will bestow on us, even without the priest’s hand, the very same blessing that it would have procured us, since it was not out of disdain for his help but out of due regard for his life, so necessary to the congregation, that we did not have him called to us. Do you gather from this example, my friends, that in some cases it is really no such easy matter to decide when a duty towards God, a precept that faith lays upon us, [p. 98] has true binding force, and when it is cancelled? This always depends solely on the nature of the consequences which this action produces even apart from everything that faith teaches: whether it has certain naturally good consequences, and whether these are perhaps cancelled in the present case by other bad consequences; whether the omission of it would not produce some scandal, and whether this scandal would be a lesser or a greater evil than all those consequences which the performance of the action itself would have. Whenever we clearly recognise that the action which is otherwise rightly called a duty towards God would this time produce more harm than benefit, it is an abuse of religion if we still insist on this duty now, if we still press for its fulfilment even now and are ready to blame the man who releases himself from this obligation, not always, but only in the present case. The more plainly it lies before everyone’s eyes that the natural consequences of the action are, in the present case, nothing but harmful, the more shamefully we abuse the good cause of religion if we still refuse to depart from this obligation. The more it becomes evident that performing the action under the circumstances prevailing here would bring great disadvantage not only to the agent himself but to other people as well, the more odious we make the doctrines of religion, and the more occasion we give for raising the complaint that the burdens which religion lays upon men are unbearable.

⁠2) But if men could not rightly find their way in the concept of duties towards God, my friends, it is still more understandable that the concept of *God’s foreknowledge* should have given rise to various misunderstandings and abuses. Let this be the second subject that we wish to consider somewhat [p. 99] more fully. Not only does Christianity say it, but natural religion proves that God the all-knowing foresees with the utmost determinacy everything, even the latest and most contingent events, even insofar as they depend on the free decisions of the will of beings. The purpose for which we are told this is absolutely no other than simply to make us content with all the fortunes that befall us on earth, to produce in us the belief that everything that is and happens in the world is adapted to the free actions of beings, and to make it comprehensible to us how a God who has foreseen everything from eternity could also from eternity make arrangements suited to what is done by us in time. Only when we use this doctrine for the purpose just described do we use it as it is meant to be used. Every other application of it is abuse, because every other produces harmful consequences. Thus it is abuse if, in considering the divine foreknowledge, we raise the question what God may well foresee concerning ourselves, and let the answer occur to us that perhaps he foresees our eternal misery. This is an abuse, I say; for it is a thought that must produce nothing but harmful effects, that can only frighten and alarm us, but not better us. Rightly, then, rightly ought we to reject it as often as it occurs to us, with the remark that it depends solely on ourselves, on our zealous or sluggish use of all the aids and opportunities for our salvation and happiness, whether God foresees eternal blessedness or eternal misery. — It is an abuse if we use the doctrine of God’s omniscience to justify through it the evil we have committed; if we maintain that we necessarily had to act as we did, because it was already fixed with God from eternity; that it cannot possibly be anything so very harmful either, since God would otherwise [p. 100] not have permitted it, if in his wisdom he did not know how to use it for the promotion of the general well-being. That, I maintain, is abuse. For even if I had not the least objection to make against the correctness of these inferences, this alone would already entitle me to reject them: that the consequences which such a way of thinking brings in its train are recognised by reason as highly pernicious. For tell me yourselves, my friends, what would things look like on earth if all men chose to persuade themselves that there is no freedom, that in everything they do they are determined by a law of necessity, that all their doing is moreover indifferent, since God by virtue of his wisdom knows how to derive the most beneficial consequences even from the most evil. What could deserve the name of an abuse, if one will not call this application of the belief in God an abuse? — Finally, it is abuse too if from God’s foreknowledge we mean to derive the conclusion that, if not all of man’s fortunes, then at least the most important, at least each person’s birth and death, are predetermined by God in such a way that we can change nothing about them by what we do or leave undone. How absurd this opinion is, each of you, my friends, surely sees for himself. For what is more obvious than that birth and death are two events which, just like every other event in the world, have their natural causes? What is more undeniable than that a man’s actions exert the greatest influence on the lengthening and shortening of his life? What is more certain than that a man will shorten his life if he ruins his body through excesses, or denies it needed nourishment, or even commits the act of suicide upon himself? — How contradictory, then, to believe that our doing and not doing are indeed within our discretion, yet death can be neither hastened nor delayed! But even if this opinion were not nearly so absurd as it perhaps [p. 101] is, it would still have to be called an abuse of the belief in God for this reason alone, that there is absolutely nothing present in nature that could lead us to it by rational inferences. It is derived solely from belief in God, and moreover by a fallacy.

⁠3) But it is time that we consider yet a third kind of abuse, namely the one that people permit themselves with the belief in *God’s wisdom*. For because God is wise, so — people say — every arrangement and every event in the world must also, since in the end it is always God’s work, be wise and purposeful, and for that very reason man ought not to wish to change it. If he dares to, he opposes the ordinances of God, and if he succeeds in carrying out what he has in mind, it is certain in advance that he will only worsen his lot instead of improving it. How often people fall into this foolish manner of reasoning you will only realise with astonishment, my friends, once you begin from now on to pay closer attention to it. Almost every time the abolition of an existing arrangement is at issue — one that was originally introduced by men alone, but has now maintained itself so long that people believe they find in this very preservation a proof of the protection God grants it — the proposed improvement is resisted, among other reasons, on the ground that to censure this arrangement would mean to call God’s wisdom into doubt. If this were as harmful as you allege, people say, how could God’s wisdom have tolerated and protected it for centuries already? If it displeased him, how could he not have abolished it long ago? — Still more often, and with a still greater appearance of right, this manner of reasoning is brought forward when someone proposes means of protection against evils which, as it seems, are brought upon us not by us men ourselves but by nature. [p. 102] Whoever tries to command the lightning-bolt to spare the human head, whoever would prescribe to it the path it is to take; whoever endeavours to free mankind from the plague of certain devastating diseases, especially those which on account of their universality seem to be an ordinance of nature: he is reproached with rebelling against God himself; it is prophesied of him that his impious undertaking, however good the fruits it may promise at first, will surely not escape punishment in the end! Indeed, what is most striking of all, in one of those very sciences whose successful cultivation must surely concern us most, the false rule — one which, if it were strictly followed, would abolish the science itself — has been adopted as a principle, that one must never disturb the course of nature. — I do not fail to recognise, my friends, that there is something true in all these errors as well. It is true that one ought not, without great caution, seek to alter anything which seems to be an arrangement of nature. For from the purposeful constitution of nature observed in countless cases we may with every right conclude that it proceeds wisely throughout, wisely even in those parts which we have not yet investigated, or where we have not yet succeeded in grasping the purpose of their arrangement. But I say only that on this ground alone we are never entitled to declare purposeless any action by which man alters the course of inanimate nature; rather, this must be decided solely from experience itself. If experience lets us observe no harmful consequences, if rather every effect that we have partly observed already and partly can expect from the future is good and beneficial, then we must also admit that this influence of human forces, and what inanimate ones have contributed to it, is purposeful. For how so? Does not man too, with all his forces, with his understanding [p. 103] and his free will, also belong to nature? If nature really has arranged everything purposefully, must it not also have taken account of him and his forces? Must it not, as it were, have expected in advance and already counted on man too achieving and accomplishing through his free activity much of what no inanimate forces are capable of? — No, my friends: whoever makes proposals for improving the condition of mankind on earth shall be welcome to us, and whether the proposals he makes to us are rational or not rational we may decide in no other way than from experiences already made or yet to be made. To want to reject him on the sole ground that his proposal looks like a kind of interference with God’s ordinance — that is an abuse of the belief in God as bad as any! That is an abuse which gives all the enemies of religion a welcome opportunity to assert that belief in God is only harmful to us, that it leads us into a thousand follies, that it holds us back in every advance towards perfection, which we would make far less hindered if we did not always believe that everything on earth is already good and wise and incapable of improvement because it comes from God. O, if the holiest of beings could ever be angry, it would have to be this abuse that aroused God’s highest displeasure! Let us then be truly watchful over ourselves, my friends, so that the manner of reasoning I have just warned against may never occur to us! Let us beware of so abusing the very belief that is meant to be the greatest means of promoting earthly happiness that it hinders us in our progress in virtue! “Belief in God,” says an ancient pagan writer, “shall be to man that which helps him onward to perfection!” May it be this, then, for all of us too, in the individual as in the whole! Amen.

### III. Joh. 9, 1–7. — III. John 9:1–7

#### Eingang. — Introduction

One of the most consoling observations that history gives us occasion to make consists, surely, my friends, in this: that in our more recent times we abuse the cause of religion far less often and to a far less high degree than happened in earlier centuries. Not without horror can we read what pernicious effects an abused belief in God and in God’s revelations produced in older times. We are amazed and can scarcely comprehend it when history tells us that even the most intelligent peoples once believed they were worshipping God when they committed the most shameful excesses, that they seriously believed they were appeasing the wrath of the gods when they carried out the most unheard-of deeds of cruelty. We grow sad when we hear that not only so many heathen peoples, but Christian ones too, often persecuted one another most violently on account of the difference of their religious opinions; that even in Christianity the belief had crept in among many thousands that “one must torment oneself on earth if one wishes one day to enjoy the joys of heaven.” What unhappy concepts these were, my friends! We cannot thank God enough that we find ourselves in an age in which these errors are almost wholly banished! Or who in our days still believes that he owes it to the honour of God to persecute those who think otherwise? who believes that he earns himself merit [p. 105] with God if he torments himself without need or purpose? Have not even those peoples who have not yet accepted the Christian faith become somewhat wiser and more enlightened through the use of some of its concepts? Are they not also beginning now to see the absurdity of certain modes of thought and gradually to abolish certain abuses? May we not with every right hope that this will happen ever more perfectly in future? So at least I hope; and therefore I also believe that it would be the most unpardonable of rash acts if one wished to root out belief in God and revelation from men’s hearts merely because of the many harmful effects it has so far produced through abuse. If in no case can it be permitted to destroy a thing very good and beneficial in itself merely on account of its abuse, then here, in the case of religion, this highest and most important good of mankind, such an endeavour would have to be called true high treason! But however culpable it would be to wish to rob men of belief in God and his revelation, so praiseworthy must it be to warn them most emphatically against its abuse. This is chiefly the duty of those who hold the office of teachers of religion. That is why I have already spoken twice on this subject, and intend to speak of it again today and — if it please God — at our next assembly as well. The Gospel text which I shall now read is to remind us of some of the most important abuses that are still met with among us even in our days.

(Text as above)

#### Abhandlung. — Exposition

⁠1) The conduct of the disciples in the Gospel we have read gives us an example, my friends, of how easily it happens even [p. 106] to one who has enjoyed better instruction in religion to make, now and again, a perverse application of its sublime truths. For three years already they had been the pupils of him whom no one has yet equalled in excellence of teaching, and no one ever will equal; and yet, when today they caught sight of a man born blind, they let themselves be led, in considering his pitiable condition, into so unseemly an application of their concepts of God that they could raise the question “whether this unfortunate man himself or whether his parents had sinned, since he came into the world blind?” The prejudice from which this question really arose is surely unmistakable; it was the same prejudice that we still so often meet among ourselves too, namely that every misfortune, at least every unusual misfortune that befalls a person, is a proof of God’s displeasure with him, a punishment; just as, conversely, every unusual good fortune is regarded as a proof of God’s good pleasure, as a divine reward. This error, which they regarded as a consequence of God’s justice, cannot be counted so heavily against the disciples of our Lord, because it was the generally prevailing opinion of the whole country, because it was the opinion that even the most enlightened part of the nation, the class of teachers itself, had adopted. The high council, which questions the man who was healed about the manner of his healing, has no hesitation in reproaching him with the natural defect from which he had suffered until then as though it were a crime. “Wilt thou teach us,” it cries, “thou who wast born a criminal already!” To be sure, this opinion no longer prevails so generally among us, my friends, and even those who still adhere to it do not permit themselves to apply it with such decisiveness to every case that occurs in life. But when someone experiences a misfortune of a quite peculiar kind, when he is afflicted with an illness that presents extremely rare symptoms and runs a very painful and protracted [p. 107] course; when someone dies a very sudden death, and that through an injury brought about not so much by men as by the forces of inanimate nature: are there not also among us Christians very many who speak of such an event with meaningful looks and hints, and add the conjecture that it is surely evident from this that the person whom this misfortune befell must have deserved, through some secret crime, to be punished thus by the just God? Indeed, not only the people who are spectators of the rare misfortune, but also the persons who bleed under its blows, often needlessly embitter their already lamentable situation by the thought that everything they suffer is a punishment decreed upon them by God. Without knowing themselves guilty of any crime of importance, people of a certain kind often prepare for themselves a veritable torment of hell by discovering in themselves now this, now that insignificant slip, and trying to find in it the whole blame for their misfortune. On the other side there are again people, and probably in greater number, who, while leading a quite culpable life, live on with a quiet conscience merely because they have never yet experienced a mishap which they would have had to regard as a punishment from Heaven, because hitherto everything has always turned out to their good fortune. They cry out with those evildoers of whom Scripture tells: “We have sinned, and what harm has befallen us? Let us then go on along the path we have trodden hither-to!” The one as well as the other, my friends, is an intolerable abuse of the belief in God and in the justice which he must display in his government of the world. You will see this as soon as you merely recall the principle I communicated to you recently. Every inference from the concept of God and God’s attributes which cannot also be proved without it to be true [p. 108] and beneficial for us I declared recently to be an inference we are not entitled to, an abuse. So it will also be permitted only where these views are manifestly conducive to virtue and happiness to regard a piece of good fortune that has fallen to someone’s lot as a divine reward, and a misfortune that has befallen him as a punishment. Thus we may by all means allow every piece of good fortune that falls to a good man’s lot to be regarded by him and others as a reward of his virtue; for this consideration will make him rejoice all the more heartily in his good fortune, will make him find in it encouragement to make still swifter progress in perfection, and will make all other people who see in him how virtue is rewarded acquire the desire to imitate him. Likewise we may allow, indeed must even demand, that every wicked man regard the misfortune with which God afflicts him as a punishment that has his improvement as its purpose; and finally that all who hear of the unhappy end of a vicious man recognise the finger of God in this dispensation and let themselves be warned and deterred by his punishment. For by such views only zeal for the good is promoted, while the desire for evil is checked; we may call them right and permissible views because in their consequences they prove beneficial. — Not so if we extend them further: if even the good and innocent man wants to regard his misfortune as a punishment, and yet does not know by what fault he incurred it and what he has to avoid in future; or if he does find a slip which may in part have occasioned the misfortune, but it is one for whose future avoidance it is not at all necessary that he torment himself with the idea that he alone is the cause of the origin of that whole misfortune, which has perhaps befallen not him alone but others with him as well. If a misfortune has already taught [p. 109] us a lesson, if it has already made us wiser and better, then the purpose for which we are to represent it to ourselves as a punishment is already attained in the most perfect way; we therefore torment ourselves quite without need or benefit if we go on entertaining this ever-disquieting idea any longer. Rather, if we wish to use our belief in God quite purposefully, we should now seek to lighten the bearing of the hard mishap by the thought that God the all-knowing presumably knows how to attain, besides our own instruction and improvement, which he has already attained, still other beneficent purposes through this misfortune. So, my friends, so thinks the enlightened man who applies his belief in God only to his improvement and happiness; and for that very reason he is far from calming himself, when his conscience reproaches him, with the circumstance that no harm has yet befallen him. Rather, instead of abandoning himself here to a false security, he confesses, moved, that it is only forbearance, undeserved forbearance, that has spared him so far, so that he may still mend his ways in good time and not at last fall into all the harsher punishments. But if we may regard even our own fate as now reward, now punishment only according to the verdict our conscience pronounces upon us, then it is obvious that we may still less permit ourselves to judge of the fate of others, whether it is reward or punishment, unless we know from some other source whether they are worthy of reward or of punishment. But if we wish to judge this first from their fate, we entirely invert the order that ought to be followed here, and apply the belief in God’s justice to a purpose for which it neither should nor can ever be applied. That Jesus thoroughly disapproved of this application is shown not only by the rebuke he gives the disciples in our text, but also by the conduct of the disciples themselves, who in the whole course of their later life never again permitted themselves a manner of reasoning such as this was.

[p. 110] 2) But if we consider somewhat more closely what our Lord said on this occasion, we learn not only which kind of inference we should avoid, but also which we should draw. “Neither he nor his parents have sinned,” said our Lord, “but he was born blind so that I might have occasion to show forth the omnipotence of God in him. For it is incumbent on me to do the works of him who sent me, as long as it is day, because soon the night falls in which no one can work any more!” — O how much more fruitful, my friends, than that idle question of curiosity which the disciples had raised at the sight of this man born blind, was the reflection that Jesus made here. The sight of so rare a misfortune as the misfortune of this blind man is to me — so he thought — a hint from God that I should exert myself to remedy it. Nor will I let myself be found sluggish in this, but right now, although it is the Sabbath, I will do my part. — O, might we all always be as fortunate as Jesus in judging what is a hint from God and what is not! But much is wanting to that; for one of the most common abuses of our faith in God consists precisely in this, that we believe we perceive hints from God where on rational grounds they can by no means be assumed — I mean where we must not also admit, quite apart from all faith in God, that it is most advisable to act precisely thus and not otherwise. What is more common than that, especially in cases where it is somewhat difficult to decide by a rational consideration of all the circumstances what is most expedient, we demand a hint from above, and in seeking it take the most arbitrary signs as our basis? What is more common than that, in a perplexity of this kind, we adopt the principle that the notion which next comes first into our mind, or which will present itself to us after we have first said our prayer with the [p. 111] greatest possible devotion, or which a lot or the utterance of an innocent child designates for us, or which some person whom we indeed hold in high esteem, but who is nevertheless not competent in the matter, will most approve — that this is the one which God himself also wills us to follow? What, finally, is more common than that, especially in undertakings which carry some danger, we should want to recognise in every obstacle we meet at the outset a hint to desist again from our undertaking? Even men, my friends, who otherwise thought in a very enlightened way, have at times betrayed striking weaknesses in this respect. Let us therefore seek to secure ourselves against every error by concepts as distinct as possible. If what I have so often said about those two sole sufficient marks for discerning the will of God is correct, then it is absurd to recognise God’s hint in any thought unless it is so constituted that we must admit, even apart from all faith in God, that to do this is the most advisable course in this situation. Let us never believe, then, should we one day find ourselves in truly entangled circumstances, that we could be relieved all at once of the labour and the duty of reflection by begging a hint from God; for it is only through reflection that we shall recognise whether and where such a hint is present for us. Let us never resolve within ourselves that we will read from this or that arbitrary sign to which side God beckons us; for what is less seemly than to wish to prescribe to God the manner in which he is to make himself known to us? Let no event, however unusual, lead us to take for a hint from God anything that does not bear on its brow the mark of conformity with reason. From a resolution which we have taken after mature deliberation and from very good intentions, no [p. 112] obstacle, however strongly it may oppose itself at the beginning of its execution, should be able to turn us aside. For what a thought dishonouring to God it would be that he should wish to hold us back from the good! We may examine afresh whether our idea, our intention, is really rational; we may reflect on the true cause by which our attempts so far have been frustrated; we may improve whatever has perhaps not yet been laid out wisely and carefully enough; but to think that God is warning us against something that on all rational grounds we must hold to be good and right is sheer superstition.

⁠3) Jesus too, my friends, had often experienced the most unpleasant consequences when he ventured to perform the beneficent work of healing the sick on a Sabbath; nevertheless he did not let this deter him from healing the man born blind in our text likewise on a Sabbath. “He prepared a kind of paste,” the Gospel tells us, “anointed the eye of the blind man, and then bade him wash himself in the pool of Siloam.” This so simple procedure of our Lord reminds us, by its contrast, of one of the worst abuses which the ignorance of earlier times has transmitted to our most recent one. It is the endeavour to earn a reward from God and to obtain a blessed effect by performances which in themselves have no value and use whatever. It is true that we know far too little about what exactly the nature was of that which Jesus undertook in order to restore to that blind man the glorious gift of eyesight; but even from the little that the Gospel relates of it, it is certain that this action of Jesus had certain naturally beneficial effects — at least this one, that it filled the blind man beforehand with good hopes, and also brought it about that he ascribed the good fortune which soon fell to his lot not to chance but to the beneficent effort of Jesus. And as in this case, so we find it with Jesus throughout. Never do we see him undertake the [p. 113] least thing that would not already in itself have had a natural use; never do we find that, after the manner of the magical arts, he sought in secret spells and signs the means that was to procure for him the assistance of his heavenly Father in his mighty deeds. How rational this was! How must not everyone who has risen to a correct concept of God’s holiness approve and praise it! For if we believe that God is holy, i.e. that only that pleases him which promotes the happiness of his creatures — how can we hope that by some action which has no use, at least no use perceptible to us, we shall earn his approval through it? Must not every action of this kind be rejected as an abuse of faith in God, according to the principle I recently communicated to you? And yet in the times in which Jesus lived, not only his own people but all the other peoples of the earth as well were thoroughly infected with this superstition! Instead of seeking to earn the approval of Heaven through virtue and the practice of good works, it was everywhere believed that for this the performance of certain prayers and other actions was necessary, from which properly no natural use whatever could be discerned. The more absurd and the more nonsensical the ingredients of which a performance was composed, the more inclined people were to believe in its secret force and efficacy. The greatest miracles — so believed the people of the Jews, the wisest of that time — the greatest miracles could be performed by whoever knew the true pronunciation of the name of God! — We Christians, it is true, are for the most part cured of so pernicious a delusion, but entirely free of it we are not yet. For however much trouble the leaders of our Church have taken through all the centuries to destroy faith in the secret efficacy of any action not already naturally beneficial, or faith in [p. 114] magic, still many a remnant of this delusion has survived among us to the present day. Indeed, what is more, and what I do not mention without a feeling of pain, Christianity itself has so far had to contribute, though in a very innocent way, to the nourishment of this delusion — namely through the excellent doctrine that it sets forth concerning the special efficacy of certain means of sanctification. The Catholic Church names seven actions whose worthy performance God rewards with the most extraordinary graces and blessings. Everyone who reflects finds that these actions all have a natural power of edification, and that it is just this natural beneficence of theirs that contains the true reason why their practice is prescribed and deemed worthy of so distinguished a promise. Everyone who reflects therefore sees that one is not in the least entitled to censure Catholic Christianity for what it teaches about these actions. But however true this is, it is nevertheless only too certain that the common multitude of Christians scarcely thinks at all of that *natural* efficacy of the means of sanctification so emphatically recommended to it; it is only too certain that it lives in the fancy that the performance of these actions is meritorious and of blessed effect only because they are commanded, whereas it is precisely the other way round. It seems to me necessary, my friends, that the people’s eyes should be opened on this point; it seems to me necessary that all Christians, the uneducated as well as the educated, should be shown how useful and how beneficial in themselves all these actions are which the Church requires of them, and of which it boasts that their proper practice is so extraordinarily blessed by God. It seems to me necessary to tell everyone that it is precisely and only in the natural efficacy of these actions that the true reason lies why the kind and wise God has bound his grace to just these actions and no others. Only when this is universally acknowledged will one be able to boast in truth that our holy religion contains not [p. 115] the least occasion for supporting any prejudice among the people. And only when this can be boasted of it, only then can I rejoice in the good fortune that Heaven has deemed me worthy to be a teacher of this religion; only then does it also befit you to help me towards the great end that Catholic Christianity may be ever more widely spread, and recognised and accepted in all its excellence. Amen.

### IV. Matth. 22, 34–40. — IV. Matthew 22:34–40

#### Eingang. — Introduction

The wisest of all who have ever lived on earth must without doubt also have known best, my friends, how the thought of God should be used by us men, and how it should not be used. The text which I have just read gives us a proof of this. A scribe of the party of the Pharisees, who was concerned not with his own instruction but only with putting Jesus, if possible, in a predicament, put to him the question, so disputed at that time: which of the various commandments in the Law was the greatest and most important? The Redeemer of the world, instead of being put in a predicament by this question, rather used it as a welcome occasion to set forth a truth which, if we understand it rightly, can secure us against almost every abuse of religious faith. “Thou shalt” — answered Jesus — “love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, with all thy soul, with all thy strength; this is the first and greatest commandment; and the second is like unto it: Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. In these two commandments is comprised everything that occurs in the Law and in the Prophets.” A very remarkable declaration, my friends, which evidently contained more than Jesus had been asked. That scribe only wished to hear which was the greatest commandment among the many. Jesus, on the other hand, named to him that commandment which was not only the greatest but the only one, [p. 117] of which all the others were a mere consequence. This he declared the commandment of the love of God to be. But however rightly this was said, it would have been just as indeterminate and exposed to just as many abuses had Jesus not added to this one commandment the second, which is, as it were, of the same rank and content with it and was to explain it; this second was the commandment of the love of one’s neighbour. The love of one’s neighbour, the Lord gave us to understand, is properly that through which love of God expresses itself. There is no other way in which man could show his love of God than by treating his neighbour lovingly, by promoting his neighbour’s well-being with the same zeal as his own. You will surely notice even without my reminder, my friends, how close this reminder of the Lord comes to that truth which in one of my last addresses I set up as the chief principle by following which we can most surely forestall every abuse of religious faith. We do not find this principle expressly in Scripture, as I admitted even then; but if we consider the whole conduct of our Lord, that particular way in which he applied faith in God and in God’s revelation to the various circumstances of life, we shall find that not a single time did he say or do anything that could not also be justified apart from the concept of God. By his very deeds, then, he taught us the principle which it would at that time have been too early to state in words. In our present time, and before educated listeners, I believed I could venture, believed I was obliged, to speak about this for once with all clarity. It seemed to me necessary not only to make the principle itself known, but also to show, by a concise assessment of the common abuses of our time, how it must be applied. This latter I will do once more today in this gathering, whose small numbers [p. 118] lead me to close with it the series of my addresses for this school year. If the wise providence of Heaven has not granted me the joy of beholding beneficial effects of my efforts in the near present, may it at least bring about by its blessing that what is sown in affliction may sprout in the future and at a distance.

#### Abhandlung. — Exposition

⁠1) It is not faith in the mere existence of God, but faith in certain revelations from him, that has produced the most blessed effects on earth, and whose abuse, for that very reason, has also been the most dangerous. If we further consider the instruments of which, according to our idea, the Godhead has made use in its revelations to us, there is no doubt that to Holy Scripture, which we Christians possess, and from which we can draw the content of the revelations that have come to us from God either completely or at least for the greatest part — to this excellent book belongs precedence over every other instrument of this kind. No other book, no other object on earth offers so much material for useful instruction as we Christians have partly already actually found in our Bible, and partly could still find in it through diligent use. The Bible alone has founded more good on earth than all that was written before it and without it, taken together! Yet even this book could not escape abuse, my friends; on the contrary, it was very often abused and is still abused today. Or is it not to be called an abuse of this book when whole Christian congregations resist acknowledging a beneficial truth, or introducing a salutary institution among themselves, because they believe they have discovered that Scripture makes no mention of it? Where one proceeds in this way, where one stubbornly [p. 119] resolves to acknowledge no truth which has not been demonstrated to us from Scripture, to admit no obligation of which it cannot be shown that the dead letter of Scripture too lays it upon us: there this holy book, instead of a means of furthering true enlightenment, is a mighty hindrance to it. — Is it not just as certainly an abuse of this book when we use the moral faults which the persons depicted in it have as an excuse for our own faults? Is that which reason and experience declare evil and harmful to the community made permissible and harmless by the fact that thousands practise it? And is everything that is related in the Bible related to us so that we should imitate it? Are not many things related to us merely so that they may serve us as a warning and as an example to deter us forever? Are persons to whom Scripture gives the praise of pious and God-pleasing persons thereby declared entirely free of faults? Are we perhaps never to advance further in perfection than men perhaps attained thousands of years ago, still without the aids of Christianity? Are we not most definitely obliged, in every century, by using the new means which that century offers us, to become wiser, better and more perfect than all earlier ages were? — It is, finally, also an abuse, and a truly vexing one, when we wish to defend certain institutions in society and certain actions of men because the authors of Scripture praise them and represent them as ordinances of God, though reason nonetheless recognises their inexpediency. It is an abuse when we unconditionally declare the constitution which Moses once gave to the people of Israel to be the most perfect that could be devised even at the present time; it is an abuse when we assert that the difference in birthright, in rank and in the financial circumstances of men must continue for all eternity, that for all [p. 120] eternity there must be free men and slaves, masters and servants, high and low, rich and poor among us, because all this is represented in the Bible as God’s ordinance, because it is written there that God himself granted kings the power to rule, and that we would find the poor among us at all times. It is, finally, an abuse when we wish to justify the barbarity of war on the ground that Scripture tells us God himself sometimes gave the command for a war. How incorrect, indeed how perverse, are all these inferences? How can one demand that the constitution which Moses gave his people should be the most expedient of all for all other peoples, and even after thousands of years? Are not the needs of men very different in different countries at different times? Can there not be many a thing that was necessary thousands of years ago but has now become superfluous, perhaps even harmful? Can there not be many an institution for which men of earlier times were not yet receptive, but which could now be introduced with the greatest benefit? We may therefore never infer merely from the fact that certain institutions once received the approval of God, that he once declared them necessary, that they always are so and that he wills that we should always retain them. Did not God himself repeal in a later period the law which he had given in an earlier one? Did he not do this to show us that we should all the less regard our own laws and ordinances as something that must be eternally unalterable? And to what other end was the assistance of the divine Spirit promised to the new Church for all times, than because in its teachings it was not always to stop at what is written; because it was to have the power to determine for itself how and in what sense Scripture must be interpreted if it is to bear the name of the divine Word by right? Let there be written, then, whatever may be written: what cannot be demonstrated by grounds of [p. 121] reason to be true, or at least beneficial, that we can and should not infer from the Bible; or else we abuse it.

⁠2) Yet this is not all that is wont to happen, my friends; it is not only the source itself from which we are to draw the divine revelations that we abuse, when we infer from it what does not lie in it, but we also often turn to our own ruin truths which God has really revealed to us. The most important of these truths, the one through whose communication God has really shown us the greatest benefits that he could show us by any revelation at all, is the assurance that we are immortal; that our soul endures even when this body crumbles to dust. Oh, to know this, and to know it with the certainty that a word given by God himself on the matter imparts to us: what an inestimable good fortune, my friends! how great the advantages this certainty can afford us when it is rightly used! but how manifold, too, the abuses to which it is exposed! When we make use of the belief in immortality as a means of encouragement to everything good; when we labour with restless zeal at the furtherance of the general well-being because the thought strengthens us that our reward will one day be great in heaven; when we gladly bring this earthly life as a sacrifice where duty commands it, because that other life into which we pass is infinitely more blessed; when in our dealings with our fellow men we avoid giving them any offence and committing any violation of duty all the more carefully, the more easily it could happen that the unhappy consequences of such a fault might endure through a whole eternity; when we soften the pain that death has caused us by tearing away beloved relatives with the thought that they still exist and are happy, and that we shall one day see them again: then indeed we make the worthiest use that can and should be made of the belief in immortality. But this [p. 122] belief is not always so fittingly applied. Are there not, even in our own days, a number of people whom the thought of another life, instead of gladdening, only disquiets; who, without being conscious of any particular crimes, can yet think only with trembling of that eternity and of the fearful judgment that is to decide their fate in it? Are there not others whom the thought that they are created for an eternity makes prodigal in the use of time? who hope to be able to make up in another world, without loss, whatever they may neglect of their cultivation in this present one? Do we not witness every year the sad example of sufferers who, weary of their existence on earth, take their own lives in the foolish opinion that they could thus flee by force into a better life? Finally, let us but ask our own heart how often it happens even to better people that in their dealings with their relatives, with their closest friends and acquaintances, precisely because they hope to be united with them for a whole eternity, they are all the more remiss in the proofs of love for which the favourable opportunity lay in the moment that has only just slipped away? Let us ask ourselves whether we would not often be far more tender in our love if we pictured to ourselves that we possess one another only through the short, uncertain time of this earthly life, and then lose one another for ever? I rightly pass over in silence those villains who make a kind of wanton sport of the belief in immortality, when they justify the murder they committed upon thousands and hundreds of thousands by the remark that according to our teaching *dying* would be no misfortune for men. Is there any need of proofs that all these are abuses? According to our principle, my friends, there can be no question whatever about this; for here people allow themselves to draw, from the belief in God and from the belief in immortality founded upon it, various [p. 123] consequences which could by no means be justified without it. But because the abuses of which I speak here are of quite particular harmfulness, God himself, the Gracious One, when he gave us the promise of another life, warned us most emphatically against the abuses of it. Or is not every arbitrary shortening of life, every one not commanded by virtue itself, whether of one’s own life or of the life of others, most strictly forbidden to us? And with what earnestness is not the most conscientious use of every moment and of every favourable opportunity for good in this earthly life commended to us, when we are assured that our lot in all eternity depends on it? How then could we be so foolish as to believe the divine revelation in the one point and not in the other also? How could we persuade ourselves that we do not abuse it, when we draw from its teachings consequences which it itself forbids?

⁠3) But the holy religion that has been communicated to us by God through Christ contains yet another exceedingly important truth, namely the truth that for Christ’s sake God forgives every crime, however great, to him who turns to him and amends with a sincere heart. What, next to the belief in the immortality of our spirit, can be more consoling to us than this truth? What would the belief in another life be to us if we did not have the hope that it will be a happy one? But as everything that is useful to man can also cause him a great deal of harm through abuse, so it is here as well. No other doctrine of Christianity has wrought so much evil as precisely the doctrine of the forgiveness of sins through the merits of Jesus Christ. From this doctrine people took occasion to devise a quite perverted concept of the nature of evil and of good. Instead of keeping to the idea that it is only evil that does harm, they began to persuade themselves that only the [p. 124] divine prohibition alone contains the true reason why this or that is not permitted. If only God has forgiven, then, so it was thought, it does not matter in the least whether one has done good or evil. And because the way to his mercy always stands open to us after all, people heaped crime upon crime, led the most unbridled life, and believed that by a late amendment they could in the end still escape all the ill consequences of sin. Indeed, people could go so far astray that in the midst of Christian lands it was asserted in full earnest that the man who has sinned much, whom God has much to forgive, is more pleasing to him than one who has always lived innocently, because only in the former could God’s infinite mercy be glorified in its fullness! — Even if we no longer harbour such gross errors nowadays, my friends, I nevertheless maintain that even enlightened Christians frequently abuse the doctrine of the forgiveness of sin, and there are surely only very few people whose own conscience, in a strict selfexamination, does not accuse them of having sometimes grown more sluggish, out of hope of forgiveness, in combating their evil inclinations and in breaking themselves of their faults. Even though we know that everything that bears the name of sin, everything that the commandments of God forbid, is by its nature harmful to the common good and cannot be made harmless by any forgiveness; even though we know that the forgiveness Christianity promises us by no means cancels every detrimental consequence of sin, that its natural punishments still remain: nevertheless the mere thought that by amendment we can obtain from God forgiveness, and thus a lessening of the punishment and the removal of all consequences not necessarily bound up with it; this thought alone, I say, is sufficient to lead us into a great deal of evil. Let each of us but examine himself whether we do not often hear a voice rising within us that calls to us: Why do you hesitate to commit this evil, since it can so quickly be atoned for again? [p. 125] what disadvantages will it bring upon you if you do a few more times what you have already often done? Will not everything be forgiven you at once through amendment? Oh, let us be ashamed, my friends, if our consciousness tells us that this voice stirs in us too and is sometimes heard. What is more certain than that this is an abuse, and a truly harmful abuse, of that mercy with which God has made us acquainted in his revelation? What is more certain than that here we use the doctrine of the forgiveness of sins quite contrary to the purpose for which it was given us by God? Is the divine promise to make us sluggish — is it not rather to make us more zealous in all that is good? Nor does Christianity really bear the least blame for this abuse that we make of its teaching. For why do we not attend to what it further makes known to us; why do we not attend to the threat that it calls out to everyone who, instead of using the thought of God’s mercy for his amendment, abuses it for his deterioration? Why do we not listen when it says that the presumption of those who sin in reliance on God’s mercy is the greatest and most punishable crime, that people who have made this sin a habit will never find forgiveness, because they would never amend? Why do we not heed what Christianity tells us of the difficulties and of the unreliability of a later amendment? of our inability to amend ourselves by our own powers and without the aid of divine grace? of the uncertainty of death, which will overtake us at an hour when we least expect it? Oh, if we hold to all these ideas, truly: the doctrine of the forgiveness of sins can then never become dangerous to us!

Let us see from these examples, my friends, how carefully our holy religion seeks to forestall every danger of abuse! let [p. 126] us see this, and let us conclude from it that it is the most excellent religion that could ever be devised to spur us on to every kind of good and to make our earthly sojourn as pleasant as possible for us! But let us not stop at mere knowledge; rather let us strive to give ourselves up with our whole soul to a faith that sets up as its chief principle and as the content of all its precepts the demand that we love one another! — Where indeed on earth should we find a religion that could bear comparison with our Christianity? If we would forsake it, whither, alas! whither would we turn? Still in our own time the word of Peter holds: “Lord! if we forsook thee, whom else should we follow? Thou alone hast the words of eternal life. We are convinced and firmly believe that thou art Christ, the Son of the living God! Amen.

### I. Von Bolzano selbst — verfaßte, bisher veröffentlichte Schriften: — I. Writings Composed by Bolzano Himself and Published So Far:

⁠1) *Philosophical* writings:

a) *Athanasia* oder Gründe für die Unsterblichkeit der Seele. Ein Buch für jeden Gebildeten, der hierüber zur Beruhigung gelangen will [Athanasia, or Grounds for the Immortality of the Soul: A Book for Every Educated Person Who Wishes to Attain Peace of Mind on This Matter]. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1827. 2nd, improved edition, enlarged with a critical appendix, ibid. 1838.

b) *Wissenschaftslehre*. Versuch einer ausführlichen, größtentheils neuen Darstellung der *Logik*, mit steter Rücksicht auf deren bisherige Bearbeitung [Theory of Science: An Attempt at a Detailed and for the Most Part New Exposition of *Logic*, with Constant Regard to Its Previous Treatment]. With a preface by Dr. J. Ch. A. Heinroth. 4 vols. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1837.

c) Ueber den Begriff *des Schönen* [On the Concept *of the* *Beautiful*]. Offprint from the Transactions of the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences. Prague: Borrosch, 1843.

d) Ueber die *Eintheilung* d. schönen Künste [On the *Classification* of the Fine Arts]. Prague: Calve, 1849.

e) Was ist *Philosophie*? [What Is *Philosophy*?]. Vienna: Braumüller, 1849.

f) Paradoxien des *Unendlichen* [Paradoxes of the *Infinite*], edited from the author’s literary remains by Dr. Fr. Prihonsky. Leipzig: C. H. Reclam, 1851.

2. *Theological* writings. a) *Schreiben* eines kath. Geistlichen an den Verf. des Buches: „Die kath. Kirche Schlesiens“ [*Letter* of a Catholic Clergyman to the Author of the Book “The Catholic Church of Silesia”] (Dr. Theiner). Sulzbach: Seidel, 1827.

Gengler [*Views* of a Liberal Catholic Theologian on the Relation between *Church* and *State*, Developed in a Critique of the Aphorisms of Mr. A. Gengler]. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1834.

[p. 128] c) Lehrbuch der *Religionswissenschaft*. Ein Abdruck der Vorlesungshefte eines ehemal. Religionslehrers an einer kath. Universität [Textbook of the *Science of Religion*: A Printing of the Lecture Notes of a Former Teacher of Religion at a Catholic University]. 4 vols. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1834.

d) *Sendschreiben* an Dr. J. F. Röhr in Weimar [*Open Letter* to Dr. J. F. Röhr in Weimar]. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1837.

e) Ueber das *Recht* der Geistlichkeit ihren Lebensunterhalt von Personen zu beziehen, die nicht ihres Glaubens sind [On the *Right* of the Clergy to Draw Their Livelihood from Persons Who Are Not of Their Faith]. An essay in canon law in Pflanz’ Freimüthige Blätter. Stuttgart: Neff, 1838, nos. 3 and 4.

f) *Prüfung* der Philosophie des sel. G. Hermes [*Examination* of the Philosophy of the Late G. Hermes]. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1840.

g) Ueber die *Perfectibilität* des Katholicismus [On the *Perfectibility* of Catholicism]. Polemical writings of two Catholic theologians (Bolzano and Stoppani); at the same time a contribution on some important concepts from Bolzano’s Religionswissenschaft. Leipzig: Voß, 1845.

h) Kurzgefaßtes *Lehrbuch* der katholisch-christlichen Religion, als der wahren göttlichen Offenbarung [Concise *Textbook* of the Catholic Christian Religion, as the True Divine Revelation]. For educated people in general, and in particular for pupils of the grammar schools. Bautzen: Weller, 1849.

4. *Mathematical* writings. a) Betrachtungen über einige Gegenstände der *Elementargeometrie* [Reflections on Some Subjects of *Elementary Geometry*] (including a theory of parallels). Prague: Barth, 1804.

[p. 129] b) *Beiträge* zu einer begründeten Darstellung der Mathematik [*Contributions* to a Well-Founded Presentation of Mathematics]. Prague: Widtmann, 1810.

c) Der *binomische* Lehrsatz und als Folgerung aus ihm der polynomische und die Reihen, die zur Berechnung der Logarithmen und Exponentialgrößen dienen, genauer als bisher erwiesen [The *Binomial* Theorem, and as Consequences of It the Polynomial Theorem and the Series That Serve for Calculating Logarithms and Exponential Quantities, Proved More Rigorously than Hitherto]. Prague: Enders, 1816.

d) *Rein analytischer* Beweis des Lehrsatzes, daß zwischen je zwei Werthen, die ein entgegengesetztes Resultat gewähren, wenigstens eine reelle Wurzel der Gleichung liege [*Purely* *Analytic* Proof of the Theorem That between Any Two Values Which Yield Results of Opposite Sign There Lies at Least One Real Root of the Equation]. Prague: Haase, 1817.

e) Die *drei Probleme* der Rectification, Complanation und Cubirung, ohne Betrachtung des unendlich Kleinen, ohne die Annahme des Archimedes und ohne irgend eine nicht streng erweisliche Voraussetzung gelöst; zugleich als Probe einer gänzlichen Umgestaltung der Raumwissenschaft, allen Mathematikern zur Prüfung vorgelegt [The *Three Problems* of Rectification, Complanation and Cubature, Solved without Considering the Infinitely Small, without the Assumption of Archimedes and without Any Presupposition Not Strictly Provable; at the Same Time Submitted to All Mathematicians for Examination as a Sample of a Complete Reshaping of the Science of Space]. Leipzig: Kummer, 1817.

f) Versuch einer *objectiven Begründung* der Lehre von der Zusammensetzung der Kräfte [Attempt at an *Objective* *Foundation* of the Doctrine of the Composition of Forces]. Prague: Kronberger, 1842. From the Transactions of the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences.

g) Versuch einer *objectiven Begründung* der Lehre von den drei Dimensionen des Raumes [Attempt at an *Objective* *Foundation* of the Doctrine of the Three Dimensions of Space]. Prague: Kronberger, 1843. From the Transactions of the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences.

⁠5) *Historical* and other writings. a) Lebensbeschreibung des Dr. B. *Bolzano* [Life of Dr. B. *Bolzano*] (autobiography), edited and provided with a preface by one of his pupils. Sulzbach: Seidel, 1836.
