Whoever wishes to win acceptance among others for his views, and to see them gradually spread, be it by way of oral instruction or through writing, must not only rest his assertions on premises that are evident to all, but must also take care that his hearers or readers perceive that his conclusion does in fact rest on these and on no other presuppositions. This, however, is not yet always achieved merely by our mentioning no unnecessary propositions in our demonstrations and bringing up only what leads, as an actual premise, to our conclusion; least of all with readers or hearers who are still unpracticed in thinking, or — what is worse — who lack the sincere will to arrive at knowledge of the truth and at a firm conviction of it. Strictly speaking, it would be necessary for us to state on every occasion, and not merely to *state* but to make evident in each of our proofs by a consideration undertaken expressly for the purpose, that and how, apart from the few propositions we have just adduced, nothing else of all that we had asserted in our preceding instruction needs to be presupposed as true. With such great prolixity, however, we can proceed at most in instruction that we impart *orally*: in *writings* we must, for more than one reason, express ourselves more briefly; and for this reason alone it is left, in books, incomparably more than in oral lecturing, to the

[p. 4]

readers’ own facility in thinking, indeed to their mere good will, whether and to what degree they become convinced of the truth of our doctrines even by the most cogent proofs that we lay before them. Hence, especially with works of considerable size and in systematic form, it is an all too common occurrence that readers to whom not everything we venture to assert in them has become perfectly clear and evident to the point of incontestable certainty live in the opinion that they need not regard as satisfactorily proven even the proposition whose proof we have made our chief task, and which forms, as it were, the *conclusion of the whole*; and that they imagine this even when, with a little attention, they might well have gathered that all those propositions in whose proofs they had still found something wanting did not in fact stand in the relation of indispensable premises to that main proposition. Who does not see how greatly this single circumstance hampers and delays the spread of new truths when they may be communicated in no other way than by a *book*! And yet there are many other circumstances besides that stand in the way here. Who does not know how difficult it is, amid the enormous mass of writings brought to light at every fair, to procure even a moderate number of attentive readers for one’s own work, however solid it may be, if one will not avail oneself of means that the modest man disdains? What an easy matter it is, after all, for the editors of learned journals and their contributors to withdraw even the most substantial work from the attention of the public, if they so wish? Who does not manage, especially when he need not even give his name, to assure the learned world that a product under discussion contains nothing new, or that all that is new in it deserves no attention? And is not such an assurance believed all the more readily when it is combined with the flattering declaration that the readers have long since soared to a far higher level of science than the author of that book? — Furthermore, how reluctantly do we

[p. 5]

all — the learned too, if I am not mistaken — set about it, once we have grown accustomed to certain views, to tear ourselves loose from them again, and to appraise justly the worth of one that has become known to us only later? Views, moreover, that only a book sets forth — do they not generally become known to us only after we have already learned all sorts of things opposed to them? Who, finally, does not understand that in oral instruction many a doubt and objection arising in the hearer’s soul can be removed on the spot, which the author of a book, even if he foresaw them, could not allow himself, merely on account of prolixity, to bring up and answer; with the result that in mere reading the conviction so often fails to appear which would no doubt have come about if we had been able to hear the same man orally on his subject! Whoever now weighs all this, and much besides that we leave untouched here, will see clearly with what overwhelming difficulties he has to contend who strives to win standing and recognition for a new doctrine, when no other means of gaining a hearing is at his command than that of the mute letter, and all oral communication, no matter on what ground, is denied him.

In this position, or rather in a considerably worse one still, *Bolzano* finds himself, insofar as he wishes to secure for his peculiar views, which fall within the domains of *philosophy* and *theology*, even only so much attention as would be required for a final decision on their worth or worthlessness. Not only *he himself*, but also all his *former pupils and followers* have — we do not quite know why? — already *for a period of fully twenty years been forbidden not merely every oral presentation of his views*; but even through *writings* can only one or another of his friends living *abroad* do anything, merely by being driven by necessity

[p. 6]

to avail themselves of the so suspect means of *anonymity*. Only timidly did they venture to bring out, in the year 1827, his “*Athanasia, or Grounds for the Immortality of the Soul*”; after a long interval, in 1834, the “*Textbook of the Science of Religion*,” and finally, in the late autumn of the year 1837, the “*Theory of Science*” — in the case of the first two works not even giving the author’s name — through the readiness of the J. E. v. *Seidel Bookshop* in *Sulzbach*. What sort of reception these works, in which a peculiar *philosophical-theological system* is set out in its basic outlines, have met with so far is related at greater length in the “*Appendix to the Second Edition of the Athanasia, Containing a Critical Overview of the Literature on Immortality* since 1827” (Sulzbach, 1838), and in the writing: “*Bolzano and His Opponents, A Contribution to the Most Recent History of Literature.*” (Sulzbach, 1839.) Whoever is willing to read through these little writings — the first, if need be, only from p. 99 to p. 114 — will find it demonstrated in the clearest way that up to now not a single one of the many peculiar doctrines that B. has set forth in the three works named has been *examined by even a single scholar in a scientific manner, and an attempt made at its refutation*. Now we know quite well, to be sure, that there may also be ephemeral productions of such utter worthlessness that, to dispose of them as they deserve, it is really not necessary to pick out all their oddities one by one and to engage in a circumstantial refutation of them; since, after all, the public may well, for once, take the word of a reporter who has already proved himself a conscientious man on a hundred other occasions, that *in this writing not even a single grain of usable truth is to be found*. But is that perhaps the case with B.’s works? Where, then, would any respectable scholar have had the courage to say that the peculiar philosophical, or even merely the theological, concepts

[p. 7]

which B. develops in his writings did not deserve at all to be examined more closely? No: we go through everything that has been put forward for or against these writings from their appearance to this very day; we survey all the favorable and adverse fortunes that they have so far experienced: and we boldly assert: B.’s writings might possess a far higher worth than the author himself ever wished to see attributed to them; they might offer the great word that solves the riddle of the present: and yet they would not have had to be received in any other, in particular any better, way than has so far been the case. The very fact that B.’s concepts must do without the means of all oral communication of thought for their dissemination, and that there are no professorial chairs from which they are delivered to a youth streaming in from all parts of Germany, permits, as we have seen, no rapid advance of them. But joined to this there is still a host of other unfavorable circumstances. B.’s principal work, the “*Theory of Science*,” which alone is really able to justify him as to why in his “*Athanasia*” as well as in the “*Science of Religion*” he followed a manner of philosophizing so thoroughly divergent from the ones now prevailing, appeared not *before*, but *after* these writings, and has been before the public for only two years and a few months; a period within which it was not yet possible at all for a scholar who was not exactly in a position, after laying aside all other work, to occupy himself with this work alone, to become acquainted with all the new assertions contained in the four stout volumes, all the grounds attached to them, and everything that is put forward for the refutation of the opposing views of others. Hence, too, the fact that no man of the field, not a single one of those who have made a name for themselves in philosophy itself, has as yet let his instructive voice be heard for or against the book. Those few who have so far expressed themselves about it in a disparaging tone took care, by the haste with which they delivered their judgment, but still more by their ill-concealed passion,

[p. 8]

to disabuse every reasonable person of the worth he might attach to their anonymous pronouncement.<sup>∗)</sup>

However absurd such a fairy tale may be, it has nevertheless — as is natural — on the assurance of so esteemed a scholar found credence with thousands, and has thereby spoiled in advance the disposition with which B.’s harmless concepts were received among Protestants, not only in this book but also in the *Theory of Science*. In this way it has actually come to the point that Protestants cannot agree with B. because they scent in him a Catholic bent on proselytizing; while Catholics — namely the great number of those who think *servilely*, especially in the clerical estate — must contradict him because his writings are on the *Index*, and because he himself does not deny that he also teaches some *sententias piarum aurium offensivas* [opinions offensive to pious ears].

But this last point already reminds us of certain faults that B. himself may reproach himself with. We do not mean that he also takes under his protection a few offensive-sounding propositions: it is quite other things that we must lay to his charge here. The very fact that the “*Theory of Science*” was published so late is his own fault. For already around the year 1822 it was worked out so carefully that it could quite properly have seen the light of day; and the censorship laws of his country, especially at that time, would hardly have prevented him; why, then, did he postpone this publication from one year to the next in such a way that the work would surely not have appeared even in 1837 if the printing had not finally been brought about without his knowledge and will? Would not the *Athanasia* as well as the *Science of Religion* have met with a quite different reception, if the Theory of Science had prepared the way for their understanding and appreciation? And would not even the latter have been viewed far

Having held a philosophical chair since 1805 and being an author in more than one field,<sup>∗)</sup> how many opportunities and occasions he had to strike up acquaintances and connections with scholars at home and abroad, and to enter now with one, now with another into written correspondence. One knows how greatly such relationships help when it is a matter of the dissemination of new views. But he who left all this unused, who never sent a copy of any of his writings to a scholar elsewhere, who except with his own pupils associated with almost nobody, and since his dismissal, however, up to the present day lives in such seclusion that one almost never gets to see him: that was and is *Bolzano*. — Even his *style of writing* places obstacles in the way of the dissemination of his concepts. It is, to be sure, as we believe, an intelligible manner of expressing oneself that he has acquired: but how monotonous and dry, how devoid of all learned finery, how in vain does the reader look about for all those favored phrases without which our most recent age is simply unable to find any taste in a book; and how clearly and naturally everything is set down, so that everyone thinks he had known that already as well! As for the *Textbook of the Science of Religion* in particular, in it we must find fault, besides a great many defects touching the *form*, chiefly with one very important fault, which B. himself, as we know, has often reproached himself with. Without enlarging the size of the book, it could nevertheless have been indicated everywhere, with a single word, whether what was asserted was necessary or not for arriving at the conclusion of the whole. By such an arrangement not every false conception of the inner connection between the doctrines of the book would, to be sure, have been made impossible, but one would nonetheless have prevented many an error in this respect, especially among readers who are not exactly ill-willed.

But worse than everything we have mentioned so far is the great difficulty which *the peculiar nature of the doctrines* of B. *themselves* causes. If we are to designate in a single word wherein the distinctive feature of the man’s whole way of thinking lies, we must call it — a *striving for distinctness*. From his youth on he was intent on procuring for all his concepts the highest possible degree of distinctness, on bringing to consciousness from which other, simpler concepts each complex concept consists, or (which means just as much) how it is to be *defined*. It is surely not to be wondered at if an effort continued through so many years has also had some success, and if accordingly a somewhat greater distinctness now prevails in B.’s concepts than is usually found in those who were never concerned to make their thoughts clear. What one might find strange is that B. assures us that he has arrived, solely through this striving for clarification, at all the peculiar

[p. 13]

doctrines that one meets with in his writings, not only the philosophical and theological but also the mathematical. At any rate it is undeniable that his writings are throughout very easily intelligible; that no learned preliminary knowledge at all, least of all a preliminary acquaintance with all the earlier systems of philosophy, is needed in order to understand what he says anywhere, and to understand on what grounds he says it, and to feel more or less convinced as well that he speaks the truth. The only thing this writer demands of his readers is that they not be altogether unfit for any rather prolonged reflection on objects that cannot be grasped with the hands, and that they have patience enough to read in order and to skip nothing, unless it be what he himself designates as investigations that one may also pass over. Now this is surely a peculiarity that one need not necessarily declare a fault, which might even be welcome to many, and appear as a sign of truth. That might perhaps be the case with some of our neighboring nations too; but in our *learned Germany* the matter stands, at least at the present time, quite differently. Never was one in Germany less disposed — indeed, to speak openly, never less able — to enter into exact determinations of one’s concepts. Hardly even to the mathematician does one nowadays wish to grant that he may remain with his sharply determined concepts; him too one would like, if possible, to lure into a mere play with wavering words and images. Nothing seems pettier to our “*modern philosophers*,” nothing disgusts them more, than lingering over concepts that are known to the commonest man. Although a lingering over them with the intention of making all sorts of pronouncements about them that throw common human understanding into amazement and confusion might still suit their taste: yet a lingering of the kind practiced by B., who merely wants to bring to consciousness what one actually thinks in connection with these concepts, and who brings forward only things to which

[p. 14]

everyone must say: *So it is!* — is quite beneath the dignity of our philosophers, who scorn every truth that is to be won at so cheap a price. Our German scholars have, after all, each of them spent such an unspeakable amount of time and energy on becoming familiar with almost all the philosophical systems that have ever existed; they have tried to make their own, as well as ever they could, the abstruse and self-canceling systems that one and the same philosopher had brought to light in periods following closely one upon another; it is natural that they do not wish to have done all this in vain, and now demand of everyone else that he take the same trouble as they. Their heads have been made so dizzy by the appropriation of so many absurd doctrines that they are no more able to distinguish true from false than top from bottom. Hence one hears them say that each of these systems is true in its way, yet nonetheless refutes the one immediately preceding, and is itself in turn refuted by the one immediately following. How, then, are we to expect from such scholars a just appraisal of a system that declares all their exertions dispensable, and demonstrates that, and in what respects, the most highly praised systems already erred in their very first propositions? —

The evil would still be somewhat tolerable, however, if B. had analyzed *only a single concept* of importance, say that of *truths in themselves* or that of *experience*, and had contented himself in this analysis with stating its nearest components; had not advanced any further, either, than to the first important consequences that result from this clarification of one of our concepts; if in this way he had laid his Theory of Science before the learned world when it was perhaps a tenth of its present extent, instead of letting it swell to a bulk of a hundred and fifty sheets. We could then certainly hope far more readily that people would make themselves acquainted with its content. But now, when one sees before one a work of such extent, and when one begins

[p. 15]

to read in it, and becomes aware that one has not yet made even half of the one divergent view familiar to oneself before the author already comes forward with a second, must not even the most vigorous reader grow weary, and feel tempted to skip, or to read in cursory haste, merely in order to reach the end? But if he does this, then nothing is more natural than that he should now misunderstand, now at any rate remain unconvinced, and each time find the fault not in himself but in the book. Nothing, too, is more natural than that the many attacks which the author — though always in the most modest manner — permits himself upon the doctrines of others should only offend the reader, indeed lead him in the end even to the suspicion that B. decides about so many subjects differently from what has hitherto been customary out of mere craving for innovation! —

How, then, is this to be remedied? A means by which all the obstacles named so far would be removed does not seem to us to lie within the realm of possibility; but something by which at least one or another of these difficulties would be diminished we should know how to indicate: B. has offered too much at once; can this fault not be remedied by a kind of abridgment? At bottom, however, it is only the two larger works, the *Theory of Science* and the *Science of Religion*, that put their readers’ patience to a somewhat harder test; for about the *Athanasia*, as far as we know, no one has yet complained that it wearied him. It seems to us, then, that by a short *overview*, keeping more to the matter than to the word, of only the most important doctrines contained in those two works, in which we should at the same time make the reader attentive to what each of them presupposes from what precedes, much would be gained insofar as it would then be made possible for everyone who does not shrink from the expenditure of a few hours to acquire a knowledge of B.’s most peculiar views. And in this way there would moreover be attained the advantage of counteracting the false and distorting reports that others have made to the public about him, at least in such a way that now, for everyone who wishes to be undeceived,

[p. 16]

it would be an easy matter to learn the truth. Finally, whoever, prompted to it either by this work itself or in some other way, wishes to become fully acquainted with B.’s philosophical-theological system from his own writings will find, we flatter ourselves, that the overview supplied here facilitates both its comprehension and its correct assessment.

### I.

## Overview of the Theory of Science.

[scan 20]

The full title of the work of whose most important doctrines we wish first to lay a critical overview before our readers runs: “Dr. B. *Bolzano’s Theory of Science. Attempt at a Detailed and Largely New Exposition of Logic, with Constant Regard to Its Previous Expositors. Edited by Several of His Friends. With a Preface by* Dr. Ch. A. *Heinroth*.” (4 vols. Sulzbach, at the J. E. v. Seidel Bookshop, 1837.)

The first thing, then, that meets our eyes on opening this book, after we have read the *forewords* of the *publisher* and of the *editors* and the *preface* by Professor *Heinroth* already mentioned on the title page, is the definition that the author gives in § 1 of the science he is to treat here, which he calls the “*theory of science*.” He says that for him it is a guide to how the entire domain of human knowledge should be divided into individual sciences, and each of these set forth in appropriate textbooks. Considered as a definition of *logic* — for B. identifies his theory of science with logic, § 3 — this may be new in its wording; in substance it departs but little from what the most celebrated logicians, from Aristotle down to the most recent times, have understood by logic — with the exception, perhaps, of the self-styled “*speculative philosophers*” of our day. This much at least is incontestable, and can be gathered, without even reading the book itself, from a mere glance through its tables of contents: that B. discusses all the subjects that it has hitherto been customary to treat in logic — (*Hegel* still excepted) — in his textbook too, for the most part merely in greater detail. Indeed, even most of what is brought up in the three volumes of *Hegel’s Logic* is touched upon by B., although he treats it in an entirely different

[p. 20]

manner, and almost always arrives at something very different from *Hegel’s* results. In *Hegel’s* Logic there is talk, in the first volume, of the question with what the *beginning* of science is to be made, then of *quality*, of *being*, *nothing*, *becoming*, *something*, *constitution*, *alteration*, *ought*, *negation*, of *finitude* as well as *infinity*, of *unity* and *plurality*, *quantity*, *number*, *magnitude*, *quantitative infinity*, the *Kantian categories*; in the second volume of *essence*, the *essential* and the *unessential*, *illusory being*, *reflection*, *ideality* and *difference*, *opposition*, the principles of *diversity*, of the *excluded middle*, of *contradiction*, of *ground*, of *form* and *matter*, the *conditioned* and the *unconditioned*, *existence*, *relation*, *possibility*, *actuality* and *contingency*, of the law of *causality*; in the third volume, finally, of the *Concept*, the *universal*, *particular* and *singular*; of *judgment*, the *positive*, *negative* and *infinite*; the *singular*, *particular* and *universal*; the *categorical*, *hypothetical* and *disjunctive*; the *assertoric*, *problematic* and *apodictic*; of the *syllogism*, the *four figures*, *induction*, *analogy*, the *categorical*, *hypothetical* and *disjunctive* syllogism; of *end* and *means*, of the *Idea*, the *individual* and the *genus*; of the concept of the *true*, of *analytic* and *synthetic* cognition, of *definition*, *division* and *theorem*: and behold! all these subjects we find discussed in B.’s textbook too, now more, now less fully than in *Hegel*. Thus in the end it is only the following points: *repulsion* and *attraction*, the *continuous* and *discrete* magnitudes, the *infinitely small*, the *direct*, *indirect* and *power ratios*, *measure*, *porosity*, *the solicitation of forces*, and finally *mechanism*, *chemism* and *life*, which are listed in Hegel’s Logic but remain unmentioned in B.

[p. 21]

But if, as we now see, his peculiar concept of logic in no way led him to set aside doctrines that have hitherto been taught in this science, then perhaps, on the contrary, he has sinned against it by taking in a host of investigations that are entirely foreign to it! Given the great size of the work, 153¼ median sheets, this does indeed very much appear to be so! How, then, do matters really stand? Our readers rightly demand to know this before they follow us any further into the interior of the book, so that it may rest with their own judgment whether, and which, of its doctrines they come to know more closely.

We must report, then, that B.’s logic, together with an introduction of 68 pages, consists of the following *five* parts.

1) *Theory of Fundamentals*, containing the proof that there are *truths in themselves*, and that we human beings also have the capacity to *know* them.

2) *Theory of Elements*, or the doctrine of ideas, propositions, true propositions and inferences *in themselves*.

3) *Theory of Knowledge*, or on the conditions to which knowledge of the truth is subject, in particular in us human beings.

4) *Art of Discovery*, or the rules for the business of thinking when it aims at knowledge of the truth.

5) *Theory of Science Proper*, or the rules for dividing the entire domain of truth into individual sciences and for setting these forth in textbooks.

Here, then, we freely admit that the very task which the author sets himself in his *Theory of Fundamentals* (vol. I, pp. 69–212) has never before been treated in a textbook of logic; and from this circumstance one may, if one likes, by all means draw the conclusion that this task will not belong in logic in all eternity either: nevertheless, to be fair, in reckoning how much the material that does not belong here amounts to, one will have to set aside all those §§

[p. 22]

in which certain concepts generally counted as belonging to logic, such as those of a *proposition*, of a *truth*, of a *judgment*, of a *cognition*, are more closely determined, as well as the § in which the *laws of thought* customary in the textbooks hitherto are considered, whereupon in the end no more than §§ 30–33 and 40–44, together amounting to scarcely 50 pages, would have to be cut out as a *πάρεργον* [incidental work].

The second, i.e. the most extensive part of the book, which runs from p. 213 of the first volume to the end of the second and fills 58 sheets, discusses throughout only doctrines that either already actually occur in every other logic under the same heading, *Theory of Elements*, or, provided only that they cannot be refuted as untrue, may yet have to be included in future. For if what is put forward in §§ 198–222 on the *objective connection* between truths in themselves were not incorrect; if it could not be refuted that the forms of inference listed in §§ 225–253 are all true, peculiar, and indispensable to thinking: would not something have to be said in future about these subjects too in every more detailed textbook? To be sure, B. has included in this part, namely in the doctrine of the various *kinds of ideas* and *propositions*, one thing and another which, by his own admission, was not exactly *necessary* to include. But if it is true, as in our view he shows very clearly, that the limit of how far one has to go in enumerating those kinds can by no means be marked out so sharply as some imagined they could by the learned magic word: *form*, then he surely deserves no such severe censure for having wished here to do rather more than too little; especially when the concepts he explained on such occasions are of such importance for several sciences as are the concepts of *time* and *space*, § 79, of *multitudes*, *sums* and *magnitudes*, § 84, of a *series*, § 85, of *opposition*, § 107, and of a *moral* proposition, § 144. These examples, combined with the mention made in § 143 of propositions that express a *psychical phenomenon*,

[p. 23]

would presumably be just about everything that could be struck out on the ground that it is perhaps too special a subject for general logic.

Something unusual in a textbook of logic is, to be sure, also a part with the heading: “*Theory of Knowledge*” (vol. III, pp. 1–292). Some, rather, give this name to a separate science, which they let follow logic but immediately precede metaphysics. If, however, one wishes to hold not to names but to the thing itself, one sees that the investigations which B. undertakes in this third part are, with the exception of two (§§ 303–305, and §§ 314 and 315), to be met with alike in a hundred other logics. Of the investigation in §§ 303–305 (on the way in which we mediate those of our empirical judgments that appear to us as if unmediated) B. himself confesses that one may skip it. As for §§ 314 and 315, however, or the question *on the limits of our cognitive faculty*: we well understand that on account of its importance it has since *Kant* been raised to the subject of a separate science; we likewise understand that, already because of the greater difficulty of comprehension of the investigations believed necessary for this, and perhaps in part also because of the still disputed outcome of them, scruples have been felt about incorporating it into logic, i.e. into that philosophical science which one had once designated only for *beginners*, and for which one moreover sought to preserve the glory that in it everything was no less *undisputed* than in mathematics. But whoever can set such considerations aside — and we should think that one may set them aside at least in certain places —: will he really fail to recognize that the question of the limits of our knowledge belongs quite properly in logic, even if logic is, for us, not yet to be a theory of science at all, but only a simple *instruction in correct thinking*? For how could we boast of teaching correct thinking if we do not teach whether there are certain objects, and which, about which one cannot pass a correct judgment, however strictly one may follow all other rules of thinking;

[p. 24]

because they altogether transcend the very limit of our cognitive faculty? —

The concept of logic just mentioned, that it is a *doctrine of thinking*, is, as is well known, the prevailing one: from this, however, it follows directly that B.’s fourth part, the *Art of Discovery* (vol. III, p. 293 to the end), containing the rules for the business of thinking when knowledge of new truths is aimed at, at bottom treats only the subject that is regarded as the proper task to be solved by logic. No one, then, can complain about what B. presents in this fourth part that it does not belong in logic; unless he wished to object — which could be the case at most with an investigation of a few pages (on the criteria of a divine revelation) in § 388 — that it does not have enough generality.

Quite otherwise is it with the last part, to which the author has given the heading: “*Theory of Science Proper*.” For since hitherto very few logicians declare in express words that the purpose of logic, its ultimate purpose, that is, is a higher one than merely to teach thinking correctly in general, that very part in which B. sees the goal of all the remaining investigations, the doctrine of the division of the whole of human knowledge into individual sciences, and the elaboration of these, must appear to them as an excrescence. About this we do not wish to quarrel with anyone; so we shall not even raise the question whether there ought not, after all, in fact to be a science that sets up the rules for the formation and elaboration of all the others? nor ask to which science this business could more fittingly be entrusted than to logic? nor even investigate whether many a thing that has hitherto been included in logic does not appear in it in its proper place merely because that purpose has tacitly been attributed to it? All this we let drop, and we give everyone who simply will not have it that logic may be a theory of science the permission to leave the whole fourth volume unread. Even so we still hope that he will meet with a good deal that is new and instructive in the work.

[p. 25]

Next to the *concept* that B. sets up of his science, what deserves to be singled out from the introduction as something striking is the way in which, in § 12, he interprets the saying *that logic is supposed to be a merely formal science*; namely only in such a way that it considers not individual but merely entire kinds of propositions. This interpretation, too, the readers may reject, and on its account already beforehand fear that the author will be led by such a view in the sequel into many a false step. By all means; we ask only that they first await the outcome itself, and, in the case of every single doctrine they wish to reject, rise to a distinct consciousness of the ground from which they wish to pronounce this judgment of rejection.

It is also noteworthy that in § 13 *logic* is declared to be *no entirely independent science*, because it borrows auxiliary propositions, if from no other science, then at least from *psychology*. Now this surely cannot give offense, at least not to anyone for whom logic is to be a *doctrine of thinking*, or who at any rate grants that in it one may speak of a connection of our ideas in the mind, of the necessity of their designation, of clear and obscure, distinct and confused ideas, and the like. For are not all these *psychological* doctrines?

Concerning the *plan*, finally, that the author sketches in § 15 for his presentation, we need, after what our readers have already learned about it, add nothing further. For the only thing that would still need a special justification here, namely the distinction that B. wants to have made between propositions and ideas *in themselves* and *thought* propositions and ideas, and his desire to consider in the *Theory of Elements* only the former, but the latter only in the *Theory of Knowledge*, will settle itself of its own accord in what follows immediately, through the explanation of this distinction.

[p. 26]

Let us begin, then, with the *first part*, i.e. with the *Theory of Fundamentals*, where in the *first chapter* the author seeks to demonstrate the proposition *that there are truths in themselves*, and in the *second* the proposition *that we human beings, too, know some of them*. Understandably, he found it necessary here first to make quite clear to the readers what he understands by *propositions and truths in themselves*, which he also sometimes calls *objective* ones, in contrast to *thought* propositions and truths. To this clarification §§ 19 and 24–26 are devoted, and §§ 21 and 27 show that others, too, have already known these concepts and designated them in a similar way. The more certain this latter point is, the more we hope that it will suffice here to say that by *propositions and truths in themselves* B. thinks absolutely nothing other than what we all think in connection with these words when we use them, say, in the following expressions: when we raise, e.g., the question whether every *truth* is *known* by some being, and every *proposition*, even every false one, *thought* or *conceived* by someone; or when we remark that through instruction in geometry the Pythagorean *proposition* is *multiplied* not *in itself*, but only the thought of it, the knowledge of it, in our minds; or finally also when we say: “If there were not a single thinking being, the *proposition* that there is not a single thinking being would itself be a *truth*.” For it is evident that in all these cases we understand by the words: *proposition* and *truth* something quite other than a kind of *thoughts*, that we rather understand only something such as can first be *grasped* by a thinking, i.e. can make up the *matter* of a thought. It is just as evident, however, that by propositions and truths we do not here (and probably nowhere) mean the *things themselves* of which they speak; for otherwise one would have to say that the proposition: there is a God, is God himself, and the proposition: there is no unicorn, is, say, the unicorn, or the non-unicorn itself. It is exactly and only as in these expressions that B. now wants the

[p. 27]

words: *proposition* and *truth* to be understood. Although, then, as those examples show, the concepts he has set up occur even in everyday life, it is nonetheless only too true that one has never yet deemed them worthy of a really detailed consideration in the writings of the philosophers; that in older works one has usually contented oneself with a mere *mention* of them, and that in the more recent ones one passes over them altogether in silence, although one cannot help making use of them occasionally (probably without being clearly conscious of it oneself). To cite just a single example of this from the writings of one of the most esteemed philosophers of our time: Professor J. H. *Fichte*, in his work *Opposition, Turning Point and Goal of Present-Day Philosophy* (Heidelberg, 1832, vol. I, p. 133), makes the following admission, which confirms our entire present inquiry: “When we there” (he writes, as he is judging a remark by *Franz Baader*) “learn that *faith* is the only beginning toward right knowledge, this can mean, in the sense of science, only the *first* formal *presupposition* of all knowing: *that there is truth at all, and that it is accessible to us*.” What one understands by “*truth*” when one asserts that it is “*accessible*” to man can be absolutely nothing other than what B. calls *objective truth*, or *truth in itself*.

It is now a question whether our readers, too, can warm to this concept of B.’s and grant it objectuality, i.e. whether they can concede that there are such propositions and truths in themselves? If they answer this in the affirmative, then the purpose of his *first* chapter is attained; for whether they find the *analysis* or *definition* of this concept that he attempts in § 28 correct or incorrect is of no consequence at all. And just as little does it matter whether they are satisfied by his proof that there are *infinitely many* truths in themselves (§ 32). For each of them will readily convince himself of this in many other ways, as soon as he merely refrains, for once, from doubting

[p. 28]

that there is at least one or a few truths. If, however, they are unable to acknowledge that concept, then they are indeed not yet compelled to reject everything that is subsequently set forth merely because the very first thing will not strike them as evident; but they will nevertheless be unable to accept *many* and *important* things. What, precisely? That we shall point out to our readers exactly, at every point, in what follows.

B.’s simple procedure in his proof is this, however. That there are at least some truths he proves — by an inference already used by others — from the fact that the opposite assertion, namely that nothing is true, cancels itself. That there are, however, several truths, indeed infinitely many, he infers from the fact that every finite multitude of truths A, B, C — when combined into a single proposition — presents a new truth, different from each single one, namely the truth: “the collection of the propositions A, B, C is a collection of nothing but true propositions.” That this new truth is not exactly of any special noteworthiness he will himself concede; but it is enough if everyone must acknowledge that this proposition is a new proposition, different from each of the individual ones A, B, C; for by this alone the opinion that the multitude of all truths is merely finite is already refuted.

For philosophers who have become *accustomed* to a quite opposite view of things it will admittedly be hard to convince themselves of the correctness of so simple an exposition, and in particular also of the claim that *the beginning of philosophizing* must be made in this way. Precisely because the matter is so simple, and so appeals even to common human understanding, one will acknowledge only reluctantly that every *philosopher*, too, can and must content himself with this exposition. Above all, will all those who imagine that there can be no other, more far-reaching beginning of philosophizing than a *psychological* one be inclined to concede that B.’s standpoint is a higher one? And yet it is so. For

[p. 29]

we can undertake investigations into our *knowing*, *psychological considerations*, only after we have — aloud or tacitly, it makes no difference — *presupposed* that there *are truths in themselves*, and that *we who judge* are in a position *to know* some of them. It is therefore entirely as *Fichte* said in the passage cited above: “The *first* formal *presupposition of all knowing is that there is truth at all, and that it is accessible to us*.” This presupposition itself, however, and in fact at first only the proposition *that there are truths at all*, we can quite properly see, and must be able to see, without *presupposing* in our reflection, i.e. using as a *premise*, the *proposition* that we *are* knowing beings; though of course not *without being such*. The difference between these two — that a certain *proposition* must be used in our reflections as a *premise* if we are to arrive at full conviction of a conclusion, and that what is stated in this proposition is necessary as a *condition* if we are to undertake these reflections with the result of conviction — this difference is, I should think, unmistakable. For all philosophizing a state of wakefulness and more besides is indispensable as a *condition*. But who will say that in all philosophizing we would have to proceed from the *premise* (the principle) that we are now awake, etc.?

Nevertheless, we must also call our readers’ attention to an apparent objection against the assertion that B. is to prove in this chapter. He himself admits that his *truths in themselves*, just like his *propositions in themselves*, are absolutely nothing *actual*, nothing *existing*; only the *thought proposition*, the *judgment*, the *known truth* have, as appearances in the mind of a thinking being, their *actuality* limited to a certain time, but not the proposition or the truth in itself. But if these are nothing actual, how, then, are they *something* at all? How is the phrase, *there are* propositions and truths in themselves, to be interpreted? and

[p. 30]

how, finally, is the heading given to this chapter, “*On the Existence of Truths in Themselves*,” to be justified? — To this we reply: Not every something has, or must have, actuality. For do we not generally speak also of things that, consisting in mere *possibility*, have as yet no actuality, indeed also of such as will one day attain actuality? It is therefore obviously false that the non-actual is *nothing*. Thus it can be said, in the same sense in which it can be said that there are possibilities that are nothing actual, that there are truths in themselves, even though these truths are nothing actual. The former, namely, has only the sense that the *concept* of a truth in itself — and the latter only the sense that the concept of a possibility — has objectuality. Language, however, also permits itself to speak of that which has no actuality in expressions that properly belong only to the actual. Thus one also says of what has mere possibility that *it is*, namely, *it is possible*. And only in this sense did the author permit himself, in that heading, the expression: *On the Existence* of Truths in Themselves. Certainly, however, he would have done better had he avoided altogether so easily misunderstood an expression.

Before we leave this first chapter, we believe we should also lay before our readers the definition that the author attempts of the concept of truth (in itself); if only so that they may the more easily recognize the incorrectness of another definition of this concept, which has occasioned many errors. B. says, then, that only *propositions*, but not mere *ideas*, can be divided into true and false; he remarks further that every proposition has an *object* with which it deals, and that it *ascribes* to this object (which it represents in its subject idea) an *attribute* (represented in its predicate idea). This proposition is called *true* if it *ascribes* to the object an attribute that *belongs* to it; and *false* if it does not do so. This definition is (it seems to us) at least very intelligible; also

[p. 31]

everyone is likely to admit that the concept to be defined does not still lie hidden, unanalyzed, in any of its components. If, on the other hand, as is very common, one says *that truth is an agreement of our ideas with their objects*: does one not, for a start, already commit the error of seeking truth in an *idea*, whereas it properly belongs only to *propositions*? and is one at all in a position to give us a distinct concept of what one has in mind by the *agreement* demanded here? After one has labored in vain to state something else, one will in the end be forced to say that an *agreement* obtains between our idea and its object when we ascribe to the object attributes that it actually has; which comes down to B.’s definition. And so one should, after all, let go once and for all the opinion that a certain *similarity* must obtain between the idea and its object, and the question that has sprung from it, *how one can convince oneself of the existence of this similarity*? a question that one has never been able to answer without falling into new errors, e.g. that idea and object must be *identical* with each other, and the like. The idea, provided it is an objectual one in the first place, represents its object without having any similarity to it greater than any two of the most dissimilar things have to one another. Whether, however, a given idea belongs to the objectual ones, and what objects it has — all this we know through inferences of an entirely different kind, into the exposition of which we cannot now, of course, enter.

In the *second chapter* the discussion is no longer of truths in themselves, but of our *knowledge* of them, hence of *judgments*, and it is to be shown *that at least some of our judgments are true*, i.e. *are cognitions*. Whoever concedes this in advance may simply skip this chapter; but whoever

[p. 32]

has already felt now and then in himself an impulse to doubt everything, let him read, and he will perhaps find some things that satisfy him. *Some* things, we say; for that everything that occurs, say, in the dialogue of § 42 should be intelligible to him, we do not expect, since various things are touched on here which receive their full light only in the later parts of the work. At any rate, everyone will learn to see at least this much: that one must do only one of two things — either refrain altogether from all judging, or concede that one nevertheless knows some truth or other; for otherwise one merely contradicts oneself.

To whoever also reads the *criterion of true judgments* given in § 43 we advise paying attention to the difference between what is said here and the usual doctrine (cf. § 44 and § 347, note). For here there is absolutely no question of any *immediately recognizable necessity* of a judgment (which can never be an object of our perception); but only of something that can quite properly be observed: whether several attempts made by us to alter the judgment have always remained unsuccessful. Of such judgments it is said here that they have reliability. And who can possibly deny this? As for what is finally said, by way of appendix, in § 45 *about the supreme laws of thought occurring in other textbooks*, it may be judged very variously; it is without any influence on what follows, since the author nowhere refers back to it, nor does he by any means deny those laws, but only says that they are not *mere subjective laws* of our human thinking, but rather *objective truths*, though by no means such as could be regarded as the *supreme grounds* of all the rest. Of several striking remarks that follow one another here in compressed space, we wish to single out for our readers only these two: that the *principle of excluded middle* holds quite generally, not (as some said) of merely *possible* or (as others wished) of *logical* objects, but of all objects; and that the *principle of contradiction* has been contested by *Schelling* and others

[p. 33]

wrongly, since what these call a *contradiction* can be so called only in a most improper sense.

We now turn at once to that part of the work which presents the most numerous and most important peculiarities in B.’s logical views, namely to the *Theory of Elements*, which treats, in four chapters, *of ideas in themselves* (vol. I, pp. 215–end), *of propositions in themselves* (vol. II, pp. 3–326), *of true propositions* (pp. 327–390) and *of inferences* (pp. 391–end).

What B. understands by *ideas in themselves*, or also *objective ideas*, in contrast to *thought* or *subjective* ones (chap. I, sect. I), no one can find difficult who has once grasped the concept of *propositions* or *truths in themselves*. For just as every *thought* proposition is composed of various parts, which we call *thought* ideas (an idea of that one object or those several objects about which something is stated in the proposition, an idea of the attribute that is ascribed to that object, and the like): so every *objective* proposition has certain parts corresponding to the former, which B. calls *objective ideas*. Just as to every *thought* proposition a *proposition in itself* corresponds, which is just then thought in that proposition, so too to every *thought* idea there corresponds an *idea in itself*, which appears to our mind in it. — This concept, too, of *objective ideas* is not new, and occurs, though more seldom, still often enough even in the language of common life, where we use for its designation now the word *idea* simply, now, and still more willingly, the word *concept*. Is it, for example, an unusual way of speaking, “that the *idea* or the *concept* that the aestheticians connect with the word *beauty* is *one and the same* with what the Greeks already thought by their κάλλος”? Here, now, we obviously understand by the words: *idea* or

[p. 34]

*concept*, not an idea in the *subjective* sense, not an *appearance* taking place at a particular time in the mind of a thinking being: for how could we then speak of a concept in the singular? Would we not rather have to express ourselves somewhat thus: “The *ideas* that our aestheticians have when they pronounce the word beauty, and the *ideas* that the word κάλλος once aroused in the Greeks, are *equal ideas*”? If, instead of speaking of several, we speak of a *single idea* that has hovered before, or still hovers before, all those thinking individuals: then it is clear that by this idea we do not think the appearances in the mind, of which there were several, but only a *something* corresponding to these appearances and grasped in them, i.e. the *objective idea*. Since the difference just touched upon between ideas and propositions *in themselves* and between *thought* propositions and ideas (that the latter are something actual, the former nothing actual) is certainly not the only one; since, for example, surely only *thought* ideas and propositions can be divided into clear and obscure, etc.: one will soon also come to terms with the fact that our author, according to his plan, wants propositions and ideas in the *objective* sense to be considered separately from *thought* propositions and ideas, assigning to the former the *Theory of Elements* and referring the latter to the *Theory of Knowledge*. One will *grant* him this, we say, even if one has as yet no inkling at all of what important disclosures about the nature of our knowing the maintenance of this distinction will help him to attain.

But whoever has already rejected the concept of *propositions in themselves* can of course admit still less that of *ideas in themselves*; yet we must remark that even such a person can accept everything that is taught in the next following section (“*Inner Attributes and Differences of Ideas*,” pp. 237–428), and still more what is taught in the later sections, understood in his own way, namely as concerning ideas that appear in the mind of a

[p. 35]

thinking being, except for the single proposition of § 54: “*Ideas in themselves have no existence*”; since he will, to be sure, concede an existence to ideas in his sense. Before we consider all this more closely, however, we must, in order to grasp quite completely which concepts B. wants connected with the words: *subjective* and *objective idea*, add two remarks: *first*, that he calls an idea *only* such a part of a proposition as is not already in itself a *whole proposition*, and *second*, that he calls *every* such part, however constituted it may otherwise be, an *idea*. The expediency of these two stipulations is (it seems to us) very evident for the use of logic (and outside it B. does not himself observe them). Others, however, are pleased to speak an entirely different language; on the one hand they want even complete *propositions* to be regarded as mere ideas, indeed even as simple intuitions; on the other hand they again want to declare not every idea, but only one that refers to a currently *absent object*, or is merely *referred* to such an object by us (by means of a judgment), indeed only an idea that is referred by us *erroneously* to an object that it does *not* in fact represent, or finally only an idea that is accompanied by a certain *sensory image*, in contrast with the *concept*, to be a proper *idea*. To complete the confusion, they ascribe to these ideas, indeed even to concepts themselves, *motion*, i.e. they let them change gradually, even turn into their opposite; hence it is, then, that they believe they may give, e.g., of one and the same concept not one definition but several different ones, of which the one is supposed to present its essence still more *truly* than the other, etc. It is now, to be sure, true that *thought* ideas, because they are properly only certain *alterations* in our inner being, are themselves subject in turn to change; and only too true that we often, without even being conscious of it, pass from the one to the other; that we ourselves gradually alter such ideas

[p. 36]

as we relate as predicates to the same object (e.g. this is something laudable, blameworthy, and the like), and indeed so alter them that our second judgment states the exact opposite of the first: yet it is just as true and evident that when we render ourselves an account of our thinking and secure ourselves as far as possible against confusion and error — the more so when we even want to instruct others — we must strive, to be sure, not to prevent that passing of one thought into another, but to make it fruitful only by bringing it to consciousness, designating each of these thoughts by a definite *word*, and giving these words unchangeable *meanings*. These meanings now — are they anything other than B.’s *objective ideas*? That he accordingly does not wish to concede motion to *objective* ideas, and that he also does not wish to say of a *subjective* idea that represents a *different* object, or consists of *different parts*, or in general corresponds to a different *idea in itself*, *that it is still the same one, which has merely moved on*, would hardly deserve censure. But that he takes the word *idea* in so wide a sense that every component of a proposition that is not itself a whole proposition is called an idea, yet does not extend it any further: this is surely justified well enough by the fact that this class of things is certainly worthy of a designation of its own, while language has no other suitable word for it.

After this we shall grasp without difficulty most of the doctrines asserted in the already mentioned *second section*. *That ideas are neither true nor false* (§ 55) follows immediately from their concept, if one determines it as B. has done above; and it holds in the same way whether one thinks of objective or of subjective ideas; for precisely because the idea is no whole proposition, and therefore asserts nothing, it can be neither true nor false. Should some nevertheless assert the

[p. 37]

opposite, this can only be either because they also counted *whole propositions* among ideas, or (as is more frequent) thought of a definite *object* to which the idea in question is supposed to apply, and so actually *related* it to that object by means of a proposition, or finally demanded of every idea that it have an object, and then declared any that did not meet this expectation to be a *false* idea, instead of calling it a merely *objectless* one. Incidentally, this dispute, too, is of no importance.

Of great influence, on the other hand, is whether we can agree with B. in his assertion in § 56 that there are *simple* as well as *complex* ideas. This too holds, if it holds, of ideas *in themselves* just as of *thought* ideas. But *Hegel* has declared us, once and for all, to be barbarians when we teach this, and say, e.g., that the concept of a *rational being* is composed of the concepts: being, rationality, and the like. Shall we now, in spite of this, venture to stay with the old doctrine? It is indeed true that every thinking being is a *living* one, and granted, too, that it follows from this that thinking itself must be something *living* or (to grant you the joy of this word!) something *organic*; granted that one may accordingly by no means subject it to the laws of a rigid mechanism, still less merely add and subtract ideas and thoughts, like dead numbers, and the like; that rather (so you like to hear it) a *spiritual interaction* obtains among our thoughts, one of which brings forth another, roughly how? — as the bud brings forth the blossom, this the fruit, etc.: all this granted, we must nevertheless insist, relying on the clearest testimony of our consciousness, insist that we have ideas that we produce from the combination of several others, which are therefore *complex*, and again others that we first make use of for such combinations, which are therefore *simple*.

[p. 38]

B. does indeed go still further and asserts in § 58 that some ideas even contain *whole propositions* as parts; as, for example, the subject idea in the proposition: “*The knowledge of the truth that God is holy* — has — great importance”; and by this he believes he is by no means contradicting his definition of the *concept* of an idea; for what includes a proposition as a component need not on that account itself be a proposition again. He even asserts that even such an idea as “*Rational being*” contains a whole proposition; since, if its components are to emerge more clearly, it would properly have to be expressed thus: “A being *that has rationality*.” He asserts further that the components of an idea are connected, not like the parts of a *multitude* in arbitrary order, but only in a determinate order; because two ideas, like “a man of much understanding but little feeling” and “a man of little understanding but much feeling” — with the same components — differ only by the order in which they are connected. He asserts in § 64 that the *components* of an idea and the ideas of the attributes of its object (*marks* of it) are to be distinguished; so that, for example, the idea may be simple, or contain only a very modest number of components, while its object possesses an infinite multitude of attributes. He asserts that there belong to an object not only attributes of which no mention is made at all in its idea, but that attributes thought in it may also be lacking to it. Thus in the idea “equilateral triangle” nothing occurs of the size of the angles, and yet it is known that all equilateral triangles have angles that are equal to one another and measure 60°. Again, in the idea “the son of a virtuous father” the attribute *virtuous* is mentioned, and yet it can be lacking to the object of this idea. This, B. holds, is the case in particular with all *negative* ideas, such as “*a lifeless being*.” For this idea, according to him, contains the concept of

[p. 39]

*life*, though combined with that of negation; it is to be grasped, namely, as the idea of a being that has *no* life, etc.

Let anyone who is able dispute these assertions; hardly anything of what B. subsequently sets forth will fall away on that account. Something similar holds of several further doctrines occurring in this section; in particular of the way in which the difference between *concrete* and *abstract* ideas is determined in § 60 (a determination that, by its clarity, contrasts sharply with the nebulous construct in which *Hegel* has shrouded this difference); of the assertion (§ 69) that there are also *redundant* ideas, i.e. ideas in which marks are combined one of which is already a consequence of the rest, e.g. a round sphere; that there are further (§ 70) *objectless* ideas, such as the concept of *nothing*, or that of a trillion men now living; even *imaginary* ones, i.e. ones that unite contradictory marks, e.g. a round square, a wooden iron, and the like. Also whether we agree with the author in the definitions that he sets up in § 80 of the concept of a *relation*, in § 81 of *form* and *matter*, in §§ 82–98 of the concepts of a *multitude*, *sum*, *series*, *unity*, *plurality* and *totality*, *magnitude*, *number* and the *infinite* — all this is at least of no further consequence for our assent to the remaining doctrines of the book, however important some of these definitions of concepts may be in many another respect. Thus it is asserted of the *infinite* that it occurs only where a *plurality* occurs, and the following very exact definition is given: “Every plurality of the kind A that appears as a member in the series one obtains by making A the first member, but deriving each following one from the preceding merely by adding a new A — is called a *finite* plurality. A plurality, on the other hand, so constituted that every *finite* one appears in it only as a part, is called an *infinite* plurality.” From this definition it can easily be shown, as the author shows, that infinite magnitudes too can be different,

[p. 40]

that there are such also in the domain of actuality, and that it is in particular nothing at all impossible that an infinite series of events should be a *past* one.

If these assertions are of the greatest importance for *physics* and *metaphysics*, they nevertheless have, as said, no further influence on logic itself. It is another matter with the distinction, carried out in §§ 72–77, between *intuitions*, *concepts* and *mixed ideas*; of which the author subsequently makes such frequent use that in fact many difficulties would arise if our readers were unable to agree with him here in any respect. For the sake of better understanding we deem it necessary to explain first a distinction, already used several times, that B. makes between the *object* that an idea *has* or *represents*, and one *to which* we have just *related* it as a predicate in a judgment. We do so by means of an example. The idea “*sensuous rational being*” belongs among those that have objectuality; it *represents*, then, not only all human beings living on earth but, in all probability, an infinite multitude of creatures besides, who live on other celestial bodies; *represents* (we say) all these objects, whether or not I know them all individually and *relate* this idea to them — (which I should then still have to represent to myself through *another* idea) — i.e. pass the judgment: “This one and that one too is a sensuous rational being.” If, therefore, I pass any judgment in which this idea is the *subject idea*, e.g. “Every sensuous rational being has a body”: then one may say that I have judged about *all* these creatures, even those of whose existence I know nothing, or of whom I am not just now thinking in particular; for my judgment extends to them all, whether I consider it or not. The idea “*body*,” on the other hand, which in precisely

[p. 41]

this judgment appears as *predicate idea*, is at present *related* by me solely to sensuous rational beings, although there are still other objects that it represents (inasmuch as beings that are not rational also have bodies). Indeed, a *predicate idea* can, in an incorrect judgment, even be *related* to objects that it does not in fact represent at all, as in the judgment: This book is by Enoch.

Now an idea that we do not merely *relate* to a single object, but that in itself *has* only a single object, e.g. the fixed star Sirius, B. calls a *singular idea*; an idea that is not composed of several others, such as the idea *Not*, he calls *simple*; and he now investigates the question whether there are also ideas that unite these two remarkable attributes (the smallest extension with the smallest content), i.e. that are simple and at the same time also have only a single object? He answers this in the affirmative by showing that all those (subjective) ideas are of this kind which arise in us *first* and *immediately* whenever we direct our attention to the alterations that a body brought before our senses, e.g. a rose, produces in our soul. The ideas that arise here *first* and *immediately* must be *simple*, because complex ideas come to light only mediately, namely through the combination of simple ones. They must, further, have something quite *singular*, namely only the alteration just now going on in our soul, as their *object*; for ideas that have several objects do arise on such an occasion too, e.g. the idea *Fragrance*, when we gradually form the judgment: “*This* (which I am just now smelling) — is — a fragrance,” and the like. But it is obvious that we were led to this latter idea only *mediately*, namely only through that simple singular idea which we designated in the example mentioned by a mere *This*, and which appeared here as the subject idea. Hence

[p. 42]

it is proved that there are also ideas, subjective ideas, which, despite all their simplicity, nevertheless represent only a single object. These, now, are what B. wants called *intuitions*; *subjective* or *thought* intuitions, if they are themselves subjective or thought ideas; the *objective idea* corresponding to them, however, he calls the *objective intuition*. Ideas, on the other hand, which are not only themselves no intuitions but do not even contain any such as parts, he calls *concepts*, *pure concepts*; all other ideas, finally, which are thus neither pure intuitions nor concepts, he calls *mixed ideas*.

What, now, is required in order to follow him in these definitions of concepts? Essentially, to be sure, nothing more than that one admit: a) there are *simple* as well as *complex* ideas; b) there are *singular ideas*, and c) a *singular idea* can also be a *simple* idea. About the first we believe we have said enough above; for that there are *only* complex ideas but no simple ones at all, no one will surely assert, since everything complex presupposes simple parts out of which it is composed, in finite or infinite multitude. As for the existence of *singular ideas*, however, we have a considerable multitude of them not only among the ideas that have hitherto already been called intuitions, e.g. *this* man, *this* rose, and the like, but even among such ideas as had hitherto also been counted among the pure concepts, such as God, the universe, the supreme moral law, and many others. How, however, both can be united; how an idea that is wholly simple can nevertheless have peculiarities enough to fit only a single object: that may admittedly seem enigmatic to anyone who supposes that every determination of an object, if it is to fit it alone, must be co-represented in the idea of it. This view, however, is refuted as erroneous by the author in § 64 by many reasons. We cite only this one: how, then, if matters stood thus,

[p. 43]

could an idea ever come about that has only a single object, when, strictly speaking, every object possesses infinitely many of these determinations? In fact, the idea that we designated in the above case by *This* was just as certainly a *singular idea* as it was also a *simple* idea. For even the determinations that we may add to the *This* in an oral communication of our thoughts to another (This, which I am just now feeling, smelling, seeing, etc.) are added by us only in order to make the This that we mean more *recognizable* to the other; but they are not added as though they belonged to the *idea itself* and first raised it to a singular idea. For that the *This* which I just now mean is something that I just now feel, smell or see, lies already in the fact that it is just the This now meant and no other *This*.

Grant only this little, and one must obviously recognize already B.’s whole distinction between *intuitions*, *concepts* and *mixed ideas* as one grounded in the nature of the matter. — On comparing it with the definitions of concepts hitherto customary, however, one is likely to find that what B. calls a *pure concept* coincides exactly with what has also hitherto been so called — (with the exception, perhaps, of the Hegelian school, for which the concept is just as much the thing itself!) — and will probably go on being so called. Only the word *intuition* has hitherto been taken commonly in a much wider sense, and has been made to include every *mixed* idea as well, provided it was a singular idea. Indeed, here and there even whole empirical propositions were counted among the intuitions. What highly important advantages result from B.’s narrower delimitation of this concept, and above all from that specification of its components, the readers will learn only in what follows, ever more fully. They may nevertheless find remarkable even the first application, to be communicated to them right now, that the author makes of his definitions to the more exact determination of the *ideas of time*

[p. 44]

and *of space* (§ 79), in that he shows, quite contrary to the prevailing view, *that there are no intuitions of either time or space*, and at the same time supplies us with the first exact definition of these concepts.

If it could be conjectured of any of the ideas relating to time or space that they are *intuitions*, they would certainly be such as, like the ideas *this particular time, this particular place*, have only a single object. For the ideas *time in general*, *place in general*, *line*, *surface*, etc., which have several objects, cannot, for that very reason, because they are not singular ideas, be pure (unmixed) intuitions, at any rate according to the definition given. That the two ideas, too, *the whole infinite time* and *the whole infinite space*, although they are true singular ideas, are nonetheless only a pair of pure concepts, is proved by the fact that we can compose them (*define* them) out of nothing but pure concepts. *Time*, namely — (if we are to venture to set these definitions down here merely to satisfy the curiosity of our readers, without being able to add anything for their elucidation or justification) — *Time* is that determination in an existent under which alone any attribute can truly be ascribed to it. The collection of *all* times, however, is called the *whole time*. The *places* of things are those determinations in them that explain to us how it comes about that, with the forces they have, they exert precisely these effects upon one another and no others. The collection of *all* places, however, is called the *whole space*. Granting this, it is now easy to show that the ideas *this time*, *this place*, and other singular ideas formed similarly to them, are indeed *mixed* ideas, containing indeed an intuition, but by no means one that would at the same time be an idea of time or of space. For by *this particular time* we certainly never understand anything other than a time that we determine through an *intuition* occurring in it, or through some relation of it to this intuition, e.g. the

[p. 45]

time in which I am just now judging, and the like. In a similar way we also understand by *this particular place* nothing other than a place that we determine through its relation to one of our intuitions, e.g. as the place of a body that has just now occasioned this or that intuition in us, etc. But if we designate the opinion that time and space are intuitions as an error, how are we to explain the origin and wide diffusion of this error? Only from the twofold circumstance that, on the one hand, ideas that contain an intuition merely as a component were already counted among the intuitions themselves, and that, on the other hand, one had not yet succeeded in resolving into their components the first concepts belonging to the doctrine of time and space, e.g. of an extended thing, of line, surface, body, and the like, and still less in deriving certain first propositions, e.g. that time has one dimension, space three, etc., from their objective grounds. The definitions of time and space indicated above have paved the way for this; from them all those concepts can be defined, and all those propositions proved; which, however, we cannot of course expound further here.

In the *third section*: “*Differences among Ideas according to Their Relation to One Another*” (pp. 428–515), one again comes upon a proposition right at the start, in § 91: “*There are not two completely equal ideas*,” which one should admit only if one admits *ideas in themselves*. But the circumstance that almost all logicians assert precisely the same thing, with the sole exception that instead of the word *idea* they commonly use the word *concept*, betrays to us that they — whether they were distinctly conscious of it or not — were thinking here of ideas or concepts in an *objective* sense. For of subjective ideas they should obviously have asserted the very opposite, namely that thousands of them can be equal to one another. — Everything else that is taught in this section

[p. 46]

is taught holds also of ideas in a subjective sense, and can therefore also be accepted by those who reject the concept of an idea in itself. Moreover, all the assertions set forth here are of such a kind that they are either generally conceded or can be rejected without any influence on what follows. We cite some by way of sample. His definition of an intuition puts the author (§ 91) in a position to resolve into its true components even the concept of *similarity*, so important for mathematics. *Similar* in the mathematical sense, namely, are things that have in common all attributes representable by *concepts* (and thus differ either only by mere intuitions or by relations). In § 93 the author distinguishes *wider* and *narrower* ideas (the idea: sons of Israel, is six times as wide as the idea: sons of Isaac); in § 94 *compatible* and *incompatible* ones; to the former belong *equipollent*, *higher* and *lower*, *overlapping* ones, etc.; to the latter *conflicting*, *contradictory* ones, etc. According to him there are also ideas that stand *nearest* to one another with regard to width or height (§ 100). Our readers should not confuse the important class of *equipollent* or *equivalent ideas* (§ 96) (which, with different content, have the same extension) with the *different names* of one and the very same idea. *Dreieck* and *Triangel* [both German words for “triangle”] are not (as is often said) equivalent concepts, but only different names of the same concept; but the concepts “a figure that has three sides” and “a figure whose angles together amount to two right angles” are very clearly distinguished, although they have the same objects. Only these are equivalent concepts. — What is important, however, is that the author explains in § 108 in what way one can extend, and has extended (without having hitherto been distinctly conscious of it), the relations of width and height even to *objectless* ideas, as is the case especially in mathematics with *equations* and in *calculation* with *imaginary quantities*. This is done, however,

[p. 47]

by regarding certain components in the given ideas as *variable* and attending to the relation that these ideas bear to one another whenever, in place of those variable parts, others are put through which they acquire objectuality. In the doctrine of propositions we shall encounter a still far more fruitful application of this thought.

The last section, which considers the “*differences among ideas*” “*that first arise from their relation to other objects*,” offers still fewer stumbling blocks. Right at the start, in § 109, it is shown, in justification of all those logicians who divide ideas into *correct* and *incorrect* (or true and false), in what sense this is indeed admissible, namely insofar as one regards a given idea as the idea of a determinate object (one already grasped through another idea). Thus, e.g., “a great commander” might be called a very correct, “a sage” on the other hand presumably an incorrect idea of Napoleon.

Here again, in § 111, we meet with an application that the author makes of his distinction between concepts and intuitions, in that he defines the *essential* (or *necessary*) *attributes* of an object as those that belong to it by virtue of a *pure concept* that we have formed of it, while the remaining ones, which cannot be derived from this concept, are called, to that extent, *inessential* (or *accidental*). Thus the possession of certain limbs is called an essential, health or sickness, however, an inessential attribute of an organic being, etc.

In the *appendix* (on the *previous mode of exposition* of the doctrine of ideas, pp. 537–571) the most important thing, but also what will arouse the most offense, will probably be the *examination of Kant’s table of the categories*, since this table still unmistakably underlies the systems of our philosophers of the most various colors,

[p. 48]

although they have already altered or added a good deal in it. If this table is to contain all the *root concepts* of the human understanding, B. asks how one can possibly imagine being able to derive from those listed there, e.g., the following concepts: *idea in itself*, *proposition in itself*, *truth*, *knowledge*, *will*, *sensation*, *duty*, and many others? Other criticisms we must, to save space, leave entirely untouched.

As we now pass to the *second* chapter: “*On Propositions in Themselves*,” and here wish to take up the *first section*, on the “*general attributes of propositions*,” we stand before a division in B.’s book that is among the most defective. He ventures, in § 127, to put forward the view that *all propositions* fall under one and the same form, namely the following: “A *has (the attribute)* b”; that they therefore always have only *three* immediate components: a *substratum* or *subject idea* A, a *predicative part* or *predicate idea* b, and the concept of *having*, which *connects* those two, and is therefore also called the *copula*. B. himself does not venture to put this view forward as a quite settled one; for he knows of no way of proving it other than by an *induction* that by its nature is only incomplete; in that he seeks to show, partly right here, partly in what follows wherever an apparently deviating form of propositions comes his way, that this expression too can, indeed must, be reduced to the form: A has b, if it is to become quite intelligible what is really being said here. Whether our readers will be satisfied everywhere with this reduction we cannot say: certain only is that the whole can never be regarded as a complete proof.

Nevertheless it is proved in a fairly satisfactory manner that at least all those propositions which ordinary usage clothes in the form: A is B (God is omniscient) (which form other logicians, as is well known, without even

[p. 49]

attempting any proof of it, declare to be the generally valid one) — must be expressed not in these words, but rather in the words: A has b (God has omniscience), insofar as the thought underlying them is to be expressed quite distinctly. That the Is in such propositions does not occur in its proper meaning — we mean, not as the assertion of *being* (as in the proposition: God *is*) — has long been recognized. This is also clearly evident from the fact that we, even in the case where nothing but the *being* (the actuality) of an object is to be asserted in the predicate, as: “God is an existent (or something actual),” indeed even in the case where we wish precisely to deny being to an object, e.g. “the merely possible is nothing existent,” — still retain that little word: Is, although in the first case it would amount to a pleonasm, in the second case downright to a *contradictio in adjecto* [contradiction in terms], if it were taken in its proper meaning. What, then, is the true meaning that this Is has as copula? None other than that of *having*. “God *is* omniscient” means absolutely nothing other than: “God — *has* — omniscience”; and likewise: “God *is* an existent” means only: “God — *has* — being (or actuality)”; and “the merely possible is nothing existent” means: “the merely possible — *has* — no actuality,” etc. That these interpretations are the correct ones also follows from the fact that the latter expressions, although they count more letters, are nevertheless in fact simpler in thought. For instead of the Is in the former there appears in the latter the *Has*, which can in any case be no more complex a concept than that of the Is in its improper meaning; in all probability, rather, it is an altogether simple concept. In place of the concrete idea *omniscient* in the first expression, on the other hand, there appears in the second the abstract one: *omniscience*, which is certainly simpler than the former, however the linguistic expressions may be related to one another. Indeed, if we examine more closely the nature of concrete ideas, such as *omniscient*: we find that the concrete B belonging to the abstract b

[p. 50]

has no other sense than: *something that has b*. Omniscient, e.g., means nothing other than: something that has omniscience. The proposition: God is omniscient, would thus, if the Is had to be retained, really have only the sense: “God is something that has omniscience.” Thus in all propositions in which one wishes to connect the subject idea with the predicate idea through an *Is*, the copula *Has* would still always come to light, and indeed in the predicate. Who does not see that this is absurd?

Since we shall more fittingly consider only later on the peculiar way in which B. reduces some other forms of propositions to his: A has b — namely where he comes to speak of these propositions at greater length — we have nothing further to mention from this entire section than the assertion in § 130 that the *extension of a proposition is always one and the same as the extension of its subject idea*. This is, understandably, said only on the supposition that all propositions can be reduced to the form: *A has b*, where then in fact A means as much as “*every* A.” But on this latter expression B. had already remarked in § 57 that it is by no means to be construed as the expression of a concept composed of the concepts A and *Every*, but that, much like the related expressions *all A, A in general* and the like, it has only the purpose of preventing us from adding in thought to the idea designated by A any determination restricting its extension.

Just as everything that the author asserted in this first section about *propositions in themselves* — with the sole exception of the assertion in § 122 that such propositions are nothing existing — already holds of *thought* propositions as well, so too the “*differences among propositions according to their internal attributes*” enumerated in the *second section* (pp. 29–91) are all of such a kind that they can be granted also by anyone who grants only thought propositions.

[p. 51]

Particularly important here is the distinction between *conceptual and intuitional propositions* (§ 133), conceived in such a way that by the former only propositions that contain nothing but concepts are to be understood, and by the latter all the rest, in whose components, therefore, some intuition or other occurs. It is, however, well worth noting that not only he who conceives the distinction between concepts and intuitions exactly as B. does, but also anyone who merely grants that it is an *objective* distinction, i.e. one found in these ideas themselves — that it therefore depends not merely on the *subject* in which these ideas are found, but is to be gathered from their *internal attributes* themselves whether they are concepts or intuitions — has, in the distinction here set up between conceptual and other propositions, a distinction that is *objective* in exactly the same sense of the word. The proposition: All celestial bodies have a rotation about their own axis, as also the proposition opposed to it: Not all celestial bodies, etc., are a pair of pure conceptual propositions; the proposition: this earth turns about its own axis, on the other hand, is an intuitional proposition — no matter whether we hold the one or the other of them to be true or not true, and no matter how we may have arrived at these judgments. For all the ideas in the two former propositions are pure concepts, whereas in the latter proposition the idea *this earth* contains an intuition that announces itself through the word *this*. B.’s division is therefore by no means to be confused with the division of our judgments into *propositions a priori* and *a posteriori* that has become so famous through *Kant*. For apart from the fact that, without presupposing the former, it cannot be explained at all what *experiences*, i.e. judgments *a posteriori* and hence also judgments *a priori*, actually are; apart also from the fact that this division can be applied only to such propositions as are at the same time *judgments*: the most important point is that it concerns something merely subjective, namely the mere manner in which we arrived at these judgments, or, after a certain improvement, *could* have arrived at them. Not every judgment, however, on which we can now decide *a posteriori*, i.e. from experiences, must

[p. 52]

for that very reason be an intuitional judgment, as the examples above at once teach. — Nor does what people have tried to give as a couple of *objective marks*, *universality* and *necessity* in judgments *a priori*, suffice for establishing such a distinction as that between conceptual and other propositions on B.’s conception. For besides the fact that both marks are applicable only to *true* propositions, but not to *propositions in general* (since, after all, one will not say of a false or still undecided proposition that it holds universally and is necessary), the mark of *universality* belongs also to many empirical truths, e.g. all sons of Jacob left descendants; whereas there are pure conceptual truths which (at least on the usual way of conceiving) have no universality, e.g. some square roots are rational, or the world is dependent. As for *necessity*, however, one can in a certain sense indeed say of all pure conceptual truths, and of them exclusively, that they have necessity. But if we are to explain what we here call necessity, we must say (as will show itself more clearly later): precisely nothing other than that the proposition is a pure conceptual truth or deducible from one. Thus the concept of necessity already presupposes that of a pure conceptual proposition.

Not nearly so important is B.’s opinion — in any case put forward only very doubtfully — that in the so-called *negative* propositions, such as: A has not b (i.e. no A has b), the negation lies not in the copula but in the predicate, in such a way that the proposition is really to be expressed thus, if its components are to stand out quite distinctly: A — *has* — *lack* of b. The circumstance opposed to this, that in all languages the negation is joined so intimately to the copula, B. explains to himself merely by the fact that in such cases one really wishes to deny nothing other than the truth of the proposition itself, just as one also, when one wishes to determine the degree of certainty of a proposition,

[p. 53]

likewise attaches the qualifying word needed for this to the copula. Thus, just as in the proposition: the earth is *hardly* alive, the *hardly* belongs not to the copula but to the whole proposition, and the sense is really: the assertion that the earth is alive has little probability; so too in the proposition: the earth is *not* alive, into which the former gradually passes, the *Not* belongs only to the whole proposition, and the sense is at bottom none other than: the assertion that the earth is alive is false.

One kind of propositions first noticed by B. — which, if he is right, have hitherto occurred under very different expressions — are the *assertions* (or also *denials*) of the *objectuality of an idea* (§§ 137 and 138). To this kind belong all those enigmatic propositions which ordinary usage expresses now by the form: *There is an A*, now by the form: *A certain S is P*, now perhaps also by the form: *Some S are P*, provided (as in syllogistic) the last expression is to be interpreted in such a way that it remains true both if all S were P and if only a single S were P. Where in the first form the subject idea is, and where the predicate idea, one leaves wholly undetermined, or one declares that the proposition really asserts nothing other than that *A has actuality*. This latter, however, is certainly incorrect, because we do apply this form, after all, also to things that have no actuality, and say, e.g., without hesitation: “There is a *possibility* that does *not* attain actuality.” — Equally unsatisfactory is it when one declares, in the second form, *a certain S*, and in the third, *some S*, to be the subject idea, without so much as attempting a closer specification of the components of which these wondrous ideas would then have to consist. For since one must concede, on the one hand, that the words: *some S*, in the proposition: *Some S are P* — (and exactly the same holds of the words: *a certain S*, in the proposition: A certain S is P) — do not necessarily designate precisely the same

[p. 54]

objects that they designate in the proposition: *Some S are Q*; and since, on the other hand, it is evident that the same idea can represent only the same objects: ought one not, in fairness, to have explained wherein the difference lies between the two ideas which one here expresses with the same words? How difficult this would have proved, our readers may gather from the fact that the kind of objects which the words: *Some S* designate in the proposition: *Some S are P*, and again in the proposition: *Some S are Q*, depends on the nature of the predicate ideas P and Q; from which it follows, then, that one would here have a subject idea in which the respective predicate idea would already have to be contained, in some way, as a component! For without P or Q being contained in that idea: *Some S*, it surely cannot become a different idea through a change of these! — All these difficulties vanish at once as soon as we assume with B. that in such propositions nothing other is asserted than — *the objectuality of an idea*; that in particular the proposition: *There is an A*, has no other sense than: *the idea A — has — objectuality*; and the propositions: *A certain S is P*, or *Some S are P*, no other sense than: *the idea of a something that would be S and P* (that would have the attributes s and p) — *has* — *objectuality*. “Some human beings are virtuous,” thus means nothing other than: the idea of a human being who is virtuous has objectuality; and “some human beings are vicious” means: the idea of a human being who is vicious has objectuality. —

Although most propositions that, according to the linguistic expression, assert the actuality or existence of a thing are at bottom to be regarded as mere assertions of the objectuality of an idea, there are nevertheless also (so B. believes) propositions of the former kind, *assertions of an existence*, e.g. this (what I am feeling just now) — is — something actual. He therefore maintains that *actuality* or being can also be attributed to a thing as *predicate*.

[p. 55]

This view, in which the author contradicts *Kant* and several others, one may doubt or reject without anything in what follows having to be changed. The same holds of the view that the author sets up in § 145 concerning *questions*, namely that they too are *complete propositions*. Already not so immaterial is the distinction between *analytic* and *synthetic* propositions (§ 148), whose peculiar way of being conceived we wish first to report, and in the following chapter to come to its most important application to *true* propositions.

The *third section*, to which we now accordingly turn: “*Differences among propositions according to their relation to one another*” (pp. 92–196), is in our opinion one of the most important in the whole work. Already in § 147 the author had mentioned that in comparing propositions — (according to § 108 this happens, as we have heard, also with *ideas*) — we often, without being distinctly aware of it, think of certain parts of them as variable, and then consider the behavior in regard to truth that all those propositions exhibit which result from the given ones when other parts, of whatever kind, take the place of the variable parts. Now he shows in §§ 154–162 that only on this tacit presupposition do several relations between propositions rest, which have hitherto been treated in every textbook of logic, indeed regarded as the most important content of logic. Of this kind are the relation of *compatibility* (the propositions A, B, C, . . . M, N . . . can be true *at the same time*), of *deducibility* or *dependence* (Whenever the propositions A, B, C . . . are true, so are M, N, O . . . or: M, N, O, . . . *follow* from A, B, C, . . .), of *equivalence* (Whenever the propositions A, B, C . . . are true, so are M, N, O, . . . and *conversely*, whenever these are true, so are those again), of *incompatibility* or *conflict* (the propositions A and M are *never at the same time* true), of *contradiction* (Whenever one of the propositions A and M is true, the other is false, and conversely, whenever the

[p. 56]

one is false, the other is true) of *probability* (Not always, when the propositions A, B, C, . . . become true, does M also become true, but the ratio of the cases in which this happens to the whole multitude of cases is m : m + n), and several others. To agree with these assertions of the author, it is not at all necessary to have shared his opinion in even a single one of his earlier doctrines that had anything peculiar about it. Let one contradict him in everything, if one can: one will still not be able to deny that, e.g., the *If* and *Then* in the proposition: “If Leipzig lies farther north than Dresden, then the winter days in Leipzig are shorter than in Dresden,” have essentially only the sense that one is to regard the ideas Leipzig and Dresden as variable, and that, whenever one puts in their place a pair of such ideas, and the proposition “L. lies farther north than D.” thereby becomes true, the proposition “In L. the winter days are shorter than in D.” also becomes true. This, after all, makes itself known to some extent already in our being accustomed to put, in place of that *If*, also an *As soon as* or a *Whenever*. For since it is settled that a proposition, so long as nothing changes in its parts, cannot become *now* true, *now* false again, the expressions mentioned already betray, after all, that we are thinking of a *change* of the proposition. But does one not sometimes express the matter far more clearly still when one says that one has to regard the places Leipzig and Dresden here merely as *examples*, in place of which any *other* places whatever could be named? In a similar way it already becomes evident from the expression used to designate the *incompatibility* of given propositions, namely that such propositions “*could never become true at the same time*,” that we consider them not as they stand, but consider in them the whole kind of propositions that comes to light when certain parts, tacitly presupposed as variable, are altered at will. Only under this presupposition, after all, can one speak of a possibility that these propositions can become now true, now false again, but never both true *at the same time* (i.e. with the same determination of their variable parts)

[p. 57]

Yet in the doctrine *of inferences* people went still further: they designated the parts that are to be regarded as variable by general signs (letters), saying, e.g.:

If all A are B, / and all B are C:

then all A are also C.

Finally, in the investigation of the relation of *probability* (with which, to be sure, the mathematicians concerned themselves more than the logicians), it was recognized so clearly that here one has to consider not merely the given propositions but a whole class of them, that people taught how to determine the degree of probability solely from this consideration and by a skillful calculation of the ratio in which the multitude of the true propositions stands to the multitude of all. Unfortunately, precisely this relation of probability was not yet regarded at all, in the textbooks of logic hitherto, as an *objective* relation between the propositions at hand, one that belongs to them even quite apart from whether anyone thinks them or not, but was always regarded as a merely *subjective* attribute of them insofar as they are *judgments*; and people did not even become distinctly aware, as it seems, that this attribute belongs to a judgment only ever in relation to certain other judgments, i.e. as a mere *relation*. However, we believe that everything B. teaches here is likely to belong to that kind of truths to which one need only once be made attentive in order to recognize them at once and to retain them forever, because everyone finds that he has at bottom always conceived the matter so, even though he had not given himself so definite an account of it. For that (to remain with the concept of probability alone) the proposition: “From this urn filled with 99 white balls and one black one someone draws one at random” — stands to the proposition: “He draws a white one,” as a *premise* to a conclusion following from it with probability,

[p. 58]

*regardless of whether anyone thinks these propositions or not*; that this relation of theirs is therefore *objective*: this is surely in fact far more obvious than that the degree of this probability, as all mathematicians know, equals 99/100.

Should our readers ask whether this clarification of our own thoughts also produces any *benefit*, we would reply that in science it always and everywhere brings its benefit when we raise to a distinct consciousness what we had hitherto only obscurely conceived. For now it may suffice to touch on this one point alone: If B.’s theory as presented here is correct, then in future, whenever one speaks of any of the enumerated relations between propositions, thus e.g. in every *hypothetical* judgment (in every If, then), one will be entitled to believe that one has stated quite distinctly what one is now actually asserting only when one has either said expressly, or when it can at least be gathered from the context of the discourse without any ambiguity, *which ideas in these propositions one wishes to have regarded as the variable ones*.

After these general indications we no longer consider it necessary to introduce our readers into the detail of this section. We wish to detain them neither with an enumeration of *all* the peculiar relations that B. sets up here (although each of them finds frequent application in scientific investigations, e.g. in mathematics), nor still less with the citation of the various theorems that he communicates about these relations, so as not to weary them, since in the following divisions of the book too there is much else for which we wish to claim their attention.

For this reason we shall also pass over entirely what is contained in the short section that occupies itself only with the discovery of the components of such propositions as express the relations considered earlier (pp. 197–211), since it makes little difference

whether or not one agrees with the author in these determinations, once it has merely been conceded to him that he has grasped the essence of those relations themselves correctly. From the last section, however — “*Some propositions that, on account of their linguistic expression, require elucidation*” (pp. 211–245) — we cannot refrain from excerpting several points.

A remarkable confirmation of two of B.’s assertions, namely that every proposition is essentially of the form: *A has b*, and that the subject idea A in every true proposition must be an objectual one, is furnished by the enigmatic propositions whose linguistic expression has as its apparent subject idea: *Nothing*, such as: Nothing is better than trust in God in suffering; or in general: *Nothing has (the attribute)* b. Were the idea Nothing in fact, as it seems, the subject idea of these propositions, they would contradict the well-known principle: *Non entis nullae sunt affectiones* [of a non-being there are no attributes]. But if we tell our readers how such propositions are expressed by B., namely: *The idea of a something that would have (the attribute) b — has — no objectuality*, they will probably agree at once, and grasp that there is now indeed an object with which the proposition deals. For it is not the idea: *Something that has (the attribute) b* itself, but only an idea *of* this idea, that forms the proper subject idea in the proposition so expressed; hence indeed an idea that has an object; namely, it has the objectless idea itself as its object. (§ 170.)

Of what logical parts the propositions of the form: *Some S are P* consist, when they are to be understood in such a way that they remain true both if every S and if only a single S is a P, we have already seen above. But very often we take such propositions also in an entirely different sense, e.g. so that we wish to say that the attribute b belongs, to be sure, to certain A, though not to all, to several in number, though not exactly to considerably many A

[p. 60]

So our proposition really reads: “The multitude of the A that are B — has — the attribute of a whole composed of a small number of parts.” — But there are still more of the meanings that we connect with this and similar linguistic expressions; in regard to these we refer to § 173. What has been said already suffices to show that the logicians’ doctrine hitherto of *particular* propositions is very deficient, since it does not distinguish all these meanings, or at least never attempts to indicate to us the components of which these various propositions consist.

No less blame would it deserve that the manifold meanings of propositions with *Either Or* have likewise not been distinguished in the textbooks hitherto. For surely no one can deny that the Either Or has an entirely different meaning in the proposition: “To become a member of that academy, one must be *either* a philosopher, *or* a mathematician *or* a historian”; and in the proposition: “We now have *either* spring *or* summer *or* autumn *or* winter”; for the first proposition is to be understood to mean that every member to be admitted must fall under at least one of the concepts: a philosopher, a mathematician, a historian, but may also fall under several; the second proposition, on the other hand, to mean that it is to be permitted to infer at once, from the presence of the one case, the absence of all the others. Just as evident is it that in the proposition: “*this flower* is either male or female or a hermaphrodite,” and in the proposition: “*Every flower* is either male or female or hermaphrodite” — the two ideas: “*this flower*” and “*every flower*,” do not cohere with the remaining parts in the same way, as one might well suppose from the mere expression. By the first proposition one wishes to declare that among the three propositions: This flower is male, is female, is a hermaphrodite — one is true; by the second proposition, however, one certainly does not wish to say that among the three propositions: every flower is male, female, a hermaphrodite — one is true. One sees, then,

[p. 61]

in any case, that in the doctrine of *disjunctive propositions* too there is still many a gap, and let one examine whether what B. (§ 181 in conjunction with § 165) advances on this point cannot be accepted. We excerpt only a few things, but leave it to the acuteness of our readers to guess which of the above examples belong under the following forms. “The idea of a true proposition among the propositions A, B, C . . . has objectuality.” — “The idea of a true proposition among the propositions A, B, C, . . . is a singular idea.” — “The idea of a true proposition among the groups of propositions that come to light when, in place of the variable parts i, j, . . . in the propositions A, B, C, . . ., any others whatever are put, but always only such that the subject ideas retain objectuality — is a singular idea.”

Assertions of *necessity*, *possibility* and *contingency*, with whose analysis § 182 is concerned, are on everyone’s lips, and the most important inquiries into God, freedom and much else depend on a correct determination of these concepts. Nevertheless, in most recent times hardly anything more has been done to this end than to define the *necessary* as *that which cannot not be*. One wished, accordingly, to derive the concept of the necessary from that of *being able*, i.e. of the *possible*. But is the latter already simple, or merely simpler? Hardly; for from time immemorial people have sought to determine both concepts through that of *contradiction*, and have defined the *necessary* as something whose assumed *non-being* leads to a contradiction, the *possible*, on the other hand, as something whose assumed being leads to no contradiction. And is this really so incorrect? The only thing still amiss was that it was not determined with what *kind* of truths the assumed being or non-being comes into contradiction. For if one wished to declare necessary everything whose assumed non-being conflicts *with any* truth whatever, and that alone

[p. 62]

possible whose assumed being conflicts with *absolutely no* truth: then the necessary as well as the possible would coincide entirely with the actual; for the assumption that something is not actual which nevertheless is actual contradicts at least the one truth that asserts its actuality; and thus every actual thing would already have to be called necessary as well; from which it then follows further that every non-actual thing would already be impossible too, and the possible would therefore always have to be actual. If, then, we nevertheless wish to distinguish the possible, the actual and the necessary in extension, we must restrict the truths that are to be taken into consideration here to a special class. And what presents itself more naturally than the class of pure *conceptual truths*? It can hardly be doubted at all, as soon as one merely hears it stated, that by the *necessary* one thinks of something whose assumed non-being contradicts a pure conceptual truth; and by the *possible*, something whose assumed being contradicts no pure conceptual truth. The question is only whether one can explain what a pure conceptual truth is without already presupposing the concepts of necessity or possibility. B. has now shown, as we already know from the above, that we are indeed able to do this, in that he defined a pure conceptual truth as one in whose components no single *intuition*, i.e. no single simple idea that has only a single object, occurs. With all distinctness, then, we know how to state what is called *possible*, what *necessary* (and also what *contingent*), as soon as we accept B.’s definition of an intuition. But even he who does not wish to concede this definition to him — provided he knows some other definition at all, or at least holds himself assured that the concept of an intuition, and consequently also that of a concept and of a conceptual truth, can be determined in a way that does not presuppose the concepts of necessity or possibility — will still have to regard the definition given here of these latter as satisfactory.

[p. 63]

A sample of how important even some investigations of the theory of science are which seem at first glance entirely unfruitful is furnished by § 183, in which B. analyzes some *propositions with determinations of time* into their logical components, and makes the very plausible remark that in propositions of the kind such as: “*the object A has at the time t the attribute b*,” the determination: *at the time t*, belongs neither to the copula nor to the predicate, but to the subject idea, so that, e.g., the proposition: this leaf is green today, must really be construed thus: this leaf on today’s date — has — a green color. Only in this way do we at last avoid the inconvenience of having to make an exception, in the case of all changeable things, to the familiar principle that *contradictory attributes belong to no object*, or of being able to save it for these only by the addition: *at the same time*. If one and the very same substance has the attribute b at the time t, but at another time t+Θ the attribute *non* b, then, if such determinations of time belong in the subject idea, in the two propositions: A at the time t — has b, and: A at the time t+Θ — has *non* b, there is talk only of the same *substance*, but not of the same *object*, because the subject ideas, i.e. object ideas, of the two propositions: A at the time t, and A at the time t+Θ, exclude one another. — But how surprising and important is not the application of this logical doctrine, which we encounter in the *second* edition of the *Athanasia*, pp. 292 and 293, where a proof of the endless continued existence of all substances is drawn from it. For if there could be, for some substance A, a point of time t at which it has no actuality, then a proposition of the form: The substance A at the time t — has — no actuality, would have to be true, and would nevertheless have a subject idea that is objectless. For since the substance A is supposed not to exist at the time t, the concept: A at the time t, i.e. the concept of a substance or of something actual at the time t, when it nevertheless has no actuality, would evidently be objectless.

[p. 64]

Like the chapter on *ideas*, B. also concludes that on *propositions* with an appendix, in which he assesses the exposition of others. The most remarkable thing is the proof furnished here that the *table of judgments* from which *Kant* derived his table of categories — that table which so many other logicians, and even *Hegel*, have still retained unaltered — rests on nothing but errors. Even the four points of view of *quantity*, *quality*, *relation* and *modality* are not only faulty in themselves, but are also applied faultily to the matter at hand. Thus, to say only a few things, the *quality* of a thing already comprises its *quantity*, provided the latter, as in the present case, is an internal attribute of the thing; but if it consists in a mere relation, then for that very reason it belongs to *relation*, to which *modality* too always belongs when one explains it as a relation to our cognitive faculty. How erroneous it is, further, to divide judgments according to *quantity* into *universal*, *particular* and *singular* ones; since the subject idea entitles one at most to the division into universal and singular judgments, according as it represents several objects or only a single one; whereas the so-called *particular* judgments (Some S are P) really belong, again, only to the singular judgments, since they assert nothing other than the objectuality of an idea (namely of the idea of a something that is both S and P)! What different forms of judgment have not been united, in the most violent fashion, under the point of view of *relation*, when it has been said that all judgments are, in the next place, either *categorical* or *hypothetical* or *disjunctive*! For all judgments are, at bottom, constituted just as one describes *categorical* judgments. The *hypothetical* form, however, where it is essential, presupposes variable components in the given propositions; yet this is not peculiar to it alone, but the same happens also in assertions of compatibility or incompatibility and several others, just as it can also be the case in *disjunctive* propositions. In general, the description that one gives of these

[p. 65]

latter fits only a single kind of them. Under the title of *quality* there are listed *affirmative*, *negative* (where the negation belongs to the copula) and *limiting* judgments (where the negation is supposed to belong to the predicate); under the title of *modality*, finally, *problematic*, *assertoric* and *apodictic* judgments (which assert a possibility, an actuality and a necessity): all the remaining forms of judgment one seeks in vain in this table.

The “*third chapter: on true propositions*” (pp. 327–390) that now follows occupies itself with setting up some attributes that belong to propositions only insofar as they are also true, hence to all *truths*. Although the discussion is still of mere propositions and truths *in themselves*, even he who grants only *thought* truths may check whether he might not likewise accept — indeed be obliged to accept — what is taught here, understood in his own way. Now what the author sets up immediately in § 196 as a general attribute of all truths, namely that their subject idea must always be an *objectual idea*, anyone can readily concede if he will consider that in a true proposition there must always be an *object* with which it *deals*, i.e. of which it asserts an attribute (one that does in fact belong to it); especially since the author has shown how those few cases that make an apparent exception are to be explained. But when it is further said that the *predicate idea* too in every proposition must be an *objectual idea*, more precisely an *idea of an attribute*, this is of course said only under the presupposition that all propositions fall under the form: *A has b*. Moreover, this is an assertion of no further consequence.

How much weight the adherents of the critical philosophy have laid on the distinction between *analytic* and *synthetic* propositions, and on the assertion that there are also synthetic truths and cognitions for us human beings

[p. 66]

is well known. Nevertheless they did not succeed in raising the matter to perfect clarity, for even at the present day there are very many who reject the whole distinction. B. now conceives, in § 148, the concept of analytic propositions more broadly, that of synthetic ones, however, more narrowly. For instead of keeping to the definition that one wishes to call *analytic* only those propositions in which the predicate idea already occurs as a component in the subject idea, hence only such as: “A *is* A,” or: “A, *which* is B, is A,” or finally: “A, *which* is B, is B,” he gives this designation to every proposition in which there is contained any idea at all that can be altered at will without impairing the truth of the proposition, so long as only the subject idea has still remained an objectual one. All the remaining propositions, and only these, he calls *synthetic*. According to such a definition, then, not only are the above three propositional forms to be called analytic, because they remain true whatever one puts in place of A and B, but even the proposition: “In *this* triangle all the angles together amount to two right angles,” will be called analytic, because in it too a component occurs, namely the idea *This*, which can be altered at will without disturbing its truth, so long as it retains a subject, i.e. so long as the *This* designates only an actual triangle. One may approve or reject this extension; B.’s proofs that there are *synthetic truths* remain serviceable nonetheless. For if there are such in his narrower sense, there must all the more certainly be such in the wider sense. Now let the readers themselves say whether they find, e.g., the following proof of B.’s, which we wish to pick out from among several in § 197 and § 64, convincing. There are certainly truths of the form: “*Some A are B*” (i.e. the idea of a something that has the attributes a and b has objectuality); and likewise also truths of the form: “*No* A is B” (i.e. the idea of a something that would have the attributes a and b has no object). Among these truths there are certainly also some in which the ideas a and b are simple;

[p. 67]

for certainly there are also simple ideas of attributes that unite, and others that cannot be united. But if a and b are simple, then those two propositions are evidently synthetic; because we cannot, after all, alter at will either the idea A or B, or any other in them, without disturbing their truth.

The most important thing in this whole chapter is the assertion (§ 198) that among truths in themselves there is an *objective* — i.e. entirely independent of the way in which we may happen to *know* them — *connection*, by virtue of which we may regard some of them as *grounds* and others as their *consequences*; to which is then added some of what is set forth in §§ 199–221 on the closer constitution of this quite peculiar relation — for the most part, to be sure, only as a view not yet proven — for further examination: such as that pure *conceptual truths* can never be grounded in *intuitional truths*; that no truth on which another *objectively depends (is grounded)* is more complex than the latter; that there are also truths that have no further ground of their truth (*fundamental truths*); that the concepts of *cause* and *effect* arise from those of *ground* and *consequence*, in such a way that any actual thing B is called the *effect* of another A if the truth: *B exists*, stands to the truth: *A exists*, in the relation of an objective grounding.

Is this concept of a *ground* and of a *consequence* belonging to it, as a relation that obtains not between *actual things* but only between *truths in themselves*, then something entirely new? Certainly not; for even the ancient Greek sages demanded that in a strictly scientific exposition one should give proofs that show not only *that* (ὅτι) something is, but also *why* (διότι) it is. Could they, in the latter, have been thinking of anything other than what B. calls the *objective grounding* of a truth? Only in our

[p. 68]

time does one seem to have almost forgotten this distinction. But is it really right not to distinguish the *premises*, through whose consideration the knowledge of a truth can be mediated in us (*grounds of knowledge*), from those truths in which its *objective ground* lies? Of the former there can be quite manifold ones, but the objective ground is only one. And how clearly it is at times distinguished from them! That one should not lie, that one should gladly let the needy have something of one’s abundance, that one should not cause pain even to the irrational animal without necessity: all these are truths that we know in many ways, e.g. also from the fact that all human beings agree about them; but the objective ground on which they rest is an entirely different one; it consists (in our opinion) only in the truth that we are obligated to everything by which the sum of the general welfare is increased.

Now the question arises, however, whether even someone who rejects the concept of *truths in themselves*, and admits only thought truths, could nevertheless assume a connection among them, more or less similar to the one that B. here describes to us under the name of the *objective* one? And this, we believe, is to be answered in the affirmative. For even such a person could quite properly conceive of a certain *relation* between the truths which might deserve the name of an *objective connection* insofar as it rests on determinate circumstances entirely independent of our *contingent way of knowing* them. And if he were to stipulate that, e.g., only such a collection of pure conceptual truths as is the *simplest* among all those from which a certain other conceptual truth can be deduced should be called its *objective ground*; if he wished to set up such *criteria*, entirely in agreement with § 221, for the existence of his relation: would there not then be the greatest similarity between his doctrine and B.’s, except for the single circumstance that he spoke of true *thoughts*, whereas B. spoke of truths

[p. 69]

*in themselves*? He too, for example, could regard the proposition concerning the promotion of the general welfare, just as well as B., as the supreme principle of the whole of moral doctrine, insofar as he found that the combination of this proposition with other pure conceptual truths yields the simplest collection of such truths from which all the remaining practical truths can be derived.

B. treats the doctrine “*of inferences*” — departing in this from other logicians — as a mere subdivision of the doctrine *of propositions* (in the *fourth chapter*, pp. 391–568), because he regards inferences as a particular kind of proposition. For when one declares (he holds) the proposition M to be a conclusion following from the premises A, B, C . . ., one thereby asserts only the following *proposition*: “Every collection of ideas which, put in the place of certain ideas regarded as variable in A, B, C, . . ., M, makes the propositions A, B, C . . . true, also makes the proposition M true.” Whether or not one goes along with this view (although we believe, and have already said, that the presence of certain variable parts in *inferences* has been acknowledged from time immemorial), this in no way prevents one from assenting to the doctrines of this chapter. The most important thing, however, and something of which anyone who has read through even a few §§ attentively should be convinced, is that those few forms of inference which are taught in the ordinary textbooks of logic — the so-called *immediate inferences*, the *syllogism*, *induction* and *analogy* — are by no means the only simple forms of inference that we make use of in our thinking and indispensably need. In concluding here our report on the “*Theory of Elements*” and turning to the *third* volume, we can at least assure our readers that from now on they will no longer meet with investigations as dry and abstract as those so far, because we shall no longer be concerned with propositions and ideas *in themselves*, but with their *appearances in the mind*, with the manner of working on them for the purpose of increasing our knowledge, and finally with the exposition of the latter in usable textbooks.

First comes the *third part* of the whole, i.e. the “*Theory of Knowledge*” (vol. III, pp. 1–292), and here our readers will, we hope, find right in the *first chapter*, “*On Ideas*” (pp. 1–107), a few things that should prove as intelligible as they are attractive. What one calls a *single* idea, what *several*, what *equal* ideas (§ 273); what the *strength* or *vividness* of an idea is (§ 275); what *clear* and *obscure*, *distinct* and *confused* ideas are (§§ 280 and 281); when an idea *begins* or *ends* (§ 282); that each leaves behind a certain *trace* (§ 283); that with more frequent use every idea that we take to be objectual is joined by an *image* of its object, distinct from the idea itself (§§ 284 and 287): all these points they will find worked out in a way that has something new about it. The whole time during which one and the same *objective* idea is entertained without interruption in our mind, albeit in various states (now more, now less vividly, and the like),

[p. 72]

the author regards as the duration of a *single subjective* idea; and only if either the same objective idea appears in the same human being at interrupted times, or if its appearance takes place in different beings, or if the *objective* idea that forms the matter of the subjective one has already become another, does he wish to see *several* subjective ideas spoken of. Nothing, surely, can be objected to this. — It is, further, undeniable that we possess the faculty of representing to ourselves, in turn, our own ideas themselves: how else could we speak of them, and say that we have them? This representing of them, however, can take place in two ways: through an idea that comprises *several* of them, e.g. when we speak “of all the ideas that we have had during this night”; or also through an *intuition* that represents only a single one of them; for without doubt we also possess the faculty, as with many another change taking place in us, so with our own ideas, of bringing them to intuition for ourselves, i.e. of forming for ourselves another, simple idea of them that concerns them alone. Now it is such ideas, again intuited by ourselves, that we call — according to the author’s assertion — *clear* ideas. Ideas, however, of which we have no intuition we call *obscure* ideas. An idea, on the other hand, is called *distinct* according to him if we also know how to state whether it is simple or, if it is complex, of what parts it consists. From the circumstance that every *idea in us*, as a determinate kind of change, lasts only so long as the cause producing it continues to act, B. infers that the state which obtained in the last moment of the action must, from then on, if no new action occurs, continue without interruption — which he calls the *trace* left behind by the idea.

If we know of any one of our ideas, or even merely mistakenly suppose, that an object corresponds to it, we are accustomed to compose for ourselves, of this object too, a separate idea of its own, and indeed out of all the attributes that have become known to us of

[p. 73]

it, especially the sensible ones, if in our opinion it has any such; and this idea, which attaches itself closely to the former and yet is essentially different from it, is what B. calls the *image* accompanying it. What a difference is to be drawn, in the case of many of our concepts, e.g. of God, of death, of the pleasures and sufferings that this or that station in life brings with it, between the concepts just mentioned and the images that accompany them (at times quite involuntarily)!

Should one, incidentally, be unable to rest content with one or another of these definitions, let one not fear that much of what follows will therefore fall away. One has still less to fear this in the case of the assertions of §§ 279, 284–286 and 289 on the difference between *sensible* and *supersensible* ideas, the concept of a *sign* and of a multitude of concepts related to it, the origin of *language*, and the most remarkable *activities* and *states* of our mind that concern *the business of having ideas*; however divergent from the usual view some of them may sound. We pick out only one point to occupy our readers’ reflection. The author ventures to voice the view that the ideas *red, sweet, fragrant*, etc., which have hitherto been commonly regarded, if not as pure, then at least as mixed intuitions, might be *pure concepts*, namely concepts *of the laws according to which the changes in our soul, which are the object of our intuitions* in the case of something red, sweet, fragrant, etc., *take place*.

[p. 74]

presumably not be counted against him as an offense even by those logicians who had not hitherto expressly taught it. That there are *unmediated* (i.e. not first deduced from others) as well as *mediated* judgments (§ 300), likewise what is said in § 302 about the manner in which the former arise — that we must, for example, be capable of passing at least some judgments merely because we possess the subject idea and predicate idea occurring in them: this may be granted. To a sharper examination all the more do we commend the peculiar way in which § 301 explains the origin of judgments *from mere probability*; since on the correctness of this explanation depends whether the explanation of *erring* attempted in § 309 solves the riddle or not. This much is surely beyond dispute: that we also accept what is merely probable with a corresponding degree of confidence, i.e. that we judge *a thing to be so* when, strictly speaking, we should judge only that *it is probable that it is so*. But whether one has adequately explained whence this comes if one merely says that it comes from the limitedness of our faculty of ideas, which lets the less important components of a more complex idea drop out of consciousness: that is what B. himself puts forward only as a conjecture, and what could be disputed.

That the assertions of the long § 303 on the origin of those of our empirical judgments *that we mediate without a distinct consciousness* can be granted or rejected without influence on anything that follows goes without saying. Since, however, it may interest several of our readers to learn here at least the course of these investigations, we remark that the author first states how we know the *temporal relations* obtaining among our own ideas. For immediately — as he maintains, and this only under certain circumstances — we are able to know nothing more in this regard than whether an idea is *at this very moment present* in our interior. But that an idea has been *earlier* than

[p. 75]

another, a third again *simultaneously* with that one, and the like, all this we must first infer. He now states from what marks we gather such things; e.g. *simultaneity*, when certain ideas occur as parts in another; *being earlier*, when judgments contradict one another and the one nevertheless needs the other as its partial cause. Furthermore he shows in what way we assure ourselves whether we have already had certain ideas simultaneously *several times*; whether the *intervals* have also been almost *equal* to one another (namely, if equal series of ideas have fallen in between); how we learn of the existence of things *outside us* (the thinking subject) (namely from the changes that take place in us); how we come to know the *forces* of those things; how we gradually become aware that our *own will* is the cause of manifold results; how we thus little by little arrive at the knowledge that a part of the external world, as *body*, i.e. much more immediately than other parts of it, is at our disposal; in which parts of this body the *seat* (the cause) of this and that sensation lies; how we judge the *spatial relations*; that the sense of *sight* is not exactly necessary for this, and much else. We particularly recommend that one not overlook what is replied, in no. 31, to the objection that, after all, *animals* and *children* also, who surely cannot carry out those reflections, know spatial relations and much else.

Let the investigations of the *third chapter*, “*On the Relation of Our Judgments to Truth*” (pp. 206–262), by all means not belong in logic: *that they are of the highest importance for philosophy itself* no one will deny. We would, however, advise anyone who wishes to survey in context what belongs here to read once more the §§ from the *Theory of Fundamentals* that concern this subject, especially if some things remained obscure to him the first time. On account of the *definitions* that the at times perhaps all too

[p. 76]

exact author lays down in advance in § 307 concerning the concepts of *knowing*, of *ignorance* and of *error*, one need on no account break with him, even if one should disapprove of them. For even by the author’s will it is to remain with the meanings that common usage connects with these words; indeed, precisely in order not to violate this usage, he believed he had to give only definitions that deviate somewhat from the usual ones. And what, indeed, could one object to this if he defines, e.g., *knowledge* of a truth as that state of our mind in which we have not only once already passed a judgment corresponding to this truth, but also, under proper circumstances, still remember it and continue to adhere to it?

Against the way in which he then, in § 308, explains *the possibility of a mere not-knowing* (namely from the finitude of our powers), scarcely anything can be objected. The same presumably holds also of the assertion in § 309 that the possibility of *erring* rests only on the circumstance that we also expect or accept as true something that has mere *probability*. That the whole riddle does not seem to us solved by this alone we have already remarked. At the same time we must add that the explanations which we have so far found in others (the most important are cited in § 310) exhaust the matter still less. Here B. also attempts (§ 311) to give, of the much-discussed words *understanding* and *reason*, definitions that would not depart too far from common usage and would at the same time also be of some scientific usefulness. He calls *understanding* the faculty for only such empirical knowledge as, even if it requires the mediation of certain pure conceptual truths, still does not require that we bring these to a distinct consciousness. Truths, on the other hand, which, although they may rest on perceptions, can be deduced from them only through distinctly thought premises, and all these pure conceptual truths themselves he ascribes to our *reason*. Since we do not expect beforehand

[p. 77]

that these definitions will be agreeable even to a single one of the philosophers of our day, we remark only that it is a matter of complete indifference for everything that follows as well as for what precedes whether one accepts or rejects them; for the author neither referred to these concepts in what precedes, nor does he refer to them in what follows, since he in general (after *Herbart’s* manner) *avoids speaking of powers of the soul*, and drawing conclusions, from arbitrarily assumed definitions of these, as to what man is able or unable to know.

Since the two following §§ demonstrate that it is possible for us to know quite a few truths without learning the *objective ground* of them — as we can, e.g., know that it is colder today than yesterday without knowing why — this gives occasion to distinguish two kinds of *grounds of knowledge*, namely *subjective* and *objective* ones. According to the remark we made above, one could grant him a distinction of a similar kind to the one the author has in mind here, even without granting his concept of objective truths itself.

Finally the author comes, in § 314, to the famous question *whether determinate limits can be specified for our cognitive faculty*? and shows, in a way that we cannot well communicate in an extract, not indeed the complete impossibility of such a specification, but at least what difficulties it has, indeed why a limit of this kind in itself *may well not exist at all*. Since *Kant* once asserted with such great determination precisely the opposite of this, and it still finds approval with so many, it was necessary to examine his reasons more circumstantially. This is done in § 315; yet whoever wants to have together everything that B. brings forward against this doctrine must consult many other §§ besides, such as 77, 79, 116, 148, 187–191, 287, 305, and others. That, however, this determination of limits is *contradictory in itself* is already shown by the single remark that one finds loc. cit., no. 5, namely because the judgment that the supersensible objects could not be judged by us synthetically at all is itself

[p. 78]

a synthetic judgment about them. That not only mathematics and physics, but even *logic* itself (not to mention *morals*) contains synthetic propositions, whose existence Kant’s theory leaves unexplained, is already evident from no. 2; or is the proposition that from two propositions of the form: A is B, B is C, a third of the form: A is C follows perhaps not a synthetic judgment? — That it is altogether impracticable to explain the origin of synthetic pure conceptual judgments from intuitions, if these judgments are to be more than merely probable, was already shown in a most illuminating way in § 305, nos. 1–8; *space* and *time* are, after all, not intuitions at all; and it is surely utterly false that the idea of *time* underlies the theorems of arithmetic just as the idea of *space* underlies those of geometry. That *Kant* was wrong to assert that there are four metaphysical antinomies that can be proved with equal rigor is shown by no. 7 in § 315, where errors are demonstrated in each of these proofs, and much else.

Neither of equal importance nor so divergent from the usual doctrine is the last chapter of this part: “*On Certainty and Probability, as Well as on Confidence in Our Judgments*” (pp. 263–292). For it is of no further consequence, but concerns a mere *word*, if the *probability* of a proposition and the *confidence* with which we accept it are distinguished. Yet it seems to us that B. is evidently in the right. For if, e.g., the *probability* of a proposition = ½, i.e. if equally strong reasons for and against it lie before us, then we surely decide neither for nor against it; we pass no judgment at all, that is: our *confidence* is = 0. In general the author finds, when the degree of *probability* of a proposition = m m + n, that the degree of *confidence* with which we pass the judgment = m − n m + n.

[p. 79]

One will, we hope, find the concepts of *reliability* and of *moral confidence* or *conviction* set up in §§ 317 and 319, as well as the determination of the concepts of *belief* and *knowledge* attempted in § 321, to be of greater usefulness, especially for *morals*. A proposition is called *reliable* when such circumstances obtain that it would be foolish or even impermissible still to heed the possibility of its opposite and to want to make preparations for it; and the *confidence* that we conceive in the proposition in accordance with this relation is called a *moral confidence*. Thus the proposition that the ceiling of this hall will not collapse today is reliable if this collapse, although I do not find it impossible, nevertheless has so low a degree of probability that it would be foolish to take it into account in my actions, and hence e.g. to leave the hall; because the danger of coming to grief that I would be seeking to escape in this way is no greater than any into which I would fall precisely through this endeavor if, e.g., I preferred to spend the night under the open sky. — “If to us” (it says in § 321) “a truth, whether immediately or only after we have directed our attention to certain grounds of it, becomes evident in such a way that we deem that from now on we ourselves, even if we wished, would not succeed in persuading ourselves of the opposite: then we say that we *know* the matter. If, on the other hand, we hold a proposition to be true without its seeming impossible to us still to pass over to the opposite judgment by the voluntary direction of our attention to grounds opposed to it, but we are disposed to keep the grounds for its truth in view: then we call this relation to the proposition a *belief*.” — We too abide by the latter; but the definition of *knowledge* seems to us at least not to correspond to the *common* usage of the word; because this usage demands of knowledge not so much a very high degree of confidence as rather a determinate relation to *truth*, namely that it should be a judging that agrees with the truth. How

[p. 80]

else could one say: “He *does know it, but only not for certain*”; and again: “He *imagines that he knows it*”; whereas it never occurs to us to say that someone *imagines he believes something*? Accordingly the setting up of this concept would rather have belonged in the third chapter.

The *fourth part* of the Theory of Science, or the “*Art of Discovery*,” which fills the still remaining half of the third volume (pp. 293–575), supplies in the *first chapter* the *general rules* of reflection, if it aims at the discovery of truth, and in the *second* a closer instruction for *33 particular problems* of thinking.

Among the *general rules* (pp. 304–389) there is, it seems to us, not a single one that one could not — indeed, would not have to — grant the author, however one may stand with him in regard to everything earlier. In general, everything that he says here has probably been — even if not exactly expressly laid down — nevertheless (as he himself believes) the practice of every more practiced thinker. Or who, e.g., would not have considered it useful, right at the beginning of his reflection, to *determine as exactly as possible* the truth that he is actually seeking; who would not have considered it expedient to try whether the *impossibility* of the chosen problem might not perhaps already be *seen in advance*; who would not himself already have applied the means of *occupying himself with certain preliminary questions* to which our problem can be reduced, or which can otherwise merely cast some light on it; who would not have pursued the solution of a problem by inferences from truths already known (*direct procedure*) as well as by tentative assumptions (*indirect procedure*) and by combining several modes of procedure with one another? etc. Of great importance, however, is surely what the author adduces in § 331 (where he speaks *of the consultation of the judgment of others and of experience*) about the *high reliability of the pronouncements of common human*

[p. 81]

*understanding*. For only if one counts among these pronouncements merely what he and they restrict to such cases alone, as he does, can one in truth and with great benefit appeal to them in all practical sciences. “As often” — these are the author’s own words — “as the object of our investigation belongs to the kind of things for whose judging nothing but reason and certain experiences at the disposal of all human beings are necessary: so often are all human beings really in a position to pass a valid judgment on it. If we now find that all, or at any rate nearly all, have judged the object in a *concordant* manner; and if it moreover turns out that their judgment *does not, say, flatter the human inclinations*, but rather restricts them: then I say that we may presuppose, with a very high degree of confidence, that this judgment (which in such a case I call a *pronouncement of common human understanding*) must conform to the truth.” — In the next § 332 (*examination of one’s own judgments already passed*) the author corrects the still current rule of *Descartes*: “*To doubt everything at least once in life*,” in that, in place of the expression *doubting*, which demands now something impossible, now something very wrong, he sets an entirely different *examining*, and defines the concept of it precisely; — finally, does not want even this examining extended to *everything*, but, after showing that for every examination some other propositions must be presupposed as true, demonstrates that we may safely omit the examining wherever we could do it in no other way than by presupposing propositions that have an even *lower degree of reliability*. — In order, finally, not to find what is said about *signs* in §§ 334–344 incomplete, let one not forget that the discussion here is only of signs which we use in the business of reflection itself, or perhaps for the preservation of what has been thought out, again only for ourselves. From another point of view this subject will be considered later.

[p. 82]

In the chapter on the “*special rules*” (pp. 390–575) there does, to be sure, occur some material that is based on the author’s peculiar views set up earlier. Whoever will not admit that concepts can be composed of others, or sees no difference between concepts of equal extension, cannot, of course, find the instruction of § 350 (“*Definition of an Idea Given Through Our Consciousness*”) usable; whoever does not allow that there are also *objectless* and *imaginary* concepts will find the task of § 352 dispensable; for whoever has no concept of what B. calls a *redundant* idea, § 354 is senseless; whoever will admit no difference between the *objective connection* of truths and their *subjective deducibility* must reject § 378 (“*Finding the Objective Ground of a Given Truth*”). But as sincerely as we admit all this, just as sincerely do we confess that we cannot see what could keep a reader who is, in whatever way, at odds with B. over his earlier assertions from following him at least in the instructions that he gives in most of the remaining §§. As an example we point to §§ 356–358, where the finding of a *similarity*, of a *difference*, of an *exclusive attribute* is taught. Particularly correct, however, and acceptable to everyone seem to us the *instructions* of §§ 369–376, “*Examination of the Truth of a Given Proposition*”; “*of the Convincing Power of a Given Proof*,” the *usual* “*Errors in Proving*,” and “the *Marks of the Faultiness of a Proof*.” Since space does not permit us to repeat here any one of the instructions given under these titles, we shall instead indicate how the author in § 377 solves several of the *well-known fallacies* that are usually cited in the textbooks of logic. The fallacy of *Diodorus*: “If a body is to move, it would have to move either in the place in which it is or in one in which it is not; both of which are absurd” still has importance for our

[p. 83]

time on account of the similar doctrines put forward by *Herbart’s* school (see e.g. *Hartenstein’s* Metaphysics, p. 401). B. now says simply that, since motion is nothing other than a change of place lasting through a certain time, it is absurd to suppose that it takes place in a single place; rather, one must assume several places in which it is to proceed. — In solving the famous fallacy of the *Achilles* (who is supposed not to catch up with a tortoise although he is a thousand times faster than it), B. shows — something that also needs attention elsewhere and is of the highest importance — that series of times made up of infinitely many members can also have elapsed, and this sometimes even within a finite time; as, for example, the entire series of times:

½ hour + ¼ h. + ⅛ h. + . . . *in inf.*

fills only a single hour. The *Sophisma pigrum* [lazy sophism] one hears in various forms almost every day: “What I could strive to bring about by my activity can concern only one of two things: an outcome that actually occurs, or one that does not actually occur. In the first case my activity is dispensable, in the second futile; hence always unwise.” Here the error lies solely in the proposition that my activity is to be called dispensable when it concerns an outcome that actually occurs. For it is precisely one of the partial causes whose combination brings that outcome about, etc.

What is said in §§ 379–384 about the *discovery of the causes of given effects* and vice versa; in § 387 about the *interpretation of given signs*; in §§ 388 and 389 about the discovery of existing *testimonies* and the testing of their *credibility*; and finally in § 390 about the determination of the credibility of a proposition *from the authority of all who are for or against it* — this we would venture to recommend to *physicists*, *historians*, *hermeneuts* and *theologians* respectively, promising that each of them, *if they pursue their science in a philosophical spirit*, will find here something that will claim their scrutiny.

[p. 84]

Yet with this we would now already have arrived at the *last* volume of the work, which sets forth the *fifth part*, or the “*Theory of Science Proper*,” for which everything preceding is to serve as a means. We have already said above that we excuse from the study of this volume anyone who simply will not at all have logic be a theory of science; only we give him to consider, before we take our leave, that in any other textbook of logic too he has put up with at least *some* of the investigations that are still to come: namely a determination of the *concept*: *science*, then whole treatises on *definition*, *division* and *proof*; indeed that these instructions were offered him here and there even under the same title, “*Theory of Science*,” under which they appear here, to be sure, augmented by many others besides. — So that he may now know what B. discusses under this title, what, then, this part scorned by him actually contains, we lay before him here the brief synopsis of it in the author’s own words (in § 392):

“1. First I shall still have to determine somewhat more precisely, than has already been done in § 1, the *concept* both of a *science* itself and of a *textbook*, and likewise to investigate whether there is not some *supreme principle* from which all the rules that are to be observed in the formation of the individual sciences as well as in the elaboration of their textbooks can be derived; and, in the affirmative case, to attach to it some of the *most immediate consequences*, insofar as it is beneficial always to keep them in view in developing the remaining rules.

“2. The next thing after this will be to set forth the rules according to which the entire domain of truth can be *divided up* into individual sciences and, at the same time, it can be judged whether a science whose concept is laid before us is appropriate.”

“3. After these rules have been set up, logic will turn to its *second* (incomparably more extensive) business, that of stating the rules to be observed in the composition of a *textbook*

[p. 85]

“of a science. Since, however, in the composition of every book *a determinate class of readers* must be thought of, the rules must now first be set up according to which it is to be judged whether a class of readers has been chosen appropriately.”

“4. Hereupon arises the question which are the *propositions* that deserve admission into our book.

“5. We shall, however, almost always do well to bring the whole collection of these propositions under various *divisions*. For this business, too, special guidance is needed.

“6. Since, furthermore, those propositions can never be brought before the reader’s consciousness all at once, but only one after another, we must also determine the *sequence* in which we wish to present them, i.e. *arrange* them; for which again guidance is to be given.

“7. Since, furthermore, all books are only a kind of *written* expositions, and since it is moreover necessary in a textbook, besides those written signs which we use in the book itself, also to recommend to our readers certain signs, partly written, partly oral, for their *own* use: some instruction will be given on the manner in which both of these are to be done.

“8. Since, finally, in all those rules which I have just announced under nos. 4–7 the discussion will be only of certain *attributes* to be imparted to the book, but not of how the *author* has *himself* to conduct himself in the process, a brief piece of instruction on this point too would probably be fitting.

“9. Finally, there are books which, although they are not proper *textbooks*, yet aim at *scientific instruction*, and for that very reason must be composed almost according to the same rules as those. It might therefore not be out of place in a textbook of logic to close with some remarks on these as well.

“From this indication there result of themselves those *nine chapters* which the reader encounters in this part.”

[p. 86]

So far B. Let us now single out from each of these chapters something for readers who wish to become acquainted with something of it.

The *first* chapter, under the heading “*general rules*,” fills only 43 pages. The author defines a *science* as a “*collection of such truths whose known and, for us, noteworthy part deserves to be written down in a book of its own in such a manner, and, where necessary, also combined with so many other propositions serving for their understanding and proof, that they receive the greatest comprehensibility and power of conviction*.” Next, it depends, to be sure, on our human receptivity for certain truths and on our need whether we call the collection of them a *science* or not; nevertheless, for the author a science according to this definition is something *objective* and *unchangeable*, because not merely the truths known to us and noteworthy for us are counted into its content, but also such as we do not yet know or shall never come to know; likewise because only the truths themselves that correspond to this species concept, but not also all those that may be required, for us, for their *subjective proof*, and that may be different at different times and for different persons, belong to the science itself. These demonstrations, even if they were attempted objective groundings, B. reckons only as belonging to the *exposition* of a science in its textbook. That this concept will meet with contradiction, despite all the reasons of justification that the author has appended to it, is to be expected; especially among all those who, when they happen to have their *philosophy* in mind, behave no differently than if this were the *only* science that exists at all. But since these very scholars, at other times and in other places, are magnanimous enough also to concede to the mathematician, the natural scientist, the physician, even the historian, that they too possess some, if only fragmentary, *knowledge*, and that they too may continue to present this

[p. 87]

in books as hitherto, support it with proofs, and then perhaps even bestow upon it, *ad honores* [honorarily], the name of a *science*: then B. will probably not be wholly wrong in essentials; but as for counting, of course, on an approving reception, how could that enter his head, since he brings in not a single one of the favorite fashionable words, mentioning neither the “*mutual interpenetration of all parts*,” nor the “*organic coherence of them*,” not even a “*living whole*”?

To be sure, he ventures — and this would not be so wrong — to set up a *supreme principle* of the whole theory of science; but, heavens! how does it read? “In dividing up the entire domain of truth into individual sciences, and in the exposition of them in textbooks of their own, so to proceed that — *the greatest possible sum of good may result from it!*” — Such a thing may well have been aimed at by an *Aristotle*, a *Bacon of Verulam*, and all truly great and praiseworthy scholars who earned fame either through the introduction of new sciences, or through the extension of those already existing, or even merely through new, thorough, lucid expositions of them: but just now our philosophers like to turn attention to the common good as little as do certain governments. It is a contemptible *utilitarianism*! — And such as the principle is, such too are the 13 “*nearest consequences*” drawn from it: e.g. that a suitable textbook must make the understanding of what is presented in it as easy and as certain as possible for its readers (§ 398) (oh, ahem!), that it should make the degree of reliability of every doctrine noticeable (§ 400); that it should demonstrate the objective connection as often as possible (§ 401); that it must be so arranged that even its possible errors cause the reader the least harm (§ 407), that it should also let the reason for its arrangements be perceived (§ 408), etc. One must admit it: not a single one of these demands accords with the taste of the age!

[p. 88]

As regards the instructions of the *second chapter*: “*On the Determination of the Domain of the Sciences*” (pp. 44–84), B. is, according to p. 81, “*prepared* for what is said here to be received partly with disdain, partly even with ridicule.” And not without reason does he expect this; for what is most essential rests, after all, only on 14 theorems (§§ 410–423), whose headings we wish to lay before our readers here, so that they may judge for themselves and not complain of disappointed expectations, should they perhaps nevertheless one day take up the work.

“1) For truths that cannot be communicated through writing there need be no science either. 2) Every truth communicable through writing, however, that is noteworthy not merely as an *auxiliary proposition*, ought to be *at home* in at least one science. 3) Not too small an extent, but certainly too great an extent can become a sufficient ground for the rejection of a science. 4) It is no sufficient ground for the rejection of a science that many, indeed all of its doctrines are already known to everyone anyway. 5) It is no sufficient ground for uniting truths merely because they have much similarity with one another; 6) nor for separating them merely because they have a great difference, in particular a different source of knowledge. 7) There may also be sciences that have certain doctrines in common, or of which the one is wholly contained in the other; 8) also such as depend on another either merely subjectively, or objectively, or in both respects; 9) indeed also stand in the relation of mutual dependence. 10) It is not to be demanded that the applications of a truth always belong with it to the same science; 11) that all truths of a science have a single objective or subjective principle. 12) It is very good to divide the truths according to such an attribute as enables one to inquire after them. 13) If any pure concept, especially a simple one, occurs exclusively in certain truths, it is very much to be expected that they deserve union in a science of their own. 14) To every investigation a place is to be assigned, in a

[p. 89]

“science in which it can be conducted most fruitfully.”

Now follows, in three individual §§ which are capable of no extract, the solution of the following three problems: 1) Examination of the appropriateness of a *given* science. 2) Discovery of the concept of a *new* appropriate science. 3) Division of the *entire domain* of truth into individual sciences.

At the close B. assures us that he “wishes with all his heart that one may soon find something that not only looks *more learned*, but is also more correct and more useful.”

In the very short *third chapter*, “*On the Choice of the Class of Readers Intended for a Textbook*” (pp. 85–91), the most important thing is likely to be the proposal of § 431 to distinguish, for every science of more general usefulness, *three kinds of textbooks* for three classes of readers: complete works for the *specialist scholar*, in which even what has for the time being found no application is deposited; textbooks *for the businessman*, which contain everything that he can use for his business; books for *everyone*, which select only what is generally useful.

The *fourth chapter*: “*On the Propositions That Are to Occur in a Textbook*,” (the most detailed of all, comprising 300 pages) begins, in § 434, with a distinction of the different ways in which propositions can occur in a textbook at all: one can, namely, present them now *propounding* them, i.e. with the expectation that the reader, if not already earlier, will at least now assent to them with a definite degree of confidence; now merely *mentioning* them, or *avowing* them, or *presupposing* them, etc. Then, in § 435, *three ways* in which the reader is to make *use* of the propositions occurring in a book (committing to memory, thinking through once, occasional looking-up) are distinguished; finally, in § 436, *three kinds of relations*

[p. 90]

of them to the science that is to be taught here; inasmuch as they can be either *essential* (*native*) propositions, or *auxiliary propositions*, or *occasional* propositions. Each of these three kinds is now dealt with in a section of its own; but since there are also propositions whose peculiarity rests not at all on their relation to the science but on other circumstances, these are discussed in addition in a *fourth* section.

In the *first section*: “*On the Essential Propositions*,” (pp. 102–130) it is investigated when a proposition is noteworthy enough to justify the demand that the reader take it up into his memory; when only to demand a single consideration of it; when, finally, for a merely occasional looking-up. The questions are discussed whether a more general truth always deserves preference over the particular one? when even immediate consequences require citation? when propositions of entirely equal validity, further propositions with imaginary, redundant ideas, identical propositions, mere corrective propositions, mere probabilities deserve inclusion? etc. We pick out one example. “In an introduction to the art of healing” (says the author, p. 119) “one can perhaps not repeat often enough the warning that the physician must *never disturb* nature in its operations, that he may only be its *servant*, only *assist* it, and the like. Yet one should never forget that in saying this one is basically saying nothing other than the *identical* proposition that the physician must always take care not to use the means of the art in the wrong place, i.e. where they are not actually prescribed. But were one to put forward such rules as principles from which the physician’s conduct at the sickbed can be derived *objectively*, this would truly be a ridiculous error.”

In the *second section*: “*On the Auxiliary Propositions*” (pp. 134–142) we commend to consideration especially the two §§: when one in a science that

[p. 91]

has pure conceptual propositions as its object may also apply empirical auxiliary propositions, and conversely? and where the proof taken from authority is to be used? It would surely be reprehensible if in a textbook of religion we wanted to derive the important truth of God’s existence and attributes from mere concepts and not also from the purposive arrangement of the structure of the world, etc.; and it is just as mistaken if in the empirical sciences one will let no inferences *a priori* count at all and shrinks from all philosophy. What is true in the assertion *that in pure conceptual sciences no authority holds* is only that here we should never stop at mere authority, but should search out the objective ground, which in empirical sciences is not always possible.

Among the “*occasional propositions*” in the *third section* (pp. 142–177) the author counts, besides much else: the determination and justification of the concept of the science to be expounded; the determination of its relation to other sciences; historical communications; statement of the rules followed in composing the book; of the class of readers for whom we have written; of the benefit that we can promise the readers from our science and from the study of this textbook; confessions of its defects; demands on the reader; applications; warnings against misunderstanding or misuse; divisions, and much else. Here too we single out only a couple of passages, on account of their important content. “If in any science (says the author in the note to § 470) *applications* in the sense given here should not be omitted, so it is in the case of *history*, in expositions intended not for the scholar but for the larger public, or especially for the young. The study of this science, after all, affords actually no benefit at all unless we make reflections on the events and deeds that have become known to us here, which are of an entirely different than historical nature, which aim at making ever more vivid to us what is right and

[p. 92]

“wrong, praiseworthy and blameworthy, prudent or imprudent, what ruinous consequences certain customs and institutions bring with them, how much man — often even the individual — can accomplish through a prudent and persistent exertion of his powers, how God’s providence guides the fortunes of our race at all times and in all countries, etc. — What is one to say, however, when propositions such as: ‘a historian should present only the bare facts, without letting any praising or blaming judgment of them flow in,’ or: ‘one should not even be able to notice in a historian the legal, moral and religious concepts to which he is devoted,’ — are even put forward as *principles*? It would be correct, to be sure, to say that a historian must take great care lest the *preconceived* opinion about the worth or worthlessness of an action lead him to an unfaithful presentation of it, now to embellishment, now to distortion. It would also be fair to demand that a historian not, say, make his political or religious profession of faith known to his readers by displaying a partisan preference for those who think as he does. But must one then necessarily, when one praises or blames, fall into the fault of exaggeration, or even into that of an unfaithful presentation of what one wants to judge? And is the open confession of what faith one belongs to not, for noble minds, if anything a special deterrent from treating the adherents of the opposing party unjustly in any way?” In the note to § 471 one reads: “Only an *uninterrupted* observance of this rule” (the warning against misunderstanding and misuse) “is needed to preserve mankind from an evil of which many assert that it must occur as an unavoidable consequence wherever the scholars take it into their heads to raise a part of the knowledge which they have hitherto possessed as their exclusive property to a common possession, and for that reason seek to spread a considerable quantity of *fragmentary knowledge* from all the sciences among the public. *Half-knowledge* is said

[p. 93]

“to be the evil that arises inevitably in this way and proves, in its effects, more ruinous than even complete ignorance. In every science, it is said, there is a certain, eternally immovable limit which the knowledge of the great multitude, indeed of all who cannot appropriate this science in its whole extent, must not exceed if it is not to become *half-knowledge*. And of certain sciences, such as theology, medicine and several others, it has even been asserted that in instruction that does not want to produce *half-knowers*, if one cannot treat them exhaustively, they ought not even to be touched upon. — I too do not at all dispute, as one already knows from § 431 and elsewhere, that exceeding certain limits, especially in textbooks which (according to the meaning of § 430) are intended for *everyone*, has important disadvantages; especially because thereby other knowledge, the learning of which would have been more necessary, suffers detriment, or because over much learning the time for acting is neglected; but what I do not concede is that by such a procedure the evil that is called *half-knowledge* must always be produced. If the word *half-knowledge* is to designate a harmful condition of the mind, indeed a condition worse than not-knowing: then we may, after all, level the reproach of mere half-knowledge only at him who, from the individual concepts of a science which he has picked up, derives conclusions that do not hold with the truth and that cause harm only to himself or to others. Not from the mere *number* of the doctrines which we have appropriated from a science may it be measured whether we deserve to be called half-knowers in this field or not; since otherwise even the most thorough and comprehensive scholar, because his knowledge too is still only patchwork, perhaps amounting to not even half of what will have been discovered in this domain in a coming century, could in a certain respect be accused of half-knowledge. But here what matters is something else, here what matters is

[p. 94]

“solely the manner in which we understand the doctrines, and what nearest consequences we deduce from them. If, then, two persons have roughly the same incomplete concepts of the efficacy of certain medicinal substances, such as one obtains from mere hearsay when one frequently associates with physicians; the one of them, however, supposes that he knows enough to be able to judge in a case that arises whether this or that remedy should be used, while the other, on the contrary, sees that his knowledge is far from sufficient for such a judgment: then we may call only the knowledge of the former, by no means that of the second, a half-knowledge. Whoever grants me this will already understand me as to how we can prevent the origin of half-knowledge, however fragmentary the instruction we give in a science may be, if only we make it a law for ourselves to examine expressly, in the case of every single doctrine, what possible misunderstandings or misuses it might occasion given the presence of this or that erroneous idea in our pupil, and then to show circumstantially that such consequences may not be drawn from our proposition. *Not a fragmentary but a clumsily given instruction produces half-knowledge.*”

In the *fourth section*: “*Components of a Textbook Whose Peculiarity Arises from Other Considerations*,” (pp. 177–391), the author deals I. with principles, II. comparisons and distinctions, III. determinations, IV. descriptions, V. proofs, VI. objections and refutations, VII. examples, VIII. considerations of mere propositions and ideas.

I. In the first subsection: “*On Principles*” (pp. 177–203) he distinguishes *objective* supreme principles, *subjective* principles of knowledge and *main propositions*. Without failing to recognize the value of all these kinds of principles, he nevertheless warns against overestimating them and against the prejudice of presupposing the existence of a supreme *objective principle*

[p. 95]

in every science. On this occasion a long note then speaks of the *striving for unity*. How mistaken it is to understand the principle *of sufficient reason* as if every truth had to be grounded in another truth, and every existent accordingly in another existent: just as little is there, either, *an unconditional demand of reason to reduce every plurality to a higher unity*. It is this striving that leads so many of our philosophers to a kind of pantheism, whose untenability the author attempts to expose. Not only does this pantheism in no case attain that *absolute unity* toward which it strives, since after all the *existent* and the *truths in themselves* can never be derived from any unity embracing both; but it can also never explain adequately how its Absolute splits into God and world. “Is it,” asks the author, “not a contradiction to say that something *absolute*, i.e. at least a being whose being is grounded in no other, which accordingly would have to be wholly independent, infinite in all its powers and properties, or at least limited only by itself, is nevertheless *changeable*; since all those changes which the finite things in this world undergo are supposed to take place in its own interior? Certainly it is incomparably more reasonable, or rather it is the only reasonable thing that can be said here, that the changes which we perceive in the world take place not in the unconditioned substance but in other, conditioned substances, in substances which owe their existence to the will and activity of the unconditioned substance. Why, then, does one balk at the assumption of such conditioned substances, whose ground of existence lies in another; provided one proceeds not from the false concept of *Spinoza’s* (*Per substantiam intelligo id, quod in se est et per se concipi potest, h. i. id, cujus conceptus non indiget conceptu alterius rei, a quo formari debeat* [By substance I understand that which is in itself and can be conceived *through itself*, i.e. that whose concept does not need the concept of another thing from which it would have to be formed]. *Eth. def. 3.*) but from the definition given in § 142?” (*Substance* is something actual that is not an attribute.)

[p. 96]

“If one had to presuppose that a substance whose existence is grounded in another must first have *come into being* in time, then one might indeed find a contradiction in the assumption of created substances. If one had to presuppose that there is only a *finite multitude* of such substances, then one might well ask: how is this finite effect to be reconciled with the infinite power ascribed to its creator? If everything infinite as such had to be *indeterminate* (as many indeed believe), then one would certainly have to take offense at the thought of a world consisting of infinitely many parts. If *spiritual* and *material* substances were distinguished in such a way that there were no gradual transition from the level of the latter to that of the former, then it might indeed be puzzling why God created two such opposed kinds of beings. If it had to follow from the fact that all created substances are of equal duration that at the same moment they all also stand at the same stage of development, then the great manifoldness of the beings we encounter in the world would indeed have to strike us as strange. If it were contradictions, and not rather quite comprehensible and demonstrable truths, that a being possessing infinite powers must also produce something with them; that the substances that subsist through its activity must, as finite substances, be engaged in reciprocal action upon one another, and thereby, and through the constant action of that infinite substance, must progress into the infinite: then one would have reason to be dissatisfied with the system of *theism*; but even then one would still have to ask: what, after all, would one gain by the assumption of *pantheism*? The friends of the latter declare the finite things now to be *parts* of the One Absolute (the primal matter), now merely *modifications* or *accidents* of this sole being, now, more definitely, mere *thoughts* of it, now again *emanations* from it, now the *effects* that

[p. 97]

“its *self-unfolding* or *self-revelation* produces, now mere *relations* present in this being, etc. *Hegel* in particular used for this explanation the *dialectical method* invented by him, by saying that the Absolute (or God) first *externalizes* itself and, in the shape of its *otherness*, appears as *nature*, in order then, through *return* to itself, to attain its *self-consciousness*, i.e. to become *spirit*. For him, then, all finite beings were nothing other than ‘transitory moments in the infinite process of development of the divine life.’ — Let us, then, consider these explanations somewhat more closely! If one calls the individual finite things that we perceive in this shape *parts* of the One Absolute, one must reckon those things as belonging to the *interior* of the Absolute, and thus necessarily concede that this Absolute undergoes changes in its inner being; which is certainly absurd. But into this very absurdity one falls also with all the other explanations, with each of which one moreover offends against some other truth. Thus it is, for example, a truth that must surely be evident to anyone who connects the generally accepted concepts with the words *substance* and *space*, that every object that occupies a particular part of space (even if only that of a point) must be a substance of its own, distinct from others. One offends against this truth if one declares the countless multitude of bodies to be mere accidents or modifications of a single infinite substance. If one declares the manifold finite things of the world to be mere *thoughts* of God, then, to be consistent, one must grant to *all* the thoughts present in God a being of a similar kind to that which the things of this world have; and will this not lead to the greatest absurdities? God thinks *all* truths, hence also those whose object is nothing existent, e.g. that this or that action which was not performed *ought* to have been performed; he also knows and thinks all mathematical concepts, the objectual

[p. 98]

“as well as the objectless, e.g. also the concepts √2, √−1, 0, etc. Which, then, are those real things that correspond to these thoughts of God? — The figurative expressions, finally, which one uses in the exposition of the *system of emanation* in its various modifications, in earlier as well as in the most recent times, can certainly satisfy only those who have never been accustomed to think anything clearly and distinctly. What is one to imagine by an *emanation* from God, what by a *self-unfolding*, *development* or *self-revelation*? — How absurd is it to say that finite beings are nothing other than transitory moments in the infinite process of development of the divine life? That God should have to externalize himself first and pass over into his otherness, in order then to attain self-consciousness through return? The dialectical method, by which these and most of the other discoveries of the absolute philosophy of identity are demonstrated, we shall consider later. Here it may suffice merely to remark that the *countless gradations* which we encounter in the real world, and the gradual transitions from the seemingly lifeless to the living, from mineral to plant, from this to animal, and from the animal to man — in fact correspond poorly to a system that finds explicable everywhere only a *triad* sprung from unity!” — His judgment on the dialectical method, promised here, B. supplies only in the last § of the work; but we must pass it over, in order not to become too lengthy.

II. The author endeavors to restore the “*Comparisons and Distinctions*” (pp. 203–212) to their almost forgotten worth by setting out their manifold advantages; but he warns in this connection against the faults of *vagueness*, of false *subtlety*, of inferring from sameness of *name* to sameness of the *thing*, as is done by those who regard the so-called *morally impossible* as a kind of the *impossible*

[p. 99]

in the proper sense. It is also argued in § 494 *that erroneous equations are, as a rule, more harmful than erroneous distinctions*. “This, for one thing, already for the reason that it is generally harder to turn back from an error of the first kind than from one of the second. For if we regard certain things as equal, we no longer consider it necessary to examine each of them more closely in particular, and for that very reason we shall hardly ever come to know the differences obtaining between them. But if we regard as unequal what is in fact equal, then our very error gives occasion for a closer examination of the things held to be different, and our error can therefore not last long,” etc.

III. By the “*Determinations*” (pp. 212–230) B. here understands only those propositions, hitherto frequently confused with *definitions*, which state an attribute belonging *exclusively* to certain objects, and hence state *equivalent ideas*. He removes various prejudices that exist concerning them, by showing, e.g., that such determinations are indeed of quite exceptional value when they state the proper *essence* of the object (i.e. those attributes of it in which all the remaining ones are *objectively grounded*), but that others too, and even merely *analytic* ones, in general deserve no rejection; that, further, very useful determinations can also consist in the statement of a mere *relation*, that they may also contain a *negation*, also a *division*, etc. Finally he states how they must be constituted if they are to serve as *distinguishing marks* for the particular purpose.

IV. A peculiar kind of propositions the author calls “*Descriptions*” (pp. 231–236), which often need only be set up so that the *image* that readers put together of an object may receive its proper character. There are only a few sciences, such as morals, the doctrine of religion, history, in which a

[p. 100]

more careful use would have to be made of such descriptions than has hitherto been the case.

V. In the important subsection “*On Proofs*” (pp. 237–299) an inquiry is made into which propositions need a proof (in which connection he quite rightly censures the endeavor to prove propositions that are already evident of themselves, unless by this proving there is rather understood merely the statement of the objective ground), then which premises and forms of inference are to be used (whether *definitions* too belong among such premises), what attributes proofs in a textbook must necessarily have (§§ 516–521), and which are merely *advisable* (§§ 522–526). Among these latter he counts ease of retention, an intelligible course of argument, an explanation of the way in which one might have found the proposition, but especially the statement of the objective ground. Much that is new and noteworthy is brought forward in the long § 530 on the *apagogic kind of proof*, and it is shown, among other things, that and in what way every such proof, insofar as its essence consists in the reduction to an *absurdity*, can be avoided. — Whether it is true, as has frequently been asserted in more recent times, that in proofs from pure concepts (*a priori*) there arises absolutely *no possibility of the opposite*; and that for that very reason there is in *mathematics*, *metaphysics* and *morals* no *opining*, so that (as *Hegel* says) one can tell at once from a man that he lacks elementary education when he speaks of *philosophical opinions*, is examined in § 532, and it is shown that error is possible even in proofs from pure concepts, because we gather only from the testimony of our memory or of our senses, hence in any case only by means of an *inference of probability*, that these or those premises used in our proof have already been recognized by us earlier as true. An example of this is every mistake made in *calculating*. The high confidence, then, with which the mathematician, the moralist, and perhaps also the metaphysician pronounce some of their theorems does not rest on their deriving these truths from pure

[p. 101]

concepts, but on other circumstances, e.g. that so many others have already examined this series of inferences and perceived no fallacy in it, and the like. Hence, too, where these circumstances are lacking, the mathematician is at least not too proud to speak of a mere *opinion*; but the philosopher who comes forward with such high confidence only makes himself ridiculous.

VI. “*Objections and Refutations*.” (pp. 299–315). We shall merely enumerate what the author demands in the case of the latter: a) if need be, first to state the sense of the objection still more clearly; b) to concede what is true in it; c) to show how this is compatible with our doctrines; d) and does not even diminish their probability; e) to uncover what is false; f) to show with what restrictions it might at most be granted; g) how defective the proofs are that have been adduced for it, as well as h) for the true element in it; i) how our doctrine can perhaps even be proved from the opponent’s own assertions; k) how self-contradictory these assertions are; finally l) how they may have arisen. — The well-known proposition: *Neganti incumbit probatio* [the burden of proof rests on the one who denies] and much else is also discussed here.

VII. “*On Examples*.” (pp. 315–326). How these must be arranged if they are to facilitate understanding, shorten the exposition, promote attention, secure retention, serve as confirmation or sometimes even as a complete proof, and finally spread useful truths on occasion.

VIII. “*On the Considerations of Mere Ideas and Propositions*.” (pp. 326–391.) Under this unassuming title there is treated, after the author has shown in general *which* ideas and propositions in a textbook deserve to become the object of a special consideration, and *on which attributes* of them the consideration is to extend — in particular A. of the definitions, B. of comparisons and distinctions, C. of divisions, and finally D. of the demonstrations of the objective connection.

[p. 102]

A. “*On the Definitions of Ideas and Propositions*.” (pp. 330–350). Without doubt one of the most important divisions. Above all, one should grasp precisely what the author understands by *explanation* or *definition*. For he calls so only the decision — usually to be accompanied by a proof of its own — of the question whether certain ideas or propositions are simple, or of what parts they are composed. For not only concepts (as is commonly done) but also mixed ideas and whole propositions often need a definition; which, in accordance with what has just been said, one must confuse neither with a *determinative proposition* nor with a mere *understanding*. I utter a *determinative proposition* when I say that it is an exclusive attribute of all triangles that they are figures whose angles all together amount to two right angles. I *come to an understanding* about this concept when I draw several triangles for someone and say in doing so that figures of this kind are called triangles. A *definition* of this concept, however, I give only when I say that it is composed of the concepts figure, side, three, etc., in the manner that the following words indicate: A triangle is a figure that has three sides. It is now examined which ideas and propositions need a definition, and which definitions need a proof, and how this is to be conducted if one declares the idea to be simple, and how if one declares it to be complex. One and the same concept has also only one and the same definition; and the so-called *nominal*, *real* and *genetic* definitions are not definitions of the same concept, but of related concepts, perhaps of such as have the same objects. If the concept to be defined is a *relational concept*, e.g. milestone, then its definition too must be effected through a relation — and if it is *negative*, e.g. oblique angle, through a negation; thus it is an incorrect canon of the logicians that a definition may never rest on a relation or be negative. Just as false is it that every definition must consist in the statement of the nearest genus and of the specific difference.

[p. 103]

The concept *Nothing* can certainly not be defined otherwise than by the words: *Not something*; it is objectless, and can therefore possess neither a nearest genus nor a specific difference. — It is also a prejudice that every definition must be *easily intelligible*. To be sure, “that one should insist on the *greatest possible* ease of understanding, as in the whole scientific exposition so in particular in the definitions (says the author, § 559), deserves all praise. But when some demand that every definition be understood *more easily* than the concept to be defined, with which one is perhaps already familiar from childhood on: then they go too far, and seem to confuse *definitions* with *understandings*. If we have the purpose of a mere *understanding*, then we indeed act foolishly if we do not choose the easiest means leading to this purpose; and if both the *concept* and the *sign* are already known, there is no need whatever of an *understanding* about it. But if the components are to be enumerated out of which we compose a concept already familiar to us since our childhood, we ourselves do not know how: then it is no sufficient ground for rejecting our attempt that it costs some trouble to grasp all the components indicated by us, to bring them into the prescribed combination, and to recognize that the concept thus arising is really the same as the one we connect with that familiar sign.” — The assertions, too, that *empirical concepts*, likewise *ideas of individuals*, *could never be defined*, the author declares erroneous; for however difficult it may be in many cases to decide whether a certain idea present in our consciousness is simple, or out of which simpler ones we compose it: can we ever be entitled to declare this *impossible*? — Etc.

C. “*On Divisions*” (pp. 353–385), where again quite a few defects that have remained in the usual doctrine are corrected. First, several purposes and kinds of divisions not hitherto distinguished are pointed out, then their special arrangement for these purposes is determined, and many an important investigation is interwoven, above all the question, so often discussed by *naturalists*, of the possibility of a “*natural system*.” Above all the author shows, very convincingly, as it seems to us, that under the expression: “*a certain system or a law in general is followed by nature*,” for the purpose of a division of systems into natural and non-natural, we must understand something more than *that the natural things are in accordance with this law* (that it is a true proposition). We can do this only insofar as we think of nature, or rather of its originator, God, as a rational and freely governing being; and only then do we find it fitting to call a certain rule a *rule followed* by nature (better, by God), when it can reasonably be asserted that there are certain beings and arrangements in the world *only for the very reason that this rule may be fulfilled*; a case which, again, occurs only *when it can be comprehended that the sum of well-being is raised thereby*. “From this one will now already gather” (says the author) “that it is a risky matter with the assertion that there is a true natural system, and still more that it consists in these and those species and genera perceived in the world. From that sole law of divine governance known to us, which aims at the bringing about of the greatest possible happiness among created beings, it can still easily be understood why there must be a countless multitude of *differences* among creatures. It is, moreover, not to be overlooked that the

[p. 105]

“countless kinds of organic as well as inorganic beings that we encounter on earth serve the purpose that every single substance may *find opportunity for a gradual development of all its powers*. Hence it is also very probable that a certain *order of rank* prevails among these manifold kinds, in the sense that some of them stand on a higher, others on a lower level of perfection, and that a transition from the latter to the former also takes place. Thus, e.g., it can hardly be doubted that an organic being (I mean the soul animating this organism) stands on a higher level than inorganic matter, and that likewise among organic beings animals as a whole stand higher than plants. The observation made by the ingenious *Oken* that in certain genera of organic beings certain organs, in others again other organs, attain a quite superior development, gives rise to the conjecture that this arrangement exists probably only in order that one and the same substance, as it gradually passes through all those various stages of existence, may obtain, on each single one, the opportunity to develop certain dispositions and powers slumbering in it, to enrich itself with ideas of a peculiar kind, and so to become ever more perfect. If we now, prompted by such considerations, made the resolve to devise a system in which all the natural products to be found on earth were ordered according to their presumed rank, so that those which seem to us to stand higher than others we would always put into a succeeding class, but those which, on account of their great similarity, seem to be of an equal rank we would put together in One class: then a system would come to light which, if any that can be furnished by men, would still most readily deserve the name of a *natural* one. For if we can anywhere be entitled to the conjecture that the creator of a certain genus of beings gave them existence only for this reason, or at least *also* for this reason, that no gap might arise in a division devised by us: then it is there,

[p. 106]

“where it seems that the existence of such a genus is necessary in order not to lose any rung of the transition on the ladder of creation. In this connection I must, however, remark that this natural system, however valuable it would be in many respects, would certainly not suffice for *all* the purposes for which systems or divisions are necessary in natural science. There would therefore still have to be others of them (*artificial* ones); in particular at least One would still have to be set up in which we arrange natural bodies merely according to certain *external*, easily ascertainable *distinguishing marks*, so that whoever wishes to become better acquainted with a body at hand may find without much trouble the place in the system where all its attributes known so far are assembled.”

D. Concerning the “*Demonstrations of the Objective Connection*” (pp. 385–391) the author sets up very moderate demands; yet even these have hitherto been very much neglected, especially in the pure conceptual sciences, in *philosophy* (the theoretical as well as the practical) and in *mathematics*, to the greatest detriment of the degree of perfection to be attained in both. The mathematician (to speak here only of the latter science) supplies in his proofs, especially in geometry, much more frequently than one seems to have yet considered, mere certifications instead of an objective grounding. Thus, e.g., the very *first proposition* in *Euclid*, that for any two points a, b there is a third, c, from which they are as far distant as they are from one another, is, as is well known, proved only from the fact that the circles described with the radius ab from a and b (in one and the same plane) must *intersect* somewhere (namely in c). But this intersecting certainly does not contain the objective ground of the existence of that point; rather, conversely, the circles intersect one another precisely because there is such a third point. A like *ὕστερον πρότερον* [the later before the earlier] is committed in a multitude of other propositions, and hence it comes about that one was unable to find the objective ground of certain

[p. 107]

other propositions (e.g. the proposition on parallels), a ground that presents itself readily enough once one has brought the doctrines into their natural position.

That so many views at variance with the hitherto prevailing doctrine as B. sets forth in this extensive chapter will make their way only with difficulty may well be presupposed without much knowledge of human nature. We counted, after all, in § 559 alone, which examines the usual doctrine of *definitions*, some sixteen, and in § 575, in which the doctrine of *divisions* is judged, some nine to eleven points that B., admittedly in all modesty, attacks. In fairness to the truth we must, meanwhile, remind those of our readers who are unprejudiced enough to ask about this circumstance that all of B.’s doctrines occurring here need, from what precedes, no other concessions than these three: 1) that there are simple as well as complex ideas; 2) that concepts and intuitions can be distinguished objectively; 3) that some difference can also be thought between the *objective connection* of truths and the mere *subjective deducibility* of one proposition from the other.

In the *fifth chapter* now following, “*On the Subdivisions of a Textbook*” (pp. 392–423), we have little to fear from the fact that the author right at the start — (actually it was already mentioned in § 472) — makes the unusual remark that subdivisions in a book, even when they bear no heading, are real *propositions*; for this exerts no further influence at all on the expositions now following. In general, in our opinion, of all of them this chapter may still most readily expect a favorable reception. For what could one possibly say against the eight *advantages* indicated in § 580 that can be attained through skillful subdivisions, or against the *general* rules for the business of subdividing in § 581, or against the *special precepts*

[p. 108]

for subdivisions that rest 1) on the particular manner in which the propositions are brought forward in the book; 2) on the inner attributes of the parts formed; 3) on their relation to one another; 4) on the objects treated; 5) on our manner of knowing the propositions; 6) on their use; 7) on their relation to our faculty of sensation; or that 8) facilitate understanding, or 9) finding, or 10) retention, or are based 11) on the relation of the propositions to our science, or finally 12) on their relation to our textbook, — have to object? That he does not entirely give up his habit here either, and, distinguishing e.g. (§ 593) the concept of an *introduction* from that of a *preparatory part*, restricts the content of the former more precisely, and wishes to see it restricted, to this: “that it contain only *incidental doctrines*, and indeed only those which one still sends ahead of the exposition of the *essential* doctrines or also of the *auxiliary doctrines* needed for them,” — this one can surely forgive him.

But the *sixth chapter* too: “*On the Order in Which the Propositions Belonging in a Textbook Are to Be Brought Forward*,” (pp. 424–499) contains, as far as we remember, nothing that could provoke the minds of readers to contradiction; for it is really the *weakest* of all; the author himself knows that here, especially in the second division, he has said many things that, being self-evident, could also have been left out. Meanwhile, the readers shall obtain a few samples from this division as well. On p. 477 the author speaks about the place that is to be assigned to *historical communications* in a textbook. “Historical communications about our science,” he maintains, “can be usefully introduced *at the most various places*. For one thing, it is fitting to note already at the very beginning whether the science that we wish to present in our book is perhaps being worked on by us for the first time, or has already existed for a longer time, and in the latter case to indicate briefly among how many peoples and in how manifold a way it

[p. 109]

“has already been worked on, and which are the most successful of its textbooks. If we then come to the exposition of its individual doctrines, it will again be expedient, in the case of each of the more important ones, to relate who its discoverer was, how he came upon it, with what difficulties one had to contend in disseminating it, what different views on this subject prevailed earlier or are still to be met with even now, etc. In most cases such remarks will best follow the statement of a doctrine; but sometimes it will nevertheless be more advisable to lay the conflicting opinions before the reader before we disclose our own opinion to him: the one, when the different opinions can be more easily *grasped* and more correctly *judged* after we have first sent ahead what appears to us as truth; the other, when thereby the *attention* of the readers is aroused and a *more unbiased judgment* can be brought about in them. Still other historical communications we can more fittingly reserve until the end of a section, or perhaps even until the point where we conclude the exposition of all the doctrines belonging to our science. So, namely, with communications that are too *lengthy* or stand in too slight a connection with our exposition,” etc. — After the necessary has been said in § 632 about the *place of definitions*, it says in the *note*: “Whoever agrees with what has just been said will not want to take under his protection the usual canon of older logicians, *that one must begin with definitions*; yet he will not find it so strange if he recalls that those logicians in their definitions commonly thought only of what I call *understandings*, or that they at any rate believed they had to dispose of both businesses, defining and coming to an understanding, always together. But what is one to say to it when *Kant* (who in this, to be sure, already had a predecessor in *Campanella*) advanced the assertion that in *philosophical treatises* one, far from being able to begin with the definition, may in the most favorable case

[p. 110]

“only end with it? I confess openly that no ground is known to me by which such an exaggeration could be justified. For however difficult it may be — as I gladly concede — to define certain philosophical concepts: there are surely also in many another science, in mathematics itself in particular, concepts whose analysis is no less difficult, e.g. the concepts of line, surface, body, direction, length of a line, area of a surface, volume of a body, curvature, and many others. Yet we do not read that *Kant* gave the mathematicians the same permission to postpone the definition of their concepts to the end of their exposition. And if it is merely not an impossibility to bring to distinct consciousness, by prolonged reflection, the components out of which one composes a concept: why must this necessarily happen only at the end of a treatise? If, moreover, the *analysis* of a concept is not distinguished in this assertion in the most determinate way from a mere *understanding* about it: to what confused talking back and forth in philosophical matters does one not give occasion and warrant, when one asserts that here it cannot become clear to the reader before the end of the whole treatise, when one has ceased to speak, which concepts one has connected or not connected with the words used?”

Only One more chapter of somewhat larger extent remains to us from the whole work; it is the *seventh*: “*On the Signs to Be Partly Proposed, Partly Used in a Textbook*” (pp. 500–596), which in two sections deals first *with the signs that are to be proposed to the readers*, then with those *that we ourselves are to use there*. In the *first section* (pp. 504–517) it is noted above all *how many kinds* of signs there are with which a careful writer must acquaint his readers; for these are not only signs by means of which they are put in a position

[p. 111]

to reflect by themselves on the subjects of our instruction, but also such by means of which they could communicate their thoughts to others, in oral conversation as well as in writing. Thereupon it is examined which attributes must belong to these signs *in common*, and which especially to the *oral* ones; their *connection*, partly among themselves, partly with the signs that we use in the book itself, is made noticeable; the *special considerations* that we have to take in determining these signs are likewise noted; finally, the arrangement of our proposals and of their justification (for they often need a justification of their own) is discussed. In the last § of this section the *title* to be given to a book is discussed; and it says among other things that a good title must a) if not be new, at least have something so peculiar to it that there is not a second one completely like it, at least with a book with which ours might easily be confused; that it must b) be short; c) not conflict too much with the prevailing taste, and in general arouse no repugnant, but also d) no such accompanying ideas as betray an all too high opinion of our book. One would often do well to give one’s book a title that, although very obscure and indeterminate, nevertheless sounds short and new, to which a closer explanation is then appended.

The *second* section is, as the more important, so also the larger (pp. 517–596). It falls into two divisions, the first of which comprises the *general*, the second the *special* rules. With good reason the author in § 614 declares *purity* (i.e. the absence of harmful accompanying ideas) to be among the essential attributes of a sign, and wants, e.g., expressions that sounded quite unobjectionable to an earlier century, but that in our days can no longer be cleansed of certain repugnant accompanying ideas, rather laid aside, lest the thing itself be lost on account of the word. — “Blameworthy,” he says in § 655, “it is only when two or more meanings are attached to a sign that have so great a similarity with one another,

[p. 112]

“that it is hard to recognize from the context of the discourse alone which of them is meant, or that it happens to the inattentive person, even after a previous warning, to jump over from the one meaning to the other. When this happens, the reader no longer thinks, at our words, what we nevertheless wanted him to think, i.e. we are *misunderstood*. And especially then shall we strive in vain to prevent such a misunderstanding of our words, when passion intrudes on the part of the readers, and they find it to their advantage to take every turn of phrase that has at times been used in a bad or foolish sense, when it occurs in our writing, in the worst sense, i.e. when with the misunderstanding there is united also a *misinterpretation*. In the field of theology this is, alas! by no means a rare phenomenon, so that one often hears the same writer decried by the one party as a disguised freethinker, by the other as an obscurantist, not because he is the one or the other, but because each party interprets his words in a way of its own.” (Is that not very nearly the fate of the author?) — “Only then,” (it says in § 660) “can it be permitted to depart from a designation that another has used before us, when either a) it is not possible to follow the example of the one without abandoning the equally noteworthy one of another; or when b) the hitherto customary designation is decidedly contrary to its purpose to such a degree that the advantages we hope to attain by changing it far outweigh the inconvenience unavoidable in the process.” — “That *technical terms* are to be formed” (one reads in § 661) “as often as the purpose of the science demands it, because we need a sign for a concept for which the language of everyday life has no suitable one, is not open to dispute. But all too often it happens that technical terms are formed even where the best interest of the science in no way requires it. Merely a more learned look does one often want to give to one’s exposition; merely through the new words which one has introduced does one want to gain the appearance as if

[p. 113]

“one had enriched the science with just as many new concepts, etc. Such a procedure is not only blameworthy in every case, but in those sciences whose doctrines it is to be wished should be spread quite widely and should pass over into social life itself, it is to be regarded as a true sin against humanity.”

The various ways described in § 668 *in which we can come to an understanding with our readers about the sense of our signs* (such *understandings* being, then, something quite different from the *determinations* and *definitions* discussed earlier) may well surprise many by their number; and it could hardly be anything other than obstinacy if one, in works in which one has to come to an understanding about concepts that are hard to grasp and still harder to define, instead of taking refuge in some of the means proposed here, wished to apply the words straight out, regardless whether and when the reader will know how to derive their meaning from the use itself. Only when we compare what the author has already contributed earlier (§§ 500–509) on *determinations* and (§§ 554–559) on *definitions* with the doctrine of *understandings* (§§ 668–670) now before us, and so do not overlook either the *note* belonging to the last § *on the complaint that someone puts into his definitions what he is supposed to prove*, and finally add as well the *examination of the views of others* supplied in § 698, will it become clear to us how great and how fraught with consequences is the difference, almost entirely overlooked down to this very day, between *determinations* of an object, *definitions* of a concept and *understandings* about a sign; and we shall begin to sense how many errors in the field of philosophy could be put an end to by this alone, that one made proper use of the latter two.

[p. 114]

We cannot refrain, before we leave this chapter, from singling out a few passages from it on account of their topical interest. What the author says in § 675 on the necessity of a *learned universal language* seems to us worthy of the most serious consideration: “Instead of introducing among themselves a *single learned language*, the scholars of Europe have resolved that each of them, besides two, or even more, so-called *ancient* languages, shall also learn the vernacular languages of all the rest, at least to the degree that he can understand at a pinch what is written in them. Gratifying as this is on the one hand, since it proves the most laudable thirst for knowledge and a mutual esteem, one may nevertheless ask whether the learning of so many languages, which we now expect of every scholar, does not detract from the learning of more important knowledge, and especially how this is to end when gradually all the remaining peoples as well (only in Europe), who speak their own languages, begin to write works worthy of general attention? Already now the number of languages whose knowledge we demand of every scholar amounts, after all, to 7 or 8; but if we go on in this way, a dozen will soon not suffice.” Indeed, that every human being (we say, *every* one; and thus do not wish to see even the members of the lowest class of the people excluded) should learn, besides his mother tongue, a second language (that *learned* one, namely), would surely be no unreasonable or hard-to-fulfill demand; and how many untold advantages it would afford, besides the fact that the scholars could use this language in composing all works that are meant to be read over the whole globe. But what are we to do if the number of peoples who write what is worth reading, as we surely must wish, doubles and triples?

In § 697 the author speaks of *the most common faults of written exposition in a textbook*, saying among other things: “I make the reproach of an *intentional obscurity* only to him who deliberately

[p. 115]

“arranges his words so that one cannot clearly gather what he means; and does this because he hopes to draw impermissible advantages for himself from such an indeterminacy of his expression; hence only to him who is obscure because he hopes that his readers will be good-natured enough to revere, in all that they do not rightly understand, profound truths, unfathomed by them for no other reason than their very profundity; or to whom the ambiguity of his expressions is to be of help, when we uncover the absurdity and the inner contradiction of his assertions, in replying with some semblance of right that we had misunderstood him. We are now, it seems to me, entitled to suspect such dishonest intentions in the obscure exposition of a writer when we become aware a) that he creates for himself a multitude of new expressions, or also takes already customary ones in new meanings, without in either case thinking of the duty first to come to an understanding with his readers about the sense of these signs; when he b) uses expressions that admit of a multiple meaning, without ever explaining which of them he means, although the context, too, leaves indeterminate the sense in which we are to take them; when he finally c), precisely where we should most expect a definite explanation, withdraws behind general figurative expressions.” — “Reluctantly do I say it” (it then says in the note), “but the duty of frank censure of what we recognize as a *pernicious abuse* obliges me not to conceal that I believe I find almost all the faults just cited united in the writings of some of the most celebrated recent philosophers, in particular in the writings of *Hegel*; indeed, what is still more distressing, that these peculiarities are not regarded as faults at all by a large part of the German public, but are admired as virtues. But so it is, alas! Writers as well as readers in Germany at present find in a style of writing that so envelops every thought in a cloud woven of obscure words that it shows through only by half, such an exclusive pleasure that books from the

[p. 116]

“field of philosophy whose authors are unwilling to pay homage to so *corrupt* a taste are almost in danger of remaining unread. What is clear and intelligible is for that very reason held in low esteem; one is ashamed to retell it; for, so one thinks, it does not sound learned. He must speak in riddles who wishes to arouse attention; and whoever understands how to veil his ignorance in a torrent of learned fashionable words so that the *commonest* thoughts, through the chiaroscuro of his expression, appear as profound wisdom, his name is celebrated. Germans! when will you at last return from an aberration which makes you only unpalatable and ridiculous to your neighbors?!”

In the *eighth chapter* now following (pp. 597–620), the author, hastening to the conclusion, describes for us the *conduct* one must adopt in composing a textbook in order to be able to give it precisely the attributes demanded in what precedes. Although here too, as above in the “*Art of Discovery*,” he brings forward nothing that has not long since been observed by good writers, still the express formulation of these precepts is likely to be all the less dispensable, the more generally it is necessary to call out audibly to us rules that we, from indolence or passion, do not like to observe. Certainly the very *first* truth set up here by B., “*that moral disposition is conducive even to the most successful treatment of every science*,” — we can never put before ourselves often enough. Very necessary, especially in our time, is also the inculcation of the duty *to acquaint oneself exactly with the achievements of one’s predecessors*. The same holds of the care that is to be devoted also to the *linguistic exposition* in a textbook. With regard, finally, to the twofold question: when one may put an end to the work, and when one may lay one’s book before the public? — (for B. remarks that these are two different things,

[p. 117]

and that one may sometimes quite properly do the latter without having yet done the former) — everyone will perhaps find that the author shows himself neither too strict nor too lenient. As the *most common faults* in the composition of a textbook, § 711 names *self-complacency*, *precipitancy*, *faulty imitation of famous predecessors*, *speaking to please* or *the homage paid to a certain party*, *vain striving for originality*, and finally the *inclination to fruitless speculations* or the *lack of practical interest*. “What can be more distressing for humanity,” the author exclaims at no. 4, “than when writers, not only those who are commissioned to provide merely for entertainment, but even those to whom humanity has entrusted *its holiest concerns, the cultivation of its sciences*, possess no honest love of truth, but out of a base craving to please, merely to win the applause of a certain party” (he might have added: in order to snatch a few thalers of money, a gold snuffbox, a little title, and the like) “delude themselves as well as their readers! And yet how small may not be, among the writers who cultivate the fields of *theology*, *politics*, *history*, the number of those who in no respect deserve such a reproach!”

Whoever, then, has read the work through to this point — surely not without frequent fatigue, which not only its great size but still more the abundance of what is new in it was bound to cause — will of his own accord probably also not leave unread the few pages of the *ninth chapter*: “*On Such Scientific Books as Are Not Textbooks Proper*” (pp. 621–636). The author speaks here 1) of *treatises*, among which he again distinguishes monographs, contributions, *repertories*, *polemical writings* and *reviews*; 2) of *auxiliary books*, which are either so arranged that they are to be used *before*, or *during*, or *after* an

[p. 118]

oral instruction; 3) of *handbooks*, which are meant to be suitable for occasional reference, such as the *dictionaries*; finally 4) of *scientific books of entertainment*, in which the purpose of entertainment predominates. What is now indicated about the arrangement of each of these particular kinds of writings is so compressed and brief that it must justly expect, if no other reproach, at least that of incompleteness.

[scan 120]

### II.

## Overview of the Science of Religion.

[scan 122]

When, some six and thirty years ago, the establishment of special professorial chairs under the name “*Chairs of the Science of Religion*” had been ordered in the Austrian Empire, the first man to be raised to such a chair in the philosophical faculty at Prague, B., believed he could infer from the mere *name* of the science he was to teach that his appointed task was *to impart instruction, couched in scientific form, in that religion* which he and those who had appointed him *regarded as the most perfect*. The name *science of religion* seemed to him — in no other sense than the one in which it designates the *science of the most perfect religion* — to be the name of a science that incontestably deserves that everyone who enjoys a higher education should devote to it a part of his time and of his powers. And that this latter is not exactly a very wrong view our readers will probably grant us even without our first referring them to the principles which the “*Theory of Science*” has laid down for judging whether the concept of a science is appropriate or not. For what object has a greater claim on our desire for knowledge than religion? and are we to content ourselves with knowledge of any religion other than the *most perfect*?

This, then, is the concept that the author also took as his basis in working out his “*Textbook*.” Since, however, he also gives definitions of his own of the concepts he connects with the expressions *science* and *most perfect religion*, we must, in order to be able to pass a complete judgment on the purpose he set himself in this book, also hear how these definitions run.

Of the word *science* the author says that he understands by it here “a collection of all the known, noteworthy assertions about a certain

[p. 122]

“object, which are so arranged that, for anyone who thinks them through in this arrangement, they produce not only conviction of their truth but also let him see the *ground* of this truth, whenever that is possible.” This definition proves to us, by its departure from what we read in the later-written Theory of Science, that the author did not stand still in his concepts; that in particular he later distinguished somewhat more exactly between *science in itself* and its *exposition in a textbook*; likewise that he later no longer demanded the *statement of the ground* (the demonstration of the objective connection) as generally as before for *every* exposition, but only for those expositions of a science that are intended for the more practiced, for the scholar. The view that alone exerted an influence on his manner of exposition — that it was incumbent upon him not to content himself with such proofs as are mere *certifications*, but that he must strive to demonstrate, as far as possible, the *objective ground* of every asserted truth; that he must, furthermore, raise every *concept* that he sets up to the greatest possible *distinctness* — this view B. possessed already very early, and his whole book is arranged in accordance with it and gives expression to this striving on every page. Whether the readers now wish to regard this as a virtue, that we must of course leave solely to their own discretion, and we confess without disguise that, in the opposite case, we do not hope that they will find any particular pleasure in B.’s Textbook of the Science of Religion.

About the *second* expression in his definition above, B. says that he calls that *religion* the *most perfect* which is the *most conducive* to our virtue and happiness, or at least so conducive that none other surpasses it. Now against this definition no one can well have anything to object who does not misunderstand it. For there is by no means any question of a merely temporal happiness, still less of a merely sensual one, but quite generally of everything that can make us happy in time and eternity; it is also, because it is not of happiness

[p. 123]

alone, but of *virtue* and *happiness* that we speak, presupposed that no kind of happiness is sought that is not compatible with the laws of virtue; finally, it is not meant that a religion could be conducive without being *true*; but just as little that we must always be in a position to recognize this truth of it without the intervention of a *divine testimony*.

Yet a new misgiving is aroused by the circumstance that B. defines the concept of *religion* too in a way of his own, and in a certain respect widens it, in another, however, at least apparently, narrows it. The former, in that he wants to have counted as belonging to religion not merely doctrines that concern God and our relations and duties toward God, but in general all those doctrines that have an influence on our virtue and happiness; the latter, in that he restricts the entire content of religion only to *moral* doctrines, i.e. (according to his own peculiar definition) only to such doctrines for or against whose acceptance some interest lying in the nature of man arises.

As to the *widening*, first of all, we may judge its appropriateness as we please; we may reject the grounds cited in § 21 from the first to the last; all this cannot — as is very easily seen — in the least entitle us to refuse to recognize as the most perfect that religion which the author demonstrates to us in his book to be the most perfect, or to find the proof conducted by him unconvincing. For if this religion meets the demands that the author makes on the *most perfect* even when more is counted in the collection of questions that such a religion has to answer for us than is commonly done otherwise: how much more securely must it pass this test if one sets his demands lower? —

It would be another matter if the author *excluded* from the domain of religion doctrines that have hitherto been reckoned to it. But this does not at all happen through his requirement that all

[p. 124]

doctrines of religion must have a *moral interest*. For he made this addition for no other purpose than to bring to distinct consciousness a component in the concept of religion that we have always, in general, connected with this word. For undeniably there is a whole host of truths which none of us counts as belonging to religion, although they are of the greatest importance now for our happiness, now even for our virtue, and moreover stand in the most intimate connection with our concepts of God and our relations and duties toward him. How important it is, e.g., to know that the onset of putrefaction is an almost never deceptive mark of death; or that frequent consumption of brandy or opium ruins a human being in body and soul, etc. How important, for one who is not yet firmly convinced of God’s goodness, is the question whether one may regard the many kinds of arrangements in nature that serve the welfare of the living as the work of a freely acting cause or not; whether there can also be infinite series of causes? and much else besides. All these doctrines and questions, B. now holds, are counted by no one as belonging to religion solely because, for all their importance, no decided interest for or against them arises in every person’s breast. Whether he errs in this or not: this much at any rate is certain, and is shown most clearly by the whole content of the book (especially by its last part), that the author did not let himself be misled by this peculiar view into banishing from the domain of religion even a single doctrine that is counted as belonging to religion by well-informed persons, by theologians themselves.

Without concern, then, that the peculiarities which the author has brought to light in the definitions heard so far will make his work unpalatable to us, we can hear what further he has to tell us. We already know that it is *Christianity*, and indeed Christianity *according to the Catholic way of understanding it*, that he declares to be the most perfect religion; and the proof of this highly important assertion he bases on this, that this

[p. 125]

religion has been *revealed* to us by God himself or (what he holds to be equivalent) *confirmed*.

Here it is necessary above all to inquire: in what sense does the author take the expression that a religion is *revealed* or *confirmed* by God? He enumerates (vol. I, §§ 23–28) four meanings in which the word *revealing* is taken; but declares that he wishes to hold only to the last, that is, the one in which it is equivalent to *confirming* or *bearing witness*. In this meaning, he says, a doctrine is called *revealed* by God as soon as we can infer from certain sensible signs that it is God’s will that we believe it, because he himself recognizes it as true. Now does this definition really already exhaust everything that people commonly imagine when they call a doctrine *revealed* by God or — (for they believe they may interchange it with this expression) — *inspired* in its first proclaimer? This question we cannot in fact avoid *answering in the negative*; for besides what B. already requires — that God has made known to us, by definite sensible signs, his will that we accept such a doctrine in faith — people mostly imagine that God has also already produced the *propositions themselves* of which this doctrine consists in a *supernatural manner*, by his *immediate action* in the mind of its first proclaimer. But that is just what our author does not at all declare necessary; in that he maintains that a doctrine can lay claim to the name of a *divinely revealed* doctrine, indeed even to that of a doctrine *inspired* in human beings, even if it cannot be proved that any *supernatural* or *immediate* activity of God has taken place at its origin in the mind of its first proclaimer — (such a one there is not even always, according to his views) — or on any other occasion. He does not venture, even with regard to Christianity, to assert and to want to demonstrate that even a few of its doctrines originated in so supernatural a manner; but he is

[p. 126]

endeavoring, in his proof of the divinity of this religion, to show us that we can leave that circumstance entirely undecided and yet be most firmly convinced that God has made known by signs that he wishes to see the Christian doctrine accepted by us as one attested by himself. — It now depends on whether everything that B. gives us to consider on this subject in several places (chiefly vol. I, §§ 34, 169–176) suffices to bring us to the insight that, and how, we in fact lose not the slightest thing if Christianity is only a revelation in the sense he has established, namely only a doctrine whose truth God has *confirmed* to us, it being left undecided by what means the first idea of each individual doctrine of it was brought about among us human beings: or whether we continue to insist that the supernatural origin of at least some Christian doctrines be demonstrated to us, because it appears to us at the very least as a diminution of the value and importance of Christianity if room is left for the thought that God, in introducing it, perhaps employed throughout nothing but natural forces and instruments. Only in the first case can the proof contained in B.’s Textbook afford us complete satisfaction: for whoever finds himself in the second case, that proof does not accomplish all that he believes he must demand for his reassurance and for the honor of Christianity. We deem it our duty to say this in advance with all candor: but with the same candor may we also be permitted to confess that we do not quite understand how anyone who has thought through the sections named can still remain attached to the opinion *that an immediate or supernatural activity of God is necessary for a revelation and, should it nevertheless have really taken place anywhere, is recognizable*. For — to speak only of the latter, upon which the former lapses of itself — the consideration of the single circumstance that we do not know all the forces of nature suffices to see that we are not entitled to assert of any perceived phenomenon

[p. 127]

however extraordinary it might be, that *it can have been mediated by no force of nature*. — As to the higher *importance* that a religion acquires in our eyes as soon as we imagine that it was introduced by an immediate or supernatural activity of God: here we indeed neither will nor can deny that it is entirely correct as regards the influence which this idea exerts on our sensibility; and we find precisely in this circumstance the reason why Christianity itself, in several of its doctrines, speaks of supernatural or immediate workings of God, as notably in the case of the sacraments. We justify such doctrines as *figurative* ones by the remark that we can never imagine those graces and benefactions of God which Christianity wants us to regard as *supernatural* too great, too important; can never display too much zeal in order to become partakers of them, and can never apply too much care in order to preserve them once they have fallen to our share. From this simple remark, whose correctness must be evident to everyone, it follows that we do quite right to make use of that idea of Christianity as an *image*, i.e. only for the purpose of producing in ourselves those feelings and resolutions that are in fact appropriate to the nature of the matter; but by no means does there follow from this the necessity of finding in this image a concept of the matter as it is in itself, and of being obliged to defend its objective truth. Rather, anyone who is willing to reflect even to some degree on this will see, with complete clarity, through his own reason, that the *true value* of a gift never depends on the means by which it is brought about by God, but solely on its inner nature; that we should therefore, e.g., certainly have no reason to esteem certain doctrines of Christianity the less because it would not be impossible that God, to whom the whole of nature is at command, made use of merely natural means to give these doctrines their origin.

[p. 128]

Moreover, we must remark that B. not only does not deny the existence of not merely some but *infinitely many immediate actions of God* upon created beings, but quite expressly asserts it, and proves it with very cogent reasons. (Vol. III, § 144.) Hereby a remedy is at hand even for one who cannot tear himself loose from the view that the value of Christianity, and the importance of the means of grace offered us in it, would after all be lost if it were not proved that an immediate activity of God took place already at the introduction of the former, and still continues to take place in the dispensation of the latter. For what B. merely does *not assert* in his proof of the truth of Christianity — because man’s reason left to itself has, in his view, no sure mark by which to distinguish immediate actions of God from mediate ones in a particular case — that he does not deny; and this we can, now that the proof delivered in the Textbook has first made it clear to us that God has confirmed the truth of Christianity, only now accept as a *doctrine* and *truth revealed* in this Christianity itself (and in our eyes no longer to be interpreted merely figuratively). Surely, if it is not a mere question of curiosity for us human beings in what manner God was active in the introduction of Christianity, and in what manner he still continues to work daily in the use of the holy sacraments; if man really has a right to put this question to God: then there is evidently no way of proving more securely that that activity was an immediate and supernatural one than if one first of all — without arrogating to reason left to itself any judgment on this — demonstrates, following B.’s guidance, that in Christianity one in any case possesses a doctrine whose truth God has confirmed, whereupon one then, and only then, fetches the affirmative answer to that question from God’s own infallible mouth.

And so we should now have seen that even persons who want Christianity to be conceived as a supernatural and immediate

[p. 129]

institution of God have not the slightest reason to reject in advance the proof conducted in B.’s Textbook as one that could be of no use to them, as soon as they merely restrict his assertion of the figurative nature of some doctrines of Christianity more narrowly than B. does. Let us, then, examine the nature of this proof with all diligence, but only after we have first heard in addition how the author wants to draw, from the fact that Christianity, especially according to the Catholic way of understanding it, can be regarded as a *revelation* given us by God, the conclusion that it is the *most perfect religion* for us.

From the mere fact that God has revealed something to us there follows, without doubt, only that it is true and deserves to be accepted and followed by us. But who would be entitled to demand that God, whenever he sees fit to reveal himself to an individual, or even to a whole society of several persons, should always choose a *complete religious doctrinal system* as the content of this revelation? Would it not have to be enough for us if God should wish, now and then, to acquaint us even with a single correct truth? But we have all the more reason, to be sure, to thank God’s all-governing care for our mortal race, when we find almost not a single people on earth that is not in possession of a certain, more or less completely developed, religious doctrinal system, concerning whose truth it believes it has received a *confirmation from God himself*. Let us now investigate whether any one of these religious doctrinal systems can be regarded also *for us* as one confirmed by God; and let us hold (like B.) that to this there belongs, as a mark, *that this doctrinal system surpass every other*, which we had to abandon for its sake, *in moral utility*; and if it is found, finally (as is likewise the case here), that the religious doctrinal system which we declare to be the one revealed to us sets up, among other things, also the proposition *that every religious truth attainable by our mere reason is, inclusively, also*

[p. 130]

*its own*: how should we then not be entitled to say that this doctrinal system is the most perfect there is for us at all? Where, then, were we to hope to obtain doctrines possessing a higher moral perfection, if everything that can be seen by mere reason already belongs to this religion anyway; and if, with regard to such doctrines as can be accepted only on God’s testimony, no other religion has anything to show that would be morally more conducive?

The proof, now, that is conducted in the Textbook for the divinity of Christianity, and indeed in its Catholic conception, rests solely on the two following propositions:

1) That we may and must regard every doctrine as one confirmed by God if we perceive two marks in it: if we a) believe, after the most conscientious examination, that we recognize it as *morally conducive* to us, and moreover b) find *extraordinary events* connected with it for whose existence we could see no use were they not to serve us precisely as signs of the divine confirmation of those doctrines.

2) That the Christian, and in particular the Catholic, doctrinal system does in fact bear in itself these two marks of a divine revelation.

The *first* or *general proposition* the author proves in the *fourth chapter* of the first volume (§§ 135–177), which bears the heading “*Possibility and Marks of a Revelation*,” in a twofold manner: first by appeal to the *pronouncements of common human understanding*, then by an attempted *exposition of its inner grounds*. Suppose someone were unable to follow him in this latter proof; the first could nonetheless already suffice him, although we do not wish to conceal that for such a purpose, namely in order to satisfy also persons who are still very unpracticed

[p. 131]

in thinking for themselves, a somewhat fuller exposition of what is only hinted at there would be desirable.

What B. calls *pronouncements of common human understanding*, and under what conditions he ascribes to them so high a degree of reliability, the readers already know from the “*Theory of Science*.” Here, now, he shows (§ 141) (yet precisely this is what should have been done far more fully) that all human beings, learned and unlearned, however different their religious concepts otherwise were, had always tacitly revealed by their conduct that they felt compelled to recognize every doctrine as one confirmed by God as soon as they had nothing to object against its moral utility (*holiness*), and moreover could not deny that certain extraordinary events had contributed to its origin, preservation or spread. That this really is so; that nothing more has ever been demanded; that even those scholars who spoke of the necessity of supernatural, indeed immediate, workings of God for the confirmation of a revelation nevertheless in the end contented themselves with events that merely had a high degree of extraordinariness; that, however, even the most extraordinary deeds that anyone performed were not sufficient to procure him the trust of reasonable people if his doctrine was an unholy one; that in such a case, if one did not know how else to explain those events, one preferred to assert that they were the Devil’s delusions rather than to admit that it is God who wishes to confirm to us the truth of such a doctrine through these extraordinary phenomena; that one, more or less distinctly conscious of the rule by which one proceeds, also judged, in the everyday events of life, whether one had to recognize in them an encouraging or a deterring *hint from God* or not: all this consists of facts that could be rigorously proved. But what must be evident without first needing many proofs is whether the peculiar judgment of common human understanding, which expresses itself in these facts, is entirely in accordance with the conditions

[p. 132]

that must obtain in such judgments if they are to be reliable. It evidently concerns a matter that must be decidable by mere *reason*, combined at most with certain experiences available to each of us; and this judgment is in fact so worded that it does not flatter our sensibility, but rather restricts it. “For since human beings” (these are B.’s own words) “admit that a doctrine may be regarded as a true divine revelation only if its acceptance is morally conducive, they thereby cut off from themselves the opportunity ever to do anything immoral on the pretext that it has been permitted by a divine revelation; on the contrary, they impose on themselves the necessity of recognizing and following, as a command issued to them by God himself, so many things against which their sensibility rebels.”

Yet this *first proof*, which the author furnishes for his theory of the marks of a revelation, he adduces here only in passing: in a scientific work he wanted to, and had to, demonstrate the truth of so important a doctrine *from its objective ground*, and this happens in the *second proof*, to the analysis of which we now turn.

In declaring a doctrine revealed, we assert that we have inferred from certain sensible signs that God wishes this doctrine to be believed by us. We thus permit ourselves to infer a *purpose* which God has in certain events in the world perceived by us (those *signs*). The doctrine of the marks of a divine revelation therefore rests essentially on the more general doctrine of the manner in which we recognize, from given events or arrangements in the world, the *divine purpose* that underlies them. With this, then, B. makes a beginning in § 143. In the “*Theory of Science*” (vol. III, §§ 386 and 379) the still far more general questions, how *purposes* can be judged from given *actions*, and *causes* in general from given *effects*, are discussed; to which, therefore, we

[p. 133]

refer those readers who wish to see this doctrine grounded still more deeply.

Here is a free, perhaps also somewhat improved, exposition of his doctrine on this point, as it suffices for our present purpose. Every thing in the world, indeed every single event in it, comes about only through God’s nearer or more remote activity or permission, and in each he aims at something that he also in fact attains; because with God there are no purposes — at least none that may be so called in the strictest sense of the word — which he would not attain precisely because he has them. This purpose, now, consists in each thing or event in nothing other than *that effect* which, among all those that it can produce — it goes without saying, through the addition of various possible circumstances and relations — is the *most conducive of all* to the welfare of created beings. So much, we believe, needs no further proof. With precisely the same degree of confidence, then, with which we can judge that a certain effect is the most conducive that an object in question could produce, and that the circumstances necessary to it all lie within the realm of possibility: with precisely the same degree of confidence we may judge that this effect is also the *actual purpose of the object*, and for that very reason expect that the circumstances still lacking will promptly present themselves, whereupon that effect, God’s intended purpose, will in fact occur. We see, e.g., someone succumb to a temptation, and conjecture, according to this rule, that God permitted this person’s fall only so that he, learning to know his weakness, may become more attentive to himself in the future; etc. In the particular case where *we can contribute through our own activity* to bringing it about that those circumstances which are required for the production of an effect conjectured by us to be God’s purpose all come together; in this particular case it becomes, as soon as the matter still presents itself to us as probable even after the most mature reflection, our duty, and not a merely probable but a *certain duty*, to do everything

[p. 134]

that we in any way deem serviceable for bringing about that effect. If that effect then really comes about, the outcome itself teaches us that it was intended by God. But if we do not attain what we were striving for in the good belief that God wills it: then we have neither reason to reproach ourselves for our striving, nor even reason to suppose that it was fruitless or contrary to God’s intentions; rather we may give ourselves over to a twofold consolation: first, that what God’s wisdom has brought about in place of the outcome we were pursuing is without doubt something much better; and second, that our seemingly vain efforts, as surely as they were well-meant, nevertheless had God’s good pleasure too, and are to be blessed with beneficial consequences.

So much in general. Let us now apply this to an individual doctrine before us, or also to a collection of several.

1. So long as we perceive in a doctrine no *moral utility* for us, i.e. cannot hope that by accepting it we should become morally more perfect: so long we certainly cannot answer the question, for what purpose God lets us become acquainted with it, by saying “that we are to accept it in faith.” For even in the case that we shall presently come to know as the most favorable, namely if we owe our acquaintance with this doctrine (its origin or the direction of our attention to it) to a confluence of the most unusual events, of which we are so utterly unable to say what they are meant to serve, if not to determine us to accept it in faith: even in this case we are not entitled to such a conclusion. For the only ground that would speak for such a conclusion is that the doctrine in question has a moral use which we are unable to see. But who does not see that this ground is perfectly outweighed by the opposite possibility that the events too which have acquainted us

[p. 135]

with that doctrine may have yet another use in the world, unknown to us?

2. But just as the extraordinary manner of a doctrine’s origin by itself, so too its *moral utility alone and as such* can furnish as yet no ground for declaring it revealed. What we rightly infer, whenever we meet with a doctrine morally conducive to us, is that it is at once incumbent upon us to investigate whether there are not grounds that demonstrate its truth. But if the doctrine is to be called revealed to us by God, these grounds must lie, at least in part, in the circumstance that we gather from some *event* that God demands of us the believing acceptance of that doctrine because he himself recognizes it as true. An event to which we should really feel compelled to ascribe, on God’s part, so *entirely special a purpose* must be such that, if this purpose were not to hold, we could conjecture no other. It must therefore be some *extraordinary* event, never yet perceived; for events that follow ordinary laws allow us to conjecture also a purpose, however unknown, yet only an ordinary one. If the event has served to bring the doctrine to light, or at least to our knowledge: then we give a satisfactory explanation of what it was there for if we say that *it was meant to be a sign to us that God attests the truth of this doctrine*. In the contrary case it remains unexplained to us.

3. It is true, to be sure, that we could have erred in judging that moral utility, easy as it is to judge: but since, on the one hand, there is, apart from these two circumstances, no other from which we could infer that God has spoken to us; and since, on the other hand, we also have no sufficient ground for the assertion that God never wishes to speak to us: we are entitled to presuppose with all confidence that he will never permit us to err to our own moral detriment, whenever we, proceeding with all caution in the judgment of the moral

[p. 136]

use of that doctrine, assume that God has spoken only after we have tried in vain to explain in some other way the purpose of the extraordinary events through which we have become acquainted with the doctrine. *As certainly, then, as our consciousness attests to us that we have inquired with honest intent, so certainly can we be sure that in a doctrine that has presented itself to us in such a manner we possess a true divine revelation.* —

This, in a few words, is B.’s theory, to which, we believe, it is scarcely possible for an unprejudiced person to refuse his approval. To be sure, the author on this occasion sets forth much else besides; thus in § 145, e.g., an exact definition is given of the concept of *moral utility*, though that concept is surely clear enough in itself, and in giving it he ascends to the concept of the supreme moral law; thus in § 147 the concept of the *extraordinary*, which is sufficiently known to everyone, is resolved into its components; thus in § 149 it is set out *how* and *by what means* it actually comes about that extraordinary events are so especially suited to the confirmation of a divine revelation; and in § 147 in what connection they must stand with the doctrine to be confirmed: in all these investigations errors might have crept in, or the readers might at least think they perceive such errors here and feel unconvinced of the correctness of his assertions on these points, without the proof of the main proposition, namely of the theorem stated above concerning the marks of a revelation, having to lose anything of its probative force on that account. For what is *morally conducive*, what *extraordinary*, in what *connection* an extraordinary event must stand with a doctrine if it is to be said *that it serves for its confirmation*: all this everyone knows, even without being conscious of the components out of which he composes these concepts with such distinctness that he could give an account of them. Our author, however, takes these expressions and phrases not in some new, but in their ordinary

[p. 137]

meaning, sufficiently fixed by previous linguistic usage. Hence, for judging the correctness or incorrectness of his assertions about the marks of a revelation, there is by no means any need first to enter into these determinations of concepts, which he finds necessary only in order to give instruction that could lay claim to the name of a *philosophical* one.

But what is really at issue here is the question *how many and which doctrines of natural religion must be presupposed by us as certain and indubitable in order to find the author’s*, i.e. essentially only the above-presented, *inferences compelling*? Here it is not to be denied that, just as in our exposition, so too in B.’s presentation of his proof on this point the existence of God, as of a being which is the ground of all others and possesses omnipotence, omniscience and most perfect holiness, has been presupposed. Nevertheless, if we go into an exact analysis of the inferences employed here, if in this respect we want only to repeat them once more in our thoughts, we shall find that B. remarked, not without justice, in vol. III, § 40, that it is not absolutely necessary to have acknowledged the existence of such a God with unshakable certainty in order to find the above doctrine of the marks of a revelation correct merely to the extent that suffices to see, guided by just these marks, a true divine revelation in Christianity or perhaps also in some other religion. We shall see that it can quite properly suffice for this purpose if someone has merely got so far that he finds the existence of a being such as we conceive God to be *not impossible*, i.e. (according to the definitions which our readers already know from the Theory of Science) merely does not find it *in contradiction* with any known truth. If such a being is for us merely *possible* (possible in the *problematic* sense of this word, as the author calls it), then the existence of a

[p. 138]

religion such as the Christian can itself serve us as a proof that that being actually exists, and that it has made known to us, in the most evident manner, precisely through the extraordinary events to which this religion owes its origin, preservation and spread, its existence and its care for our race. The matter stands, namely, much as when we infer the presence of some human beings or other rational beings in a place where we perceive traces of purposeful activity. Does such an inference require anything more than that we find beings of the kind we wish to assume for the explanation of what is perceived merely not *impossible* in this place, and that without presupposing them we do not know how to explain those phenomena?

But we must still call attention to one circumstance. Out of enmity toward Christianity, as toward every other religion that gives itself out as revealed; but partly also owing to those false concepts that people had commonly formed of the nature of the activity of God necessary for a revelation, in that they supposed that some immediate, or at least supernatural, action of God belongs to it, quite a few scholars have brought forward a multitude of more or less plausible *objections to the possibility of a revelation*, even when the existence of God himself is not called into doubt. Now if these objections, or at least some of them, seemed well-founded to us, if we doubted the *possibility* of a revelation, then we could not believe in its actuality either; hence an express mention of this possibility was also made in our exposition above. It was therefore very necessary that those objections too be taken note of and removed by the author. With this he occupies himself in the remaining part of this chapter (§§ 152–163). However many objections he may adduce here, it is always possible that others occur to the reader, or that he comes across, somewhere, others not answered here. We hope, however, that whoever merely

[p. 139]

has duly thought through the objections discussed here (in any case the most deceptive that have been put forward so far), together with everything that is said for their refutation, will also readily know how to answer for himself every other objection. For all these objections have arisen only from incorrect concepts of what a revelation is, or has to accomplish, and vanish of themselves as soon as one has become acquainted with the more correct concepts that the author already set up on this matter in what precedes. What has been put forward with a learned air about *categories* and *laws of thought* that a revelation of supersensible objects would have to contradict; about the *law of continuity* that it would violate; about the *incapacity* of human *language* to express its doctrines: all this can truly confuse only people who are still very unpracticed in thinking. That God *cannot act upon our senses* (namely immediately); that the first proclaimer of a revelation would have to be *infallible*; that *miracles* (immediate workings of God) have *no recognizability* for us, and also cannot be *historically authenticated*, and many others are objections that cannot find a place at all in B.’s theory; which should just show that his doctrine deserves a more careful examination on the part of those competent in the matter. These, however, would have to direct their attention already, too, to the author’s sharper determination of the concept of *possibility* (§§ 136–138) and to the assertion that only a *problematic* possibility of a revelation needs to be proved, and that the *perfect possibility* of a thing whose realization depends only on God himself already has its actuality as a consequence.

A special kind of objection to the possibility of a higher revelation is, as is well known, drawn from the claim *that it would not be necessary, not even conducive, to the human race*. With the removal of these objections and the proof of the contrary the author was already occupied in the preceding, namely the *third* chapter (§§ 95–134), to which he gave the heading:

[p. 140]

“*On the Necessity of a Revelation*.” It might seem a violation of good order that this chapter should precede the one on the *possibility* and the marks of a revelation; for if the necessity of a thing has already been proved, what need is there of many proofs for its possibility? One would be right to say so if B. understood by necessity here what is so called in the strictest sense; but he tells us expressly in § 97 that by the necessity of a revelation he understands nothing other than a high *desirability* of it, and even this he wishes to assert and prove here only with the restrictive addition: “*so far as we human beings are able to judge the matter*”; by which it is meant to be signified that God may well have reasons for the sake of which — without being unjust or cruel, or even merely unkind — he withholds the gift of a divine revelation from individual human beings, or also from whole peoples and ages. A necessity understood in this way can now indeed be demonstrated easily, before one has yet placed its possibility beyond doubt.

The author proceeds in the following way: he sets forth

1) how necessary a higher revelation is even for the *most cultivated* of our race, by enumerating what obscurities natural religion has even in its most perfect form; he then shows

2) how much greater still is the need of a revelation *for the human race as a whole*; which he again demonstrates in a twofold manner:

a) from the history of the *aberrations* into which not only the great mass of human beings but the learned too fell at all times;

b) from *the nature of the matter*; in that he sets out the difficulties that would stand in the way of a general diffusion of natural religion in some degree of purity and completeness, if no revelation came to its aid.

[p. 141]

3) Finally follows an *examination of the principal objections* that have been raised against the necessity of a revelation.

If now the present writer believes that the author’s procedure in this chapter, or elsewhere, offers something worthy of approval, indeed of imitation: then it is (to say this here once and for all) always only the train of thought, the plan, never the actual execution, which is always too brief and incomplete, and in the historical parts especially shows little labor expended on it.

Since, if there were no revelation, we should understandably have to hold only to *natural religion* — (so the author calls precisely that religion whose doctrines can be known *without* a divine testimony) — would this inquiry then be concerned above all with the question which exposition of natural religion must be taken as the basis here, since different persons also determine its content differently? This much is evident: only if we prove that natural religion itself in its most perfect form (which it perhaps attained only with the help of many a divine revelation) is still deficient enough to leave the wish for a higher instruction, shall we demonstrate the necessity of the latter as evidently as is possible in itself. But where, now, is this most perfect form of natural religion to be found? Could the author have been so vain as to believe that the collection of just those religious theorems which appear to *him*, according to his own philosophical system, as the only correct ones and as demonstrable by our mere reason, forms the content of natural religion in its most perfect form? He thought nothing of the kind, but held that even the most learned man, however many new views, hidden from the rest of humanity, he might have arrived at through his own reflection, can hardly give them unconditional trust; that he can rather regard as an established truth only *that* about which others too, indeed

[p. 142]

all people agree with him; that he will therefore be inclined to count, among the content of natural religion in its most perfect form, at least among its *certain* content, nothing more than what one could call, according to §§ 20 and 64, the *natural religion of the human race in general*. It is only this, then, that the author has taken as a basis in his investigations in this chapter and also everywhere else, in particular in what follows, in the assessment of the individual doctrines of Christianity. Can we complain that he presupposes too much when he does this? Who could proceed more safely than he who assumes nothing other than that on which all people on earth are agreed? In fact, however, it would matter little even if we could not agree with some assertion that the author sets up in this chapter: it is enough if, through all that is said partly here, partly only in what follows, in the investigation of the moral utility of the Christian, in particular of the Catholic-Christian doctrinal system, we arrive at the conviction that a religion which adds such additions to the natural one would, through its more general diffusion among human beings, exert a predominantly beneficial influence on the improvement of their morals and on their well-being; indeed it is already enough if, after a conscientious examination of the influence which the acceptance of this religion promises for our *own morality*, we have every reason to hope that it will prove conducive *for us*.

Meanwhile we nevertheless still wish to relate in a few words which deficiencies it chiefly is that the author perceives in natural religion in its most perfect form, for whose sake he finds a higher revelation desirable even for the wisest. Even the *doctrine of God*, he holds, is enveloped in many obscurities in natural religion, since it is so difficult for our understanding to conceive of an infinite being. — Our *immortality*, the endless continued existence of our being with consciousness and recollection, cannot, according to him, even be declared to be

[p. 143]

a *certain* theorem of the natural religion of the human race, since it was, after all, so often doubted, and still is, and since the great multitude of human beings who assumed a continued existence assumed it, properly speaking, not as a truth demonstrable through *reason* but as a *revealed* truth. — Still less does human reason feel itself able, of itself, to decide the important *question of the forgiveness of sins*; which the author shows partly from the highly inadequate means of atonement and satisfaction found among all peoples, partly from the dispute of the philosophers over this subject. — No less great does B. find our natural incapacity in explaining the *origin* as well as the *purpose* of the manifold *evils* that we meet with in the world; and this incapacity he proves partly from the aberrations into which human beings fell through the endeavor to explain these evils, partly from the deficiencies that even the most successful theodicies have. — In its *ethics*, the author says further, natural religion is comparatively still the most perfect; yet here too a revelation could render important services, in that it could determine *disputed* or very *difficult* duties more definitely and present them as divine commandments. Especially poor, however, is (in the author’s opinion) natural religion in its *ascetics* or *doctrine of the means of virtue*, e.g. for instilling fresh courage in the habitual sinner who has almost despaired already of the possibility of his improvement, etc. Can one now really point to anything exaggerated in any of these assertions? — But let us hear briefly, too, how the author, at the close of this chapter (§ 123), answers the question, “*whether the need of a revelation is greater for the educated or for the uneducated, and by whom it is more keenly felt?*” The *uneducated* man, he says, suffers greater *harm* if no revelation falls to his lot; but the *benefit* that the *educated* man is able to draw from the possession of a revelation is greater. Nevertheless the *feeling* of that harm in the uneducated man and

[p. 144]

his *wish* for a revelation are *weaker*; but the *educated* man will in fact feel his need, which is in itself smaller, *more strongly*. — Can anyone contradict this?

Our readers now know approximately the most important of what the author advances for his theory of the possibility and the marks of a revelation; to that extent, are they also already in a position to judge what will have been gained with it, if it stands the test? We shall then, when it is to be investigated whether a religion has been divinely revealed, no longer ask for *supernatural* events, or even for miracles that could only have come about through God’s *immediate* activity, but it will suffice us as an *external mark* if this religion owes its origin, preservation or spread to a concurrence of several unusual events. For this very reason we shall have no need at all to pass a definite judgment on the *actual course* of these events, provided it is only evident that something unusual must have happened, whether the outcome has been brought about in this way or in some other; we shall never regard it as an objection to be raised against the divinity of a doctrine that human *errors* and *passions* too have contributed something to its origin or spread; but neither shall we ever content ourselves with a mere proof of the extraordinary, or if one will, even supernatural origin of a doctrine, however convincingly it may be established, but shall demand that proof also be given us, in addition, of the *moral utility* of this doctrine, and hence also of its *conformity to reason*; if there are *several* religious doctrinal systems that arose, were preserved or were spread through very unusual events, we shall not want to decide the question which of these doctrinal systems may and must be regarded as revealed *to us* by historical, or even linguistic, investigations, but shall grasp that this depends solely on the moral utility of these doctrinal systems, hence on a

[p. 145]

purely *psychological*, or, more precisely still, on a *moral* discussion; finally, we shall also no longer fear that, from the imperfection of our natural-scientific, historical or even philosophical knowledge, some error has crept into our religious convictions; and the necessity of an alteration of them we shall acknowledge only in the case — but then also gladly — that we are shown that a doctrine which we have hitherto rejected as contrary to reason very well admits also of a rational understanding and a moral use. — Should some of these hastily stated conclusions not be immediately evident to our readers, we ask them only to go through what has been said again, or if necessary to consult the further exposition of the book itself; and we hope that they too will come over to views which, without having yet stated them distinctly, people already have an inkling of everywhere in our time.

Since it was chiefly only the *fourth* and *third* chapters of the first volume with whose content we have so far made our readers acquainted, we must, before we go further, nevertheless report to them also what occurs in the two earlier chapters. The *first* chapter bears the heading: “*On the Concept of Religion, Its Different Kinds, and the Conduct toward It that Accords with Duty*” (§§ 9–59), and contains, besides what we have already taken from it, only a single further definition that can be of some influence on what follows; it is the definition of the concept of a *society religion* (§ 22), which is not without importance for this reason alone, that B. holds the Catholic religion to be such a society religion, and therefore also determines solely by that definition what one has to count, or not to count, as part of its content. He says, however, that, keeping to linguistic usage and to the rule followed in similar word formations, one may properly understand by the *religion of a certain society* nothing other than a collection of all those religious doctrines to which all, or at least *almost all*

[p. 146]

members of this society, for whom those doctrines have intelligibility and religious importance, profess adherence. We do not see, in fact, how one could contradict him in this? For how necessary the restrictive addition is — that with any doctrine one has to heed only the judgment of those for whom it has intelligibility and religious importance — must become evident to everyone as soon as he recalls that in every religious society there are also children and persons who stand at a still very low stage of development, and whom one for that very reason reasonably spares instruction for which they have as yet no need.

What else, now, occurs in this chapter — namely everything that is adduced in §§ 37–59 *on the dutiful conduct* that every one of us *has to observe, partly toward our own, partly toward the religious convictions of our fellow human beings*; likewise on the commonest offenses of which we let ourselves be guilty in this respect: all this stands there without any connection with what follows, and without any application to it. Nevertheless there is to be found precisely in this division of the book, and indeed in § 38, an utterance that, however innocent the intention in which the author communicates it, has nonetheless done unspeakable harm to him, or rather to his system, because it gave occasion to cast suspicion on his character and to assert that in his whole system he was nowhere concerned with *truth*, but instead only with *utility*, or rather with something still worse! It is the utterance that we, when once preponderant grounds for an opinion are at hand, and when we moreover clearly recognize that our acceptance of it, even in the case that it should nevertheless be erroneous, would bring no disadvantages at all, — do well to withdraw the attention of our mind from the doubts and objections opposed to it (which could still never cancel its preponderant probability). We do not wish to say a single syllable in defense of this precept; let it be as false, and

[p. 147]

as reprehensible as one will; let it deserve in the fullest sense of the word to be called *a spawn of hell* (as *Krug* has called it): only this much do we declare here once more, *not the slightest application of this rule does the author make in his ensuing proof for Catholicism.* And this much must be evident to everyone, too, who has only his eyes open: in such a proof there is not even the remotest *opportunity* for making use of that rule. For not while one is still investigating the question whether Catholicism is also morally beneficial and can be regarded as confirmed by God, but at the earliest only after the *conclusion* of our proof could we, if it should perhaps leave behind some questions that we do not know how to answer, but of which we nevertheless recognize with all clarity that their answer could in no case overturn it, make of the said rule this application: that we, no longer lingering over the discussion of those idle questions, direct our attention to other useful objects.

More important is the *second* chapter, which supplies, in §§ 64–95, a “*brief outline of natural religion*.” There are set forth here, however, not merely those doctrines which B., because they are accepted unanimously by all, or at least almost all, human beings who have reflected on the matter, counts among the *settled truths of the natural religion of the human race*, but he also communicates here, as occasion arises, several views that he can give out only *as his own*. Of the latter as well as of the former he endeavors, in order that his exposition may deserve the name of a *scientific* one, to indicate briefly the *objective grounds* on which they may perhaps rest. Although, now, as we have already said, in his proof for Christianity he presupposes nothing but such doctrines of natural religion as can be counted among its settled truths: there is nevertheless found

[p. 148]

also among what he sets forth here merely as his subjective view a good deal that we, once we were convinced of its correctness, could very well use to ground our proof for Christianity still more deeply, or that otherwise admits of some other application. We shall lay some of it before the reader.

What is said in the *Insertion* (§§ 60–63) about *the critical philosophy* and about *the newest manner of philosophizing in Germany in general* has only the purpose of justifying the author to some extent for having attached himself neither to the one nor to the other, but having followed a system of his own. This we can the more readily pass over here, since our readers already know the most important part from the overview we have given them of the “*Theory of Science*.”

Of *God* the author gives in § 66 the definition that he is the *unconditionally actual*; a definition from which it is at once evident that his existence cannot be *objectively grounded*, but that a *certification* of this existence may and must well be attempted. However little the proof supplied in § 67 leaves to be desired in this respect, it seems nevertheless not to have satisfied the author, since in the later-written “*Athanasia*” he attempted a still more detailed demonstration (in the first edition pp. 292 ff., in the second pp. 321 ff.). What is remarkable in both proofs is that the author does not, as is commonly done, presuppose that a series of causes or conditions cannot go on to infinity, but rather (§ 68) demonstrates the erroneousness of this presupposition itself. — Peculiar, too, is his concept of the *all-perfection* of God, which he defines in § 74 to the effect that God unites all powers that are possible side by side with one another, and each of them in that highest degree in which it is possible alongside the others. By this definition a multitude of difficulties is removed which one otherwise found in the doctrine of God’s attributes, in that one believed the one of them to be in conflict with the other, e.g. goodness with justice and the like.

[p. 149]

What, moreover, was advanced by *Schelling* and *Hegel* against the doctrine of the divine attributes rests on such unclear concepts (rectified in the *Theory of Science*), and contradicts itself so obviously, that it needed no special refutation at all. — Among the “*corollaries*” that the author draws in § 81 from God’s attributes one reads that there must at all times have been, and be, an infinite multitude of creatures capable of happiness; that none of them can serve as a mere means for the rest; that, on the other hand, one cannot demand either that God should have originally equipped all with equal powers, or that he should treat those created alike in the same way; but certainly that he should let an increase of happiness follow upon every morally good action, and a diminution of happiness upon every morally evil one, etc. — More cautiously than usual does the author proceed in § 83 when he wishes to prove that “*the constitution of the world, too, confirms the existence of God and his attributes*,” i.e. when he sets forth in its basic outlines the so-called *physico-theological proof*. He asserts, namely, that only then are we “entitled to assume that *a certain object is the work of a rational being and has been produced by it for a determined purpose*, if we a) find no reason to hold the existence and the arrangements of this object to be necessary in themselves; also b) recognize no impossibility in the supposition that a being such as the one to whom we wish to ascribe its existence exists, has produced it and has so arranged it; if we rather c) perceive that the object, by its arrangements, is in fact suited to the production of that effect which we are willing to indicate as its purpose; that further d) this effect is a result of such a kind that a being like ours could very well have intended it; and if it finally e) with a different arrangement would no longer be in a position to produce precisely the same effect. The greater, then, the number of those attributes, not necessary in themselves,

[p. 150]

“of the object, and the less it would still remain fit to produce the stated effect if even only some of them were different: the more confidently can we say that it was produced by that rational being, and only for this purpose.”

The doctrine *of immortality* B., as we already know, does not consider an entirely indubitable theorem in the natural religion of the human race; in § 85, however, he gives us on 12 pages a concise survey of his most important reasons for the simplicity of our soul and its endless continued existence with consciousness and recollection; whereby, however, it goes without saying that these reasons are worked out far more completely in the “*Athanasia*” and augmented by various others.<sup>∗)</sup>

For the *practical* sciences, *morals* and the *doctrine of right*, nothing could be more important than the author’s peculiar concept of the *supreme moral law*, should it be recognized as correct: namely, that it is a practical truth from which all other practical truths follow objectively, by means of a merely theoretical minor premise, as consequences from their ground. And so we may, as we hope, still pass on, hand in hand with our readers, to the consideration of the *second* or *particular proposition*, which in B.’s inference for the truth of Catholicism forms the *minor premise*.

[p. 153]

This consists of two parts. The Christian, and more precisely the Catholic, doctrinal system possesses the highest moral utility for us, and — it owes its origin, preservation and spread to a concurrence of extraordinary events. Out of consideration for his hearers B. found himself induced to put the proof of the second part ahead of that of the first. Accordingly, in the *second main part* of the work (which forms its second volume) he is occupied with the proof that extraordinary occurrences cooperated in the origin, preservation and spread of Christianity; in the *third main part*, however (which on account of its size is divided into two volumes), the author examines the moral utility of Christianity according to the Catholic conception of it. The reader, understandably, would not need to keep strictly to this order of the book; he could take the third and fourth volumes first, and conclude with the second. But if he wishes to follow B.’s exposition exactly, we remind him not to overlook that the latter by no means expects him to acknowledge the extraordinary occurrences of which the second part tells as genuine *signs* that serve to confirm Christianity before he has yet convinced himself of the moral utility of one of the Christian doctrinal systems.

In order, however, to justify to some extent in advance why the author now directs his attention precisely to Christianity, he begins, in the *first chapter* of the second volume (§§ 4–12), with what he himself calls an “*external or authority proof of the moral utility of the Christian, and more precisely the Catholic, doctrinal system*.” This proof the readers may, if it pleases them, pass over entirely, since the author himself does not want them, contenting themselves with it, to omit the investigation of the Christian doctrinal systems according to their inner constitution and their relation to themselves; as indeed, even in the case that we could hold ourselves convinced of the truth of one of these doctrinal systems before we have yet become acquainted with its doctrines

[p. 154]

in detail, it would become our duty, at least from the moment this has happened, to become acquainted with this detail and to ask ourselves to what it can be applied.

Meanwhile, the kind of investigation that the author conducts here — taken in general, that is, the investigation of how much can be decided for or against the truth of a proposition, without going into its inner grounds, merely from a consideration of those who have declared themselves for or against it — is of such importance and of such manifold application that B. has deemed it, not without reason, worth the trouble to give, already in the “*Theory of Science*,” § 390, a special instruction on how such investigations are to be set up. In its application to the present case, however, here again only the thought itself, not its incomplete execution (in no more than 27 pages), can be credited with some value. Instead of describing more closely the mere course that the author follows in this investigation, we prefer to single out just one thought that seems to us particularly correct. With a society religion such as the Catholic, it may very easily turn out that, besides the doctrines which we find so generally spread that they must be regarded as actual doctrines of this society itself, we also encounter a multitude of prejudices which — although they are contradicted by some — are defended by others only all too zealously, and which exert a pernicious influence on the welfare of the society no less than on its morality. Through this, and through the detrimental influence that other circumstances not belonging to religion at all also have — e.g. the civil as well as ecclesiastical laws prevailing in a country, and much else — it can happen that a people in whose religious doctrinal system not the slightest fault is to be found nevertheless finds itself in a morally and physically worse condition than another in whose religion some obviously false propositions can be demonstrated.

[p. 155]

The *second* chapter, too: “*On the Nature of Historical Cognitions, Especially with Regard to Miracles*” (§§ 13–30), we could skip; for the only things that could have any influence on the proof to be conducted here are the theorem of § 27, *that even miracles* (namely extraordinary occurrences) *can be historically attested*, and that of § 30, *that historical judgments of such a kind as faith in a revelation demands can attain precisely the same degree of certainty as judgments a priori can*. But both truths would probably be evident of themselves to anyone who has once grasped and accepted the theory set forth here of the marks of a revelation, even if he should not know how to set out their grounds distinctly. To our readers these latter are for the most part already known. All historical judgments are ultimately grounded on certain immediate perceptions, to explain which we assume an event that took place in the past, from among several other possible ones, by a mere inference of probability. Here it may well turn out, however great the inner improbability of an event related to us by historians may be, that it attains a very high degree of reliability, as soon as only (which is not at all impossible) the circumstances are of such a kind that every other assumption by which we should want to explain our existing perceptions (e.g. that the narrators have all erred or lied, etc.) would have a still far greater improbability. In fact, however, in order merely to become certain that a doctrine owes its spread to unusual events, it is never necessary to decide about the actual course of these events, and consequently also never necessary that among the several assumptions that we make to explain our immediate perceptions one can be demonstrated which surpasses all the rest as the comparatively most probable. And precisely on this, and on the consideration that God cannot permit that we, to our detriment,

[p. 156]

err, if according to our best insight we assume that he has attested something to us as true — on these two circumstances our assertion is based that we can be more certainly convinced of the possession of a divine revelation than even mathematicians are of their theorems.

What else occurs in this chapter, the more precise determinations of the requirements for a credible testimony (e.g. that *love of truth* is not necessarily required for it), what is said about *plurality of witnesses and contradiction among witnesses*, about the manner in which one has to examine the *authenticity and integrity* of a book — may perhaps deserve the attention of those who wish to set up theories on such matters; for the purpose now before us it is entirely a matter of indifference.

This very judgment we pass without hesitation also on the whole *third chapter*, which treats “*of the authenticity, integrity and historical credibility of the books of the New Testament*” (§§ 31–54). For these are investigations on which, in the view of the author and of ourselves, in complete agreement with him in this, the proof of the truth of Christianity in general and of Catholicism in particular neither should nor can be made dependent. To this must be added that we cannot even ascribe to this chapter — however approvingly it has been judged by some reviewers — the merit that we should be inclined to concede to the two former, namely that, for all the deficiency of their execution, they nevertheless offer some new *thoughts* worthy of notice. Let it suffice, then, to indicate here only briefly the plan according to which B., after first adducing the grounds that speak for the authenticity and integrity of those books, seeks to demonstrate their historical credibility. 1) The writers of the New Testament (he asserts first) had not only *full opportunity* but also *every incentive* to procure for themselves knowledge of the events that we wish to learn from them; they were, moreover, neither so *credulous* nor such *fanatics* as some want to make out of

[p. 157]

them; it is also not exactly incomprehensible in what way they were able to hand down unfalsified *the discourses of Jesus* which they relate to us. 2) They narrate, further, *intelligibly and with the most serious intention*. 3) They *in fact report truth*. This last is evident a) from that *approval* which their writings received in preference to other history books on the same subject; b) from the high *inner excellence* that both, the religious doctrine of the New Testament as well as the history of Jesus told in these books, possess; c) from a *comparison of the narratives* that we meet with in the various books of this collection *about one and the same event*; d) from the *lack of a sufficient motive for lying* on the part of the authors of these books; e) from the *proofs of sincerity and love of truth* that they give us; finally f) from the *comparison of their narratives with the narratives of other historians*. (pp. 105–144).

Roughly thus, B. thought, one must express oneself about this subject if one wishes to discuss it *in general terms*; but science is little served by precisely that. Every particular event in the life of Jesus, every one of his individual discourses and deeds, has its own degree of credibility, now greater, now less, since the inner as well as the outer grounds of probability that speak for or against each of these events are different. To investigate these degrees of credibility in a thoroughly unprejudiced way would be a task through whose solution, not yet completed so far, science — not, to be sure, the *science of religion*, but one of the most important branches of *historical science*, *the study of the deeds and fates of notable persons* — would receive a most gratefully welcome increase of perfection. Such utterances we remember having heard from B. more than thirty years ago!

[p. 158]

The only chapter in this volume, then, that we essentially need is the *fourth*, in which the “*Proofs for the Existence of the External Mark of a Revelation in Christianity*” (§§ 55–75) are set forth. Let us note, however, here too a *separation* that the author has made not without reason. For under the heading “*First Division. General Proofs*,” he first presents only some such proofs for the existence of the external mark of a revelation in Christianity *in which the historical credibility of the books of the New Testament need not be presupposed at all*, of which he believes, in general, that they would have to be entirely irrefutable and satisfying to everyone who reflects on them with an unprejudiced mind. Only after this has been done does he condescend to adduce, in the *second division*, under the heading “*Individual Miracles*,” events whose narration is taken from the books of the New Testament.

In the first division (§§ 56–61) he supplies *five* proofs: 1) from *the existence of the Bible*, 2) from *the preaching of the apostles*, 3) from *the faith of the first Christians*, 4) from the conduct of *the enemies* of Christianity, and finally 5) from *the judgment of the scholars of our time*. The last is probably the most irresistible; hence we content ourselves with laying only this one before the readers. “In our most recent times,” says the author, “the history of the origin and spread of Christianity was subjected, not once but countless times, to the strictest examination; frequently with the most decided inclination to doubt and deny in advance everything that would look like a miracle and would attest this religion as a divine one: and the result of these examinations was as follows:

“1) The greater part of these scholars, and indeed precisely the class of those who seem to have undertaken their examination with the most conscientious impartiality, who had also shown themselves, through other investigations, to be the most modest

[p. 159]

“investigators and the most thorough judges of antiquity, *decided for Christianity*; decided that in its origin and spread there had indeed taken place *many and undeniable miracles* (namely precisely those that the books of the New Testament relate to us).

“2) It was only a very small part that *decided against Christianity*, i.e. that denied that in its origin or spread *miracles of the kind* had taken place that the books of the New Testament relate. On closer consideration, however, it appears that even what these scholars conceded is already entirely sufficient to attest the truth of Christianity as a divine revelation. For these scholars assert, at bottom, nothing other than that there is something not quite right about this or that individual occurrence which the books of the New Testament relate to us, e.g. the resurrection of Jesus; they assert, further, that in the origin and spread of Christianity miracles in the sense that has hitherto been regarded in the schools as the only valid one had nowhere taken place, namely that *no supernatural or immediate effects of God* had taken place. But *they all see themselves compelled to concede that, in favor of Christianity, very many unusual events, which could also have turned out quite differently, had occurred*.” — “And so,” (the author concludes) “the cause of Christianity has been examined, through a period of *eighteen centuries*, by enemies and friends; and not only those who decided *for*, but also those who decided *against* it asserted (the latter without knowing or intending it) things from which it follows that this religion has the external mark of a divine revelation. Whoever, accordingly, does not want to think himself wiser than all these persons will also have to concede that in one point, *in which they all agree*, they have not erred. But whoever should nevertheless wish to assert that they had erred would have to assume, precisely in

[p. 160]

“this error, an event which is so unusual, and serves so greatly to the advantage of Christianity, that one would once again have to regard it as a miracle proving the divinity of this religion.” — We should like to know who is able to advance anything tenable against this proof. Or would one perhaps refuse to be satisfied with it merely *because it is so short and simple*?

In the *second division* (§§ 62–73) the following miracles are considered: 1) A *prophecy concerning Christianity* (namely its rapid spread); 2) *Prophecies of Jesus* concerning *the Jewish people*; 3) A remarkable event in the attempted *rebuilding of the Jewish temple*; 4) Some miracles that the *Acts of the Apostles* relates to us (the occurrence at the feast of Pentecost, the conversion of Paul, among others); 5) *Miracles of Jesus* (a. general reports of miraculous healings of the sick by Jesus, b. some individual miracles); 6) *The resurrection of Jesus*; 7) *Messianic prophecies*. — Clearly enough it shines forth from the choice, arrangement and treatment of these materials how the author holds the view that an enlightened thinker will always be more inclined to concede that an event has taken place, and thus also to recognize in it a *divine sign*, when he can grasp through the cooperation of what natural forces it might perhaps have been brought about, than when the opposite is the case. Of this kind are obviously the miracles 1, 2, 3 and 7; for that it does not occur to B., in no. 7, to demand of a *Messianic prophecy* that the Old Testament writer should have known beforehand that here he was writing something that would one day be fulfilled in this and this way: this we need not first point out. The *fact of the resurrection of Jesus*, however, he wants to see considered from a twofold standpoint, not yet precisely distinguished until now: from the *one*, when we use it merely as a *sign* serving as proof of Christianity; from the *other*, when we wish to find in it a *factual proof of the continued*

[p. 161]

*existence* of our personality even after death. In the first case it suffices to show that, however one may ever picture the actual course of the matter, some highly unusual coincidence of many very fortuitous circumstances must always be presupposed. — To show this is now so easy a matter that B., probably not without reason, counts the event here in question among the *greatest and most decisive* miracles of Christianity.

Quite a different way of treating the subject is necessary in the *second* case, where it must be investigated whether and to what degree it can be made historically certain that Jesus a) really died on the cross, b) on the third day presented himself again alive, and this, moreover, c) no longer in an ordinary mortal human body, but in a transfigured body, designed for a higher stage of existence. We believe that a modest reader, as soon as he has so much as looked at the *title* of the book, will not demand that the author should have made his young hearers familiar with every objection that can impair our confident presupposition of such a fact. However that may be, we shall nevertheless be permitted on this occasion to communicate to our readers a reflection of B.’s that belongs entirely here, which we have read in a letter written by him several years ago to a very sensible woman (one of his former pupils, if we are not mistaken C. L., née R.). It is, after all, he remarked there, indisputable that not only the *apostles* and all the *first* Christians, but also in the sequel, through all the centuries of the Christian era down to the present day, *millions of followers of Jesus* believed that through him there had been fulfilled for us human beings what we had always wished for as the strongest and most palpable proof of our immortality: namely, that one of our brothers should return after his decease, to attest to us the existence of a life beyond the grave. As many as believed this, just as many imagined that it had been an event brought about only by God’s special arrangement

[p. 162]

which had the purpose of strengthening our belief in immortality, i.e. they saw in it a true *divine revelation*. Is it, then, conceivable that among so countless a multitude of human beings there should not also have been very many who, in accepting this belief, proceeded with all the caution possible to them, according to their best insight, in the most conscientious manner? But if this happened, can we then well impute to an all-ruling providence of God that those human beings should have been deceived, deceived in any point that is of even some importance, i.e. in respect of which they could complain if the matter were not in fact as they had accepted it, trusting in God’s testimony? Could God permit that we, in consequence of what took place in those days, should believe in a continued existence of our personality and in an eternal life, if there were not in fact such a life? — Suppose, then, that even in our present days, at so great a distance in time and with the incomplete reports that remain to us, we could not arrive at a wholly indubitable decision about every circumstance of this event that seems important to us; suppose that we could not even make ourselves perfectly sure whether the risen one appeared to his friends in a transfigured body or in the earthly human body still subject to mortality: will it not be permitted us to assert that even in this latter case there is nothing to be altered in our belief that there is another life, and a state of higher perfection in it? May we not roughly conclude as follows: If that body in which the risen one showed himself did not yet have the whole constitution which the bodies of the blessed have in the other life, this happened without doubt because only a body like his had been perceptible to our senses, or because even such a one had already sufficed to produce the conviction intended here alone? Whatever it may have been that took place in those days, it was of such a kind that it had sufficed to convince several hundred of our brothers by

[p. 163]

their own senses that “*he who had been put to death had come forth again from the grave as victor over death and hell, and had soared up to heaven:*” can we then still remain in doubt for what purpose all this happened, and what truth it is that God has wished thereby to vouch for to human beings?

This, roughly, is the train of thought in that letter! Let us now return just once more to the *second* volume, in order to learn how the author at its close (§ 74) settles the question *concerning the connection of those extraordinary events* that have been enumerated so far *with the Catholic doctrinal system*. Very brief and convincing is his declaration (no. 1) *that not only with the Catholic one, but with all doctrinal systems that owe their origin and spread to the correct or incorrect opinion that they are only a further development of the doctrine of Jesus*, there exists a connection with the events mentioned that is just as close as one is ever entitled to demand between a doctrine and the events that are supposed to confirm it. As for what he then goes on to say (no. 2), that the Catholic doctrinal system stands in a *still closer connection* with those events than other Christian doctrinal systems, we may gladly leave that undecided. It will be disputed; but who can deny the former?

Thus everything will now depend only on this: of which Christian doctrinal system it will be shown, after an unprejudiced examination, that, besides the *external* mark common to all of them, it also possesses the so-called *inner mark* of a divine revelation, namely *the greatest moral utility*, in such a way that at every single point we must admit: we feel ourselves more strongly encouraged to the fulfillment of our duties when we believe as here than when we believe what elsewhere

[p. 164]

is taught. Note well, then, that what is at issue here is by no means an investigation of whether this or that doctrinal system has moral utility *for all human beings*; still less whether a certain doctrine might not perhaps become harmful through misunderstanding or misuse, or perhaps already has become so: rather, what is now at issue is solely to judge whether *we ourselves*, at any rate, can hope to gain in moral perfection through such a doctrine, and indeed in that peculiar manner in which precisely *we* would know how to interpret it for ourselves and to apply it; likewise whether there is no other view of the same subject whose acceptance would promise us a greater moral utility?

Since, however, it is evident that we could never accept a doctrine in faith if we noticed that it stands in contradiction with some known truth, it follows that, just as much as we are obliged to investigate the *moral utility* of every doctrine, we are also entitled to test its *conformity to reason*. Let us recall, however, what was said right at the beginning of this essay about the *figurative nature* of many religious doctrines; then it becomes clear to us that it would be mere precipitancy if we were at once to reject every doctrine that, taken in its *literal* sense, leads to a contradiction, as contrary to reason and hence also as false and not revealed by God; since it would very well be possible that it is to be taken *merely* figuratively, and in this figurative sense can be fully justified. For the latter, however, nothing more is required than that the idea that the matter stands as this doctrine states should awaken in us appropriate feelings and resolutions of the will, i.e. possess moral utility for us. From this it follows, then, that in investigating the *conformity to reason* of a doctrine we may never regard an unfavorable result, and hence a judgment rejecting it, as well founded unless we first also consider whether it cannot be understood figuratively and what usefulness it then attests. The recommendation of this caution is all the more necessary since

[p. 165]

we may never, or at any rate only in the rarest cases, expect of revelation itself that it should (for us) expressly designate its figurative doctrines as such; especially when it is given to us in the form of a society religion. For as regards those members of the society who have so much insight as to perceive a contradiction in the literal interpretation of a doctrine, they have, for that very reason, also insight enough to tell themselves that a mere image lies before them here; for others, however, who are still too unpracticed in thinking and still possess too little preliminary knowledge to find anything offensive in this doctrine even on an entirely literal understanding, would it not be confusing if they were told: “It is a mere image that you see here before you; only in order to enliven your feelings and resolutions are you to imagine that the matter is so; in reality, however, it is not so”?

With such dispositions, then, let each one go to the examination of the many Christian doctrinal systems; and we cannot, to be sure, predict for him whether the result of his examination will be Catholicism or some other Christian religion; but with precisely the degree of confidence with which we credit him with proceeding conscientiously, we also trust that God will not let him make a mistake to his moral detriment. As for the author, we already know that it is the *Catholic-Christian doctrinal system* that he presents to us as the morally most perfect. Whether one agrees with him in this or not, one is, we believe, at least not entitled to think that he did so without that innermost conviction which has come about through a preceding examination of the same kind as that which he demands of others; rather, one sees clearly enough from the whole book *why it is only and precisely Catholicism that satisfies him*, and understands that he, if not with this one, could all the less have warmed to any other Christian doctrinal system.

But the question is *whether what the author here lays before us as Catholicism is also genuine Catholicism*?

[p. 166]

That has indeed already been disputed, and more than one Catholic reviewer has accused B. of neologism and of a flattening of the Catholic dogmas. We must therefore investigate precisely how matters stand in this regard!

Misgivings are aroused already by the author’s peculiar definition of the concept of a *society religion*, and by the assertion that Catholicism is one. For our theologians will hardly be inclined to concede that no proposition can be regarded as a Catholic dogma until all the laity, for whom it has intelligibility and religious importance, have accepted it. Since, however, the author declares in vol. III, § 2 that, as soon as a doctrine is merely found in all *publicly used textbooks*, one may presuppose that it is also accepted by the entire clergy and by the remaining part of society: one sees that he is by no means disposed to avail himself of his peculiar definition in order, say, to strike from the list of Catholic dogmas doctrines that are unwelcome to him. But we must take a closer look at the arrangement that he has given to his “*third main part*,” namely “*the systematic exposition of the doctrine of Catholicism according to its inner excellence*.”

By his own admission B. should here, for each proposition that he adduces as a Catholic doctrine, have demonstrated that it is found clearly stated in the most commonly used textbooks of the Catholic Church, or at least tacitly presupposed in what is said there. This, however, he has by no means done; instead he has preferred, for each doctrine, under the curious heading “*Historical Proof of This Doctrine*,” to supply usually only certain *Bible texts* from which the Catholic theologians wish to have the doctrine in question inferred. To this procedure — we can readily imagine — various reasons may have moved him. For his pupils the citation of so many, often very long, passages from the most various textbooks, indeed even the

[p. 167]

mere reference to these passages would have been entirely without purpose or use. By a not overly copious citation of those *scriptural texts*, on the other hand, they were not only made familiar with a multitude of splendid Bible passages, which they could make use of throughout their whole future life for their own edification as well as that of others; but they also learned in this way to understand to some extent how each doctrine arose, and what means God employed to make it, in the course of time, generally prevalent. How instructive such reflections can often become, and what proofs they offer of the existence of a providence that cares for the moral progress of the whole of humanity, and therefore above all for the development of religious concepts among us!

But however good all this may have been for his pupils, our readers — if they wish to be sure — now find themselves under the necessity, unless they have perhaps long since done so as theologians, of undertaking for themselves what the author omitted, and of conducting an investigation in the textbooks that are the most esteemed in the Catholic Church. And what will they then find? First, that the author has left scarcely a single one of those doctrines of the Catholic Church that are regarded as *dogmas* in the narrower sense entirely untouched; but then (what they perhaps would not have expected) that he has included among the doctrines of Catholicism also some theorems that one does not encounter in the ordinary textbooks. These are, as it turns out, propositions that B. regards as established *truths of reason* which, even if they have so far not been set out in our textbooks, in his opinion *deserve* to be expressly laid down there. Although, now, whenever he allows himself such an addition he usually admits it in the historical proof, and even if he omitted to do so might presuppose that no error would result from it: still we cannot help finding in this procedure (to which he was evidently led only by the wish to procure more attention for such truths, and to bring it about as soon as possible that they be generally accepted) — something

[p. 168]

inappropriate; and we are certain that he, who does not, after all, suffer from the fault of self-complacency, would wish that this and much else in his book were otherwise! — This much at any rate is certain: should it turn out that a reader of the textbook disagreed with one of these articles, for whatever reason, this would indeed furnish a ground for accusing B., but not the Catholic doctrinal system.

Will it now, after what we have just said about the author’s purpose in all the §§ provided with the heading “*historical proof*,” be necessary to remark that one is never to look here for a proof of the *truth* of a doctrine under discussion? That it would be merely ridiculous if one wished to derive from the circumstance “*that many a text does not say what has been inferred from it*” a reproach against the author? Will it be necessary to remind our readers that it is altogether impossible ever to draw from the content of these §§ a well-founded objection to B.’s proof of the truth of Catholicism, since they contain absolutely nothing that belongs, as a premise, to this proof? — For our purpose, then, we can set these §§ entirely aside and leave them unread; especially since, in order to assure ourselves whether B. presents the doctrine of Catholicism to us faithfully, we in any case can and must employ quite other means.

No less dispensable for our undertaking are also those §§ which, under the heading “*Actual Utility of This Doctrine*,” attempt a rough estimate of the utility that a doctrine of Catholicism under discussion may have produced from the time of its emergence down to the present day; whereby the author, to be sure, never forgets to mention also the harm that it has caused through misunderstanding, abuse, disputes provoked, and the like, but seeks to show that the former has probably outweighed the latter even hitherto. Such discussions can indeed engender the *hope* that a faith which has proved salutary to thousands will also be beneficial to us; they can, moreover, remove many a doubt about the guiding providence of God; they can

[p. 169]

finally (which is probably the most important point) furnish us with special rules of caution to be observed in the presentation of these doctrines: but the question whether the Catholic doctrinal system, in the particular manner in which *we* ourselves would know how to interpret and apply it, promises to further our own morality; this question, on which alone it actually depends here, we can decide with sufficient certainty without entering into those historical investigations. However much, then, we might have to object to what B. in the said §§ in any case indicates only very deficiently: in the belief that we thereby shake his proof of the truth of Catholicism, we could truly not advance such objections unless we wished to make plain that we do not understand the inner connection of the book.

So it is, then, the §§ that speak of the *conformity to reason* and the *moral utility* of each doctrine, into the examination of which the reader must necessarily enter, yet in such a way that here too, when a doctrine taken literally presupposes, e.g., events that seem to him insufficiently proven, he will not be entitled at once to complain that he is being asked to content himself in religious matters with mere probability; provided, that is, that closer investigation shows that that doctrine, even when understood figuratively, still retains for him an indisputable moral usefulness.

So much in general; we can say more only if our readers permit us to accompany them to the individual doctrines themselves that this part contains. We promise, in so doing, always to be brief and to touch only on what is most important.

The *first doctrine* that meets them here is the “*doctrine of Catholicism on the sources of knowledge of its doctrines*,” with whose consideration the author occupies himself in the whole *first chapter* (§§ 3–30). Here it says in § 3 that the Catholics, on the question *according to*

[p. 170]

*which rule one is to be guided in adopting one’s religious opinions*, do not think altogether uniformly (no. 1); but that all are agreed that they may regard that part of their opinions on which all think uniformly *as a truth confirmed by God himself* (no. 2); because Christ himself promised the Church the *assistance of the Holy Spirit*, which makes it infallible in its general decisions, and this indeed *unconditionally* (nos. 3 and 5); yet only in matters that concern *religion*, so that *disciplinary regulations*, purely *scientific* and merely *historical* questions are expressly excepted (no. 6). Hence, it is said, there is a *progress* in the development of the Catholic doctrinal system, and no book yet exists that contains this doctrinal system in the most complete development that it is ever to attain (nos. 7 and 8). Nevertheless, Holy *Scripture* (of the Old and New Testaments) is a book that contains most of the doctrines and, when properly used, is suited to our instruction and edification in so high a degree that we venerate it, not wrongly, as a work written through God’s *inspiration*, which often extends to the very words (no. 8).

What, then, will our readers say to this *first* doctrine of Catholicism? The circumstance that neither the rule of submitting to the papal decision nor any other is taught with general agreement affords us — this must surely be evident to them — the important advantage that in every case that arises we can do precisely what reason presents to us as the most fitting. — Whether no. 2 is true, however, the readers can, and according to B.’s wish should, of course judge only after they have examined *all* the doctrines of Catholicism individually and found them in accordance with reason and morally beneficial. — The opinion that *Jesus founded the Church and himself promised it the prerogative of infallibility* they need by no means first laboriously investigate with regard to its objective correctness. Even if it should be historically unprovable

[p. 171]

that it was the intention of Jesus to found a religious society of the kind that the Catholic Church subsequently became: can that hinder us from nonetheless regarding this Church, in those of its institutions that are undeniably something good, as his institution (occasioned by him), and also from believing that its doctrinal system came about under the guidance of the divine Spirit, if we find it altogether in accordance with reason and morally beneficial? — That this assistance of the divine Spirit is promised to the Church *unconditionally*, i.e. also for the case that some of its members do not proceed with due conscientiousness in adopting their religious views: this could offend us at most if we forgot that this assistance is a benefit that accrues not to him who is unworthy of it but to the rest; and likewise that a promise bound to that condition would be as good as none at all, since that condition is surely never fulfilled on all sides and in its entire extent. — What some of our readers, however, would perhaps seriously wish is that the gift of infallibility, if it is really to dwell in the Church, might extend to more, in particular also to the *ordinances* that its superiors issue, and which through their inexpediency have so often prevented so much good and brought about so much harm! The mere *wish*, now, that this were otherwise we may not condemn, provided only that no one goes so far as to demand it of God with determination — which would be about as unreasonable as if we wished to require of God that he had also granted the gift of infallibility to *all secular* authorities (whose directives are often of no lesser importance). In the book itself they will find set out in still greater detail that and why their demand could in no case be met. — Since it is evident only to people who stand at an already somewhat higher level of education how necessary a gradual *progress in the development of the Christian doctrinal system* is, if it is to be and remain the most perfect religion for all ages: we may not demand

[p. 172]

that the Catholics’ doctrine on this point be found in every popular catechism. — But what is taught about Holy *Scripture* can be hard to believe only for one who, at the word *inspiration*, thinks of an immediate divine influence on the mind of the sacred writers. Whoever has adopted the author’s concepts, can he hesitate to say that it could have come about only through a confluence of the rarest circumstances that we e.g. read in the books of the Old Testament so many passages pointing to events in the life of the Lord Jesus, without the authors of them having had the least foreknowledge that and how it would one day be fulfilled in Jesus himself, indeed that they were speaking here at all of anything future?

But to return now once more to the most important, i.e. the *second* point, which essentially distinguishes Catholicism from every other religion, we challenge each of our readers to refute, if he is able, one of the following three assertions of B.’s: 1) that no society religion can dispense with the theorem of the infallibility of the collective faith, if we are to regard it as a revelation; 2) that even without possessing any divine instruction to that effect we cannot do better than to embrace with the utmost confidence only that on which millions — indeed all who proceed from the same fundamental views as we do — have agreed; 3) that only the said theorem of Catholicism corresponds perfectly to the definite principle that God, in the case of every morally good end to whose attainment we human beings can ourselves contribute something, must first hold up to us that we are to do all that is ours to do, but then may complete, for the good of the whole, by his own intervention what we have failed to accomplish from incapacity or even sloth. — Let anyone but read the whole chapter, or at least compare §§ 6, 14, 17, 26 and 28: and then answer for himself the question whether there does not perhaps lie, precisely in certain irrefutable remarks that are to be found here (admittedly throughout only hinted at rather than properly worked out), the true reason why

[p. 173]

B.’s textbook has been judged by some Protestant theologians with only too visible bitterness, and even designated a *dangerous* book?

In the *second* chapter of this third main part, which, as the most extensive, runs from § 31 to § 234, the author treats of “*Christian-Catholic dogmatics*,” in that, after some general remarks on “*concept, conformity to reason and moral utility of the doctrines of mystery*” (§§ 32–34), he arranges the most important theoretical doctrines of Catholicism under the following six divisions:

1) Doctrines of God,

2) of the world and of creatures,

3) of man in particular,

4) of the relations of God to us,

5) of the relations of creatures to one another,

6) of rewards and punishments,

without wishing to maintain that there could not be a better arrangement of them.

In the *first* section, “*Doctrines of God*,” (§§ 35–140) he sets forth the doctrines: of the *existence* of a God single in his being, of his *all-perfection*, *incomprehensibility*, infinite *understanding*, infinitely perfect *will*, and in particular of his *omnipotence*, *freedom* and *holiness* (the latter of which he again divides into the kinds: *justice*, *truthfulness*, *impartiality*, *goodness* and *grace*), of God’s infinite *blessedness*, *incorporeality*, *omnipresence* and *eternity*, of God’s *threefold personality* and finally of his *decrees*.

In all these doctrines, if we for now still except the penultimate one, one will hardly find anything objectionable, provided only that one holds fast to the *definitions* set up by the author and gives up the prejudice that the infinity of God demanded that each of his attributes

[p. 174]

be *unlimited*. Thus it does indeed follow, to be sure, from God’s all-perfection that his *cognitive power*, because it is limited by no other power, is unlimited, hence *omniscient*; for God’s *volitional power*, however, and the *power of acting outwardly* connected with it, there exists undeniably a twofold limit: the one in his *cognitive power*, according to which he can will nothing that would be morally evil, i.e. of which reason declares that one *should* not will it; the other in the absolutely *impossible*, according to which he can will nothing that contradicts a pure conceptual truth. If we call God’s will *holy* in the first respect, then *justice*, *goodness*, etc. are to be regarded only as particular *kinds* of holiness; and God’s justice consists, e.g., in his doing nothing of which even we human beings ourselves could see that it would not be conducive to the welfare of the whole or, in general, would not be morally good; his *goodness* in his showing his creatures many a benefaction that does not follow from mere justice, i.e. of which we do not recognize that the welfare of the whole requires it, but which in fact must nevertheless accord with this welfare or with the moral law, etc.

In the doctrine “*of God’s threefold personality*” (§§ 110–135) we do indeed encounter a doctrine that has already countless times been charged, by the weak as well as by the ill-disposed, with the most palpable contradictions with reason. With what right will soon be seen. Instead of the somewhat more extensive exposition to be read in § 110, it will suffice for our purpose to give the extract that the author himself has supplied in his essay “*My Faith*” (printed in the book “*Religious Confessions of Two Friends of Reason*.” Sulzbach, 1835). “Through the doctrine of the Trinity,” it says there on p. 83 ff., “we are to learn,

“1) that all of God’s benefactions and works can in general be brought under three kinds, in such a way that we may ascribe the works of the one kind to God as *Father*, those

[p. 175]

“of the second to God as *Son*, those of the third, finally, to God as *Holy Spirit*. Without giving a general criterion from which we could tell at once in advance to which of these three kinds of divine acting and willing each individual one of his works belongs, we are told only by way of example that we have to thank God as the Father for the creation, preservation and governance of the whole universe, God as the Son for the redemption of the human race, God as the Holy Spirit for the enlightenment, sanctification and bestowal of blessedness upon each individual human being.

“2) Father, Son and Holy Spirit are thus not, say, three mere *names* of one and the same God, with no inner *difference* in God, designated by them, lying at their ground; nor are they names of three mere *modes of operation* of God; rather, they are three *grounds present in God himself, inner and real*, i.e. grounds having actuality, from which those various effects proceed only in time; they are three *divine persons*.

“3) To each of these three persons Godhead is to be ascribed, as well as omniscience, omnipotence, holiness — in short, every one of the so-called *natural predicates* of the Godhead; and nevertheless one cannot say that there are three Gods, three omniscient or omnipotent beings; rather, the same divine being, the same cognitive power, the same volitional power, etc., that is in the Father is found also in the Son and in the Holy Spirit.

“4) For all the equality in the respects named, however, certain *differences*, and indeed *inner* ones, do obtain among the three persons of the one being of the Godhead. For the person of the Father has no further ground of its existence; that of the Son, however, has the ground of its existence in the Father, that of the Holy Spirit, finally, in both. Of the Son we say that he is *begotten* of the Father, and indeed *from eternity*; of the Holy Spirit, however, that he *proceeds* from the Father and from the Son, likewise *from eternity*.

[p. 176]

“5) Although the Son as well as the Holy Spirit have a ground of their existence, they are nonetheless not *creatures*; nor are they even to be called *dependent* on the Father, or *lesser* than he.”

Every theologian will grant us that, if only these five points can be perfectly reconciled with reason, the few determinations of the Church that perhaps still remain are subject to no further difficulty. We, however, say that one need hold fast only to the following *two remarks* in order to see every semblance of a contradiction vanish from the five articles above. The *first* is that in the concept which the theologians teach us to connect with the word *person* there lies absolutely nothing that would prevent us from thinking that *several* persons can be distinguished in a single being; as indeed even one and the same human being very often unites in himself several of the persons, e.g. that of a scholar, of a citizen, of a family member, etc. The *other* remark is that it is no contradiction to teach that the Son and the Spirit have the ground of their existence in the Father and yet are just as *eternal* as he; exactly as God’s decrees, too, have the ground of their existence in God’s cognitions, and yet for that reason must be regarded not as something that first arose in time, but as something eternal. The author’s peculiar way, in § 132, of separating the spheres of action of the three persons in God from one another, when he ascribes to the *Father* all those arrangements that are required for the welfare of *all* creatures; to the *Son* the arrangements that concern the welfare of the *whole human race*; to the *Holy Spirit*, finally, those divine influences that are exerted on the soul of each *individual human being* for his enlightenment, sanctification and bestowal of blessedness — this explanation will indeed, if we accept it, make easier for us *the understanding of the connection* between the individual articles of the whole doctrine: for the purpose, however, of merely finding it free from every contradiction and in accordance with reason, the two remarks above are entirely sufficient, and there is

[p. 177]

absolutely no need of any of those artificial interpretations, doing mere violence to the dogma, which have been attempted from the age of *Tertullian* down to the present day (e.g. by *Schelling*, *Hegel* and others).

For as to the first point, that distinction of *three kinds of divine action*, who would not be able to find his way in it, even if he did not at all approve of the ground of division indicated by the author? Are creation — redemption — sanctification not distinguished clearly enough? But if, corresponding to these different kinds of divine action, three *persons* are also distinguished in God: is one not at least as much entitled to do so as to distinguish different persons in one and the same human being? May we not assume *personal* effects wherever we encounter effects that proceed through understanding and will? But just as little as we need three *beings* in order to distinguish three persons in God, so little need we presuppose in him a threefold *cognitive* or *volitional power*, since precisely the same cognitive power that judges how God is to act as Father also judges how he is to act as Son and Spirit, etc. What, then, becomes of the absurd objection that one cannot be three and three cannot be one? That the Son has his existence from the Father, the Spirit his existence from Father and Son, is indeed already comprehensible from the circumstance that the works of the Son, such as redemption, presuppose those of the Father, such as the creation of the world; and the works of the Spirit — the enlightenment, sanctification and bestowal of blessedness upon each individual among us — presuppose the works of the Father and of the Son at once. If, further, nothing is to be objected to the *eternity* of the Son and of the Spirit merely on the ground that they have a ground of their existence: then it is still easier to grasp that they may not be called *creatures*, since they are indeed no *beings* distinct from the Father but persons in one and the same being. *Dependent* on its ground, however, and of *lesser rank* than it, a consequence may indeed very often be called: why, nevertheless, the Church

[p. 178]

is unwilling to permit us to call the Son and Spirit dependent and lesser than the Father, can be understood from the *moral utility* of the doctrine.

This one too we wish to communicate here in addition, word for word, just as the author presents it on a single page (p. 87) in the essay mentioned: “The very fact that the doctrine of the Triune God includes *mysteries*, and thereby gives us occasion to become aware how little all of us — even were we the wisest — are wise enough to comprehend God: this alone I already regard as a moral advantage of that doctrine. Another is that through this doctrine a clearer concept of the manifold benefactions of God is imparted to us. For we hear here that each of the divine persons, i.e. each of the three grounds of action present in God, has proved active for us from the very beginning, and will continue to prove active also in eternity. How should this not uplift us, how should it not fill us with lively gratitude toward God! How suited, furthermore, are even the *names* that God deigns to take on in this mystery to awaken and sustain in us the feelings of reverence, love and trust! God, our Creator, Preserver and Governor, is thus also a *Father*, and is so not merely to his only-begotten Son, but also to *all* of us, whom this Son has called his brothers. And the Spirit of God wants to take up his dwelling in our hearts, and wants to consecrate us as his living temple, that our members might become members of the Spirit of God! And lest we fear that the arrangements in the world that aim at the best of our race — all the more surely those that aim only at the best of a single individual among us — might vanish as something utterly insignificant, because we ourselves and our whole race vanish when compared with the infinite universe: it is impressed upon us that God proves himself *no less* as Son and Spirit than as Father; that he with equal omnipotence, wisdom and goodness, as for the whole, for each

[p. 179]

“individual cares. What a number of points of contact, finally, for the remembrance of God this doctrine offers us through the many concepts and names, borrowed in part even from sensible objects! how greatly it thereby facilitates for us that constant thinking of God without which, as the heathen *Seneca* already recognized, no one becomes a truly good human being!” — We doubt whether even a single one of those who rejected the doctrine of the Trinity as, in the most favorable case, a useless speculation has ever taken these important advantages of it into consideration?

From the doctrine “*of God’s decrees*” (§§ 136–140) the author removes everything objectionable, in that he a) by way of precaution against the thought that God’s *unconditional* decrees had no ground at all, explains that their chief ground lies precisely not in the being which they directly concern, but in the promotion of the welfare of others; then b) seeks the difference between *effecting* and merely *permitting* decrees of God not in the greater or lesser share that God’s activity takes in their realization, but solely in this, that the former concern something good in itself, the latter, by contrast, something that is evil in itself but cannot be prevented without some greater evil. This latter becomes all the more comprehensible to us the more the author has already taken care, in what precedes (and in the “*Theory of Science*”), to remove the erroneous notion that all pure conceptual truths, like all theorems of pure mathematics and physics, rested merely on God’s pleasure. A multitude of errors that belong here, finally, is forestalled merely by the author’s distinguishing more precisely the various meanings in which we take the word *determination*. On this, however, in order not to become too prolix, we must refer to the book itself.

In the *second* section, “*Catholic Cosmology*,” (§§ 141–166) the doctrine “*of God’s*

[p. 180]

*creation and governance of the world*,” as well as that “*of the angels*” is so agreeable to sober reason that we scarcely fear an objection on the part of our readers; especially since anyone who could not reconcile himself with the wording of some articles in the latter, such as that on the evil spirits, will certainly see everything objectionable vanish as soon as he has taken refuge in a figurative interpretation, which is not barred to him. That the author conceives *creating* not as an act that has taken place in time but as a constant existence of the limited substances through the constant willing of the one infinite substance, let one contest and refute, if one can; let one even call it, without being able to do so, a doctrine hard to grasp for the uneducated and therefore dangerous: to designate it as a *heresy*, however, one should not allow it to enter one’s head. For everyone must surely see that the offensiveness that this doctrine has for the uneducated derives solely from the fact that such persons are accustomed to see the effect always occur later than its cause (namely not the complete cause, but only a mere partial cause); that to the more practiced thinker, on the other hand, it is very easy to grasp this, and that it greatly heightens the feelings of admiration and gratitude that he wishes to pay to God when he may imagine that the world has existed not merely six thousand years, nor even six thousand times six thousand years, but is from eternity, and likewise extends into the infinite as to space.

In the doctrine “*of the ultimate purpose of the world*” the author perhaps wrongly assumes that the glorification of God is designated as this ultimate purpose with such generality; yet once one has heard the author’s further elucidation, namely that this is only *the highest purpose we have to conceive*, but not the *objectively* highest, that the latter lies rather in the greatest possible promotion of the virtue and happiness of created beings, then surely no one will take offense at this doctrine. From the “*Doctrine of the Angels*” (§§ 159–166) we wish to single out only that the author permits himself

[p. 181]

also to ascribe certain bodies to them, and believes he has already satisfied the opposing doctrine if he merely adds: they are not *sensible* bodies, and their pleasures are therefore not our *sensible* ones. And that is really quite enough, because it suffices completely for that purpose for whose sake it is so taught. This purpose, namely, is none other than to prevent the sensible pleasures from appearing to us all the more valuable if we imagined that higher spirits too take delight in such a manner.

The *third* section, “*Christian-Catholic Anthropology*” (§§ 167–196), opens with a short doctrine “*of the components and powers of man*.” In order to be able to agree with the little that is here doctrine of faith in the proper sense, it suffices merely to concede, in some sense or other, an opposition between *soul* and *body*, and merely not to hold the continued existence of our consciousness after death to be impossible! For whoever thinks — and many think so — that the grounds which our mere reason offers for the immortality of the soul indeed show its possibility, but by no means suffice to give to this “most desirable of all truths” that degree of certainty which we long for: he must surely regard the assurance that Christianity gives us on this point as one of the greatest benefactions that can ever fall to our lot through a revelation. But even he who would be so unfortunate as to hold the continued existence of our consciousness after death to be something absolutely impossible, who thus could not accept as true, in its literal sense, the Christian doctrine here under discussion, could and must nevertheless hold it in honor as a figurative idea that makes life more agreeable to us and makes death immensely easier. Only for him to whom the thought of a continued existence with recollection is a fearful thought does Christianity know no consolation: yet to such a one we venture to call out that there must be some essential defect in his moral constitution, and only when

[p. 182]

he has corrected this will he too find the doctrine of immortality beneficial. We may not judge so harshly those who do not precisely fear the continued existence of their personality, but who also will not recognize anything at all pleasing in this belief, in particular no encouragement at all to an all the more perfect conduct on earth. Of these we ask only how long they have actually thought so already, and whether their disposition has indeed already stood all tests? Moreover, what has no value for them we do not wish to force upon them, for that very reason. Perhaps the time will yet come when they too learn to think differently.

What is commonly called the doctrine “*of the descent of all human beings from a single pair*,” B. designates and conceives as a doctrine “*of the essential equality of all human beings*” (§§ 172–176), i.e. as the proposition that all human beings have essentially equal *rights* and *claims* to the enjoyment of earthly goods. For this important truth is grounded, as he remarks, not on the demonstrable descent of all human beings from a single pair, but solely on this, that all human beings have an essentially equal *capacity* for the enjoyment of the said goods. Let us see whether anyone will refute this?

The permission granted to man (the uncorrupted) to regard himself as the *image of God* on earth the author bases on the fact that man, among all (visible) inhabitants of the earth, possesses the greatest likeness to God.

That, however, the condition in which our race finds itself down to the present day is still by no means of such a kind that one could be content with it; that rather a *great moral as well as physical corruption prevails among us*, B. regards as a truth that no rational religion may conceal; and he shows in § 182 that Christianity depicts this corruption in the most vivid colors, but also insists that work be done unceasingly on the improvement of this condition. In order to enliven our faith in the *possibility* of growing better, and at the same time also to give us some hints as to what

[p. 183]

we must chiefly aim at for this purpose, it sets up, according to B.’s views, the three intimately connected doctrines *of the perfection of man’s original state, of the Fall, and of original sin* (§§ 183–196), of which B. was always wont to assert that they belong among the most beautiful and most important that a religion can ever set up. What causes some difficulty in these doctrines is solely the clumsy conception of them, when one wants, e.g., to see the events that are presupposed in them according to the literal sense — that our whole race took its beginning from a single pair, that this first pair was adorned with such outstanding powers and excellences of body and soul, that it was suddenly cast down from its high level of perfection through a single sin, etc. — beyond every doubt *historically established*. For these are indeed narratives whose historical truth, in the judgment of many, has never yet been proven. After all, the author of the ancient composition from which we commonly draw them does not seem to have wished, even himself, that his words be interpreted as the narrative of an actual event. But what compels us to decide on all this first, if all the *practical consequences* that the Church wants to have drawn from these events *are and remain incontrovertibly true*, however matters may stand with the actual course of events itself? What is the purpose of the narratives that we read in Gen. 2:7 ff.? What consequences are we to draw from them? and what truths are they to make perceptible to us? The most important are certainly only the following: that God, already at the origin of our race, bore the most fatherly care for us, and brought us forth in a manner that attests the nobility of our moral nature; that the present weakness and frailty of our body (through which, e.g., childbirth is bound up with such great pains and dangers, and much else besides) by no means rests on unalterable laws of nature, that, on the contrary, we have brought about such evils only through our own vices and follies,

[p. 184]

and by a return to virtue and wisdom might hope to free ourselves from them again at least in part; that it is sin alone that has brought death into the world, since it alone is what lends it everything terrible and first makes death into death; that through every deviation from God’s law we can harm not only ourselves and our immediate surroundings but also our most remote descendants, so that the unhappy consequences of a single evil deed sometimes extend to whole generations; that the strong propensity to sensuality that we feel within us, and our great excitability toward evil, have been brought about only gradually by ourselves; but that these faults, once they are present, make necessary for us, on God’s part, a harsher treatment, a treatment that almost has the look of his being angry with us; that the first step toward evil commonly draws very many others after it; that it is for this very reason not unfair if God often punishes even this first step with the utmost severity, etc. Is not all this true, and does it not remain true, even if there had never been a paradise, indeed never an Adam and an Eve? ——

We must observe, however, that the author has augmented the doctrine of the Fall with an article that is not to be met with in the ordinary dogmatics, namely that not only did the first sin do harm, but that also all *subsequent sins and follies* that have been committed by any human being, or are still being committed, have contributed, and still contribute, to the increase of evil on earth. We do not fear that anyone will deny this truth, or maintain that it does not belong to the doctrine of the Fall. — In the doctrine *of original sin*, finally, B. maintains that this is not a *proper (actual) sin*, i.e. not a voluntary transgression of the moral law, but only sin in that wider meaning in which one understands by it something *displeasing* to God that has its *origin* in sin. Of this *displeasure* (which in any case wants to be understood merely *figuratively*, because pleasure or displeasure in the literal sense

[p. 185]

cannot be aroused in God by creatures) the author maintains that only the two *limits* of it are specified; “a) it is *too great* for God to be able to let man, who still bears original sin, enjoy those higher *supernatural blessednesses* which he had destined for the human race while it was still without sin; b) it is *not so great* that it could lead the holy God, in his treatment of men, away from that rule of *justice* according to which he punishes only the wicked and rewards every virtuous person.”

It can now be justified without difficulty, both that there is such an original sin, and that individual persons, in particular Jesus, were not subject to it, likewise that we are freed from it by admission into the Christian Church. For indeed, the manner, most painful and dangerous both for ourselves and for our mothers, in which we are born into the world; the many diseases to which we are exposed, which carry off again the greater part of us already in the first years of our life; the many other sufferings and unfavorable circumstances that are the cause of the fact that among hundreds scarcely one of us finds an opportunity to develop his mental powers and dispositions properly; the great excitability toward evil that manifests itself already so early in all of us and, in combination with the bad examples that we see around us, sweeps us along as if with overwhelming force into similar sins and follies; the pernicious institutions that have struck such deep roots in our civil constitutions that it is almost impossible to foresee how we shall ever tear ourselves loose from them again; the far more dreadful religious errors, the delusion of polytheism and others, in whose fetters the greatest part of the human race still continually languishes; the hostile relation in which not only the whole animal creation but also lifeless beings, indeed all the elements, stand to us, the supposed lords of this earth; finally the sad experience that man, as he now once and for all is, cannot bear good days at all, because

[p. 186]

they would soon make him merely arrogant, dissolute, sluggish and vicious: does not all this attest, only too plainly, that our race has not become that for which it originally, to be sure, had a disposition; and does it not follow from this that God must subject us to a harsher treatment than that which he could have bestowed on us if we had never sinned? Can it thus not be said with full right that we are an object of *divine displeasure*? that in each one of us, from birth on, there is something that is *displeasing* to God, and which, because it has its ground in the sins and follies of our forebears, may quite fittingly be called a *sin inherited* by us from them? — That individuals, however, can nevertheless be exempted from this original sin, that in particular nothing displeasing to God was surely to be found in Jesus Christ, that rather everything that could be perceived in him from his birth on presents him to us as an object of the most eminent divine good pleasure: who would dispute that? Who, finally, would not grant that all those, too, who are so fortunate as to be received as members into the society of the Christian Church may at once give way to the joyful thought that they are no longer displeasing to God on account of that which is found in them merely by way of inheritance? It is true, to be sure, that through the holy act of baptism their nature is by no means transformed, that they remain, now as before, excitable, inclined to evil, etc.: but just as true that they are now received into an ecclesiastical society in which so many natural as well as supernatural aids for the mastery of their desires, so many graces of God, are offered to them, that it absolutely will not do to say that they are even now still objects of divine displeasure.

The *fourth* section: “*of the relations of God to us human beings*” (§§ 197–220), begins with a doctrine “*of God’s paternal disposition*,” (§§ 198–202),

[p. 187]

which one does not find set up in the ordinary dogmatics. Nevertheless we do not fear that it will be said that it is not genuinely Catholic. But just as little will anyone want to raise any objection against the conformity to reason or the moral utility of this doctrine.

Incomparably more difficult is — indeed, next to the doctrine of the Trinity it has always been regarded as the most difficult — the one that now follows, “*of the incarnation of the Son of God*,” which, for that very reason, we wish to cite word for word as the author presents it in § 203:

“1) At the will of the *Father* in God the *Son*, who is of the same being with him, resolved, in the course of time, *to take on human nature*.

“2) To this end, some eighteen hundred years ago, a *virgin* by the name of *Mary*, without the cooperation of a man, solely through the power of the Spirit of God, became pregnant with a child who received the name *Jesus* (i.e. Redeemer).

“3) This child was conceived *without original sin*.

“4) It was a *real*, not a merely apparent, *human being*, who had a real body and a real soul.

“5) With this human being the *second divine person* was, from the first moment of his existence (thus already in the womb of his virgin mother), most intimately *united*.

“6) This union was a union of *two natures*, namely of the divine (of the Son) and of the human (of the human being Jesus), *into one single person*, which bears the names of the God-man, of the *Redeemer*, *Messiah*, *Christ*.

“7) This union was thus no *commingling* or *mixture* of the two natures into a third, which would have had neither human nor divine attributes.

“8) In the God-man there is found, rather,

[p. 188]

“a) a *double understanding*, namely a divine one, that of the Son, and a human one, that of the human being Jesus; and this latter learns, through the enlightenment by the former, as much as a human understanding can grasp in itself, and as Jesus in particular needed to know for the execution of his lofty purposes, e.g. as teacher of the most perfect religion.

“b) A *double will*, namely a divine one, that of the Son, and a human one, that of the human being Jesus. This latter, however, always agreed so perfectly with the former that Jesus remained the only human being without any sin at all.

“9) Of the God-man too one may say that he (namely as man, or in respect of his human nature) had *human duties* and obligations, felt *pleasure and pain*, even felt himself *exposed to temptations*, was mortal in respect of the *body* and really died.

“10) From the other side one may also say of this God-man that he — namely as God’s Son — is almighty, omniscient, etc.

“11) The union that began between the Son of God and the human being Jesus from the first moment of the latter’s coming into being *continues ever since without interruption* and will never be dissolved again.

“12) One may use the expressions: *God has become man*, *God has suffered for us* and has *died on the cross*, and the like; but one is not to say: A *man* has become God; or *the Godhead* has become man, has died, and the like.”

This doctrine too contains historical components that might seem to some insufficiently proven. But whoever merely understands how to distinguish properly from which premises a conclusion actually follows, and which other propositions may fall without that conclusion being made to waver in the slightest, will not fear

[p. 189]

that what he is to believe as a Christian could be shaken by any objection that might be raised from the historical side against the doctrine of the incarnation. Let us merely go through the articles of this doctrine now before us with regard to the moral consequences derived from them in § 206. Articles 1, 3, 6, 10 and 12 present themselves as doctrines that contain something figurative, and, once our readers have first agreed with the rest, will certainly cause them no further difficulty. For that the *Son* of God resolved, in accordance with the will of the *Father*, to assume human nature in the course of time means, after all, nothing other than that the business of the redemption of humankind belongs, as a component, to the plan of the whole governance of the world. That no *original sin* attached to Jesus means (as we already know) only that nothing displeasing to God was found in his nature. That the union of the divine with the human nature in Christ was a union into a single *person* shows us only that, in consequence of this union, the will of the man never came into conflict with the will of God. That one may say of this person, with regard to God, that he is omniscient, almighty, etc., is open to no objection whatever. That one has accepted the expression “*God* (a divine *person*) became man,” but not “*the divinity* (the divine *essence*) became man,” still less “*a man* became God,” is understood and justified of itself. — What gives religious importance to the *second* article, that Jesus was conceived of a virgin by the power of God, is solely the twofold application of it indicated in § 206, no. 2: to make the dignity of the virginal state and the dignity of Jesus more vivid to us. But as for what is truly estimable and meritorious in the virginal state, must not reason concede this to it for all time, whether matters stood one way or another with the origin of Jesus’ human nature? And Jesus’ own dignity: who does not grasp that the true worth of a human being is never determined by the manner of his origin but by

[p. 190]

his inner constitution alone? — As regards articles 4, 7 and 9 — that Jesus was a real man, that the union of God with him was no commingling or blending of the two natures, that Jesus, finally, had human duties, feelings and temptations — on these no doubt arises in our day. The *fifth* article, that the union of the Son of God with Jesus began already with the first moment of his existence, is meant only to remind us of the important truth that for the development of the human mind even the impressions it receives while still in its mother’s womb are not a matter of indifference. And could we doubt this truth? Or would the doctrine of the incarnation even have to cease to be a reminder of it for us, if we were able to call in question the peculiar activity of God that is presupposed in this article; although, to be quite honest, I do not comprehend how, given the indeterminacy in which the Church leaves the *manner* of this activity, one could so much as feel tempted to such a doubt. Everything, then, now depends only on the *eighth* article, in which the influence that the union of God with the man Jesus had on his understanding and will is determined more precisely. This article, however, produces, and is meant to produce in us nothing other than these two things: that on the one hand we do not doubt the high perfectibility of our nature, by virtue of which it would be possible for us, with God’s assistance, to live entirely without sin; and that on the other hand we make our own all those teachings of the Lord that the Gospel sets before us, and all those dispositions that reveal themselves to us from his conduct as narrated there. The first remains in any case incontestable; the second, however, might indeed have its dangers, if the Church did not expressly declare that we may never accept as genuine any interpretation of those teachings and dispositions which it rejects, or according to which Jesus would have done or said something that contradicts reason. If we hold to this rule of interpretation, what have we then to put at risk, whatever the reality

[p. 191]

may have been? If, finally, the 11th article requires us to believe that the union begun between the Son of God and Jesus Christ has never again ceased, nor will ever cease, this is meant to accomplish two things for us: a confirmation of the general truth that God departs from no one who has not first departed from him himself, and the hope that Jesus too, even now and to all eternity, continues to work for the bestowal of blessedness on humankind. The first concerns a truth that stands unshaken even if this confirmation of it could fall away; the second grounds a hope of which not even he could fear that it will ever be put to shame who, for whatever reason, could not quite believe that the *immediate* working of Jesus for our race will continue to all eternity. For what creatures cannot do immediately, their Creator can do, and does, if he has promised it. And so it turns out that in the end even the most obstinate doubter of history can do the Catholic doctrine of the incarnation no harm, unless he wishes to make war on recognized truths of reason.

In what has just been said, the points of view have already been partly indicated from which the following doctrine “*on redemption*” (§§ 208–212) also wants to be considered, if every objection that may be brought forward on historical grounds is to be deprived of all its sharpness in advance. But misunderstanding has raised against this doctrine objections of another kind as well; it has wanted to find in it also something absurd in itself and contradicting reason. Let us see how much the mere plain exposition can do to invalidate those objections, before one has yet entered upon any learned investigation. We read § 208 as follows:

“The incarnation of the Son of God has, as Christianity assures us, produced a multitude of the *most beatific consequences* for the whole human race. The foremost of these are:

[p. 192]

“1) The Son of God made man became the *first teacher and founder of the most perfect religious doctrinal system* that the human race is ever capable of receiving, which is also to endure until the end of time and one day to become the common portion of all human beings.

“2) The God-man has given us, in his *own conduct* on earth, *a model of human perfection*, from which we can gather how we too ought to act in the most various circumstances of life.

“3) He has *freed us from our sins, our own as well as original sin*, by bearing in our stead the punishments that we would have deserved. He did this *voluntarily*, purely out of obedience to the Father. He suffered and died *for the whole human race*, for the good as well as for the wicked, for those who lived earlier as well as for those who live later. We are here to imagine, figuratively, that the Son of God, as it were, also took part in the sufferings of the man, and precisely in this image to recognize the greatness of God’s love toward us and the abominableness of sin.

“4) The God-man has won back for us anew the *prospect of heaven* and God’s good pleasure, lost through the Fall.

“5) Even now he does not cease to work for us and to do us good; he governs the fortunes of the Church founded by him on earth, as its invisible head. Etc.”

Only the *third* point in this exposition is the one that might still cause our readers some difficulties, which they will, however — we believe we can promise them — see removed in the book itself to their complete satisfaction. For let us only first free ourselves from all the

[p. 193]

false ideas that legal scholars, philosophers and theologians have spread on this subject through their unfortunate theories of punishment; let us hold simply to what is already to be regarded as decided by mere sound human understanding, and what can be grounded most perfectly according to the concepts set up by the author: then it will become clearly evident to us on what circumstance alone it depends whether God, for the complete forgiveness of our sins, may require of us absolutely nothing other than the onset of improvement, or may add a further condition; namely, that it depends only on whether the one or the other is more conducive to virtue. It is now easy to grasp that an arrangement of the kind that is presupposed in this doctrine of Christianity is far more beneficial to virtue than an unconditional forgiveness, that is, one bound to improvement alone. Or can it be denied that through this ordinance, in the first place, Jesus himself (our Lord) was already given the opportunity to soar to the highest stage of perfection attainable by man, when he undertook to atone for the sins of the whole race? Can we deny that his example, too, now becomes infinitely more instructive for us? that only in this way is that pernicious levity checked as far as possible which imagines that by a late improvement it can make everything it had formerly perpetrated as good as undone? Can we deny that every mind not yet wholly hardened must find an incentive as powerful as it is noble toward the completion of its moral improvement in the thought that Jesus’ precious blood may not, after all, have been shed in vain for us, and much else? Looked at with these sound concepts, the foolish objections vanish of themselves — that this doctrine depicts God as a bloodthirsty being, that guilt and merit are something personal that cannot be transferred, etc. However, whoever demands an exhaustive refutation will, as already said, not look for it in vain in the book.

[p. 194]

The two remaining doctrines of this section, “*on the influence of the Holy Spirit*” (§§ 213–216) and “*on grace*” (§§ 217–220), will hardly cause our readers any offense; for if they disregard the fact that God, insofar as he produces the effects here in question, is called “*divine Spirit*,” and a few other merely figurative expressions: what is there in this doctrine that reason, left to itself, would not also have to recognize as true? Or are we perhaps not to ascribe every stage of perfection that we ascend, every single good and God-pleasing deed that we perform, to the grace of God, and to call it his own work in us? Or do we perhaps wish to doubt whether God imparts to every human being as much of his grace as is sufficient for him? or that he sometimes also gives us graces that we do not use, even abuse for evil? etc.

The *fifth section*: “*on the relations of creatures to one another*” (§§ 221–225) contains a single doctrine, which the author calls “*the doctrine of the sphere of action of man*.” We wish to lay this one, too, before our readers without abridgment:

“Catholic Christianity” (says the author, § 221) “endeavors to show us that our sphere of action is in general *greater, and reaches much farther*, than we are commonly accustomed to imagine, and than we would perhaps even be entitled to believe without being able to make use of the disclosures of a divine revelation about it. It tells us that we

“1) by every morally good action and resolution of the will contribute *more* to the promotion of the virtue and happiness of the whole *than we* distinctly *can perceive*. It says that we should in particular

“2) be assured that *not a single undertaking begun with good intention ever wholly fails*, but that, even if we do not see the outcome that we had at first intended occur,

[p. 195]

“if it even looks as though only evil proceeds from what we began with good intent, this is always only apparent, and our undertaking brings about, unnoticed by us, a multitude of beneficial consequences that far outweigh the bad ones that catch our eye.

“3) If our own powers do not suffice for the execution of a certain good end, then even the *mere lively wish* that this end may be attained, *if we present it to God*, that is, if we bear in mind that God knows of our wish and is kind and powerful enough to take it into account and to fulfill it — in a word, *our prayer* — is never without all effect; rather, God always hears and grants our petition in such a way that he bestows on us one of two things: either precisely what we ask for, or something still better.

“4) We may hope for this answering from God not only when we pray for ourselves but also when we pray for *the welfare of others*; God also accepts *intercessions*.

“5) We can intercede with success not merely for the living but also *for those already deceased*.

“6) And just as we can do this for them, so, and even more, can the *deceased, indeed all beings standing on a higher stage of existence*, intercede with success for us who live.

“7) We can also *invoke* these beings with benefit for their intercession; and such an invocation of them will not be in vain even if those beings do not actually become aware of it.

“8) All our prayers are *the more effective, the morally better* we are.

“9) We can also greatly increase the effectiveness of each single one of our prayers by connecting *with it*

[p. 196]

“*certain good works*, or even mere *pious vows*, that is, by undertaking various morally good actions from the motive of obtaining the fulfillment of our petition from God all the more surely, or by forming, with this very intention, the firm resolve, in case our petition is granted, to do this or that good thing that we would probably not have carried out had we not regarded it as a means of securing for ourselves the fulfillment of our petition.

“10) The *prayers of Christians*, when they are properly arranged, enjoy a *still more complete answering* than those of non-Christians. And prayers offered by Christians for Christians are likewise more effective than for non-Christians.

“11) Every belief, on the other hand, as though we could — by the performance of a certain action, of whatever kind, *that has neither a benefit recognizable by our mere reason nor one indicated to us by a demonstrable divine revelation* — nevertheless procure help for ourselves or for others, Christianity declares to be a *superstition* as harmful as it is culpable.”

With regard to the first two points, which we would look for in vain in the manuals of dogmatics hitherto, the author contents himself with showing, in the “*historical proof*,” that they result as a *consequence* from other doctrines of Christianity; and surely no one will in fact deny that both propositions are true and deserve in a high degree to be generally recognized. — What Christianity asserts, in common with every better religion, about the *answerability of our petitionary prayers* is proved so clearly in § 223, and the objections raised against it are refuted so completely, that it should become evident to everyone that it can be so and not otherwise. For a providence which, in guiding our fortunes, would leave now our moral condition,

[p. 197]

now our wishes and needs entirely unheeded, could hardly be called either wise or kind. But if everything is to be taken into account, there is no doubt that our petitionary prayers, especially when they are supported by good actions and resolutions, must be regarded not as a ground *against* but only as a ground *for* the fulfillment of our wishes; and so we can with full confidence expect that God, provided they otherwise have the proper arrangement, never leaves our petitions unanswered.

It is certainly not out of place when the author, at the close of his doctrine of the sphere of action of man, also mentions *the vain and sinful ways* in which people have only too often striven for an enlargement of their sphere of action. It may well also be quite correct when he designates as such vain and sinful ways everything wherein we expect from our doing a result which we are entitled to expect neither by mere grounds of reason (experiences hitherto made and the like) nor by promises of divine revelation itself. Nevertheless it is a mistake that he sets this up so flatly as a *doctrine of Christianity*, and presupposes without further ado that the designations *superstition* and — (for he alternates with this word in what follows) — *sorcery* denote precisely this error and nothing else; whereas in fact the first word has a wider, the second a narrower meaning. For *superstition* is by no means merely the groundless expectation of an *effect*, but also quite a number of other groundless opinions; but *sorcery* we presumably call only the endeavor to bring about certain effects by the help of superhuman, ill-disposed spirits.

The *last section* of Christian-Catholic dogmatics, “*the doctrine of rewards and punishments in this life and in the next*” (§§ 226–234), divides into “*general theorems*” and “*theorems on rewards and punishments in the other life*.” The most important of the former are:

[p. 198]

“1) Every morally *good* action that we perform offers God a ground for letting a certain *happiness as a reward* flow to us; every morally *evil* one, a ground for allotting us a certain *unhappiness as punishment*. These grounds are what we call *merit* and *guilt*. And it is well to note that in many cases it may be said that the guilt has been *taken away* from us, while yet a *punishment* (a finite one) still *remains*.

“2) Among the *morally good* actions there are some whose performance earns a reward without their omission being connected with an actual punishment, unless one wished to call the mere absence of the reward already a punishment. Such actions one is accustomed to call *meritorious*, the rest *strict duties*.

“3) Among the *morally evil* actions there are not only those that draw after them a merely temporal punishment (*venial sins*), but also some that incur an eternal punishment (*mortal sins*).

“4) All *beneficial* consequences that a person *expects* from his action, and for whose sake he also resolves to undertake it, increase its *meritoriousness*, regardless of whether they actually occur or not. All *those harmful to the community* that he expected, and that nevertheless could not hold him back, increase his *guilt*, whether they occur or not.

“5) Whoever undertakes a good action from a moral ground, to him even those beneficial consequences that he had never foreseen shall be reckoned, to a certain though lesser degree, as *merit*. On the contrary, whoever does evil, to him even those bad consequences that he did not foresee, indeed *could* not even foresee, shall be reckoned, to a certain though lesser degree, as *guilt*.

[p. 199]

“6) Thus in particular all *other people’s sins* too, that is, sins of another, which we have *occasioned* through some *evil action of our own*, shall be reckoned to us as guilt.

“7) For the *bad*, on the other hand, which results from an action performed by us with good intention and proper caution, wholly *against our will*, we have *nothing to answer for*. Equally, however, we may conversely expect from that *good* which, through God’s wise guidance, springs from our evil actions *no diminution* of our guilt; although, if we have repented of the evil intention and have improved, it may serve us as some consolation.

“8) Whoever lives *virtuously*, that is, has the dominant disposition to comply with the moral law in all respects, may, even if he does not yet succeed in avoiding every *sin of frailty* or *venial sin*, nevertheless hope for a preponderant reward of his virtue. Whoever, on the other hand, lives in a *vicious* disposition, that is, has the dominant will to transgress the moral law in certain respects, may not in the least console himself with *all the other good actions* that he performs besides. So far is it from being the case that the guilt of his evil deeds could be offset by their merit that, on the contrary, they cannot even be reckoned to him as *true merit*, because they are not really but only *apparently* good. Nevertheless it happens through God’s mercy that such actions sometimes *diminish* the punishment of the sinner, indeed may even become the occasion of his *final improvement*.

“9) Whoever has formerly lived virtuously but then falls into a dominant, morally evil disposition: to him, from now on, even those really good actions that he performed in his still virtuous state will not snatch him from punishment, but they are now all, as it were, *extinguished*. Yet not forever and irretrievably;

[p. 200]

“but if he one day improves again, those good actions too revive, to be sure, but no longer entirely in their former splendor.

“10) Whoever lives in a virtuous state of mind may regard every good fortune that falls to his lot as a proof of divine good pleasure, as a reward; but every misfortune he may regard as a suffering that God sends him only for his trial and perfection, and that, if he bears it with patient resignation and turns it to account for his moral perfection, will resolve itself only into good fortune and salvation for him. The vicious man, on the other hand, indeed everyone who has committed a deliberate sin, must regard every good fortune that falls to his lot as an undeserved summons from God to his improvement, but every misfortune as a beginning of the *punishments* imposed on him by God.”

This is indeed what Christianity teaches, even though it has perhaps not been put together in just this way in any dogmatics so far; and so, and not otherwise, must every religion teach if it wishes to deserve the praise that its doctrine (on this point) possesses the greatest moral utility for us. The distinction between actions that are definite *duty and obligation* and those that are called merely *meritorious* has sometimes been contested out of mere misunderstanding; common human understanding presupposes it everywhere, and one must in fact not know what one is saying if one demands of God that he should punish by positive evils and sufferings everything that he cannot reward with heightened happiness.

Yet that, as said, is a misunderstanding of which only *scholars* have made themselves guilty; but the theorem that there are also *eternal* punishments, and the well-known Catholic doctrines that the author considers in § 231 ff., that only *a wholly guiltless person* can enter, immediately after death, into a *state of wholly pure, untroubled blessedness*, that others must first be purified through more or less painful, longer

[p. 201]

or shorter lasting sufferings, and finally that the number of the elect is even to be thought of as a comparatively very small number: these are doctrines which not only everyone of vicious disposition finds intolerable, for a very comprehensible reason, but even the virtuous man, who perhaps does not fear hell for his own person, is nevertheless saddened by the thought that there is even a single being in God’s creation for whom the lot of eternal damnation should be in store. But let one say what one will, these doctrines of Catholicism must remain as they are; nothing may be altered in them: and yet we hope, not only of our readers but of all rational human beings, that they will soon unanimously recognize their correctness and their necessity. For does it require more than the consideration of a single thought to see these doctrines most perfectly justified? does it require more than the thought that he does not err who pursues the salvation of his soul, through constant moral perfecting of it, with all the zeal that those doctrines can instill in him, but that only he deceives himself, to his own greatest and irretrievable disadvantage, who — because he does not believe — becomes more lax? Let all the rest that B. says in his proof of the conformity to reason of these articles (§§ 229 and 233) be open to doubt: if our readers are only unable to deny this one thing, which is as bright as daylight, they will also not deny that those doctrines have at least a figurative truth and deserve to be steadfastly held fast in it.

That figurative ideas are contained in the article “*on the Last Judgment*” (§ 231, no. 4), to whom would that not be evident of itself? But you ask how far this figurative element extends? We answer quickly: you may extend it as far as seems necessary to you, and you will always find that these ideas, even if considered only as mere images, are in the highest degree appropriate

[p. 202]

because they could not be better arranged to fill us with a holy awe before every sin, even the most secret, to fill us with comfort and joyfulness in misfortune of every kind, especially when the world misjudges us, and with quite a number of other salutary feelings that are enumerated in § 234, no. 4.

What is finally said in the last number of § 231, “*on the fate of non-Christians*,” finds in the remarks of § 233, no. 10, so complete a justification that we do not believe that any of our readers will feel compelled, on this point, to take refuge in a merely figurative interpretation. B. says:

“If the rejection of the Christian faith does not stem from an invincible ignorance, but passion, sloth and the like are the cause of it, then it is obviously very culpable, and the more so, the more distinct the consciousness is that one is resisting knowledge of the truth. — But that a rejection of the faith out of invincible ignorance can be imputed to no one as guilt and sin is a necessary truth of reason. — But that, on the other hand too, it is *not possible* to enjoy in their whole extent the higher advantages that Jesus Christ has obtained for the human race, if one has not first come to know him and accepted his teaching in faith, can be understood on more than one ground:

“α) Catholic Christianity acquaints us with a multitude of very important concepts of the true essence of human virtue, with a multitude of new and powerful means and incentives to virtue, with a multitude of most consoling and gratifying disclosures about the future, with a multitude of the most salutary truths: whoever, therefore, does not come to know this Catholic Christianity, even through no fault of his own, must nevertheless do without all the advantages that are a mere effect of these cognitions; he advances more slowly in his moral

[p. 203]

“perfection, and for that very reason also in his happiness.

“β) As for other advantages, for the enjoyment of which the teaching of Jesus would perhaps not be quite so indispensably necessary as the existence of the cause is for the coming into being of the effect: it seems, on another ground, not fitting that they should be imparted to those who do not know Jesus at all in just the same way as to those who know and revere him. For rational beings are never to enjoy a benefaction without knowing its author at least to some extent. For through knowledge of him the enjoyment becomes more refreshing and more salutary for them. They must rejoice more vividly in a good fortune that they owe not to blind chance but to the benevolent disposition of another; they obtain an opportunity to practice the duty of gratitude toward him; they feel, finally, a stronger obligation to use such a good fortune wholly according to the will of its author.

“γ) Let us finally suppose that the other life is a life of *social activity*, and that its blessedness depends chiefly on the appropriate constitution which the head of this society, the God-man himself, has given it, and on the exact observance of all his precepts: then it is quite comprehensible that no one can enter the heaven of Christians who has not first recognized Jesus as the rightful head in this state.

“*Note.* On the other hand, this very assumption permits the hope that, if we find ourselves there in society with other beings and there too continually increase in knowledge and moral perfection, then sooner or later even those human beings who, through no fault of their own, did not come to know Jesus on earth will have an opportunity there to become acquainted with him. If they then make quite the proper use of this acquaintance with Jesus, it may be hoped that God will in the end also make them partakers of that same higher blessedness,

[p. 204]

“which is prepared for us, who enjoy the good fortune of knowing him already on earth.”

The *third* and last chapter of the work furnishes, under the heading “*Christian-Catholic Morals*” (§§ 255–300), not, as it should, an outline of this morality, however condensed, yet complete and in systematic connection, but almost nothing other than *some doctrines peculiar to Christianity and in particular to Catholicism, belonging for the most part to ascetics*. That it was not possible for the author, in his position as a teacher, to do more, that he sought to make up in part, in another way — namely in his edifying discourses — for the disadvantage that the omission of so many doctrines from morals entailed: all this may be true; yet it remains a deficiency of the present work, considered as a *textbook of the science of religion*, that one misses in it a systematic survey of morals. However, this deficiency is by no means of such a kind that it would weaken the proof to be furnished here of the truth of Christianity, and specifically of Catholicism. For all the doctrines passed over here are doctrines of which it is expressly admitted that, at least in accordance with what has already been taught in dogmatics, they *can be recognized from mere grounds of reason*, and may also be interpreted only as they can be reconciled with reason. If, then, only *all* the doctrines that B. has *incorporated* in his book are compatible with reason and morally beneficial, it is already proved that this holds of *all doctrines of Catholicism*.

The author now singles out, in the *first* division, which is headed “*Christian-Catholic Ethics*” (§§ 236–271), first the doctrine of *existence* (§§ 236–238), then that of the *domain of the moral law* (§§ 239–243), then discusses very briefly the distinction, always firmly maintained in Catholic Christianity, between *commandments* and

[p. 205]

*counsels*, which is already justified by the distinction established in dogmatics between *obligation* and *merit*; after which he lists, under a designation peculiar to him, “*most general moral laws*” (§§ 249–253), several formulas current in Christianity that are so constituted that from each of them, if not all, yet *nearly all* duties of man can be derived in some way. As such he states the propositions:

“1) Act always as the general good (the welfare of the whole) requires.

“2) Follow in everything the will of God.

“3) Seek in everything only the promotion of the honor of God.

“4) Act always so that you become more like God.

“5) Love God above all.

“6) Imitate the example of Jesus.

“7) Love your neighbor as yourself.

“8) Treat everyone as you would wish him to treat you.”

Whatever special views of morals and of its supreme law anyone may have, he will be able to agree with what the author says here. For since it is left entirely open whether any of the formulas listed here is the true supreme law or not, he who finds his principle here cannot complain about the place that has been assigned to it. But he who does not find it will doubtless himself not deny that it is a proposition that has so far not enjoyed any general recognition. — That all these propositions, however, have the peculiarity that in any case a large part of our duties can be derived from them, hardly anyone will contradict.

The existence of *revealed* duties, too, asserted in §§ 254–256, our readers will certainly not find offensive when they consider with what *restriction* — tacitly always added in thought, but first expressed quite distinctly by our author — the proposition is to be understood,

[p. 206]

namely that a mode of action can be raised to a duty by revelation only if it also already appears to us, by our mere reason, as beneficial to morality.

The doctrine “*of right*,” which the author sets up in §§ 257–260, consists almost only of the few propositions:

“1) There are *rightful* as well as *wrongful* actions.

“2) All *morally good actions*, at least all that are so not merely subjectively but also *objectively*, we are for that very reason to count among the *rightful* ones, but not, conversely, to regard every rightful action forthwith as one that is also *morally permitted*.

“3) There are also *rightfully enforceable actions*; and not always are they, when he to whom the right of enforcing them belongs makes no use of it, also *duties*. A duty that is not enforceable, hence *free*, is not for that reason always a *lesser* one than the enforceable one.”

Against these few propositions, then, probably no one will have anything to object, to *whatever theory of right* he may be attached. However, we wish to append the definition of the concept of right adopted by the author merely to this end: that our readers, if it pleases them, might try with what ease not only the above but also many other doctrines of the science of right can be derived from this definition. B. says, namely, that an action is *rightful* if we are obliged to *tolerate* it, i.e. by no application of coercion to prevent it, however well we might perhaps be able to.<sup>∗)</sup> —

∗) On this occasion we permit ourselves to remark that further elaborations of this concept are also to be found in the “*Views of a Liberal-Minded Theologian on Church and State*” (Sulzbach 1834), and in the “*Freimüthige*

[p. 207]

In the doctrine “*of the authorities*” (§§ 261–264), too, the author sets up only the following few propositions:

“1) There are also *legitimate*, i.e. such *authorities* as one ought not to disturb in their power, even if one were able to.

“2) It never happens without the guidance of a special divine care that certain persons come into possession of a legitimate supreme power over others.

“3) Their commands, insofar as they do not concern something quite obviously evil, oblige subordinates to obedience, and this not merely because of the danger of punishment, but even in the case where no punishment is to be feared.

“4) But just as subordinates have the duty of obedience, so superiors have the duty to command only what is good and truly beneficial to the community, and will render account to God for the use of their power.”

Since we do not fear that anyone will fail to agree with these points, we merely remind the reader that the author, in § 263, attempts to determine the third article more closely according to his views. Even something that in itself is not beneficial to the welfare of the whole, and to that extent may be called *evil*, can become a duty through the command of a legitimate authority, as the author proves by several cases. Only then, he maintains, does the duty of obedience suffer an exception, when by obeying we would obviously bring about more evil than by the scandal of disobedience.

From the doctrine “*on promises and vows*” (§§ 265–267) we cite only the two assertions

“*Blätter von Pflug*” (Stuttgart 1838, vols. 14 and 15, in the essay: “*On the Right of the Clergy to Draw Their Livelihood from Persons Who Are Not of Their Faith*;”).

[p. 208]

that the obligation arising from *promises* or *agreements* ceases if it concerned something *absolutely evil*, of which even he who took the promise from us must have known that it is impermissible; and that *vows* made to God cease as soon as we do, in place of what was vowed, something else of which we clearly see that it is far better; although in such cases, not trusting our own judgment alone, we should, if possible, leave the matter to the decision of a reasonable counselor in matters of conscience.

The two remaining doctrines of this division: “*on the particular manner in which we human beings know the moral law, and the obligations that we have in this regard*” (§§ 268–269), and “*on the varying goodness and badness of man*” (§§ 270, 271), we must pass over entirely, so that we may keep room for several doctrines *peculiar* to Catholicism, which the *second division* sets forth from the “*Christian ascetics*” (§§ 272–300).

Under the heading: “*the most general doctrines on the use of the means of virtue*” (§§ 272, 273) it is merely asserted, and shown by reference to what has already been proved in natural ascetics, that there are means of virtue at all, that their application is a duty, that this holds in particular also of the use of the so-called *motives to virtue*, and that the most appropriate of all incentives to virtue is the one that is derived *from the will of God*. For these assertions mere sound human understanding does indeed already decide; nevertheless we hope that even those whom *Kantian purism* perhaps still confuses will learn to see, through the author’s clear exposition, how untenable and self-contradictory a theory is that, on the one hand, demands unconditionally, and without being able to point to any ground, that virtue and happiness shall enter into harmony, and from this infers even the existence of God and immortality,

[p. 209]

and on the other hand nevertheless forbids us, as something impermissible, the one thing for which this would be good, namely to encourage us, by looking to this arrangement, in our striving after virtue. —

The author then distinguishes *two kinds of means of virtue*, namely *natural* and *supernatural* ones, which one also calls *sacraments* or *means of sanctification* (so to speak *κατ’ ἐξοχὴν* [par excellence]). As for the distinction between natural and supernatural effects, however, our readers have no need to accept as correct the definition given by B. (which for that very reason he should not have woven into the exposition of the doctrine itself); although we think that his definition states exactly what has been had in mind in that distinction as the only true and essential thing; namely that the sacraments, when properly used, impart to us graces that are so great and extraordinary that we would never be entitled to presuppose their existence if revelation itself did not assure us of it, whereas the other means of virtue, e.g. prayer, have only such beneficial effects (e.g. its being answered) the existence of which we already recognize through our mere reason. Nothing, as it seems to us, can be easier than to show, according to this definition, the conformity to reason of the Catholic doctrine of the sacraments. For since each of them prescribes actions whose proper performance exerts the most beneficial influence on our morality: what is supposed to be incomprehensible in the fact that God promises to reward the worthy use of them also with other, greater graces and blessings than we would be entitled to expect? On precise consideration, the one thought that is already sufficient for the justification of all these means of sanctification is, after all, this: “that we never suffer any moral harm from it, can never in any respect regret it, if we perform those holy actions with all the devotion and all the trust that the idea that God has promised to bless them in so extraordinary a manner can ever inspire.”

[p. 210]

As we now propose to go through, first, what B. sets forth for us on the so-called *natural means of virtue in detail* (§§ 274–280), we must remind the reader that everything the Catholic Church teaches here is supposed, in the opinion of its own theologians, to be demonstrable also from mere reason. If, then, any assertion that is presented in these §§ as a doctrine of the whole Church admitted of a twofold interpretation — one according to which it is a pure truth of reason, and another according to which its truth could not be proved from mere reason — fairness would demand that it be understood in the first way. But as for what is not even a doctrine of the Church, that is, of the *universal* one (and there is much that B. sets forth here intermingled which is not one in the strictest sense of the word, but merely a doctrine *prevailing* in the Church; and there is other matter that is entirely just his *own opinion*; as, generally, what he gives us as the *ideal purpose* that ought to be laid at the foundation of this and that religious exercise): — in all this it goes without saying that one may reject it, and nevertheless always find conclusive the proof of the truth of Catholicism that he furnishes in this book. There is discussion here of 1) *prayer*; 2) *public worship*; 3) the exercises in the art of *self-control*; 4) the exercises of *beneficence* toward the poor and the suffering; 5) the veneration and imitation of *glorified friends of virtue*; 6) the use of the *fine arts* for moral purposes; 7) the appropriate cultivation of one’s *own inclinations*. We can single out only one thing and another.

After § 274 has spoken of the different *kinds of prayer*, of the use of *prayer formulas*, and other matters, one reads in no. 6: “Since, however, it is known that the most useful thing is often left undone if it is not assigned a *definite time*: so the Catholic Church not only gives, in general, the counsel to pray often, indeed *without ceasing* (1 Thess. 5:17);

[p. 211]

“but there are also *fixed*, exceedingly fitting *times* for prayer proposed to us; we are in particular exhorted to consecrate by prayer the *morning* and *evening* of each day, the *noon hour*, but especially the *beginning of every important undertaking*. To this end we are reminded, at fixed hours of the day, even by the *call of a bell*, to make a brief pause in our affairs, in order to raise our spirit, united with thousands who are doing the same at this very moment, to our common Creator.”

As the *purpose of public worship* the author gives in § 275 — in opposition to others, who in his view conceive this purpose too one-sidedly — “*the sum of all the good that is at all attainable through it*; thus, e.g., making the greatness and majesty of God perceptible to the senses, before whom we all here, even the most eminent among us, see ourselves prostrate in the dust; enlivening of all religious feelings through the perception that we feel them not alone, but in common with so many others; symbolic representation of the essential equality of all human beings; fraternal drawing together of them, etc.” — Speaking of the *adornment* of the temples, he voices the thought: “whether everything that is costly and splendid should not be set up only in the temples” (and other public buildings) “so that, instead of feeding the vanity of individuals, it may serve solely for the glorification of God?” — “One must,” it says directly afterwards, “deliberately misinterpret the doctrine of the Church if one construes its regulations as though, in its view, man were in need of the clergy as a certain absolutely necessary mediator between himself and God; as though in his prayers he might by no means turn directly to God himself, etc. Only for the sake of good order, only in order to give public worship more perfection, does the Church have it conducted by its clergy, as persons specially instructed for this purpose.”

[p. 212]

— The *public processions* the author takes under his protection; and the *pilgrimages* too, he thinks, deserve, given a *proper* organization, always to be retained. “For journeys,” (he says) “now shorter, now longer, are after all beneficial for almost every person, and can serve now to strengthen his health, now to broaden his concepts, now for still other purposes. By being raised to a *pilgrimage*, i.e. by being joined to a religious purpose, with the firm resolve to make the best use on this journey of every occasion that serves his edification, they become all the more beneficial. And if such journeys are undertaken in good company and under proper supervision, many of the dangers to which body and soul can be exposed on journeys fall away on such pilgrimages.”

“Although the heads of the Catholic Church,” it says in § 276, “knew very well that there are all manner of desires on which we have occasion to practice the *art of self-control*; although they also did not fail to draw our attention specially to various of them in this regard (namely to the desires to hear, to see, to say something, etc.): they nevertheless recognized that almost no other lends itself as the object of an *exercise to be prescribed generally* than the — *desire for food*. The occasion to practice mastery of this arises for everyone daily, and at the same time this exercise saves a considerable quantity of foodstuffs, which in turn can serve others for enjoyment; i.e. it is an exercise that even in itself yields an advantage for the whole.” Etc.

In § 278 one reads that according to the principles of the Catholic Church it is to be regarded as *a loss for humanity*, *if any example of virtue remains in concealment*; all those, therefore, who know a virtuous person would be called upon to bring his example to more general knowledge;

[p. 213]

but only after his death; it is incumbent on the heads of the Church to bring about as faithful and credible a history of his life as possible; in this it is not to be demanded that the person have been wholly perfect, but only that there have been so many and such sublime virtues that one may expect their presentation to have an edifying effect; above all one should endeavor to set up *models of every kind of virtue*; also to single out such models, where possible, from every sex, age, estate and trade, people and epoch, etc.

In § 279 B. asserts: “*nothing is perfectly beautiful that is not also morally good*; in the religious ideas of Catholicism, in the narratives of the Bible, of the Old as well as the New Testament, in the lives of the saints, etc., there is an inexhaustible store of material for representations in every kind of the fine arts,” etc.

Characteristic of the author’s endeavor to show, of every institution still existing, to what use it ought to be put, without thereby speaking up for its continuance, is the long note to § 280 on the *evangelical counsels and monastic vows*, the communication of which, however, space forbids us.

The doctrine of the *sacraments* the author begins, § 281, with the “*doctrine of their valid and dutiful use in general*,” so misinterpreted from the Protestant side. It turns out that one may not teach otherwise than the Catholic Church does here, if on the one hand the lax person is not to be set at ease but, on the contrary, is to recognize the full magnitude of the punishability of his offense, while on the other hand he who has done everything that lay in his power is to know how to console himself over the worry whether the other too has fully discharged his duty. Must not, for this purpose, a twofold,

[p. 214]

a merely *valid* as well as a *worthy use* of every means of sanctification be distinguished, and it be said of the former that it takes place whenever the minister merely performs, with outward earnestness, certain acts declared *essential* by the Church, and the recipient, if he is an adult, has manifested his will to receive the sacrament? How terrible, and yet at the same time how true, when it is now added that such an administration, if it was unworthy in the doing; likewise also such a reception, precisely on account of that validity, entails, for him who acted unworthily in it, not merely ordinary but *supernatural* penalties, i.e. such penalties as are known only from revelation itself? What reassurance, on the other hand, for everyone who has taken pains to receive not merely validly but also worthily, or only to administer, when he is assured that the other’s guilt, perhaps wholly hidden from him, does not rob him of the blessings of heaven. But let us now pass on to the individual means of sanctification.

In the “*Doctrine of Catholicism on Holy Baptism, or Admission Ceremony*” (§§ 282–284) one reads:

“1) Whoever, after mature examination of Catholic Christianity, has recognized the truth of it, upon him this very Christianity places the demand that he make known the conviction that has come to him as publicly as possible, and at the same time solemnly vow to arrange his life in future wholly according to its precepts.

“2) Upon this vow he is to be received into the number of Christians, as solemnly as possible, by a head of the congregation, if it may be presumed that he is in earnest about it and that he will not live to the Church’s shame. The sign of this admission is to be a cleansing of the body, or else a pouring or sprinkling with natural water that merely indicates this cleansing; whereby the actual purpose of the act is to be expressed by the definite words: *I hereby baptize* (i.e. dedicate)

[p. 215]

“*you to the worship of God the Father, of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*,” etc.

What the Church here presents as alone essential and unalterable is so simple an act, and of such evident expediency, that it is hardly possible to raise anything against it. The additions which the Church itself admits to be defective and, according to time and circumstances, subject to alteration may seem to the reader to be in need of an improvement ever so great: since it is not satisfaction with everything that *exists and is practiced* in the Church, but solely the acceptance of what it presents *to be believed*, through which we are and become Catholics, views of such a kind can never become a sufficient ground for the rejection of Catholicism.

The effects ascribed to baptism in no. 3 (they are the usual ones) no one can find objectionable who has already acknowledged as rational the preceding doctrines of the forgiveness of our sins, of original sin, and of the workings of the Holy Spirit. The *infant baptism* mentioned in no. 5 is again merely a *custom* of the Church, which someone might disapprove of, provided he does not go so far as to assert that such a baptism is wholly invalid. That, however, he will hardly feel tempted to do who has read the justification given in § 284, no. 5. For he will then certainly see that such an act, even if it cannot edify the child, at least not at the time when it is performed on him, nevertheless has other beneficial effects. And if he now finds that the same theologians who defend infant baptism declare a baptism attempted against the will of the person to be baptized, or (if he is a child) against the will of his parents or guardians, not only unlawful but even invalid; if, just as with baptism, he becomes aware with every other sacrament too that it is only there, where the *natural consequences* of the act cease to be morally beneficial, that one also no longer

[p. 216]

wishes to admit that supernatural consequences of a beneficial kind are to take place: will he not then feel compelled to recognize in the decisions of the Church the guidance of the Spirit of God all the more certainly, the less he is willing to credit the individual teachers themselves with having been clearly conscious of the rule according to which they were to frame their decisions?

“The assertion that baptism impresses an *indelible mark* on the soul” (writes the author, § 284, no. 6), “has only the sense that one who has been baptized is from now on to be regarded forever as wholly different from one not yet baptized. And this is very reasonable. Strictly speaking, every act which a human being performs either himself, or which others perform on him, has consequences extending into the infinite, so that from that time on he could be regarded forever as another than he would be if this act had not taken place. But it would not be good to pursue this thought with every act. Only with performances which are of special importance, which further have certain very beneficial consequences, and which finally impose on the human being particular duties and obligations, will it be advisable to take this to heart. Nowhere more advisable, then, than with the act of holy baptism. From this point of view, namely, baptism appears to the human being in its whole importance, in that he considers that it has consequences extending into eternity. Thus he rejoices all the more deeply in the good fortune of being baptized; thus he also feels all the more strongly the obligation to remain, throughout his whole future life, by the vows made in it; thus too all his fellow Christians have the duty, as long as he lives, to exert themselves more zealously on his behalf, to regard him, even if he should have fallen away from the faith, still as a Christian, and so to work for his being brought back,” etc.

The “*Doctrine of Holy Confirmation, or Ceremony of Ratification*” (§ 285) the author begins thus:

[p. 217]

“1) Whoever has been received through baptism as a member of the Christian congregation is to make known, if he received the former in his childhood, or at any rate not in an entirely solemn manner and not from the bishop himself, at an age when he already has complete command of the doctrines of Catholicism, his conviction of their truth, and his vow to live according to them, before the congregation to which he belongs as a member, and before its bishop.

“2) This declaration is to be received with joy by the bishop and the rest of the congregation, if it may be presumed, on the judgment of special persons (the *confirmation sponsors*), that it is seriously meant, and is to be answered by their jointly interceding for him with God, that the Holy Spirit may be pleased to equip him with his gifts and graces and to strengthen him for the fulfillment of his laudable resolution. As a sign that this strengthening will also actually take place, the bishop is to anoint the one to be confirmed, as fighters are anointed, with holy oil,” etc.

The rest is as usual; but we should like to ask whether anyone could well find anything to object to in this ceremony, other than that admission to this holy act is often permitted at an age that is far too youthful, and that for this very reason the confirmand is generally allowed to take too little active part in it himself? Faults whose correction lies wholly within the power of our ecclesiastical heads.

“The *Doctrine of the Holy Lord’s Supper*” reads, with some abridgments, as follows:

“1) The Catholic Church requires of its members that, as circumstances permit, they hold frequent assemblies in order to worship God in common as well. If now a bishop or a priest is present, there is to be celebrated, among other things, also a *common meal*, which is to be an imitation of that *Supper* with which Jesus took leave of his disciples. The bishop or priest is to represent on this occasion the person of the Lord, the rest of the faithful, however, the apostles. The former

[p. 218]

“is to take bread and wine, in imitation of what we read in the Gospel about the Lord, and, after he has spoken over them the words of the Lord, ‘*This is my body, my blood*,’ partake of them and also offer them to those present for their partaking.

“2) As soon as those words over the bread and the wine are merely pronounced, *the essence* of the bread and of the wine has already ceased to be there, and under the mere *forms* that still linger here Jesus Christ is truly and essentially present according to his divine as well as his human nature; not, say, in such a way that he fills the space of these forms and in one part of them is present with this part of himself, in another with another of his parts, and, if one acts upon these forms so as to change them, would himself also undergo changes: rather Christ is wholly present in every part of them; yet in such a way that his presence ceases as soon as they themselves cease to be forms of bread and wine (edible foodstuffs).

“3) We are not only permitted, but it is even our duty, to adore the God-man who presents himself to us under these forms; to treat these forms, accordingly, with the greatest possible reverence, etc. In particular, in their presence we are to apply ourselves to as fervent a devotion as we should deem our duty if our Lord appeared before us in human form.

“4) And if we do this, and — after we have first prepared ourselves properly by all the means at our disposal — in humble trust in Jesus’ own invitation venture to partake of something of those blessed forms as well: then our soul will be nourished and strengthened by Jesus in a way just as beneficial as bread and wine are wont to be the most strengthening foods of the body, and just as intimately as such foods unite with the body, Jesus too will unite himself with our soul.

[p. 219]

“5) Even if, in the feeling of our unworthiness, or out of concern that frequent repetition might make us indifferent, we abstain from the actual partaking of the holy meal, but are nevertheless present with all the devotion possible to us, and ask for the imparting of some blessings: we do not ask in vain.

“6) Whoever, on the other hand, attends the holy act without devotion merely makes himself liable to punishment; and whoever dares to receive the body of the Lord without first having put himself in a state of mind pleasing to God, eats and drinks his own judgment.

“7) To become a partaker of the blessed effects of the holy meal, it suffices to receive it even under one form only.

“8) The custom, which existed among almost all peoples, of honoring God, among other ways, by *sacrifices*, is to continue among us as well; yet in such a way that we no longer believe we become pleasing to God if we deprive one of his creatures of life, or destroy any useful object: rather we are to know that God requires no other sacrifice than the renunciation of every advantage of our own, and even of life, when the common good and the laws of virtue demand it; that for this very reason, of all the sacrifices ever offered to God, none has been more pleasing to him than the sacrifice which Jesus Christ offered through his voluntary atoning death on the cross. We are further to believe that Christ, as often as we celebrate this holy meal on earth, learns of it in heaven, that it is pleasing to him, that he intercedes with the Father for us, indeed, to speak figuratively, reminds the latter anew of his merits toward us, and so makes him more favorably disposed toward the answering of our petitions. For this very reason we are, each of us making use of this occasion, to pray not only for ourselves but also for others, relatives and friends, living and dead, with the most certain success of being heard,” etc.

[p. 220]

From § 288, which discusses the *rationality* and the *moral utility* of this doctrine, we pick out only a few disconnected remarks: “We can say already that the *essence* of the bread and wine is no longer there, if only the effects which the materials present under these forms exert upon us have, through the addition of certain other circumstances, now become entirely different and infinitely more beneficial than the effects of ordinary bread or wine can ever become.” — “Whether the activity of Jesus in the holy Lord’s Supper is an entirely *unmediated* one or not must be a matter of indifference to us, if we think reasonably; and for that very reason the decision of this question belongs to a merely idle curiosity, not at all to religion and to the domain of ecclesiastical infallibility.” — “To say that in the holy Lord’s Supper we may assume nothing more than a *merely figurative* presence of Jesus is to say that we may promise ourselves from the partaking of this meal absolutely no other and greater benefit than we would have from any lively remembrance of Jesus. To assert that Jesus is indeed really present, but *only in the partaking*, is to abolish one of the most important natural advantages that faith in this presence could afford us.” — “It is a remarkable example of how enlightened the heads of the Catholic Church were in their thinking when they rejected as a *superstition* the opinion that one must partake of the holy Lord’s Supper under *both* forms in order to share in its blessings,” etc.

With regard to the Catholic doctrine “*of the institution of penance and amendment*,” as it is presented and justified in §§ 289–291, we fear no objections on the part of our readers, provided they are in genuine earnest about their own moral development, which we must presuppose. But whoever is accustomed to heed the promptings of his sensuality more than the demands of his reason, and also wishes to retain the freedom to go on doing so,

[p. 221]

him, indeed, what the author says here will not convince, for he does not *want* to be convinced. For him everything, too, that we could remark here on the independence of the doctrine at hand from the author’s other views and assertions would be in vain.

Concerning the Catholic doctrine “*of the married state*” (§§ 292–294), too, we have nothing to remark; for it must be evident to everyone that only by such principles as are set forth here is the best provision made for the propagation and upbringing of the human race, and at the same time a natural satisfaction of the sexual instinct made possible for the greatest number of individuals. It is only to be lamented that the influence which the concepts of Christianity could and should exercise upon the gradual improvement of our morals, customs and institutions has not to this day yet succeeded in removing from our civil as well as ecclesiastical constitution everything that directly contradicts those beneficent purposes. Moreover, the Catholic Church teaches nothing here that mere reason too would not already declare necessary, and that all Christian religions would not have retained; unless it be the proposition that God blesses the holy act of the marriage bond, if we perform it in the proper manner, *supernaturally*, i.e. as abundantly as we would not be entitled to hope for at all without his own declaration. Whether now the welfare of humanity demands that this proposition be struck out the sooner the better, let each judge for himself. So much is evident, that neither the *indissolubility of marriage* (a disciplinary ordinance subject to alteration, existing only in the *Latin* Church), nor the reprehensibility of every marriage bond entered into by a Catholic with one of another faith can rightly be inferred from the sacramental dignity of this bond.

In the doctrine now following, “*of the Church and of its heads, or the clerical*

[p. 222]

“*estate*” (§ 295) the author has again not separated clearly enough from the generally accepted doctrines some of his private views, e.g. on the *purpose of the Church* (no. 2), on its *members* (no. 5), on the *limits of ecclesiastical power* (no. 21), and several others. Moreover, with the great number of points set forth here, everything is given too briefly and indefinitely; and still less was there room enough, in the justification of all these points (§ 297), to expound the views, in part very peculiar, as would be necessary for their full understanding. He had to be prepared, then, for the fact that not everyone would agree with him throughout in this §. We shall pick out the most important points, and add to some of them at once something of what he has put forward in justification of them.

“1) The Catholic doctrinal system makes it the duty of everyone who has convinced himself of its truth to join the religious society which Jesus founded, and which he also continually heads as its supreme leader and lawgiver.

“The concept of a *religious society* or church is precisely this, that it is a society which it becomes our duty to join already merely on the ground that we have become convinced of the truth of a certain religion. It is reasonable that every religion should demand of its adherents that they come together into a society of their own.

“2) The purpose of this society, however, consists in general in the attainment of *all that good in time and eternity* which can be brought about through *such a sum of forces* as come together there.

“From the purpose that brought about the *origin* of a society, or even merely our joining it, the purpose that the union of human beings, once *in existence*, *has to set* itself, must always be distinguished,

[p. 223]

“and the latter be made to consist in the attainment of all the good that is ever possible through it.

“5) *Members* of the Church are in general all those through whose being counted as belonging to it some *overriding advantage* is attained. Hence, e.g., under certain circumstances even *children* not yet of age.

“7) One can belong to the Church now only *inwardly* (in disposition), now only *outwardly*, now in both ways at once.

“8) Whoever has the *orthodox disposition*, i.e. is prepared to believe everything that God has revealed, or what presents itself to him at all as truth, and also wishes to live accordingly, belongs to the Church by this alone already *inwardly*; supposing even that some unjust verdict of earthly judges deprives him of the outward signs, or that he has never yet received any such signs of admission, or has not even yet come to know the doctrines of the Catholic religion.

“17) This Church can truthfully assert that one must belong to it at least *inwardly* in order to partake of eternal blessedness.

“18) In the *visible* Church there is always to be a special estate whose task it is first of all to make itself perfectly familiar with the truths of religion, and then also to instruct others in them.

“20) Besides the business of instruction, the direction of divine worship and the administration of the means of sanctification are also to be entrusted to this estate.

“21) The prestige which the aforementioned rights and duties give this estate, and the power which the secular authorities concede to it, it is to use in order to issue many *salutary ordinances*.

“It is reasonable to determine the *limits of ecclesiastical power* in no other way than by the

[p. 224]

“limits of *possibility* itself; for any other determination would be faulty, or at most would hold for a time only. Thus it is, e.g., wholly false to say: only what concerns *virtue* and the *salvation of the soul*, on that would the *ecclesiastical authority* have to decide. For since hardly anything is wholly indifferent to these ends, the clergy, on this principle, would have to be entitled to issue decrees about all things, without exception. — Sound reason says that every human being ought to do as much good as he is in any way able to, among other ways also by commanding it to others who are ready to obey him. Hence it goes without saying that the ecclesiastical authority too has the duty to issue ordinances about everything in which the faithful are willing to obey it, and in which the secular authority does not hinder it. But since the attitudes of the latter differ in different countries and times, no more definite rule than the foregoing can for that very reason be given.

“22) The members of the clerical estate are to serve the rest as a *model for imitation*; hence to stand on a decidedly higher level of perfection.

“24) If the secular authority permits it, the *congregation* is to exercise the right of election.

“26) There are to be in this estate both *different gradations* in the capacity conceded to each for the *administration of the means of sanctification* and in the outward *official authority* actually granted him.

“28) No one is to be admitted to a higher degree of ordination without having first received all the subordinate ones, and having proved himself worthy of receiving a higher ordination by a laudable administration of certain offices suited to these.

“30) With regard to *offices* there are to be in the Catholic Church:

[p. 225]

“a) *One primate*, i.e. head of the whole of Christendom, who in this capacity is to be, as it were, the vicar of Christ. (At present the *bishop of Rome*).

“b) To each of the other *bishops* the office of supervision is to be assigned over a definite, not too extensive part of Catholic Christendom.

“c) If the latter cannot satisfy all the spiritual needs of his congregation alone, he is to appoint subordinate pastors to supervise smaller parts, etc.

“The necessity of a *primate* is not hard to demonstrate. Even if Jesus has promised that he himself would remain the invisible head of the Church through all times, it goes without saying that he did not thereby intend to relieve us of the duty of making all those arrangements by which, in human judgment, evil can most readily be prevented and good brought about. And among these a suitably organized *primacy* unquestionably belongs. In every society that is to endure for long, it will have decided advantages if there is a single individual on whom, by declaring him the *head of the whole society*, one has laid the obligation of continually reflecting on what the society is to do, and of not merely making known what seems best to him, but of working, by requests, exhortations, commands and the like, toward its being carried out; etc. — That, however, the rights and duties of the primate are not laid down by revelation itself has its good ground. Otherwise nothing in them could properly be altered, which at different times is nevertheless extremely necessary. Certainly there were times when it was very good that the primate of the Church merely put forward, by way of request, what he wished to see carried out by others; and again other times when he might, and should, gradually adopt a commanding tone. There were also times when it was quite salutary that the pope took it upon himself to decide in matters of which it is said in our days that they lie wholly outside the sphere of his power.

[p. 226]

“And again there is now a time when it is necessary that he desist from such demands and merely request some things, etc.

“35. No clergyman may regard the *income* that his ecclesiastical office brings him as his property acquired through these services; he is rather to regard it as a good *belonging to the Church*, i.e. as a good that may be applied only to *beneficent purposes*, e.g. in particular to the support of the poor. For himself he may, only when he cannot procure his livelihood in any other way, take as much as he necessarily needs for such a purpose. Whoever has taken more is obliged to restore it.

“What is here made a duty for the clergyman is really an obligation incumbent also on all other human beings, only not in the same degree. The difference between what we call our *property* and what is called *common property* does not consist in the purpose to which it is to be applied; it is not permitted us to consume property merely for ourselves, regardless of what we could accomplish with it for others; rather, like common property, we are always to use it only in such a way that the common good gains most by it; thus for ourselves, if we need it more than others, for others, if they need it more than we. The only difference is that for our use of property we have to render account only to our *conscience*, but for our use of common property also to *others* (the state, for example). In conscience, then, all human beings are really bound to regard their property as common property, and to consume of it, for the satisfaction of their own needs, only as much as is absolutely necessary, but to apply the rest to the poor, or at any rate to purposes that are of public benefit. For the clerical estate one could raise this procedure to an all the more stringent obligation; in the first place already for the reason that it is to light the way for the rest by its example;

[p. 227]

“and then also because the income attached to ecclesiastical offices has generally arisen only from the pious gifts of the faithful, who gave them only with the definite intention that they be applied to the poor or to other beneficent purposes, so that the clergy would themselves have a partial claim to it only insofar as they were to be counted among the poor, i.e. insofar as they would otherwise be quite unable to live.” —

What now, in all these assertions, is actually *doctrine of the Church* is so evident to mere sound human understanding that a fair-minded thinker will hardly wish to dispute it. For do not even the non-Catholic parties agree with us on most of these points? and have not the differences that are nevertheless found between their doctrine and ours all arisen in a very explicable way? arisen, namely, only from vexation at the outrageous abuses to which those doctrines had given occasion precisely at that time? Would the necessity of a primacy ever have been misjudged, had not so many popes arrogated to themselves a power really too great, and harmful in its working, for the welfare of the Church? Would one ever have hesitated to concede to the ordination to the clerical estate a power of blessing promised by God, had one not feared the inferences which — though quite without warrant — were drawn from such a premise?

Whoever, however, has found in the doctrines of Catholicism discussed so far no ground for rejecting it will certainly not find one in the doctrine that the author has put last, in the doctrine “*of the help to be rendered to the sick and dying, or of the last anointing*” (§§ 298–300).

“1) If a Christian” (says the author) “is prevented for a longer time by circumstances of illness, indeed perhaps by other causes as well, from attending the assemblies for worship, he is to be entitled to have the clergy of the congregation called to him

[p. 228]

“so that they may impart to him the gift of instruction he has been missing, instruct him according to his needs, console him, pray with him in common, provide him with the holy sacraments of penance and of the altar, etc.

“2) The priests are to let themselves be deterred neither by the hardships of the journey, nor by the dangers of contagion, nor by other regard for merely bodily goods, from complying with the request of anyone who asks them for such a service to be rendered to the soul, whoever he may be.

“3) If the danger of death grows greater, the clergyman is to be called again, and after he has sent up to the throne of God many a heartfelt prayer for the forgiveness of the sick man’s sins, for the alleviation of his sufferings, indeed also (if it is for his soul’s salvation) for his complete recovery, he is to anoint him with holy oil on various parts of the body, where propriety permits, such as on the five organs of sense.

“4) For the sake of these prayers and anointings God will, if it is conducive to the sick man’s salvation, bring about his recovery, and if this is not so, bestow on him those supernatural graces which he needs in order to die a blessed death.”

Whoever has once raised himself to that purified concept of a revelation which was set up right in the first volume of the Textbook will not demand, for the essence of a sacrament, that it have been instituted directly and in express words by Christ; for that very reason he will also take no exception to the means of sanctification just discussed, or to the others of the Catholic Church, merely because such words of institution cannot be shown for them in the Bible; he will not believe that anything more is necessary in order to accept all these sacraments with the most perfect confidence than to recognize what his innermost feeling attests to him, that they are acts which are in the highest degree edifying and morally beneficial to us.

[p. 229]

And so we have now faithfully indicated how few, and how easily conceded, are the presuppositions that one must grant in order to find the proof which is conducted for the truth of Catholic Christianity in B.’s Textbook convincing for oneself as well. Before we conclude, we have only to guard ourselves against a misinterpretation of our intention, which is the more to be feared the more often experience has already taught that certain malevolent people, or even merely people unpracticed in thinking, find offense, through an incorrect interpretation, even in the most innocent things that B. or the friends of his views say. For it is neither our opinion, nor was it ever B.’s opinion, that the readers of his book should regard everything that he does not precisely presuppose in his proof as *a necessary concession* as something *undecided*, or even dispute and reject it as something *demonstrably false*. If a mathematician draws our attention to the fact that various propositions from which we have hitherto derived some important theorem of this science need not necessarily be presupposed for it, since there is a much shorter series of inferences from which it can be concluded (as B. himself has done with regard to many mathematical doctrines): to whom does it occur to believe that he wishes to declare at once unreliable, or even false, the propositions which he does not need for his proof? The same holds for every other science; only with this difference, that in the case of *religious doctrines*, because of people’s great inclination to call everything into doubt here, it is a special merit of importance to demonstrate such truths *by the shortest possible way and from presuppositions that can hardly be disputed in any way*. By no means need he who supplies such simpler proofs, who seeks to show us how many of the presuppositions made hitherto can quite properly be dropped — by no means need he do this because he believes in his own mind that those presuppositions which he has made dispensable were all false, or even merely unreliable.

[p. 230]

Only because he knows that they have been disputed by *others*, or fears that they could, if not hitherto, then perhaps one day still *be* disputed, does he consider it, and certainly not without reason, a worthwhile undertaking to show how we would not necessarily have to concede all of them first in order to convince ourselves that a certain religious doctrine has its irrefutable correctness and will retain it forever, whatever discoveries time may one day yet bring to light. In this, *the degree of reliability* which those propositions may possess remains wholly undiminished; nor is their *usefulness in other respects*, if they have any, taken from them. We may get to know them; we may, now as before, assert their truth and prove it by all the grounds that can be found for them. All this, we say, remains permitted to us in future not only as before, but the task of determining the degree of their reliability in a manner conformable to the truth can and must be made considerably easier for us by this: that we can consider them *more impartially*, because our *religion*, i.e. that part of our convictions which surpasses all the others in importance, stands firm quite independently of the outcome of our investigations.

If, to close these general considerations with an example which is certainly the most important, — if, according to B.’s views, it is not unavoidably necessary to have first convinced oneself of the *historical* credibility of the books of the New Testament in order to be convinced of the truth of Catholic Christianity: may we, on that account, go so far as to concede *no importance at all* any longer to the Gospel history? Certainly not; to learn what we can learn from it alone, how the most perfect of all mortals was disposed, and how he conducted himself in the most varied circumstances of life, that is and must remain, for every educated person, a matter of high significance. However true it may be, then, that, for example, *Strauss’s* well-known book on the Life of Jesus, like many another older work of similar content,

[p. 231]

need not concern us so long as we are occupied simply with investigating the truth or falsity of Christianity (especially as Catholics understand it): far be it from us to regard and receive such writings with indifference; far be it from us not to think it worth the trouble to examine them, and to examine them with the greatest diligence! And if we do this with all that tranquility of mind which is made possible precisely by the thought that our *religious faith* cannot in any case be shaken, whatever the result of our examination might be: will the truth then escape us? —
