## LA PSYCHOLOGIE DU MYSTICISME — THE PSYCHOLOGY OF MYSTICISM

If it were to be studied from every side, this subject would be extremely complex: it would fall within the province of theology, of literature, of history, of the physical and moral sciences. We could not, in particular, dispense with approaching it from the physiological and pathological point of view, as well as from that of inner observation. But the division of labor has its place even in science, and no doubt it is not impossible, without considering the organic manifestations of mysticism, to disentangle some of its features that are interesting and useful to know.

The mystics were often great psychologists themselves. The observation of the inner life was always their dominant concern. Now, unless we take them all for sick people, we must make a point of noting the discoveries they believed they had made in the domain of the human soul.

It is true that the mystics are sometimes presented as mere sick people. If that were so, we should certainly have to give up speaking of mysticism without studying [p. 6] its physiological and pathological side; but, taking the word in its broad and historical sense, it does not seem that we have the right to class the mystics straightaway among the sick.

An attempt has been made to show that Socrates was a sick man, because some leaning toward mysticism was found in him. Nothing is more contrary to likelihood. His was a sound and robust mind, a tireless reasoner, celebrating and practicing above all else self-possession. Were a Francis of Assisi, a Saint Bernard, a Spinoza, a Schleiermacher, in whom the share of mysticism is great or preponderant, sick men? Pascal will be cited, and the abyss he constantly saw at his side, and the accident on the bridge of Neuilly, which had unsettled his brain. But these anecdotes are without foundation, and present-day criticism strikes them from his biography. As for the rapture he experienced on November 23, 1664 [recte 1654], the memory of which he recorded in a piece that may be called his memorial, this phenomenon, in part physiological, was not a cause but an effect of mysticism. It was thought concentrated for months on one and the same object that, at a given moment, produced corresponding sensations in the organism. Something, not similar but analogous, happened to the man of the most sober good sense, to Descartes.

#### I

Mysticism consists, according to a fine definition that I find in Plotinus, in seeing with the eyes closed (μύσαντα ὄψιν [having closed the eyes]), in seeing with the eyes of the soul while the eyes of the body are closed. The essential phenomenon of mysticism is what is called ecstasy, a state [p. 7] in which, all communication with the outer world being broken off, the soul has the feeling that it is communicating with an inner object which is the infinite being, God.

But it would be forming an incomplete idea of mysticism to concentrate it entirely in this phenomenon, which is its culminating point. Mysticism is essentially a life, a movement, a development, of a determinate character and direction. To tell the truth, not all the phases of this development present themselves in an equally distinct or manifest way in all the mystics; one can nevertheless, by comparing the accounts of the greatest of them, come to form a fairly clear and fairly unified idea of what the whole of the mystical development is, in its normal and complete form. I am going to try, so far as it is possible to reduce life to formulas, to mark the stages of this development.

The point of departure, the first moment, is a state of soul difficult to define, which the German word *Sehnsucht* characterizes rather well. It is a state of vague, uneasy desire, very real and capable of being very intense as a passion of the soul, very indeterminate or rather very inexplicable in its object and in its cause. It is an aspiration toward something unknown, toward a good necessary to the heart and unrepresentable to the intellect. A state of this kind may, to tell the truth, be met with in very diverse men and have very different meanings. In the mystic it is deep and lasting, it works upon the soul, and little by little the soul forms an idea of the object of its aspiration. This revelation is not direct. But, according to mystical experience, more or less suddenly, the things amid which we live, on which it seemed that our judgment was settled, [p. 8] appear to us in another light. What used to charm us loses its color; what we admired becomes debased; our dearest affections cease to fill our heart. The objects of the world no longer hold us, but each of them has the effect of awakening in us the idea of its contrary. In everything that offers itself to our gaze we see only the deformation, the vain image, dull and dead, of a living, perfect and infinite model, which perceptible realities are powerless to express. We conceive, as the supreme object of our desires: the infinite, the eternal, the perfect, God. And reflecting on the feeling that was the point of departure of this conception, we understand why uneasiness was mingled in it with need, why we could neither withdraw from this feeling nor satisfy it. It was the still unconscious idea of an infinite object that created in our consciousness an indefinable unease in connection with the possession of all finite objects. In the passage of this idea from the sphere of the unconscious to that of distinct consciousness consists the first phase of the mystical development.

The second will be the effort to transform oneself inwardly, in conformity with this idea. This effort necessarily expresses itself in a struggle. Indeed, all these objects that surround us, and which we now regard as unworthy of us, we are attached to them by a thousand bonds, we are accustomed to them, we live by them, our heart takes its pleasure in them. Now we know at present that we ought not to love them, that God alone is the worthy object of the human soul. But an idea is not a feeling: the problem that presents itself is precisely to transform the idea into feeling. From then on an inner combat begins between what we would like to be and what we are, between an idea that is as yet only an abstraction, and [p. 9] feelings which, although henceforth condemned by the intellect, have lost nothing of their reality and their force.

The means the mystic employs to act upon his feelings and transform them are purification and asceticism: κάθαρσις [purification] and ἄσκησις [ascetic exercise]. The mortifications of the body must, in his thought, set the soul free and make it docile to the dictates of the intellect.

The struggle thus begun becomes more and more painful as there is revealed, thanks to the very effort we make to break it, the whole force of our attachment to the world. At first it seemed to us that we could dispose of ourselves, that it was enough to will. Soon we understand that inertia itself is a resistance, a latent force in which past actions are summed up and persist; and the more we struggle, the more distant and difficult victory appears to us.

There is thus a first advance in which the soul suffers more and more and experiences temptations to discouragement. But soon, in one who has persevered with a firm faith, the change pursued begins to take place, and the suffering of the struggle is mingled with satisfaction and hope. The soul is happy to suffer, feeling that its sufferings are fruitful and are leading it toward a state of peace and joy. And little by little joy penetrates and transfigures the suffering and emerges from it, triumphant. This is the second moment.

The third is what is called ecstasy; it is the abrupt, instantaneous passage from the temporal, mobile, composite, imperfect life to the motionless, one, simple, eternal, perfect and divine life. Ecstasy is the reunion of the soul with its object. No more intermediary between him and it: it sees him, it touches him, it possesses him, it is in him, it is he. It is no longer faith, which [p. 10] believes without seeing; it is more than science itself, which grasps being only in its idea: it is a perfect union, in which the soul feels itself exist fully by the very fact that it gives itself and renounces itself, for he to whom it gives itself is being and life itself.

The feeling of this union is love. Love alone has this power of uniting persons without absorbing them into one another, but on the contrary by increasing their reality and their consciousness as persons themselves. To love, which expresses the union of the soul with its object, is joined moreover the intuition of the intellect, pure and complete light, certainty in the full sense of the word. And love and light engender in the soul beatitude, perfect joy in harmony and in the presentiment of eternity. Such is the third stage.

But ecstasy can be, in a finite and temporal creature, only an accident. Human life soon begins again, with its movement and its imperfection, with its struggles and its disappointing victories. At least the memory of the things seen in the instant of ecstasy will henceforth be, in the mystic, the guiding principle of the intellect and of life.

In the light of the truth it has contemplated, the mind looks into itself and goes back over its former life: that life appears to it quite other than it saw it in the time of struggle that preceded the conversion. Then it believed that it was rising toward God of itself and by itself. And the order of generation of the states of its soul seemed to it to be: first the idea, second the feeling, third the action. But that is an illusion of immediate consciousness. In reality, all progress comes from above, and it is the perfect that itself creates in us the very disposition to seek it and to desire it.

[p. 11] Goethe said: *Das Vollkommene muss uns erst stimmen und uns nach und nach zu sich hinauf heben* [The perfect must first attune us and little by little raise us up to itself]. These words express admirably the mystical point of view. It is not the idea that engenders the feeling; it is its translation, its expression in clear consciousness. And the feeling itself, the desire, the aspiration, is not the principle of the possession or of the act that is its end. It is because the soul is already, in the depths of its being, united in some measure with its object, that it aspires to be united with it fully, to know and to see itself united with it, to enjoy this union. “Take comfort,” Jesus Christ says to Pascal, “you would not seek me if you had not found me.”

Thus the true order of events, the order in which they are engendered, is the inverse of the order in which they appear to immediate consciousness. In the beginning is the action, the union of the soul with God; then comes the feeling, namely the desire to persevere in this union or to restore it in its integrity if it is diminished; finally the abstract idea, the representation, objective in a sense, in the mirror of the intellect, of this feeling, the inner mainspring of the soul. The end, the object of our effort, is its term only because it is its principle.

Considering from this point of view the state of straying in which he found himself at the outset, the mystic forms a notion of illness and suffering quite other than that of the natural man. The latter, judging illness by suffering, seeks to rid himself of the suffering, and believes himself cured when, in one way or another, he has succeeded in doing so. But in reality he was ill before he noticed it. It was even the latent character of this illness that constituted its gravity; and what, in our aversion to suffering, we call disorder [p. 12] and illness is, on the contrary, the effort of the healthy part of ourselves, the effort of the pure being to which we hold, to reject and eliminate the germs of destruction that were accumulating within us. What we call illness is in reality a salutary crisis, a first step toward recovery. And far from its being the knowledge of our illness that determines us to seek its remedy, it is in proportion as we are cured of an evil that we discover its existence, its nature, its extent. Evil is perceived as evil only in the resistance it offers to the good that comes to combat it.

Such is the fourth phase: a return upon the former life, and a new orientation given to judgment and conduct.

There remains the fifth and last phase. This supernatural life, the presentiment of which has awakened in him, the mystic sets himself to develop, to realize in its fullness.

In this respect the mystics are divided, it seems, into two categories. A certain number attach themselves exclusively to the contemplation of the perfect being, and henceforth regard earthly life and temporal things only as an obstacle that separates them from the object of their desires; they are, from now on, strangers, passers-by in this world. Their constant care is to die to it from this very hour. They represent what may be called ascetic mysticism. It is not the only one. There is also a mysticism that may be called joyous, and which consists in transfiguring natural life by infusing into it the supernatural principle. For a Francis of Assisi, for a Jacob Boehme, the world is evil only if one looks at it with the eyes of the flesh. But to the spirit it is given to perceive it [p. 13] from the side from which God himself sees it; and how could what is touched by the divine gaze be wholly evil and wholly corruption? Far from the mystic’s necessarily condemning himself to flee out of the world, to feel nothing for it but contempt and horror, he is permitted to see in the union of the soul with God the very principle that rehabilitates the world and renders its use innocent and salutary. *Omnia sana sanis* [All things are sound to the sound].

Thus it is that, by diverse ways, the mystics make their way toward the end at which they aim, which is the infinite enlargement of that consciousness in which the natural man believes himself shut in and as it were imprisoned. Man is born an individual; he wants to become a person. He will succeed in this by going back to the source of all personality, to spirit, and by deriving his own life from this universal principle. And in loving God he will love all creatures, for it is by the very love we have for one another that we know that we love God. This possibility, for consciousnesses, of breaking through their material envelope, of penetrating one another; this faculty, for beings who seem strangers to one another, of understanding one another, of truly loving one another, and, without being annihilated as distinct beings, of living a common life; and union with God as the principle of this universal communion: such is the idea that presides over the mystical life.

You remember Goethe’s words:

*Dann geht die Seelenkraft dir auf, / Wie spricht ein Geist zum andern Geist.* / [Then the power of the soul opens in you, / How one spirit speaks to another spirit.]

“Then the power of the soul unfolds in you, and you hear the spirit speak to your spirit!”

It is this direct communication of spirits through bodies, under the action of God, that mysticism dreams of. [p. 14] Pascal rendered the idea well in these very simple words, which are, if I am not mistaken, very rich in meaning: “All is one, the one is the other, as in the three persons.” The Christian Trinity is precisely the expression of this union proper to persons, in which the distinction of consciousnesses subsists within a close and perfect community.

#### II

Such, according to the principal representatives of mysticism, is the summary of the mystical life and doctrine. In order to determine its meaning and value, it is of interest to place ourselves first of all at the point of view of the mystics themselves. A study conducted according to this principle would constitute what may be called the subjective psychology of mysticism.

A first feature that such a study would bring out is the remarkable way in which the mystics conceive inner observation or introspection. This mode of knowledge is often understood as an observation analogous to external observation, that is to say, one aiming to grasp the facts of consciousness, in their immediately given form, together with the relations that are manifested in them. Such is not mystical observation. It is not content to look at the surface of the soul; it goes deeper. The mystic believes that, by the effort of his reflection, he can penetrate ever further into his inner being. He would like to succeed in touching its ground. For him, doing is only the phenomenon of being, and being escapes that superficial consciousness which suffices for our practical and even our scientific activity. There are many more things in our soul than are dreamt of in our philosophy. There are faults [p. 15] concealed, which, without our knowing it, incline us to evil; there are indestructible and divine forces which enable us to rise again from our falls. In a word, beneath the conscious there is the unconscious: the true ground of our being, and one more and more accessible to a consciousness that, methodically and with increasing intensity, seeks out the ultimate reasons of our thoughts and the most secret motives of our actions.

A second procedure of psychological method is likewise brought to light by mystical practices: inner experimentation. The possibility of this operation is disputed. The whole mystical life is nothing but a series of experiments. The general problem consists, given the abstract idea of certain feelings, of certain states of soul, in realizing these feelings, these states of soul, in the soul. “You wait,” exclaims Pascal, “until you have faith before giving up pleasures. But I say to you: you would soon have faith if you had given up pleasures. Now it is for you to begin. You can certainly give up pleasures, and test whether what I say is true.” Whereas, according to common opinion, we have at our disposal, in a certain measure, our actions, but little or not at all our feelings, and cannot, for example, love at will, the mystic, who values actions only insofar as they express a feeling, applies himself to arousing within himself, by means of the moral or physical conditions over which we have a hold, the feelings by which the true life of the soul is nourished.

If from the examination of the method we pass to that of the results, we are struck first of all by the relation that the mystic establishes between knowledge and feeling or action. It is the latter that is primary; knowledge depends on it and comes only after [p. 16] it. *Tantum intelligitur Deus quantum diligitur.* [God is understood only so far as he is loved.] Action is revelatory of power; love is vision. It is the mode of our activity that determines the point of view and the scope of our intelligence, for the principles of the latter are only the summary of our experience. We see only what we know; we know only what we do.

This conception of the origin of knowledge leads the mystic to transform, in a general way, the apparent relations of exteriority and transcendence into relations of interiority and immanence. The notion of God the creator and lord, toward whom the world cries out from the depths of its nothingness, resolves itself into that of grace, or divine action present within ourselves; and grace, little by little, becomes no longer only the support, the law of our freedom, but this very freedom, perceived or sensed in its ground of spontaneity, superior to its temporal conditions of determination. In all things, the determined, the finite, the given real is now only the imperfect and fleeting symbol of the infinite and the ideal.

The freedom that the mystic is thus led to posit as the true origin of action and of knowledge cannot, moreover, be in his eyes the abstract form of a principle in itself indeterminate. His intimate experience makes him feel in it the infinite generosity of love, for true love has no need of motives, of conditions, in order to give itself and devote itself. It does not return like for like; it does not wait until one has deserved it before pouring itself out. It gives from the abundance of the heart, out of pure goodness, without weighing, without counting. This love, not of self in others but of others in oneself, a full and fruitful love in which being realizes itself by giving itself, is, in the eyes of the mystic, the true [p. 17] mover of the universe. “The eternal virgin,” said Goethe in fine verses often translated in a ridiculous manner, “the love of devotion and sacrifice which is the divine essence of the feminine, draws us to herself toward the heights.”

*Das Ewig-Weibliche / Zieht uns hinan.* / [The Eternal-Feminine / Draws us upward.]

This ideal love is the ground of being and the ground of our own selves. We are not, then, despite appearances, strangers to one another. “Fool,” said Victor Hugo, “who believe that I am not you!” In vain do bodies, which are in space, set the impenetrability and irreducibility of matter against our desire to think and to feel in common. Even in this life souls seek and find one another. Is it not true, that fine saying of Uhland, rendered by Longfellow in these terms:

*Yet what binds us, friend to friend, / But that soul with soul can blend?*

This doctrine of an original community of souls, of a principle of life, one, infinite and perfect, in which we can be united, find ourselves again, and each attain our fullest development, not at the expense of other beings but thanks to their very development—a principle that humanity calls God—this doctrine appears to us as the end in which all the experiences and all the reflections of the mystics culminate.

#### III

Such would be the outlines of a subjective psychology of mysticism. The remarkable coordination of these ideas, their efficacy upon minds and [p. 18] wills certainly demonstrate their interest and value. But we cannot help asking ourselves what would remain of them if they were considered no longer from within, from the point of view of the mystic himself, but from without, from the point of view of the pure scientist, the impartial and indifferent observer of human nature. Do these marvelous objects, to which the mystic clings, truly exist, or are they only products of his imagination, subjective projections of his states of soul? Do these states of soul themselves have, as the mystic believes, something special, something superior about them, or are they only varieties of common or even morbid phenomena? To study these questions would be to approach the objective psychology of mysticism.

If the mystic himself were consulted on this matter, I believe he would concede the game in advance to the most uncompromising objectivism. For he professes, for his part, that, seen from without, mystical phenomena do not exist as such, that they take on their meaning only in the consciousness of the mystic, as expressions of the very life that develops in the depths of his soul. The mystic believes that the faculties come into act only in those who exercise them, and that there is a mode of knowledge which is a property of love. In him, then, who observes without loving, this knowledge is impossible.

To one who, placing himself at a purely objective point of view, denies the reality of spiritual objects, the mystic would reply, like Faust to Mephistopheles:

*In deinem Nichts hoff’ ich das All zu finden:* / [In your Nothing I hope to find the All:]

“It is in what, in your eyes, is nothing, that I, for my part, hope to find the all!”

[p. 19] Now, it does seem that, if things are considered from without, mystical phenomena must be reduced to two affections of the mind which, indeed, seem hardly compatible with the reality of the objects of mysticism: namely, autosuggestion and monoideism.

The whole life of the mystic is autosuggestion. He himself knows it, and makes this procedure his method. He first gives himself a certain idea, and he then employs all the means that offer themselves to him to transform this idea into force, into feeling, into desire, into intimate and substantial reality. He suggests to himself that he find contemptible the earthly joys in which he used to delight, and infinite the spiritual joys, which seemed to him empty. He is satisfied only when the idea, which at first was external to him, has become incorporated into his soul and his limbs.

And likewise this idea must, in the mystic’s thought, by its excellence, efface all others. The mystic himself works to free his soul from all foreign thoughts, and he believes he has reached the goal of his efforts when indeed, in ecstasy, a single idea occupies, without a rival, the whole field of his consciousness.

Autosuggestion and monoideism: objectively, there is nothing more in the manifestations of mysticism.

Does this mean that we must see in them nothing but individual illusions, without any reality, without any universal value? Such a conclusion would be too summary.

No doubt autosuggestion and monoideism often present themselves as special, abnormal, or pathological states. But it is not always so. The man of genius, too, is possessed by a [p. 20] idea, suggests to himself that he find it great and beautiful, and comes to act as if automatically in accordance with this idea. And it is not only the man of genius, still a neighbor of the mystic, who offers examples of autosuggestion and monoideism. These two phenomena are met with in every man of action, in all those who give themselves to a cause, to a mission, to a task. I do indeed believe that both are, in the last analysis, for every man who reflects, conditions of existence. What is the good of living, why struggle, toil, make efforts, if our life has no value? And how are we to be assured that our life has a value, that the universe is interested in the maintenance of this ephemeral assemblage of atoms which constitutes our individuality, unless autosuggestion comes here to fill the gaps in knowledge? I approve of myself for holding on to life because I imagine that I am good for something. And is not the concentration of our faculties on a single idea, in a general way, the condition, the very principle of action? It is in the measure in which they become exclusive that our ideas cease to be mere ideas, so as to draw to themselves the living forces of the soul and change into volitions and acts.

Nothing, then, has been stated that determines the absolute value of mysticism when it has been reduced to autosuggestion and monoideism. Everything depends on the value of the idea that the mystic proposes to consciousness as its supreme and exclusive object. Is this idea the more or less symbolic expression of a reality, inaccessible perhaps, but recognizable by its powerful and beneficent effects, like the idea of the divine, present and active, to which a Beethoven ascribed his sublime creations; or must it be [p. 21] likened to the vain mirages in which morbid imaginations delight?

It does seem that the mystical idea, taken in its essential meaning, is one of those that cannot be treated as mere states of soul, wholly relative and subjective. The mere fact that it exists, with the characteristics we have pointed out, the fact that a great number of men, among them superior men, have attached themselves to it and lived by it, poses to the psychologist and to the philosopher, among other problems, the following two:

In the first place, is there for us, as conscious beings, besides the individual life, a universal life that is possible and, in some measure, already real? Is our reflective and distinct consciousness, according to which we are external to one another, an absolute reality, or a mere phenomenon, beneath which is hidden the universal interpenetration of souls in a single principle?

In the second place, if there are thus two existences for us, the one developed and immediately visible, individual existence, the other still almost unconscious but superior, universal existence, what is the relation between these two existences, and what method must we follow to bring the second to full reality?

Many mystics hold to the ascetic method, that is to say, they consider the two existences as contradictory to each other, and make the abolition of the one the condition of the development of the other. No community, in this system, except through the destruction of individuals; no divine city except through the annihilation of the human and natural city.

Mysticism, however, suggests the idea of a [p. 22] different method. If even now the individual and egoistic life is not the only one that exists in us, if we are already secretly united with one another through our common participation in the life of the universal spirit, there is no ground for establishing an incompatibility between the individual life and the universal life. They are reconcilable, since at bottom, in a certain measure, they are already reconciled. It would be possible, in that case, to go beyond nature without leaving nature. Individual consciousnesses could, without being shattered, enlarge themselves and make themselves penetrable to one another. And it would be granted to humanity to become one, without the individuals, the families, the nations, the groups that already have a unity and whose existence is beautiful and good being condemned on that account to disappear. Pascal’s idea would be realizable: “Unity and multiplicity. It is an error to exclude either of the two.”

If these reflections have some foundation, it seems that a broad and complete study of mysticism offers not merely an interest of curiosity, even scientific curiosity, but also concerns, very directly, the life and destiny of individuals and of humanity.
