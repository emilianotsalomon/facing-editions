## LA CULTURA LATINA Y NUESTRA AMERICA — LATIN CULTURE AND OUR AMERICA

Most Excellent Señor Rector,

Ladies,

Gentlemen:

nity. Were progress to be eclipsed in the ancient world, it would rise again in our American universe. We must believe that America assures the definitive ascendancy of Civilization.

Three, to my mind, are the capital works of the cultural elaboration of the Latin race, that is, of the civilization derived from Rome. The incomparable constellation of spirits who, from Dante to Tasso, from Giotto to Leonardo and Titian, from Machiavelli and Savonarola to Columbus and Galileo, formed the very bases of modern evolution; the discovery of America by the Iberian peoples; and the proclamation of the rights of man in the apocalyptic assemblies of the Revolution. That is to say: the Italian work, the Renaissance; the Iberian 2 work, the Discovery; and the French work, the Revolution. Or, if one prefers, three divine findings: intellectual emancipation; the geographical integration of the planet; and the moral and juridical liberation of 1789.

By the first of these three capital events, the spiritual atmosphere of the modern world was prepared—individualistic and reasoning, complex and diverse, restless and irreverent as it is; by the second, the material possibility of History was completed; by the third, its moral possibility was perfected. The Discovery created the geography truly worthy of that name. Without it, science would have been impossible, or very nearly so. Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, Newton were sages whose action derives in a straight line from the heroic enterprise of Cristóbal Colón, Vasco de Gama and Magallanes. Astronomy, Physics, Mathematics, Natural and Social History; however many fundamental scientific disciplines were later to be developed, thanks to a better understanding of the things of the world and their attributes, all derived from the Discovery. The knowledge of Man, which the humanists began, was perfected by the knowledge of the World, which Portuguese and Spaniards, discoverers and conquerors, carried out together.

The revolutionary work of France is equivalent, in the moral order, to the Iberian work in the social and economic order. The guarantees and the rights of man, elaborated into a juridical synthesis, secured human coexistence upon a new basis which, in scarcely a century, would radically transform the historical constitution of the nations, by referring it not only to the traditional determinations of law but, above all, to the eternal formula of the ideal. The reconciliation of the monarchical principle with democratic elaboration would be attempted throughout the history of the nineteenth century: in France herself, thanks to the Napoleonic experiments and the Bourbon restorations; in Germany, in Austria, in Italy, in Spain; but in the end the last international catastrophe would defeat the monarchical principle forever—despite whatever claims might yet be pressed—and would establish democracy upon the ruins of the European thrones. Hohenzollern, Habsburg, Romanoff would fall resoundingly in the synchronism of the times, thus demonstrating the incongruity of their traditional principle with the democratic principle.

Now then, our Latin America is a glorious summation of the three great elements of culture: the Renaissance, which gave us Colón; the Discovery, which bestowed on us Magallanes, Núñez de Balboa, Alvares Cabral, Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro; and the Revolution, which engendered in America the immortal figures of Hidalgo, Bolívar and San Martín. We are the fruit of the most original and audacious historical synthesis. Cortés and Pizarro, facing the Aztec and Quechua empires, are like Gonzalo de Córdoba or Alejandro Farnesio in the moments of a miraculous utopia, standing before the Babylon of Nebuchadnezzar or the Egypt of Ramses.....

And just as the Renaissance founded our race, the Revolution engendered our autonomy. In the name of the highest prestige of the Latin genius our peoples arose to independent life, and they go on unfolding in their dramatic history, filled with extraordinary vicissitudes, at times tragic as none other, but always demonstrative of the great virtue inherent in Latin culture—broad, generous and human; a culture suckled at the breasts of the She-Wolf of the Capitol, and which will yet furnish in our hemisphere new occasions of astonishment and gratitude to the most remote posterity.

In my long journey across our America, through Peru, Chile, the Argentine Republic, Uruguay and Brazil, I believe I have reached the culminating point. Before, I traversed only the immense zone of Spanish influence; today I find myself in the vast country that Portugal civilized. In Mexico, Lima, Santiago, Montevideo or Buenos Aires, one can feel only in part the imperious voice of the race. In Río de Janeiro, the last capital I shall touch before embarking for New York, I feel the magnanimous heart of the lineage beating. Its divine radiant life pours into my soul, and there are drawn close together the love for the purple banner of Castile and the reverent devotion for the holy flag of Portugal.

I come to bring you a message from the Mexican intellectual world; to tell you that in my country the principle of our nobility of spirit is preserved clean and pure; to assure you that each day the belief in our destiny grows firmer; to congratulate you, most fully, upon your collective work, which astonishes the world and makes it reckon with Brazil as with a power of the first order, one that weighs seriously in the European international congresses, where the voice of her jurists of universal renown is heard with respect and deference.

Your triumphs are ours. Your successes hearten us as well. Each time you increase your prestige, ours will be increased in turn; and if you refine it, it will likewise be purified for us. In our political relations there is not a trace of wariness or envy. We desire your good fortune with the sincerity of brothers; we covet, as though for ourselves, the broadest and most vigorous power for Brazil. If some day you should become the center of the Ibero-American union in the world, Mexico would applaud so happy an event with the sympathy that overflows from our souls and that translates itself, at the very least, into a cordial and wholly spontaneous augury of felicity.

The homage which the Mexican Republic received not long ago from the United States of Brazil, when you declared the day of our political emancipation a national holiday, proves once again the solidarity of the race and allows us to hope that time will draw it still closer and perfect it ever more. Let your will declare: "So be it, in the name of History and Reason; of the ineluctable facts that have come to pass and of the eternal moral principles!"

Divine is the work you accomplish in striving for American pan-Iberianism. Count upon Mexico for its definitive attainment. My people has always known how to suffer and to wait. It has learned more in its great sorrows than in the ephemeral season of its good fortune. A single racial aspiration that never dares to deny the peculiar shadings of each Latin American nation; a noble amphictyony of the peoples who in this part of the Globe speak the twin tongues of Cervantes and Camoens—such must be the substantial creed of our legislators, teachers, politicians, philanthropists and statesmen. Woe to the nation, small or great, that sets race and fatherland in the pressing terms of an antithesis!.... We had a common origin. Let us strive to fulfill, united, one and the same destiny!

Most Excellent Señor Rector:

Receive, in the name of the National University of Mexico, the greeting of the Mexican intellectual world. Know that the sons of that house of studies are honored to offer you, with their purest admiration, their best wishes that you may fulfill, in the name of God, your dearest ideals, in the fruitful shade of peace and always for the honor and profit of the Brazilian fatherland, so worthy of being exalted by her sons as an emblem of effort and of virtue.

## EL GENIO ESPAÑOL — THE SPANISH GENIUS

To the poet Francisco Villaespesa.

Today one speaks of the psychology of peoples, of national souls, of the spiritual individuality of the various human groups. The German psychologists of the last century—Lazarus, Steinthal, Wundt—were the authors of the concept. According to their thesis, peoples, like men, have their own genuine, proper, unique personality.

For many philosophers, however, it is an uncertain and contradictory raving to affirm the existence of the collective spirit. It is a matter, in their opinion, of resurrecting, out of season, scholastic entities as mysterious as they are useless. Only the human individual is real, they teach, each concrete personal being. The "collective soul" is a phantom, a metaphysical idol. Universals are names, said the enemies of medieval Platonism; exactly what the uncompromising nominalists, our contemporaries, repeat.

The adepts of the collective soul do not affirm that it is a real substance distinct from individual souls; rather they maintain its social character, that is to say, its historical reality.

Make a human group speak, believe and think in common, and its language—the reflection of its reason—its religious beliefs—the shelter and symbol of its sentiment—its customs and traditions—the systematic exercise of its will—will engender a new psychic and historical reality that will acquire the spiritual contours of an individual, of a personality.

Great peoples are, like men of genius, unmistakable realities, nominalism or exclusive individualism notwithstanding. Even within this vigorous hypothesis, what one does in declaring the personality of Athens or Rome is certainly not to fabricate ontological phantoms, but to affirm the existence of new complex individuals.

Only once has the Greek miracle been, and only once shall it be, as Renán said. Only once, and in no more than a single point of space and time, did Cervantes and Molière flower. As real is the personality of a man of genius as that of an illustrious people; for just as the individual spirit makes use of a brain for its outward manifestations, and molds and transforms it by its inner force, so the collective genius makes use of its written and spoken tongue, which goes on bending to the powerful impulse of its historical spontaneity.

So akin are thought and language that, as Schopenhauer observes, the Greeks used the same word for word and for reason. In Castilian, discurso signifies indifferently reasoning and expression. To speak a language is to possess a part of the collective soul. To think in Spanish is to enjoy a certain common spirituality, to make common cause with a definite human group and to accept in advance the Castilian genius.

"The French language," said Ganivet, "is like an overcoat. The Spanish like a cloak. There is no garment more individualistic nor more difficult to wear than the cloak; above all when it is of stout cloth and long to the feet." Good writers wear the ample Spanish cloak with despejo—that is, with grace, with unspeakable gallantry.

The Castilian genius consists precisely in the intellectual and moral spontaneity that distinguishes it; in its heroic and mystical individualism, in its peninsular character. Menéndez y Pelayo affirmed: "Spain is a people of armed theologians." True; but the theologian is always a mystic, and the warrior unified, in the banner of Castile, race and faith.

Not long ago, in a brief and incisive article as irreverent as it was original<sup>1</sup>, the philosopher Diego Ruiz cited the famous verses that resolve the metaphysical enigma of Spain:

The Spaniards are admirable improvisers. Lope, says Menéndez y Pelayo, improvised dramas as Raimundo Lulio improvised philosophical systems; as—we shall add—Spain herself improvised nations. These Ibero-American nations of ours.

Read the mystics and the ascetics, the dramatists, the lyric poets, the novelists; think upon the prowess of the conquerors, of the religious founders, and the facility of spiritual production will produce in you dazzlement, fascination itself. It is a perpetual invention, a continual revelation. In exchange, do not ask for perfection.

The French is like an overcoat; the Spanish, like a cloak. Out of a cloak, with good will, many overcoats may be made. Nevertheless, you will never gird your body with the cloak as with the overcoat. The perfect is not the Spanish, not even in Don Miguel de Cervantes himself.<sup>3</sup>

So it is, in truth. Séneca represents in anticipation the characteristics inherent in that "natural and human" stoicism which forms the marrow of the moral lucubrations of the great thinkers of Spanish stock.

This stoicism, throbbing in some of the most intense dramatic creations of Rojas and Calderón, in the golden books of Castilian mysticism and asceticism, in various of the poetic works of fray Luis de León and of Quevedo, in the incomparable "Epístola Moral" attributed to Fernández de Andrada, and even in the stupendous literary conception of Cervantes himself; that irreducible vigor of a personality capable of emerging victorious from all the trials life imposes, is a substantial form of race, and it has accompanied and followed her everywhere, imposing itself, as the seal of her genius, upon the character of the peoples she engendered for Western civilization and culture. believed in, adored or sung. When the throbbing entrails pour themselves over the objects apt to arouse admiration, how should one not believe in the supreme reality that ipso facto [by that very fact] takes shape and is realized? How should he who escapes from himself and roots his soul in the object of his love or of his passion not believe in his demon, in his god, in his work of art? It would be as much as not to believe in his own substance. From it the soul alienated itself in transmuting it into lines, color, myth or song. For this reason Catholicism has been, at one and the same time, for the Iberian civilizations, fatherland, aesthetic canon and religion.

A nation of heroes and mystics, of artists and moralists theoretical and practical. A race far more intuitive than discursive, far more heroic than discreet, as Gracián would say; a collective psyche always identical with itself, free, autonomous, metaphysically free. Such is Spain. Could not Renán verify that, beneath the rod of the Inquisition, the thought of the race remained intact and active?.....

Poet: return to the fatherland of superhuman enterprises and the everlasting crusade. Today, in this apocalypse, as it were, of History, your race stands erect. Haven of salvation, shelter of outraged right, refuge of the ideal, the world calls her. Her legendary energy has not abandoned her. The rise of her industrial and peaceful life has not depressed the nobility of spirit of her sons, the zeal of her sages, the intuition of her artists, nor the Christian prayer of the good. Today, as when the Cid defended them, God is honored in Castilian breasts; but to the murmur of the prayers is fulfilled the beneficent miracle of action. Not by prayers alone does man live in the twentieth century of Christianity.

Return!.....; but before departing, accept, as a good friend, a single promise, and make us one as well. We promise you to labor for Spain. Labor there for us. Louis XIV, the proud French monarch, said to his grandson Don Felipe, upon the establishment of the Bourbon dynasty: "There are no longer any Pyrenees." The words of the king—such often are royal words—were, on that occasion, no more than a trope of rhetoric, a flower of rag. From that quarter the interpenetration of peoples will never be achieved. Let us say, with better will: There is no longer any Atlantic to disunite, no sea to separate, no ocean to divide. We want a single supreme collective soul here and there, a Hispano-American civilization. Let our will declare: Let the destiny be fulfilled which the caravels of Colón and Isabel began!.... And let it also declare: poet, God keep you!

## EL DESCUBRIMIENTO DE AMERICA — THE DISCOVERY OF AMERICA

So great is the significance of the discovery of America for universal civilization that it seems to me it may properly be contained within the terms of this simple statement: America is not a new accessory theater of European culture, but the natural seat of its firmest unfolding; it is not something extrinsic and accidental, but an indispensable element of its development. Without America as a new fatherland, the possibilities of success for the culture of humanity would have been considerably diminished.

The memorable odysseys of Vasco de Gama and Magallanes gave Civilization numerous lands of conquest and mercantile exploitation, zones of political influence, protectorates and opulent colonies. Colón, on the other hand, gave the Europeans a new and ampler fatherland. He offered England a new England, Spain a new Spain; to the Anglo-Saxon genius a station of development and power beyond compare, far richer and more propitious than its native islands; to the Latin genius a new land in which it might perpetuate itself down the centuries, in ceaseless evolution.

In Asia, in Africa, in the archipelagoes of Oceania, Europe will always be a foreigner. In America she never will be. Western civilization necessarily prolongs itself in the virgin regions of America. Here will be elaborated, in its highest forms, the culture of the world.

The supreme treasure of humanity, which the Indo-European race began to fashion in prehistoric or protohistoric times upon the sacred plateau of Iran, and which, ever growing greater, passed, through the vicissitudes of history, from the Indians and the Persians to the Greeks, from the Greeks to the Romans, from the Romans in decadence to the Germanic invaders and, from these, at the close of the slow and dramatic medieval gestation, to the whole world, in an irrepressible radiation, continues and will continue its exodus in the Continent of Colón.

If Europe, which today suffers the horror of the deadliest of fratricidal wars, should at last succumb, as an exemplary punishment for her egoism, to the onslaught of the Asiatic peoples; if, by some great historical calamity—not impossible in truth, however improbable—the Mongol race, redeemed, organized and militarized by Japan, were to fall upon the peoples of the Old World and vanquish them by the number, the force and the discipline of its armies, Civilization, faltering among the peoples that have so loved her, would always have the refuge of this world jealously defended by the utterly free waters of its oceans. Here, in our climates, would be her shelter and her future. If progress were checked in one world, it would take deeper root in the other. America assures Civilization its immortality.

But it is not only in the ill-starred hypothesis of so great a catastrophe that America—let us rather say Colombia, in testimony of filial gratitude—would be a land of promise. Even in the case, fortunately far less remote, of the concomitant development of culture in both continents, without the occurrence of apocalyptic hecatombs; even if, as until today, the great good of men, their final reason for struggling and existing, continued to be elaborated jointly by those nations and by ours, America represents the resplendent future by virtue of profound reasons that every impartial critic will readily appreciate.

The European peoples carry within them, as all peoples do, the principle of their destruction. They will not be immortal. Nothing human is, if we except the ideal; though perhaps the ideal is the only divine thing that exists. And whether a victim of constricting militarism, of libertarian socialism, or of unbridled and over-productive industrialism; or perhaps by the synthesis of all these causes and others besides that a minute analysis would disclose, France, Germany, Italy, the great and small powers will decline, will dwindle, will become places of veneration for humanity, sanctuaries of pious remembrance.

Only Spain and England have their immortality assured. Rome succumbed, but her spirit remains, breathing from the Romania of Trajan's expeditions to the Iberia of Séneca and Martial. England and Spain will succumb; but there is another and vigorous England—we Mexicans know it well—and many heroic Spains in this land of Colón.

"If the great navigator whose mighty exploit we commemorate today had not, on the seventh of October, 1492, changed the direction of his course, which was from East to West, turning to the Southwest, he would have encountered the Gulf Stream, and it would have carried him toward Florida, and perhaps from there to Cape Hatteras and to Virginia—an incident of immense importance, because it might have given the United States, instead of an English Protestant population, a Spanish Catholic one."<sup>1</sup> Such are the great events of history. They often depend on chance. Colón goes from East to Southwest and Spanish America is born; had he turned toward the West, perhaps the English Puritans would have founded their colonies in Central America, and what was New Spain would have been a new England. With reason did Pascal hold that the attributes of Cleopatra's nose have been of incalculable consequence in the destinies of humanity. A turn of the helm given at random ordains history. Chance is a supreme law which the historians of civilization generally do not take into account. But the course of the Genoese's caravels decided history on that day of October, 1492, and Spain undertook the epic of the Conquest.

No people was more apt to accomplish it. She had all the characteristics of the conquering races: the adventurous and heroic spirit tempered in combat, an incoercible covetousness, an exalted imagination, enthusiasm, faith. For seven centuries the Spaniards were the foremost Christian champions facing the Muslim world. The crusades, mere rhythmic episodes in the history of Europe, became for them the genuine atmosphere of national life. Byzantium was falling precisely under the yoke of Islam when they, in the West, were containing the impetus of the war-hardened Saracen hosts, achieving over the religion of the Prophet a secular victory. From that time onward, the Spanish kingdoms, consecrated to the Catholic Church, mingled with the eternal principles of Christianity the greatest dose of violence and pain; and, vigorous and personal in their conception of the faith, making of religion and fatherland a single idea, and of the Christ of the beatitudes the inflexible Christ of the Inquisition, they received as the prize of their exploits—so at least their exclusive patriotic mysticism, armed with the crusader's sword and the inquisitor's anathema, believed—together with the attainment of political unity, thanks to the happy dynastic alliance of Castile and Aragon, the New World which genius laid at the feet of both illustrious sovereigns: the astute Fernando, a prince after the subtle doctrine that Machiavelli would develop, and the Great Isabel, one of the most venerated women of history; enduring symbol of the unity of Spain and of the America that speaks Spanish.

But the everlasting crusade did not end. Formerly it was waged on its own soil and against the invading Moors; afterward it was to be prolonged in the New World against other infidels who, outside the truth of the Gospel, had fulfilled their historical mission, living their slow and mysterious existence within the collective consciousness of stern barbarian monarchies, as great and illustrious as those of Nineveh and Babylon which the Old Testament evokes with sublime stupor.

Here, on the Central Plateau of Anáhuac, and on the lofty tablelands of Peru, two great and opulent empires governed their own life in mutual ignorance, as though each might claim, without contradiction, for its own pride, the legend of its celestial origin. And in the Peninsula which, as an outpost of the autochthonous culture, plunges and bathes its brow in the waters of the Caribbean Sea, the favored art of the Mayas, the hieratic revelation par excellence that is Architecture, raised above the astonishment of the earth enigmatic monuments worthy to rival the cyclopean constructions of Memphis and of Thebes, in which the majesty of the whole by no means spared the intricate delicacy of the decoration. Bold and discreet nations that raised, at times, as an eloquent orator has said, their institutions as far as the Republic, their poetry as far as the Epic, their pagan cult so far as to erect to the unknown god of Netzahualcóyotl pyramids of adoration, and "their science so far as to enclose the days of the year and the seasons in a circle of porphyry, from whose center the priest revealed the mysterious expedition of the Sun through the Zodiac."<sup>1</sup> Incipient republics or great feudal empires in which war, as for the Spaniards, was the common cult of god and of fatherland, and which, isolated from Western life, nonetheless showed that without foreign aid or European civilization humanity can fulfill its destiny and undertake its eternal work beneath the peace of God.

A few Spanish soldiers of the sort that Gonzalo de Córdoba, Juan de Austria and Alejandro Farnesio captained in the wars of Europe; that is to say, a few invincible soldiers, under the orders of Pizarro and Cortés, threw themselves into an adventure without rival. Camoens and Ercilla, epic poets of the race, have de-

clared, in the pomp of their royal octaves, brilliant as the shields of heroes, the audacity, the fear and the hope of the Iberian soul opening its way over time and space with the point of its triumphant steel. New seas and lands came out to meet the Conquerors; America displayed before their astonished eyes the grandeur of her provident nature: the banana trees and the palms of the burning plains that scarcely rise above the level of the sea. On the slopes of the cordilleras, high in the valleys or in damp and shadowy clefts, the arborescent ferns and the cinchona that yields the fever-quelling bark. The geometric thorny cacti that the silica raises up and the sun scorches. The alpine rose of the Andes, magnificent befaria that forms, as Humboldt says, a purple girdle about the jutting peaks. The cold moorlands lashed by hurricanes. The silent lakes opened like pupils thousands of meters above the sea, and the whiteness of the snow on the summits of mountains anointed in the glory of the sun..... Upon this opulent nature the crusader of history planted the Cross.

That is to say, he imposed with the new belief the new fatherland. The purple banner of Castile is as much race as faith. Hernando Cortés and Francisco Pizarro brought not only an intact force, but a religion or, what is the same, a new spirit. They might have exclaimed with the Apostle: "Old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new." The American idols—fetishes, as the Portuguese called the African gods—would have to bow down and disappear. Men of America and gods of America had to be vanquished. The fearful struggle was beginning, the systematic destruction of the past. Upon the Teocalli, the Church; upon the Indian, the Spaniard; and in the hecatombs of Cholula and Tenochtitlán there was engendered, with the din of a mythological collapse, the new race, our Hispano-American race, which received Christian baptism upon a sea of blood and of horror.

Why must it be that at the origin of everything great there stands a sacrifice? Why should martyrdom announce redemption? Thou knowest, perhaps, Inca Atahualpa. Cuauhtémoc, hero, father, lord: why, for the glory of thy sons, did the Castilian Captain burn the soles of thy feet and at last hang thee defenseless? Prudent historians have said in his exculpation, and the poets have said it too: the crime was of the time that was running, not of Spain. A profound reason. Will you permit, nevertheless, that a moralist without historical sense, a Christian moralist, condemn on the glorious anniversary of the Discovery the frenzy of the Conquest? Ah! we Americans love Spain; we know that the Conquest was even more implacable toward the spirit of the Indian than toward his material power; we know that little, very little remains of our autochthonous culture, and we shall not waste time deploring it; but that old tear which a melancholy and subtle poet has sung, the burning tear of the vanquished race, still falls silently upon our heart and makes it shudder at the memory of how the throbbing entrails of our forebears were broken beneath the hooves of Cortés's horse.

There are those who, in bitter and disconsolate phrases, have dared to deny the collective soul of the Hispano-American race, declaring its historical affirmation a matter of imagination or scholastic raving. There are those who, skeptics or pessimists, seek to deny so lofty a reality by deciding that such a collective spirit does not, in the end, manage to appear anywhere with peculiar characteristics. Skepticism and pessimism have denied not only this but various other august realities, in order to oblige us to fall upon our knees before petty and inadmissible fetishes, before shades devoid of personality, before individuals less real, less individual, I would say, in order to mark, within so exclusive an anarchist individualism, my whole thought and my whole conviction.

I, on the contrary, believe, as no doubt you believe, in the reality of those intuitions that furnish the key to the explanation of the universe and the firmest lodestar of human conduct; in those substantial forms of life, in those arcane syntheses of humanity: peoples, fatherlands, nationalities or races; sacrosanct affirmations that at every instant make past and future one with the present, and that simplify with true simplification history, which, thought within them and not merely through the clash or the alliance of individual activities, ceases to be a chaotic and disproportionate interminable drama, and becomes a luminously complex tragedy—yes, infinitely complex and disconcerting at first, but reducible, in sum, to the metaphysical clarity of law and of order, harmonious and divine as a work of art.

When men are united among themselves by the bonds of language, faith and custom; when down the centuries they drag their chain of sorrows, while at the same time they merge their inextinguishable ideals and make brothers and one of their immortal hopes, and, what is more fundamental still than all that, of their incoercible aspiration toward justice and the good; then little by little the ineffable prodigy is engendered and perfected: there is born a supreme collective soul in which spirits are quickened together, in which the generations are traditionally continued, in which the life of the fathers is infused into the sons, in which heroisms are linked and hidden hopes drawn close as in a circle of love, in which, finally, death is annihilated, and from which, as through the amplest channel of a fecundating and silent river, civilization and life miraculously flow. This soul of a thousand souls is the Race, a reality that does not breathe the ephemeral duration of matter, but perpetuates itself in the course of time, growing and developing from a beginning and always in perennial evolution.

You will understand how such a reality claims a cult of its own. You will understand all the human intensity of the trivial epithets that call the Fatherland self-denying, holy, maternal; you will understand also the whole reach of that signal moral formula which demands sacrifice as an offering of filial gratitude, because perhaps you will think with me that the cult is something spontaneous in the spirit which externalizes itself sen-

And sibly in the form of actions, rites and images, in order to affirm the evidence of beings that affectively and rationally dominate us.

Everything noble and lofty that we feel to be ours—but not because we possess it or wholly encompass it, for then we should consider it an accessory or a prolongation of our personality; everything in which we participate, but which exceeds us as much, as very much, as religious things exceed all the others that are not wholly so—makes the depths of our being tremble, obliges our lips to break forth in praises, while our hands engrave its religious symbol and our hearts sing with solemn rhythm the miraculous grandeur of its ideal significance.

Thus the Fatherland, the Race, in which we all participate but which subjugate us by the dictate of our intimate feeling of dependence, as the mystic Tolstoi would say, oblige us, by irrefragable prescription, to render them a cult, imposing upon us their severe liturgy and consecrating us in the tenderness of their love, definitively faithful..... The Fatherland!...... I have seen her on these clear October nights wandering in the half-light of the immense valley at the foot of the high mountains. Afflicted like the virgins of ancient tragedy. Her hair loose to the wind, her ample garments rent and her miraculous brow bowed with sorrow. Her forehead blackened with the smoke of homicidal powder, her most loving breast spattered with blood, her arms clenched and spent from sustaining, alone, in the misfortune of her sons, the treasure of humanity they believed theirs, and in her divine countenance the pale fear of losing in a single day the secular inheritance of the generations. I have seen her, afterward, erect above her infinite tribulations, her knees firm, her hands indomitable and her heart aflame with sacred wrath, "shod with the divine sandals that bear her, as they bear the immortal Athena, above the sea and the immense earth with the swiftness of the wind, grasping her sturdy spear, bronze-tipped, ponderous, long, robust, with which she destroys whole ranks of men whenever she is moved to anger against them;"<sup>1</sup> and, wrathful and magnificent, she leads us in the vanguard of victory, while in the mournful bosom of fields and cities the cannon thundered.

<sup>1</sup> Homer.

Enough now of crimes in the history of America! It is time that the peoples of this part of the world should demonstrate our aptitude for civilization; that the Mexicans

especially, far from killing one another as we have done with singular predilection ever since national Independence was consummated, should know how to fulfill our destiny on earth and in history, facing the great Saxon people beyond the Bravo, as outposts of the Race, of that Homeric phantom bound up in our traditions and which would be capable of leading us to sacrifice in defense of the eternal charters of humanity....

Let the consciousness of our own fatherland and of the Hispano-American race lead us once more to honor Colón. He was the idea that turned into reality, the dream that became a world, the longing that took shape as humanity.... Marvelous equivalences of Destiny! Let us honor the valiant navigator, the seer, the immortal. The Greeks would have made him a god. Let us Americans make of him a father. They would have sung his legend as they went singing the legends of Prometheus, stealer of the divine fire, of Homeric Achilles, of the ingenious Ulysses who so many times launched his ship upon the liquid plain of the sea. Colón, profound as Ulysses and audacious as Prometheus, stole from the old god Ocean the Atlantis and delivered it to the men, stupefied, of his century and of all the centuries, to make of it a prosperous and happy dwelling. Let us bow before his glory, and let each year, when October returns, the prow of the miraculous caravels be adorned with the symbolic palms and the crowns of laurel.

## CATOLICISMO, JACOBINISMO Y POSITIVISMO — CATHOLICISM, JACOBINISM AND POSITIVISM

One commonly speaks of Latin idealism and Latin-American idealism. The nations of Latin culture reserve to themselves the privilege of the ideal and leave to the Germanic and Saxon peoples the prestige of force and the practical sense of civilization. It seems to them that the ideal consists in undertaking immoderate, disproportionate enterprises; in the chivalric and absurd sense of life; in a quixotism generous, in truth, but illogical, without critical tendency or philosophical balance.

Speaking absolutely, the only true idealism, because it is the only real one, belongs to man, to humanity entire. In all strictness of doctrine, there are no idealist peoples or races nor materialist peoples or races. Every man aspires to the attainment of something better, as the poet said. Only imbeciles do not interrupt their miserable organic felicity with disillusion of the present or hopes of the future. A people without imagination is as impossible as a people without memory.

Our Hispano-American idealism does not exist. We are the most realistic peoples of European or semi-European civilization in history. We have spent our life in works of war or of inept mercantilism. In cultural enterprises, no; in humanitarian labors, still less. The indigenous race has not counted upon Latin-American apostles; and what better incentive could be desired to stir the charitable and Christian zeal of truly apostolic men; what better object of redemption could a saint covet! Our countries of Spanish tradition inherited the realism terre à terre [down to earth] of the classic race of the picaresque novel—of Celestinas, Lazarillos and Buscones—and of the perfect painting of dwarfs and beggars, together with the irrealism of Don Quijote, who knows how to fight against windmills and to make of a coarse and mannish peasant girl the perennially ridiculous type of epic patriarchism. What we did not inherit from Spain was genius. Genius, in general, has not been our attribute. We all know it and we are not going to try to conceal it out of a spirit of shameless adulation. We know it well.

But our realism, indifferent if not hostile to noble and lofty ideals, nourished on the dense, homogeneous and suffering mass of illiterate Indians—our tropical, lazy realism, the realism of Calibans—never felt upon its fleshy and massive loins the magic breath, the luminous lash of Ariel, but rather the subterranean inspiration of irrealism, of unintelligence of life and the world, which Spain instilled in us with her dominion, her blood and her predilection.

"A people of medullas, this Spain of ours," says the notable Spanish thinker Diego Ruiz in his notes entitled: My Doctrine and the Thought of my Race. "The psychic life of the Spaniards ends at the level of the medulla oblongata. The spinal cord has prayed among the Spaniards; it has been eloquent; it has written the Theater; it was lyric; it was a painter, in the proper absence of the brain. The cerebral is not the Spanish. This people of medullas has had no need of thought. Or very rarely and by exception."

"Now and then, by a phenomenon of biology whose study..... is yet to be undertaken, those millions of medullas are illumined by some

L cerebral reflection. They are the brief moments in which, in history, one Spaniard has thought for all. Those moments have come to be called Vives, Sánchez, for example. But the fleetingness of that reflection and (say what one will) its scant national character indicate clearly—to whoever does not systematically identify history with apologia—that the Spaniard is the aphilosophical man par excellence. Like the good mystic that he is."

Such are the first attributes of the race. (The concept of race is today almost exclusively psychological.) The ingenuous realism of the low satisfaction of biological needs (characteristic of the races of inferior civilization), and Spanish irrealism. Combining both, one will have cleared up the first unknown, or at least the first datum of the first unknown, of our historical and moral psyche.

There is no contradiction in affirming that essential realism and irrealism coexist in an individual or collective consciousness. The ideal is always congruous and proportionate in the classic civilizations, in the intelligent peoples who, like superior men, aspire to attain states more perfect than the present ones because these pass away; but who, at the same time, know how to graduate the intrinsic force of ideal action, without acting by virtue of habitual syntheses of hyperbole or ellipsis, which end at last in the failure of action. Not wings, but lead, Bacon counseled the philosophical thought of his century. Wings and lead must peoples and men have; that is to say: prudence, understanding of the problems of life, respect for the conditions that predetermine them in one sense or another, and, at the same time, a clear representation of a more perfect state to be attained, and courage and fortitude to make it accessible and to fulfill it. The unintelligible ideal, because it is the fruit of generous unintelligence, is irrealism. Not only is there no contradiction between the two terms (realism and irrealism) which seem, at first sight, to imply one; there is rather a most profound harmony: the realist individual or people who aspires to the ideal, without adequate intelligence of his aspiration, falls into irrealism, into the orbit of confusion and obscurity of madness, bordering upon the tragic region of crime. For this reason, despite her intrinsic generosity, Spain is the most realistic, the most irrealistic and the least fortunate of the great civilizing peoples of modern history.

The Jacobinism of the Mexican liberals is, perhaps, one of the forms of Spanish irrealism, one of the developments of quixotism. Two great Latin cultures—the French in second place, and the Spanish in first—have informed our own. Spain gave herself to imitating, in the political Constitution of the year 1812, as so many other peoples of Europe did, France and her brand-new revolutionary principles, as good, like all those proclaimed by the other revolutions of History, for destroying a past, but inept for constructing, for building anything. Revolutions ought not to be judged by what they engender, but by what they annihilate. A revolution is a grave crisis capable of healing by destroying, and which helps one to live and saves by means of pain, which is the great moral fount of all true redemptions.

If French culture, above all during the nineteenth century, has been the essential datum of our intellectual development, it has been so only through Spain. In the last days of the Viceroyalty the influence of France already makes itself felt in the political sphere; but such influence is indirect, it proceeds from Spain. For in the eighteenth century French hegemony, French ideas, French books spread through Spain as through the rest of Europe, causing in various places, but above all in the Iberian peninsula, a real weakening of the national spirit which, by dint of becoming Frenchified, lost almost entirely its dazzling and colorful, its utterly free and somewhat disordered fantasy, only to wither within the meager canons of pseudo-classical French taste, so diverse from the creative fertility of Spain, so opposed to that noble ease and constant haste which, in the golden centuries of Spanish letters, prevented the achievement of perfect works of art, like the Greek and the Latin, but engendered in the theater, the novel and the lyric, in all the forms of literary thought, a personal and most abundant multitude of works of genius that make Spanish literature the most original of all modern literatures. "Here in Spain," declares Menéndez y Pelayo in his Discourse on Ramón Lull,<sup>1</sup> "force has always manifested itself through abundance, and instead of concentrating itself in one masterpiece, it has scattered itself in infinite ones. Every Spaniard, in science, in art and even in political life, is by his nature an improviser. Lulio improvised systems as Lope improvised dramas."

<sup>1</sup> La Ciencia Española. Volume III.

Heedlessness, an excessive but little reflective liveliness, force scattered

without critical balance, the producing of oneself with all the nervous facility of instinct, half in jest—that is the essential thing about the race that gave us language and customs; thus were we born to independent life, through the generous and picturesque Castilian heedlessness.

The Mexican liberals, without bearing in mind that axiom which ought to have been dear to the realism of Sancho Panza—Zamora was not won in an hour—said to themselves: Let us give our people, not the liberties that belong to it, but the liberties of Man. Let us give it the absolute rights of Man, not the contingent and historical rights that would have managed to make its relative happiness; let us grant it the perfect form of government, the only one worthy of Man, the one that will work the prodigy of transforming a collectivity subjected to the inhuman regime of Spanish colonization into a City of the Sun, into an ideal Republic, into the most felicitous country of Utopia.

Our legislators were victims of that theatrical idolatry of which Bacon would speak, common to all the thinkers of the eighteenth century (the eighteenth century has been prolonged in Mexico down to our own days); they communed with what Spencer called the rationalist superstition, as false and noxious as all superstitions; and intrepidly, without the scruples or vacillations of timorous conservatives, spirited and resolute in defining absolute principles, after the manner of thaumaturges for whom each word pronounced engenders tangible and social realities; without remembering that they were legislating not for eternity nor for the incorruptible archetypes of Plato, but for Mexicans, they decided that: "It is the will of the people of Mexico to constitute itself a democratic, representative and federal Republic;" and tradition, the Spanish colonial past, the misunderstood reality, in sum, brought it about that the Constitution of '57, drafted for the absolute Man of Plato, did not govern the political life of Mexico, and was disputed, not only in the heat of parliamentary debates, but in the bloody and formidable dialectic of History.

When one reads the chronicle of the sessions of the Constituent Congress, through the apocalyptic speeches of this or that poet of Quintanesque odes and impossible palingeneses, there is outlined the battered silhouette of Don Quijote, drunk as our forefathers were with humanity and with justice, irrealist like them, and like them too a victim of holy reality, so docile to whoever governs it by first investigating it, so rebellious, and with such unquestionable titles, toward whoever seeks to violate it without understanding it.

Jacobinism triumphant and the Republic restored, the Austrian Archduke who called himself Emperor of Mexico sacrificed—he who symbolizes the failure of the political ends, quixotic as well, of the Latin imperialism of Napoleon III—President Juárez summoned, to undertake the work of the moral and intellectual reconstruction of the Nation, a certain Mexican physician who had heard in Paris the lessons of Auguste Comte.

When Comtian positivism offered itself, in the middle of the last century, to our politicians, philosophers and educators, its ascendancy in our milieu was assured, by virtue of several profound reasons. The entire Republic clamored for peace, after a bloody epoch of unleashed and irreconcilable political passions. It was the moment of constructive reaction, of organic progress, of social stability, attained at the cost of tremendous sacrifices. Don Gabino Barreda—such was the name of the physician, disciple of Comte—undertook the work of regeneration. He was our positivist messiah, as the great French thinker had been for revolutionary Europe. He was going to establish the progress of Mexico upon science. To the tumultuous politics of the generous but recalcitrantly irrealist Jacobins would succeed the serene politics based upon sociological inductions, upon historical deductions, upon the tradition of peoples. Barreda was going to struggle preferably in the School; to form the soul of the new Mexican generations. Neither Catholic theology, foundation of the Spanish colonial regime, nor Jacobin metaphysics, inspirer of interminable revolutions, but science, positive science, facts explained by more general facts, harmonious syntheses of indisputable natural phenomena: such would be the spiritual nourishment of the new generations of the Republic. Certainly Comtism implied a new faith: the Religion of humanity; but Comte's work would be mutilated in the lecture halls of secondary education, in order to take from it the indestructible portion, the truly positive and scientific philosophy, and with it as sacred Bible the great effort would be undertaken. Mexico was wedding herself to positivism in the most moving and magnificent of possible espousals. The new French philosophy was going to engender upon the rubble of the past, as an opulent fruit, a new fatherland.

Comtism suited the race perfectly. Our innate, tropical, lazy realism found in positive philosophy its sanction. This philosophy spared one the trouble of thinking; it declared barren the effort of the great metaphysicians, builders of systems; it legitimized the national idiosyncrasy, indifferent to the perfection of knowledge.

Besides, Comtism is, as

Huxley said, a Catholicism without Christianity—that is to say, something which, by its external form, by its despotic hierarchization, suited a people that has had nothing of the Christian and much of the idolater; that had lived for centuries constrained within the regular, iron and tyrannical form of Spanish Catholicism. The substitution of the old Catholic fetishes by the new Comtian fetishes could be easy. For some fanatics it was almost imperceptible; it would be, even today, imperceptible.

Catholicism inspires our middle age as it inspired the European Middle Ages. From the industries which the noble charity of Vasco de Quiroga bestowed upon the indigenous race of Michoacán, and the form of collective property that adapts itself so well, even in our days, to the gentle life of the Indian; from the genuinely idolatrous superstition of our proletarian classes, to the Jacobinism of irreligious frenzies and the humanitarian and semi-Catholic Comtism, everything in Mexico—art, science, philosophical thought, literature, politics, customs and traditions—is Catholic by natural development or by inevitable and spontaneous reaction.

When one travels through our country and passes from one city to another, from one town to another, from one hamlet to another hamlet; when in the most populous centers and the most prosperous and industrious districts, as well as in the zones of slow agricultural life or in the mountainous and little-inhabited places where at times nature has reconquered what was formerly won for civilization, one sees standing the Churrigueresque church with its monotonous architecture—monotonous in the end, despite its motley swarm of little columns, moldings and images—one understands how profoundly the Catholic regime took root in New Spain, and how formidable revolutions such as that of the Reform have not sufficed to destroy it, nor even to shake it, notwithstanding that many of the houses of religion, the very convents and churches, were one day demolished in honor of the nascent pseudo-liberties that Jacobinism proclaimed, in honor of the ideas or, better said, of the sentiments momentarily triumphant. There remain the old temples or their secular ruins, in the midst of the deserts or in the heart of the towns, as symbols of the historical fatality that gave us for a cradle the militant Catholicism of the Spanish renaissance, a religion of magnificent exteriority and mournful sensual and pious depths.

Finally, the spirit of the nineteenth century, during its closing decades, was propitious to the increase of positivist tendencies in Mexico. Industry, commerce, material good, economic wealth were the human desiderata. Naturally, world peace had to be reflected in our culture, together with the forgetting of the nobler spiritual interests. The prudent man, who ventures neither in business nor in metaphysical speculation, who feels the need of finding a philosophical system that will justify before his limited conscience his psychological attitude, by means of positivist negations and the morality of ego-altruism; the prudent man—indifferent, judicious, submissive, petty in sum—such was the director of the life of Mexico in the senate, in the lecture hall, in the forum, in the magistracy, in the clinic, during the pacific dictatorship of Porfirio Díaz. Positivism had triumphed, establishing the government that never proposed an ideal to itself, proper to individuals who seemed to have none.

The National Preparatory School fulfilled, for the first time in the world, the project of positivist secondary education. There were taught, for many years, the abstract sciences, according to the Comtian hierarchy, from mathematics to sociology, in conformity with the famous logical, historical and didactic series extolled by the brilliant French philosopher. Only in these latter times has a reform of the preparatory school been attempted, consecrating its classrooms not exclusively to the study of science, but also to that of national, Spanish and foreign literatures, to that of history, both of Mexico and of civilization in general, and to the unjustly and clumsily postponed philosophical disciplines.

Mexico was always a country of traditions of classical culture. During the eighteenth century she was honored with the names of the learned Jesuits Alegre and Abad, consummate masters of Latin humanities. The names of Alejandro Arango y Escandón, author of the best critical and biographical essay written in the Spanish tongue on the greatest of Castilian poets, the Platonic Fray Luis de León; of Joaquín García Icazbalceta, who is for Mexican erudition as great as Menéndez y Pelayo for the Spanish; of José María Vigil, translator of Persius and profound master of the language; of Ignacio Montes de Oca y Obregón, translator of the lyric and bucolic poets of Greece; of Joaquín Arcadio Pagaza, sweet poet of the best Castilian stock and consummate Latinist—still other names besides—prolong with their attendant prestige, during the nineteenth century, the classical traditions, and suffice to affirm the prosperity that such studies enjoyed in the Republic.

The work of positivism was, in this respect, disastrous. The secular direction of such noble mental disciplines was interrupted, and absurd theses sustained by certain positivists against the teaching of the humanities prevented, for long years, the young generations from bathing their intelligence in the lustral waters of Greco-Latin culture and tempering their spirit in the perennial examples of classical history.

In the celebrated philosophical drama of Shakespearean inspiration that symbolizes aristocratic Renanism, Caliban, être informe à peine dégrossi, en voie de devenir homme [a formless being barely roughed out, on the way to becoming man], says to the rioting populace he captains against Prospero: Guerre aux livres. Ce sont les pires ennemis du peuple. Ceux qui les possèdent ont des pouvoirs sur leurs semblables. L'homme qui sait le latin commande aux autres hommes. À bas le latin! [War on books. They are the worst enemies of the people. Those who possess them have powers over their fellows. The man who knows Latin commands other men. Down with Latin!] Citizen Caliban professed positivism and had to erase Latin and the humanities from the list of subjects of secondary education.

Positivism formed a generation of men avid for material well-being, jealous of their economic prosperity, who, for thirty years, collaborated in the political work of Porfirio Díaz, led and directed by the Minister of Finance José Ives Limantour, a good minister for filling the coffers of the Porfirian government, but inept at knowing how to employ the accumulated wealth—gathered thanks to the good economic conditions of the country and of the whole world—in enterprises of culture, in efforts of morality and of civic spirit.

The failure of Mexican positivism is demonstrated. It is one of the direct conditions of the tremendous moral crisis the Republic suffers. Against Porfirian positivism the Revolution rose up. Jacobinism and positivism, the two forms of our collective thought, have already been assayed in the dialectical process of history. Both proved failures. But the irrealism of the framers of the Constitution will always be remembered by the people, on account of its ample quixotic generosity, its gallant attitude, its revolutionary spirit, and above all for having combated with valor and with sacred patriotism the alliance of the conservatives and the Europeans, eternal enemies of the liberties of America. Whereas the work of positivism—the work of indifference toward the ideal, the work of an education founded solely on science (a one-sided education which disdained, without possible justification, artistic, moral, civic, religious, historical and human culture), false as well, but not generous—will never manage to gather the suffrages of the generations to come.

The triumphant Revolution, which ought not to be flattered but ought indeed to be profoundly admired as a movement of just moral reaction against the assassination of President Madero—an act which is, one might say, adopting a famous expression of Richelieu, the most barbarous of which the History of America makes mention; the constitutionalist Revolution has at this moment before it the greatest of problems, the gravest of responsibilities. It has destroyed the immediate past; it must now give us the preparation of a better future.

Neither Jacobinism nor positivism. Neither irrealist quixotism nor positivist sanchism. Neither unrealizable ideals nor undeviating subordination to imperfect reality; but wings and lead, as Bacon wished; force to overcome the causes that thwart the ideal, and ideals broad and human that do not find themselves denied on coming into contact with life.

That is what we men of good will hope for; that is what American civilization hopes for; that is what the world hopes for. We shall all bless the revolutionaries who give us attainable liberties instead of sublime chimeras. In our time one no longer legislates with hollow phrases, with sonorous words. Sonorous words have been the misfortune of Mexico. Would that austere words might serve the Fatherland for something! Let the pulse of national life be felt. Let the history of our political vicissitudes be consulted. Let there be combined, in sum, the bovarism of the law with the discreet counsel of reason; and let truth, the classic Aristotelian golden mean, shine forth at last above the old systems, above the ideas defeated in the dialectic of our history: fantastical Jacobinism and indifferent positivism. Virtue, science and beauty are always a synthesis of opposites.

Creole indolence and Hispanic realism and quixotism have borne their fruits. According to the maxim of the Gospel we may judge the tree that engendered them. True human prudence and superior enthusiasm; love of tradition and philosophical idealism, which in other peoples, happier than ours, are achieving the gradual, truly organic progress of Civilization, have not been ours..... Will they be so some day?.....

## EL BOVARISMO NACIONAL — NATIONAL BOVARISM

Jules de Gaultier is the name of one of the most distinguished thinkers of France. His cardinal conception came from meditation upon Flaubert's book, "Madame Bovary," the immortal novel which, in the literary history of the last century, represents the most complete synthesis of romantic idealism and naturalist realism.

Aristotle said that poetry is more serious and profound than history, because history refers to what is contingent and characteristic in humanity, in each place and time, and poetry to eternal values: "The true difference between the two lies in this, that the historian relates what has been and the poet what might have been. This is what makes poetry something more philosophical and serious than history."

Let us recall in what manner the great artists define. The individualization of enthusiasm—in the strictly etymological sense of that beautiful word, that is, the possession of the soul by the god—is Don Quijote. Sancho Panza, his perpetual ally, is, quite simply, common sense, "diminished reason." Lady Macbeth is heroic and criminal ambition. Hamlet, sorrow as individuality; Segismundo, the dream; Pedro Crespo, duty; García del Castañar, Castilian honor. Dickens, Balzac, Tolstoi were also definers by "Platonic ideas," by eternal types of humanity.

Artists of the first order define, in their personal intuition of life, absolute sentiments, universal aspects of the human soul, such as the wrath of Achilles, the prudence of Ulysses, the fidelity of Penelope. Regimbard—patriot, republican, "absolutely empty," but with his great instinct of self-preservation that keeps him from despising himself, with his undoubted double personality; Homais, a bovarist too, his brother in Flaubert's fantasy, like Bouvard and Pécuchet, the two Ajaxes of impotent dialectic—all have one and the same blemish, one and the same malady: "they conceive of themselves as other than they are in reality."

Jules de Gaultier has studied minutely this curious malady, which is symbolized in all of Flaubert's characters and which Emma Bovary displays in relief: the faculty of conceiving oneself different from what one is.

Every man, at bottom, is a bovarist, an unconscious disciple of the famous French heroine. Each one thinks that he is fit for ends that are not those he would innately have to fulfill. We are born with our own mission to accomplish, but social life, human intercourse, the school, religion, politics, etc., divert us from our original mission, set before our eyes the mirage of what we should like to be. It is the construction of an angle which, having the individual himself for vertex, has for its sides the two diverse directions, the innate and the acquired, the proper and the fictitious.

And since it suffices that an idea appear in consciousness for it to tend to become reality; since scarcely do we imagine something than we already incline toward its realization, the fictitious self, the individual we have forged in our dreams, what we wish to be and not what we are, goes little by little incorporating the outward being through the work of our life; we go on sacrificing ourselves to our lie, to our ideal, to our dream. We have, as Gaultier says, "the faculty of thinking ourselves diverse from what we are in reality," and of making our thought plastic, transforming it into our own life, into objective and tangible reality.

Clearly an authentic hero, a true superman, can impose upon himself the task of creating himself anew, engendering from his own being a new being. Clearly a genius who, like Colón, conceives the possibility of a new interoceanic communication may find a new world in his path; but the greater part of us men are not capable of realizing our fictitious self. We believe ourselves thinkers and we are not; poets, perhaps, and we are not poets; sublime martyrs or redeeming saints, and only in the delirium of our fantasy do we redeem and sanctify ourselves.

Hence the tragedy of bovarism, which the French philosopher has described so well. This idealist humanity goes through life with the lure of what it wishes to be, and neglects the reality it possesses and the world it might enjoy, for the attainment of an impossible world, of a vain reality of legend.

Nevertheless, Jules de Gaultier concludes, bovarism is not a law of human morbidities and weaknesses, but a universal principle of existence. The genius and the "snob" are two bovarists. Humanity has realized what it has conceived, insofar as it has been able to do so; and the process of realization of the strong does not differ from the process of realization of the small, save by the grandeur of the results obtained.

The final conclusion of Gaultier's study is, in the end, consoling, since the same psychic effort creates good and evil. The men who have managed to modify the conditions of history, imposing their dream upon the masses, were bovarists. The weak who, in order to realize their useless dream, fell into the prison, the hospital or the madhouse, were bovarists. Children, women, men, heroes, martyrs—all of us go imposing our ideal upon life. We could all conquer if what we dreamed were not barren or chimerical. Peoples, like individuals, are also bovarists. At times they think they are other than they are in reality. But if they believe themselves free, they will come to be so one day. Mexico seeks her liberty through her history. Each of her revolutions brings the fatherland nearer to the realization of its destiny. Life is, in sum, more tolerable with bovarism than without it. Constrained within our individuality, we should be devoured by the despair of never emerging from our own misery.

## DE LA MARMITA AL CUENTA GOTAS — FROM THE STEWPOT TO THE DROPPER

"And he taught them many things by parables...."

MARK, IV, 2,

The stewpot is a humble utensil of practical and banal pottery which is commonly set over a live fire on the burner of the brazier to obtain the cooking of prosaic vegetables and hearty victuals.

The dropper is a distinguished apothecary's implement which discreetly and mathematically expels from its bosom precious essences and subtle poisons of chemical preparation.

Human instruments too possess their peculiar psychology. The stewpot is boisterous, trivial, garrulous as a gossip. The broth boils in it in great bubbles.

The dropper is aristocratic, reticent, sparing as a strict or tacit confession, as a distrustful understanding, as a perfect poet of the newest vessel, as an apprentice essayist, assiduous reader of Walter Pater and Robert Louis Stevenson.

Who would dare deny that, like domestic animals, the creations of industry reflect at times diverse aspects of the spirit that engendered them? The sharp, uncomfortable form of the last of the French shoemaker is a copy of the fine wit of France; whereas the pragmatist spirit of the nation of Edison and Roosevelt inspires the vast and comfortable conception of the shoes of Saint Louis.

A profound and intrinsic difference is to be noted between the dropper and the pot; a difference which, "subtle but real," as a mystical writer of classical India says, has suggested to me the irreverent metaphor of likening to the plebeian stewpot our old Hispano-American literature, so verbose and disheveled, and to the precise and hermetic dropper this new literature that "gushes in pulsations," as Alfonso Reyes said somewhere, I know not with what purpose.

Read Heredia, Olmedo, Andrade, Sarmiento, and you will find the plebeian impetus, the incoherent and generous effort, the everlasting youthful petulance.

Guillermo Prieto, Altamirano, Riva Palacio were, in the work of our national production, men of letters and poets of the olla podrida, of the boiling stewpot, of an incongruous and democratic genius.

Open a book by some contemporary, and the vernacular, Hispano-American effort will evoke for you the micrometric exactitude of the dropper.

Everything in these books of today is said by halves or by quarters. As for Voltaire, it is believed with both feet planted that the secret of causing tedium lies in saying everything clearly. It must be barely suggested, leaving the reader his spiritual autonomy, making him an intelligent collaborator of the one who writes; without tyrannizing him with tempestuous hendecasyllables; without vexing him with explosions of patriotic bad taste; without demagogic contortions; without noise....

Nietzsche wrote of Michelet—the celebrated historian of the Revolution—that he represents "enthusiasm in shirtsleeves." More and better than the great romantic prose writer, our old American lyric poets would today deserve the philosopher's sarcasm. Recall, if not, Heredia before Niagara:

"Tune my lyre; give it to me, for I feel in my shaken and troubled soul inspiration burning...."

Some reader of the Cuban poet will easily imagine a great lyre of oak like the one Chateaubriand placed in the hands of his Eudorus, an enormous and massive lyre of oak that might cause some serious physical harm to its own owner. Literature or poetry "of the stewpot," this of Heredia.

The French romantics, like M. Edgar Quinet—a Jacobin twin of our own—earned cruel ironies from posterity. Barbey D'Aurevilly said of Michelet's friend and fellow partisan this bloody phrase: "La Création! — Pouf! Rien de moins que la création! et par Edgar Quinet encore, ce qui l'augmente!" ["The Creation! — Pooh! Nothing less than the creation! and by Edgar Quinet no less, which makes it worse!"] One might say of the illustrious Cuban: "Niagara! Nothing less than Niagara, by José María Heredia; ce qui l'augmente! [which makes it worse!]" At least, so will some adept of the symbolic dropper say.

Nevertheless, sorely needed today in our national letters and, in general, in American letters, are poets who, like Chocano and Almafuerte, possess the grandiloquent inspiration of the old singers of America. Literary wits like Sarmiento are lacking. Popular writers are lacking, in the noblest acceptation of the word, which is, of course, the only one tolerable to good taste and to criticism.

We are in a period of refinement and transmutation of literary values. We are already emancipating ourselves from the unpoetic plebeianism of our forebears; we feel a thousand leagues from their hollow and inane clamor. Gutiérrez Nájera, José Asunción Silva, Martí, Rubén Darío rectified in the last century our artistic conscience, and they rectified it forever.

The romantic dishevelment will not return, the spirited poetry of riot and uprising, nor all that which we inevitably flout; but it is urgent to determine whether, in gaining what we have acquired, we did not have to lose something of what our fathers possessed and which we neither know how nor are able to have.

Let us be friends of the dropper; but let us remember, despite our self-sufficiency and our chemical procedures, that for many a lustrum the plebeian and generous stewpot of our grandparents sang trivially over the fire of the ancestral hearth.

## RUSIA Y MEXICO — RUSSIA AND MEXICO

Mexico is the classic country of intestine revolutions. Schopenhauer, in his "Parerga"—aphorisms on the wisdom of life—wrote in the year 1850: "In the Republic of letters everything happens as in the Mexican Republic, where each man thinks only of himself and his own peculiar interests, without caring for the nation, which marches to its ruin."

Some ingenious and patient historian has taken the trouble to count the barracks revolts and revolutions that have occurred during the single century that Mexico has been independent, and has come to a total, I believe, of 225 acts of insurrection, which yields the gallant average of 2.25 revolutions per year. To ask for more would be sheer greed.

It will surprise no one, then, that a Mexican should feel a lively interest in the dramatic social situation of Russia.

Revolution is, for us, the habitual, "classic" form, one might say, in which the life of peoples manifests itself. "One swallow does not make a summer," runs the most prudent proverb. Chile, Argentina—who or what others!—will not make one either. Revolutions horrify the western Europeans as their infamous wars horrify us. In America we live within systematic revolution. In it "we move and have our being," we might say, recalling opportunely and irreverently the Gospel.

Russia resembles the Latin republics. She is, like us, a civilization by halves or quarters; a race that "is making itself," that is being elaborated at present in the great chemico-moral laboratory of a revolution consubstantial and organic, with all the formidable warlike explosions of her history. She has problems that merge with our own. She must convert her genuine, oriental despotism into free, popular and representative life. And this is precisely our fearful national problem, from the day the republic was proclaimed upon the imperial disaster of don Agustín de Iturbide.

Russia's problem lies in passing from the ukase to the law; and this is the problem we Mexicans are resolving, through our inveterate intestine revolutions.

A particular friend of ours, who practices diplomacy, told us not long ago that in his relations with the diplomats of various countries he fraternized preferably with Russians and Poles rather than with his colleagues of other European nationalities, by reason, our friend told us, of a certain profound similarity of characters and inclinations.

In the Russian books, so profound and realistic; in the great novels of Gogol, Dostoiewsky and Tolstoi, we have managed to perceive, now and then, moral and psychic attributes that seem torn from the social life of Mexico; and on finishing this or that illustrious page—novelistic in form, philosophical and ethical in intention—the holy image of the Fatherland has haunted us, and her name has been spontaneously pronounced by our lips. It is the same defective, improvised, tragic social organization. The same constricting bureaucracy, the same radical difference of classes, of castes, and the same egoism here as there. Administrative corruption—which, as in the epoch of Huerta, spent the wealth of the people on the enrichment of a few optimates—is the determining cause of the fall of the Romanoffs.

The war of the European nations will be important for the moral evolution of humanity if it does not contract into a sordid biological struggle of finances, industries and races; if, as has happened in Russia, it engenders in reaction, among the belligerent peoples, the triumph of necessary ideals, of new juridical, social and political forms.

What does it matter, after all, whether Alsace be German or French? There are excellent reasons, which we all know, for making Lorraine an organic part of France; but German civilization is as great and illustrious as the French. Both are that same European culture elaborated jointly by the Pasteurs and the Haeckels, the Goethes and the Voltaires, the Rousseaus and the Kants.

What matters is that Ireland should be autonomous and free like England. What is urgent is that Doctor Liebknecht should replace Kaiser Wilhelm in the political direction of his people, as Professor Miliukoff has replaced Tsar Nicholas. The geographical limits of the great powers are a matter of little moment. They always were in history. The variable dimensions of an irregular blue or red blotch on the map of Europe ought to concern only the children who study political geography in the primary schools.

The laborious population of the Russian Empire, Tolstoi said, is too busy providing for the securing of its existence to be able to feel interest in the political questions that form the moral basis of patriotism. "The problems of Russian influence in the Orient, of German unity, of the restitution to France of the provinces she lost in the last war with Prussia, etc., do not matter to the Russian people, not only because it almost never knows what conditions gave birth to those problems, but because the interests of its life are wholly independent of its political interests."

The contemporary catastrophe justifies Tolstoi's profound social and political thought. When not only is "there something rotten in Denmark," but the whole kingdom is one immense rot-heap, it is secondary whether Riga and Warsaw be Russian or German. What is urgent is to kill the minister who steals the bread of the people and to dethrone the Tsar.

Let us piously accompany "Holy Russia" in the conquest of her liberty.

## EL CREPUSCULO DE MAQUIAVELO — THE TWILIGHT OF MACHIAVELLI

Justly or not, the name of the brilliant historian, secretary of the Republic of Florence, symbolizes the astute and crafty politics that deals its blows with perfidy and duplicity. Machiavellianism has always been, until now, not only the politics directly inspired by the maxims of the author of "The Prince," but all human politics.

Deception, which conscience reproves in the relations of individuals, diplomacy not only tolerates but stimulates and applauds in the relations of peoples. One morality is for men and another for nations; one law for individuals and another for fatherlands. At bottom, barbarism still reigns, sustaining the zoological triumph of the strong people over the weak; and what is called crime is transmuted into virtue, and infamy into glory, if one passes from a nation considered individually to the universal bosom of the nations.

Delinquent peoples lack juridical sanction. Scarcely does the majestic synthesis of international conscience begin to arise with the contemporary war. Poland revives; Serbia, Rumania, Belgium are more vigorous today, when the imperial armies outrage them, than before, when they were derisively defended by useless conventions that could be flouted and broken. The world is on the eve of a new international justice. Since the celebrated treaties of Westphalia, there has not been witnessed so true a renewal of international customs. Then the Habsburgs of Madrid and of Vienna were humbled; now it will be necessary to defeat the Hohenzollern of Berlin. It seems that German power has always been adverse to the destinies of civilization. It was necessary to be rid forever of the Holy Germanic Empire. Force does not suffice to found right. "Quel dominio é solo durabile, che é volontario" ["Only that dominion is durable which is voluntary"], said the Florentine Citizen. German domination collides with the unbreakable will of peoples; that is why it is so ephemeral that it could not even be born.

In his last address to the American Congress, President Wilson solemnized the funeral of Machiavellianism. No more secret treaties between the nations. No more perfidy. In the light of day must the pact of honor be sealed. Henceforward the disastrous maxim of deception will be exchanged for the sentence of duty. International customs will not prevent wars, as penalties do not prevent the commission of crimes; but that people bold enough to disturb by force the order of the world will receive the punishment of the world. Today there are twenty nations that fight against the Germans. Tomorrow all humanity will rise against the new and reckless aggressor. to consider

As citizens of a weak people, we Mexicans ought to pause and weigh profoundly the unique importance of this great episode of history. With whom shall we stand?...... With those who represent the right of force, or with those who strive to secure the incorruptible force of right? Who protects us? Who protects the small peoples in this instant of tremendous affliction?

It is the apocalypse of ambition without measure, of power for power's sake! We are not strong. Shall we stand with those who proclaim force as the sole virtue? We do not have numerous armies or munitions like the military empires of the Old World. Shall we applaud the savage hosts that subjugated Belgium and annihilated Montenegro? In critical moments, errors are crimes.

It falls to President Wilson to hold very high the labarum of justice that he says he follows. To be just toward humanity in the face of Germany is great. To be just with Mexico is to be immortal. The principles the democratic leader sustains will have their most brilliant confirmation when applied to our people. If it be true that justice is not founded upon force; if imperialism is an outrage and the equality of nations an essential principle, Mexico will have nothing to fear from the United States. Wilhelm II will be a magnate of the past and President Wilson a moralist of the future.

But it is necessary that action corroborate the commandment; that the principle be impregnated with reality; because he who thinks well and acts ill was always more condemnable than he who thinks ill and acts accordingly. The moralist has spoken. Let his conduct vouch for his opinion.

Ideas are subtle and vigorous forces. When a luminous thought springs from a consciousness, it begins to be a reality. The defeat of the diplomatic lie has begun. Wilson has seen clearly that sophism is the root of crime and war the monstrous inflorescence of perfidy. For why—if we condemn the prestige of the lie—should we exalt it as a political weapon?

"Un buono e sabio Principe deve amare la pace e fuggire la guerra" ["A good and wise Prince ought to love peace and flee war"], said the subtle Florentine. Yes, it is true; but the way to banish war is not to deceive, but precisely the contrary.

Let the struggle of civilized humanity continue in its gigantic development until it achieves the triumph of justice. Let blood—that good historical solvent of wickedness—efface, as it is shed, the recalcitrant sophisms and make the new discreet and honorable politics prosper. Let peoples and men live in a house of glass; "in the broad day," as Auguste Comte taught in his vigorous French aphorism.

Only then will there dawn in history the twilight of Machiavelli.

## LA DOCTRINA WILSON, SIN WILSON — THE WILSON DOCTRINE, WITHOUT WILSON

The political world of European civilization at present counts three heads of state of universal reputation: Kaiser Wilhelm II, a military monarch spirited and vigorous, a copy of an Otto or a Frederick, hero of contemporary aggressive pan-Germanism, and the presidents Wilson and Poincaré.

France and the United States gave, not long ago, an admirable example to all countries of democratic constitution, by raising, respectively, to the supreme magistracy in each great nation, and almost simultaneously, M. Raymond Poincaré and Mr. Woodrow Wilson.

As a guarantee of national success and prosperity, there must always be an intimate consortium between the elements that constitute the organization of government and the social cream that represents the mentality of the people: thinkers, men of letters, publicists, priests, etc. In other terms: rulers and intellectual directors, far from living apart from one another, ought to help each other mutually in the science of politics, which is a complex and diverse activity, a discipline that Plato entrusted in his ideal Republic to the philosophers. Rulers and intellectuals, as elements of what Novicow calls the social sensorium, must march together in order to assure the organic and coherent development of public activity.

Poincaré and Wilson are accomplished representatives of the social élite of their own peoples and distinguished rulers. They seem to announce to the world that the Platonic utopia is being fulfilled through the vicissitudes of History. Sophocracy, the government of the wise, so ardently extolled by Renán, begins to be a reality on earth as the fruit or precious inflorescence of the two great modern democracies: the Yankee and the French.

Mr. Wilson appears as an enemy of imperialism and the trusts and a scourge of simonies and prevarications. He is a severe professor of justice and humanity who has not feared to measure his moral force against the overwhelming material energy, corrupting and corrupted, of powerful capitalist and delinquent associations. May God preserve him at the head of his people if he is always to be just and chivalrous!

It is not a question, however, in this brief article, of the national importance of the American President, but of some aspect of his international significance, which so greatly matters to Mexicans, since—no one can deny it—during these last times he has been a most popular or most unpopular personage, but always a superlative one, of our internal political life.

Mr. Wilson is the author of the Wilson Doctrine, famous like that of Monroe, of which, strictly speaking, it is a most recent prolongation, a brief diplomatic corollary. The recent doctrine is famous, however restricted its application may be up to this moment. The case of Mexico has made the reputation of the puritan president's political theory. It provoked political phenomena as important as the first combined action of three South American powers, and the fall of the dictatorship of Huerta in Mexico. What is the Wilson Doctrine?

Without possessing the precise and complex culture of the diplomat and the jurist, we venture to define it synthetically as the categorical declaration that the United States will recognize only those Hispano-American governments that conform to the relevant constitutional law, and that originate from it or in accordance with it. Extra-juridical or anti-juridical governments will not be recognized by the omnipotent chancellery of Washington.

Nothing, in appearance, more rational and prudent. Nothing, at bottom, more dangerous for the America that speaks Spanish.

To begin with, International Law, which makes of each State a full and independent entity, suffers, to our detriment, a grave exception. Our governments will not be recognized if they are not legitimate. President Wilson, with a single stroke of the pen, diminishes our sovereignty or constrains it within the limits of the constitutionality of our governments. Such is the most grave decision that has just met with success, for the first time, applied to Mexico.

Besides, no government of any people on Earth has been or is fully constitutional. There are laws because there are imperfections. If peoples fulfilled their moral ends absolutely, there would be no laws, nor would there be governments. Government is a necessary imperfection founded upon the fact that men unfortunately must obey and therefore must be commanded. But to command and to obey are two profoundly disagreeable actions. Perfect man will fulfill his collective actions by inspiration and spontaneous collaboration. The law is given for its fulfillment, because it is never fulfilled. If it were always fulfilled it would not be ordained. What is useless ought not to be ordained. Neither democratic Switzerland, archetype of democracies, nor France, nor still less the United States, comply with their constitutional prescriptions...., and one may ask: would Mr. Wilson exclude from the benefits of International Law the three great democracies of the world?

This other elementary reflection, which perhaps did not occur to the Yankee president, may next be proposed. These Spanish peoples of America did not organize themselves politically by carrying into their written official Constitutions attributes proper to national reality, reflecting in the precepts of their laws the needs peculiar to each society. The Constitution did not spring up in Mexico as a genuine expression of what there is at home; rather it was ordered from abroad, like the tools of our industries, like the fashions of our women ordered from Paris or from New York. We have the clearest and most patent constitutional bovarism: the faculty of conceiving ourselves politically other than we are in reality. Such a faculty engendered our law, as it has engendered all the laws of all peoples. It is the ruler of our political ideals, stamped as supreme norms of national life in the prescriptions of the Magna Carta.

We gave ourselves a democratic Constitution by imitation. The prestige of the United States and the French Revolution are the causes of our fundamental legislation. For this reason it is easy to demonstrate that the Constitution of 1857 has never been in force in our political milieu, despite the time that has passed since it was drafted and promulgated.

If, then, President Wilson wishes to evict non-constitutional governments, he may bring about most grave effects that perhaps he does not expect, because there will hardly be among us an absolutely constitutional government; because in no nation is the government absolutely constitutional.

The dictatorship of Huerta was unspeakable, monstrous. Nevertheless, during such a regime the people learned one day that the Yankee squadrons had invaded the first port of the Republic, and asked the tyrant to hurl himself into combat against the invasion. He did not do so; he was not great then nor ever; less then than ever, and the Revolution has triumphed with the purpose, which it must fulfill, of restoring to us the liberties and rights that were trampled; but if afterward, as time runs its interminable course, a new revolution should rise up threateningly, the Government of the United States would act ill before History and Humanity if it took the side of the rebels; and yet the intrinsic logic of its doctrine requires that the American Government be on the side of all possible revolutions against all effective governments, since none will be fully constitutional, fully and perfectly democratic.

The United States will be the perpetual ally of the ruin of the American nations, and the puritanism of the illustrious President the moral basis of many immoralities.

When, tomorrow, President Wilson yields his post to a creature of the oil trust, to a devotee of violent conquest, to a republican heir of the traditions of his party; when the professor of humanity and of justice sees himself replaced by a mystic of power and of the necessary expansion of the race, American aggression will fall upon our unfortunate peoples, will oppress them as it has oppressed them, will dismember them as it has dismembered them, will outrage them in various ways as it has outraged them—let impartial and serene history say if it be not so—and the doctrine that proposed to impose peace will in fact impose war. As grave as these may be the consequences of Wilson's theory once its author has disappeared; so grave will be, if destiny allows it, the effects of the Wilson Doctrine, without Wilson.

A powerful nation, a severe chancellery and an international moralist turned ruler by the hazards of History demand of us the constitutionality of our Government. Since to live within such a regime of constitutionality is a good, let us be prudent; let us reform our Magna Carta to make it adaptable to the conditions of our moral and political society; and, once reformed, let us fulfill it with a constant and decided resolve to live within the law and its prescribed forms. The United States constrain us to it. Their tutelage is unjustifiable, but the desire the Wilson Doctrine expresses favors us. Let us be loyal to ourselves. Let us strengthen this resolve of juridical life, this firm longing to practice liberty within the law, and let us remember every day that, now that Wilson's theory has been born, there exists a new reason to be devotees of the law.

THE INTERNAL CONFLICT OF OUR DEMOCRACY

The contemporary world governs itself democratically or tends to do so in an irresistible manner, on account of one of those capital reasons which, thanks to their evidence, spare further developments and subsidiary corroborations: by virtue of the fact that it cannot govern itself in any other way. Sovereignty resides essentially and originally in the people, as the Political Constitution of the United Mexican States says, because, as the centuries have passed, however many origins might have been assigned to public force—or rather, however many human respects and deferences existed capable of rooting and sustaining political institutions—have disappeared; all the traditions, formerly venerated, have been undone and lost, or perhaps have been summed up in the one respect, in the sole tradition that constitutes the unequivocal source of sovereignty: the human mass, the people.

When it is said that the contemporary world governs itself democratically, it is not meant, however, that true government of the people by the people, which is what etymologically is to be understood by the word democracy, has been achieved everywhere. It is only too well known that perfect democracy has not existed, and that perhaps such a supreme regime is never to exist. It is an essential law, an inflexible principle of human efforts individual and social, political and non-political, to tend constantly toward ultimate and eternal ends, and not to achieve, despite this, the full attainment, the full ascendancy of the holiest and dearest ideals; but surely it is worth more to strive to obtain a better situation, even though such a state should never be achieved, than to content oneself with present reality, imperfect and condemnable for more than one reason. It is worth more to know how to hope than to know how to resign oneself.

Thus, then, democracy, which for unquestionable motives must be the sole political formula of modern peoples, is at the same time an urgent necessity and an insuperable ideal; something that springs from the very conditions of contemporary civilization, and something that, in the purest sphere of ideas, answers to the highest notion of justice: the government of all by all, universal participation in political organization, the atomization of force, the collective harmony of governors and governed.

When about one man—the king, the emperor, the autocrat, scion of an illustrious lineage, depositary and symbol of the grandeurs of a people, material and human part of the immaterial fatherland, most individual representation of the collective soul of a nation—the subjects gathered as about a standard of victory, sovereignty resided really and positively in the monarch by the unanimous consent of the multitude; in him was summed up, without metaphor, all the splendor of the race; and his designs, approved and heeded by the people, were the designs of the collectivity in which he held sway. But today, what king, what emperor, what leader, what man concentrates in himself so glorious an empire? In what European or American nation would the people let the autocrat rule with absolute dominion? The constitutional regime of the world has established, everywhere, that interesting unstable equilibrium of kings and parliaments; and a day will come, not far off, when the equilibrium will break; and once the last respect, the last secular tradition is suppressed, kings—venerable remains of traditions that are dying before our eyes—will disappear forever in Portugal as in England, in Russia as in Spain. Toward that precise term the evolution of modern civilizations is moving.

In our America the king does not exist and has almost never existed in History; but the caudillo exists and has always existed: a temporary king, generally more authoritarian and vigorous than many monarchs, a king by precarious title who, at a given instant, governs outside the law, above the law, against the law itself; and he governs until the people grants him its favor, its acceptance, its respect; but a day comes when the people flouts the caudillo, returns to the law, abominates the man; and then all his majesty is annihilated, his services are disowned, his dictatorship is condemned, and he who before was feared as a god becomes later the object of the derision and ingratitude of men.

Today each man is born conscious of his autonomy, more or less circumstanced and relative. We all know that essentially we are free and masters of ourselves, and we reject traditional government as we have rejected many other political, civil and religious traditions. In this constant collapse of habits and traditions which is one of the noble characteristics of our time, government must be democratic even were it imperfect. Our liberty is subordinated only to the liberty of all; our personality, which would feel itself diminished in bowing before a man, is not injured when it submits to the will of the whole. For this reason we must be democrats; because if government must exist, it can be based only on mutual consent, on cooperation within the law, on the conviction that, in submitting ourselves to a regime, to a power, we are a part of that regime and there resides in us a particle of that force. Thus is less lamentable this sad social necessity of obeying, and less irritating this painful prerogative of commanding.

But if it is sought to rule while denying us all participation in command, assuredly we protest, assuredly we shall claim the political enjoyment of thinking that in commanding and in obeying we do nothing but rule and fulfill what we ourselves have willed; and, sooner or later, he who at a given instant could believe that he would govern without us will be reduced to impotence. Democracy is the political formula of modern individualism, ever more intrepid and jealous of its essential prerogatives; and since it is, at the same time, the absolute political regime, the perfect collaboration of the whole in the public thing, the ideal that inspires us and the reality that sustains us work together in favor of the fated development of democratic ideas and sentiments. To seek to oppose such an evolutionary impetus is profoundly to misunderstand the great social currents; to insist on constraining the overwhelming thrust of History.

In synthesis it may be said that the internal conflict of all modern democracies is this: such a formula of government imposes itself by virtue of pressing social and psychological necessities, it is the latest progress of political institutions; but even though no other form of government be conceivable, men are still far from being able to live as they have to live and as they ought to live politically; poverty, ignorance and injustice still prevent the advent of the era of perfect democracies.

Applying to our own social environment the general ideas set forth above, it is possible to declare the conditions peculiar to our democracy and to form an adequate opinion of the obstacles that oppose, within our political being, the triumph of democratic practices. Let us point out, in rapid synthesis, the timely remedies for our present situation—undecided, disquieting, dramatic.

The Constitution of '57 proclaims that Mexico is a democratic, representative, federal Republic; that is to say, that our nation is to be ruled by an ideally perfect government. It may be affirmed that from the year 1857 to this year of 1913 the Constitution has ruled sovereignly on the venerable paper on which it is written, but not for half an hour has it been, as it ought to be, the fundamental law of our ruler, the supreme norm of our national activities. Does this mean that all our governments have been bad or that the Constitution is something completely unrealizable? Does the verification of this fact signify that we must modify the Federal Pact in order to be happy, or that we shall go on being unhappy within the prescriptions of the Federal Pact? Is Mexico, perchance, a people unadaptable to the conditions of contemporary civilization, or are the present circumstances of our political life, mutatis mutandis [the necessary changes having been made], those of all the peoples of the earth? Here

T are the questions our conscience formulates concerning the internal drama of our democracy. Will the Constitution of '57 be the cause of all our bad governments and of our incomparable intestine revolutions?.... The answer imposes itself: The prescriptions of the Constitution must be adapted to the historical and moral environment, and, after the reform, the firm will to fulfill the modified and formulated precepts must be put into practice. The law is always a bovarism, an attempt at the sanction of the social good; but the bovarism that wholly ignores the conditions of reality is an evil, and that which manages to synthesize them with the imperative forms of the ideal is the greatest of juridical and political goods. It is foolish to declare that democracy cannot be proposed to our people as its form of government; but it is madness to wish to attain perfection without the combined effort of the generations. a

The general concepts noted above serve to furnish us with a first solution, the beginning of a solution, to the grave hesitations that were likewise proposed earlier. In sum, what we verify incontrovertibly is that Mexico is an imperfect democracy, at times tragically imperfect; but even so, Mexico is not an exception among peoples nor a monster among nations. It is simply one of so many contemporary societies in which democracy, despite being the only possible form of government and the only one extolled by absolute justice, cannot be fully realized; in which, perhaps, there exist more negative causes, more conditions thwarting the political ideal that is anxiously sought.

We were a Spanish colony and our forefathers hastily made of us a federal and democratic republic. We are a conglomerate of distinct races, a somewhat motley synthesis of diverse cultures, a country of vast territory and, besides, a poor country, with formidable ethnic and agrarian problems, with fearful problems relating to public education, to the organization of justice, to the organic exercise of suffrage. But there exist other nations, twins of ours, daughters of Spain and her traditions, heiresses of her collective genius, which, like us, have the same problems, the same political organization, the same ethnic origin, the same enormous potential wealth of our soil and the same real and present lack of economic resources.

Our forefathers declared us prematurely republicans and democrats.—Oscar Wilde has said, with his habitual wit of brilliant paradoxes: what is premature is perfect.—We are not going to believe Oscar Wilde; but let us excuse the quixotic attitude of the Jacobins by saying in their favor that, when they erected us into a democratic republic, they held the contemporary ideal, they had already lost their respect for the political traditions of the colonial regime, and the Mexican people was going to lose it too, as they had.

It would have been better, doubtless, a less abrupt transition, a less dramatic national history, of slow evolution, of unhurried gestation, measured, uniformly accelerated, as the mathematicians say; but nations do not march in measured step, because they are guided not by pure reason but by sentiment; nor is their destiny geometric and exact; nor, above all, is it now time to think of what could have or ought to have happened, but to accept what has happened—all that has happened, errors and truths, bovarisms and generous and imperfect quixotisms—to strengthen our love for Mexico such as it is and has been, and to subordinate lesser interests to the supreme interest of the fatherland.

It happens in various cases that a revolutionary, an apostle, denounces before the people the defects of a given government; the people is convinced of the justice on the side of the one who shows them, follows him, acclaims him, makes him triumph; and when he in turn comes to be the ruler, the noted defects reappear, and those who formerly condemned them now commit them, giving occasion for others, like them, to set about denouncing them, only to be unable afterward to avoid them when they manage to climb to the supreme magistracies.

This is the scheme of our revolutions, the synthesis of our vicissitudes; but it must be observed that, at every step, something is gained. The liberties that were not formerly possessed are enjoyed later, however much they may seem momentarily diminished; the impulses that had never been felt are felt and spread; and, in sum, there always remains an approach to the ultimate purpose, to the higher end pursued through the years and which is being achieved every day, without ever being obtained completely. That is individual and collective life: the search for absolute happiness, which is never found, and the finding of something better that is being built each day.

To accelerate the movement, already secular, of our democracy, three supreme necessities must be satisfied: the economic, the juridical, the intellectual; in other terms: we ought not to think of being able to become a strong people if we do not possess the three corresponding virtues: wealth, justice, enlightenment; cardinal virtues of the true democracies such as France, such as Switzerland, such as the United States. But it is useless to struggle for their attainment if we do not live in the propitious atmosphere of liberty. An adequate agrarian policy cannot be established without the more or less imperfect exercise of democracy; the adequate functioning of justice cannot be attained without that same basis of liberty; nor will the national policy of public instruction be opportune outside the free exercise of suffrage. The error of Porfirio Díaz consisted in systematically preferring the development of economic factors, in believing that wealth is the sole seat of strong governments and, above all, in thinking that national well-being required the suppression of democratic practices; for this reason his government, which counseled the motto little politics and much administration, fell vanquished. The political apostolate of Francisco I. Madero extolled the contrary, and this is his principal merit, his noble virtue for History, amid the grave or slight errors into which he might have fallen, into which he surely did fall. Economic prosperity—social, not industrial, not commercial, not capitalist, not circumscribed; justice; public instruction: such must be the desiderata of Mexicans of good will. But so long as our people does not demand of its governments the practice of liberal institutions, the prescriptions of law will be illusory, our political life will suffer from its characteristic defects, from its perennial imperfections, and the internal conflict of our democracy will persist with its dramatic effects, feeding constantly upon itself.

## COMENTO BREVE DE LA ODA A LA MUSICA DE FRAY LUIS DE LEON — BRIEF COMMENTARY ON THE ODE TO MUSIC OF FRAY LUIS DE LEÓN

Gentlemen Academicians:

Great is my boldness in bringing to you, as an offering of imperishable gratitude for the honor and favor you granted me in electing me to enjoy the gifts of your company, some humble philosophical thoughts upon the immortal Ode of Fray Luis de León, which begins:

"The air grows serene and clothes itself in beauty and unwonted light, Salinas.....

An Ode in which thought soars as high

(Address delivered to obtain the title of Corresponding Academician of the Royal Spanish Academy, on the night of Tuesday the fourth of January, 1921, at the solemn session which the Mexican Academy of the Language held to that end.) as emotion flowers pure; a poem, among all those in Castilian, of supreme formal perfection; and which, being music in itself like the Music it sings, one would say is rather the concord of a lyre which the Architect of the world himself attuned in his wisdom to the arcane rhythm upon which rests the mobile equilibrium of the Universe as upon a subtle foundation of unfathomable beauty and truth.

How many times, in the solitude of meditation, at the hour when a new day is stealthily prepared in the shadow—a day as singular within its genuine beauty as all the others to come and past—has the Ode to Francisco Salinas, learned by heart in youth and recited in the intimacy of consciousness, spoken to me of the infinite and of perfection! How many times has it drawn me away from the life that flowed, to plunge me, if only for an instant, into the repose of eternity! There was one occasion so near to rapture that I almost lost contact with the earth—at least my enthusiasm believed so—and I felt wings being born to my being, and that thus, free and clean of carnal attributes, I ascended or plunged, I could not tell you which, into the luminous darkness of God.

Fray Antolín Merino, in the prologue he set to the works of the illustrious poet, says that "Fray Luis sings with his spirit looking to heav-

"/For en, but with his feet fettered to the earth, and his song is sorrowful as the sigh of the exile." For this reason his lyric moves me more deeply than the pure mystical raptures of the blessed Juan de la Cruz. Perhaps a seraph would listen with greater delight to the poet of Carmel. His song is only bliss, only the triumph of love. But the human creature, the exiled and nostalgic creature that is neither an initiated star nor an unconscious stone, but a mixture or synthesis of beast and god, will always hear more intelligibly the song of Fray Luis, will better perceive the drama of joyous detachment that his genius conceived, than the pure mystical and ecstatic trembling of San Juan de la Cruz. At least, let me tell you that I prefer—without imposing dogmas or declaring supremacy—the nostalgia of the Vida Retirada and the Noche Serena to the effusive throbbing of the Cántico Espiritual between the Soul and its bridegroom Jesus.

And the detachment from the "low, dark prison"—abandonment or renunciation—is uttered, even more immaterially than the great lyric poet's other poems, by his Ode to music: because the tenuous bond of sound, the harmonious number of musical vibration are, of all the physical bonds that refer us to matter, the most subtle and the closest to our spiritual essence,

The Ode to the Music of the blind Salinas begins with a most gentle stanza which, in its first two lines, sets forth the peace and liberation of the soul transfixed by the rapture of sound:

"The air grows serene unwonted...."

And clothes itself in beauty and unwonted light...."

A sentiment of pure harmony and reciprocal assent of things to the arcanum of rhythm; the Pythagorean concordance of world and soul which the poet will declare later with sublime stupor; but, from the beginning, as the hand of the blind musician governs the exquisite and subtle melody, the air is illumined, the wind is calmed and clothes itself in beauty.

The lyre sounded and the enchantment began. It is, precisely, the spell of Art which, since it enjoys the world not for the base satisfaction of appetite nor for the realization of individual ends, always petty, but for the disinterested contemplation that bears its end within itself, suppresses at a stroke the ardor of every moment's struggle, the deception of senseless and confused activity, and finds, behind the reality of life, the Platonic Archetype, the incorruptible Idea that offers and communicates itself in all its inviolate truth.

And with the aesthetic revelation, the spirit emerges from its carnal forgetfulness, quickens memory and adds to the lesser rapture of art the mystical recollection of its essence:

"At whose divine sound my soul, that lies sunk in forgetfulness, recovers its bearings, and the lost memory of its first illustrious origin."

The economic ends of existence—the soul knowing itself—it disdains; it betters itself in thoughts, withdraws from the vulgar, and even objective beauty itself seems to it deceitful and perishable. So exalted is

Then the musical harmony of corporeal things ceases, or rather passes to a second plane; it awaits being no more than the humble retinue of the Uncreated Harmony. The mystical listener

"Passes through all the air until he reaches the highest sphere, and there he hears another mode of imperishable music that is the first of all."

Do you not perceive something like a hidden movement that seems to animate the stanza in its beautiful inner structure?:

...."another mode of imperishable music"....

Do you feel how a rhythm is dislocated or broken in order to realize itself in another and higher one?....

In the fifth stanza is the vision of the Christ the Musician. Pythagoras—Iamblichus relates—had invented canticles to be applied to the different situations of the spirit. Some destined to calm anger; others to kindle the downcast will; others, finally, to infuse the human soul into the cosmic number, unifying it in this way, as in the indistinct ringing of a bell, with the all-powerful formula that generates gods and men.

Fray Luis de León, who like Pythagoras heard the music of the spheres, makes it proceed from the Great Artist of the worlds. He himself sounds the sacred cithara whose harmony sustains the fabric of the universe. What a subject to inspire a great Christian painter, like Fra Angelico of Fiesole! Had the painter of Italy been able to know the verses of the Castilian poet—perhaps as assiduous a frequenter of angels as he himself was—he would have bequeathed us the pictorial image of the Christ the Musician, and we might today, perhaps, admire the glorious apparition after the manner of a Byzantine icon: a gentle Christ of wavy hair, long and golden beard and lips of benediction, whose rare delicacy would stand out in the blue and gold of an enduring enamel, amid choirs of seraphim and archangels who, fused in a mystical rainbow like those Dante saw, would launch to the winds of the Rose and would sing in chorus with their trumpets and cymbals the sounding of the divine cithara. The rhythm of the art of the blind Salinas is fused—the poet warns us—with the uncreated music. The music of the world becomes an effusion of universal music, and the hand of the professor of Salamanca girds itself inscrutably to the measure that moves the cithara of God.

But the generosity of Fray Luis is not to remain more than an instant in aesthetic and mystical enjoyment without calling upon men, like Beethoven in the final chorus of the Ninth Symphony, to share in the bliss that overwhelms him. He will rejoice in the good of others. A Christian knows that his selfish happiness is only a satanic chimera, a base and accursed illusion. Whoever wishes to be happy must place his dream in another being, in all the beings capable of felicity. How could he be content if he knew that there are others discontented? How could he rejoice if there were the sorrowful? How could he love God if he did not strive to make all men love Him?....

For this reason, the quiet of his singular good is suddenly interrupted by the remembrance of his brothers; and, a disciple of the Jesus who preached on the Mount the Beatitudes, he cries with tender voice:

Glory of the sacred Apollonian choir, friends whom I love above all treasure, for all the rest is sorrowful weeping.

And while the friends arrive at the poet's call, he makes his vows that the music of Salinas may not cease to sound, snatching him away to the spheres of contemplation, while his senses remain forever dead to the daily and confused struggle of living. Gentlemen Academicians:

The heroic Augustinian friar—heroic according to the gallant technical usage of Gracián—is, perhaps, among all Castilian poets, the most perfect. He recalls by his enthusiastic and serene lyric the noble classical poetry; and although, as a distinguished critic said, there were in his art reliefs of Horace, there vibrates in his heart the divine spark of Calvary. The bloody flower of Christianity perfumed his plectrum and crystallized his ideal; the heaven of the martyrs formed his paradise; the superior virtue of his genius was charity. Precisely for this reason he must be counted among the great lyric poets of history. For what is Western civilization but the marvelous synthesis of the eternal smile of Greece and the tears of Jesus?....

I picture Fray Luis to myself in the very city of Minerva and in the glorious days of Pericles and Plato. I see him discoursing through the streets of Athens, certainly not as an interloper, but as a legitimate citizen of that exemplary city. The marbles and the men of that time would perhaps feel that the foreign friar was not wholly strange to them; and the wind itself, ever a friend of Athena, would blow, perhaps, gently, stirring the folds of the Augustinian habit and making them take the harmonious disposition of those of the statues that Phidias carved; because the Castilian poet, in his finished prose as in his enduring odes and his perfect sonnets of love, transmitted to us, alive again, the secret of the entire beauty that Apollo poured in floods over the soil of Attica, propitious to the Ideal.

There let Fray Luis remain, restored to his victorious brothers, the Greek and Latin lyric poets; but nearer to us for his Nazarene love and his blessed self-denial. Let him remain with his Christ in his hands and his profound piety in his soul, and let him feel, perhaps, that the goddess of the gray-green astonished pupils offers him one of her favored glances, while in his breast the blaze of the Cross is kindled.

## JUSTO SIERRA, EL AMANTE, EL ESCÉPTICO, EL HISTORIADOR — JUSTO SIERRA: THE LOVER, THE SKEPTIC, THE HISTORIAN

"L'amour est la véritable clef de l'histoire." ["Love is the true key of history."]—Paul Sabatier.

It is a matter for astonishment to find genuine representatives of that form of universal sympathy which, according to Höffding, is the historical sense; spirits who, like that of the illustrious Justo Sierra, knew how to join to History the philosophical intuition; to the thought of the complex and the singular, that of the universal and the simple; to the preoccupation with defining the invariable, the knowledge of the various, the search for the perennial in the changing; and to pure intelligence, its severe forms and its intrinsic logic, intuition, that is to say, love. Justo Sierra was a Platonist, because he was, like Plato, a lover.

He knew how to love with divine fire both great things and small: his powerful intuition went always, on the wings of his insatiable love, in pursuit of moral certitude and of science; for this reason he penetrated where the cold and pure reason of discursive temperaments cannot reach—that clairvoyant reason, but incapable of founding itself; for this reason in his books of History and in his pedagogical and civic addresses (consecrated to the Mexican nation to exalt and dignify her, as those of Fichte to the German nation, to rouse her from the patriotic torpor in which she lay when, at the beginning of the last century, she was outraged by the armies of Bonaparte) there throbs the knowledge of Humanity at the bottom of a sincere, indeed apostolic optimism, which kisses "with profound piety," in spite of all ironies and all skepticisms, "the hand of the Christian martyr who lit the lamp of the Catacombs"—a knowledge capable of attaining its object because it investigates it with all the resources of the soul, because it solicits it with the irresistible attraction of the lover, who engenders the object of his lucubrations and derives it, without effort, from his own psychological attitude, from his own substance.

His was an ardent and irrepressible love, philosophical, after the manner of the amply human mysticism of the Spanish mystics; not like that of Tolstoi, merciful and cold, as doña Emilia Pardo Bazán has so well defined it. It was a love tinged with irony, but not subject to it, not its vassal, but always ruling and always fundamental. That great and illustrious old man—whose solid head, whose robust breast were the lodging only of noble and virile enthusiasms—had, in truth, an incomprehensible wealth of passion; and whether he described a historical epoch filled with heroisms and plumed with victories, or spoke, like a good father of a family, of the fatherland or of the school, or traced, in a few lines, the portrait of a great man, or bent, with religious unction, to auscultate the heart of the humble, to kiss where the people kisses, "through faith or through love," always from his passion, from his enthusiasm, sprang the first act and the first organic formula of his historical knowledge, thereafter to extend and diffuse itself in the rhythm of his magnificent prose, until it completed a harmonious whole in which the resurrection of the past was accomplished, before his readers or his hearers, with the real deception of psychological hallucinations.

"With his spirit ever full of placid curiosity, immutably serene—as he himself said of some Mexican educator—but constantly urged by the eagerness to know any social phenomenon in any of its manifestations, even the most microscopic," Justo Sierra consecrated himself to the analysis of our political evolution, and it may be affirmed without scruple that his effort managed to express, in the chapters owed to him of the book entitled México, su evolución social, and in Juárez, su obra y su tiempo, the clearest and most complete synthesis we possess, up to now, of the epoch of the Reform and of the restoration of the Republic; the most real and definitive enunciation of that dramatic moment in which there began upon our soil the decisive conquest of liberty; that is to say, the full securing of the fatherland; which constitutes one of the essential episodes of the constitutional history of America. These two books will surely suffice to maintain his name as a great historian in posterity.

I said before that Justo Sierra was a Platonist because he was a lover; he was one also by his irony, victorious over error—or at least the enemy of error, of the multiple human errors—like the Socratic irony; but not corrosive of the ideal, calculating and hedonistic, like that of the solitary master of the Parerga and Paralipomena; never a counselor of bitter egoism. Plato was ironic, was skeptical, the intellectual father of ironists and skeptics. The great interpreter of Platonism, Walter Pater, with no intention of stitching together paradoxes but with the purpose of verifying the truly unfathomable influence of Plato upon thinking Humanity, says that the divine Athenian philosopher, compendium of all the intellectual and aesthetic perfections of the Greek genius, has had a double historical posterity: that of the great idealist dogmatists, from Plotinus to Hegel, which everyone recognizes in him and has always recognized, and that of the skeptics, in which there shines with luminous glory that sovereign rieur [laugher] and most human skeptic, as great in the history of letters as in that of philosophical thought, the French man of the Renaissance Montaigne, one of the greatest heroes of the great historical epoch of modern times. Montaigne was a Platonist in his temperamental skepticism, undulant and diverse, and his book of Essays, consubstantial with its author—that is to say, capricious and unabashed—is full of healthy and strong irony and of invincible skepticism.

And Ferdinand Brunetière affirms: "all the qualities of the divine Plato could not make us forget that the incomparable writer, the happy rival and conqueror of the Gorgiases and the Protagorases, did not for that cease to be the king of the sophists; and it is a problem to ascertain whether his sophistry was engendered by the charms of his style, or whether, inversely, the graces of his style were engendered by the subtlety of his sophistry; but what is beyond doubt is the intimacy of that style with the sophistry."

So also in our loving and ironic Mexican master, so also in Justo Sierra, skepticism is undulant and diverse, and his books remain sealed with the seal of his literary personality, like all books of History that are not a simple assemblage of data or a mere exercise in polemic; like all books that are not reduced to simple chapters of erudition strung one to another more or less congruously and patiently; consubstantial books, as Montaigne said of his own.

But even more intrinsic and consubstantial are, if it may be so affirmed, among all the literary productions of Justo Sierra, some of the numerous lessons of his long and complex magistracy, and, above them all perhaps, the memorable oration he pronounced in honor of doctor Barreda, on the twenty-second day of March, nineteen hundred and eight, before an immense public of young and enthusiastic disciples.

For the Mexican positivist, "the truths that science has demonstrated, in which all are agreed, embrace the whole of knowledge, from mathematics, in which the laws are axioms, to the laws that govern the immense complexity of social phenomena, which science will one day reduce to axioms as well: they furnish a sure, indisputable basis to all mental activity; there there is no longer any battle, there there is nothing but knowing; all is light: there the spirit, sole god of the relative, has set its tabernacle, as the God of the Bible erected His upon the glittering sphere of the sun."

Sierra said, in the face of such categorical affirmations of narrow dogmatic positivism, what all the great philosophers occupied in thinking science say today: "Let us doubt; let us agree with the great believer whose teachings, whose acts rather, were called to reach into the whole future of the Mexican intellect, that what is rigorously logical would be that fraternity beneath the tent of science in which all fit as beneath the tent of Isaiah. The religion of the indisputable forms a religion of truth that can have no heretics; science, that is the indisputable!.... Let us doubt; in the first place, because if science is nothing more than the systematic knowledge of the relative, if objects in themselves cannot be known, if we can know only their constant relations, if this is true science, how should it not be in perpetual evolution, in perpetual discussion, in perpetual struggle? What great fundamental truth has not been disputed, or is not being disputed at this moment?....

And after verifying, in all the orders of knowledge—from mathematics, unshakable as was believed since the times of Pythagoras and Philolaus, to contemporary biology—the constant movement, the incessant palpitation of scientific life which, like every form of life, is a process of adaptation to new and variable environing conditions, the internal struggle of hypotheses, the tireless logical duel of ideas, ever more vigorous, ampler and more liberating, he exclaimed, in a transport of supreme philosophical eloquence, extending his Socratic doubt to metaphysics and religion: "Does not this species of earthquake beneath the great scientific theories suffice to make it understood that the banner of science is not an ensign of peace? And it is not, no. Upon the sciences have been erected those immense edifices of ideas which, in attempting to explain the universe and the destiny of man, have taken on in the philosophical systems every aspect, and have served as fortress and shelter to all the passions; because the fate of ideas is and always will be that, on becoming sentiments—the sole means of stirring the soul of peoples—they are humanized, so to speak, and take on the color of all temperaments, and are kindled with the warmth of all hearts, and float like banners in the struggles and are called spiritualism, materialism, and today agnosticism and tomorrow pragmatism. And it is that in their metaphysical forms those great syntheses, completely hypothetical and probably chimerical (because in the hands of experimentation spirit has become complex and matter has vanished into forces), border upon religion and are in reality its soul; and religions kill their dissidents morally when they cannot do so materially; they kill the word with the anathema; they kill resistance with excommunication; they kill heresy with the stake; they are stupendous living organisms which, like all that lives, need the perennial refection of death."

What can be saved from this radical, incoercible doubt, which feels a constant earthquake beneath the great scientific theories and looks upon religions as stupendous living organisms bordering upon metaphysics, and upon that very mental synthesis as something completely hypothetical and probably chimerical? Upon what seat, that is not friable, are we to found our life and build our ideal, if neither the sciences, nor philosophy, nor the religions furnish it to us?

Only yesterday, at the end of his long career, when his intelligence had already suffered all the disillusionments and his heart could consecrate itself, without measure, to the fascination that the superstitions of childhood exercise upon the old: on going "as a tourist and not as a believer to savor the wine of hope in that great vessel of devotion called Lourdes," the lover, the skeptic, the ironic spiritual descendant of Plato and of Montaigne, "son of his time and of his century, but son above all of his mother, who suckled and reared him in the belief in the supernatural as in the most natural thing in the world," on finding himself in the atmosphere of the Catholic religion, within the very grotto of the miracle, instead of being born again and converting to the faith of his fathers, combines his aesthetic enthusiasm with his historical sense and forges a Platonic myth, like those which the religious legends of his race inspired in the immortal disciple of Socrates; and "the Basilica, upon its double pedestal of churches, standing out with a most white precision from the dark and sinister mass of the Pyrenees," seems to him "a prayer, an orison of Bernadette, the shepherdess herself turned into a temple and ascending toward the Immaculate One in an ecstatic surge."

The illustrious master would have said, perhaps, like Renán, to whoever had dared to inquire from his own lips the depths of his intimate convictions: "Who could determine whether or not the strength of intelligence consists in abstaining from concluding? This is neither indifference nor skepticism, mark you; it is criticism: one is not a historian save on condition of reproducing in oneself the different types of life of the past in order to comprehend their originality and to find them, at the same time, legitimate and defective, beautiful and ugly, worthy of love and of hatred."

"To reproduce in oneself the different types of life of the past," as Renán thinks, such was the moral exercise that Justo Sierra chose, obeying his peculiar aptitude, in order to speak his inner message, the good news of his spirit, to his disciples and his fellow citizens. He possessed, along with his innate idealism, that cult of tradition, "universal society of intelligences and hearts," that sense of the past, so scarce and so admirable in these Latin-American peoples, more or less conscious victims of systematic utopias and impossible redemptions; he knew how to harbor the clearest awareness that not in vain was the social and political formula that predetermines our destinies elaborated by our fathers, and he managed to define it in his work and, what is worth still more, to make us love it as grateful and devoted sons.

Here is how he declares the vocation of his spirit for History: "The unfolding of a primitive soul that has a character for its nucleus, that receives color from events and tends to react upon them, and with them grows complex and transforms itself, in its turn, into an event determining series of happenings whose vibration propagates itself indefinitely in time, is a supreme spectacle; I do not know whether there is another equal to it for the spirit; it is equivalent to the creation of a world, to the discovery of a fundamental truth. It is more interesting because it contains more drama, because it impassions more, because it intensifies life more."

"For if this drama takes on the proportions of a historical revolution, if it comes to serve as a measure of the quantity of influence that the history of a human group can have upon Humanity, then it becomes for the contemplator something surprising and unique."

History is more interesting than scientific discovery, "because it contains more drama, because it impassions more, because it intensifies life more"; that is to say, because even though the whole world, living and inert, is struggle, contention, eternal conflict, drama, the tragedy that we men live is the most dramatic of all; it is a supreme spectacle that can be contemplated only by overcoming the pain that the struggles of the centuries cause in the consciousness we have of the species, by virtue of the ample longing for reconstruction, for resurrection, which is equivalent to the pure ideal contemplation of the philosopher and to the creation of a world. The lover is a being who lives on the pathetic, on the essentially dramatic that there is in the universe; for this reason History and Legend are his propitious atmosphere, his natural concomitant medium; for this reason Justo Sierra, if the definition given of his essential qualities be true, consecrated himself to writing it with his whole soul vibrant with generosity and with science, making frequent excursions through the vast orbit of human civilization, teaching Universal History to the young; but, above all, reviving in his books the past of the Mexican fatherland, a fatherland afflicted as none other, born in the warmth of legends grand as epics and afterward mingled and confounded with Spain, the most impassioning, most dramatic nation of modern times.

And his vocation for History inevitably redeems the skeptic, the ironist who would assiduously doubt religions and philosophies: "ideal Antigones, tragic images that lead Oedipus, who sees through the eyes of his daughter the only thing worth seeing in this world, that which does not end, that which is eternal." He no longer doubts, he never really doubted, his patriotic intuition, his historical faith: he has his categorical imperative sui generis [of its own kind], like Kant, that critic, that skeptic who is at the same time the patriarch of all the moral dogmatists; he confesses the "harsh religion of duty," he communes, transfixed with fervor, with the grave destiny of his race: he knows how, if anyone should offend or deny them, to "wipe away the blackness of the smoke," as Horace said, from the great heroic representations of his people; he maintains in the souls of the young the prestige of the national sanctuary, he himself installs it in their fervent and longing hearts; and master of his moral conscience, without a scruple, without a discouragement, he fulfills the power he received in his soul, he becomes dogmatic. The depth of his conscience prevails over his undulant and diverse thought, and just as others create a morality and found a religion upon the rubble of all the metaphysical systems, taking mystical refuge in faith to save themselves from their own intelligence and to save it, he writes the history of his people and teaches it to children and to men, supporting it upon the nobility of spirit of his soul, nourishing it with his activity and tinting it, at times, with the "impalpable gold dust" of his incomparable irony.

"All mortal things," says Diotima of Mantinea, wisely invoked by Socrates in the most illustrious page of the sublime Platonic Symposium, "tend with all their power toward immortality, which can be acquired only by means of the generation that puts the young in the place of the old." Love will be, then, the guiding thread of History, the fundamental manner of resolving the successive secret of the ages. And the vicissitudes of individuals and of institutions, the fatherland itself, will be, as the philosopher teaches, co-essential tendencies of human things toward immortality.

In order for the historian to know what ultimate end the secular movement of the generations carries implicit within it, he must feel in his individual soul the species of collective immortality to which the soul of a thousand souls of his people mysteriously and gradually aspires; he must love it always as Justo Sierra loved the things of his nation, the differential attributes of his race; with that sentiment at once copious and synthetic which made him exclaim, before the critics and the systematic deniers: "Neither idolaters nor iconoclasts. Free men; but men of gratitude, men of fatherland."

The psychological development of the master of Mexican youth is the clearest example of the firmest and noblest intellectual constancy; it constitutes a profound lesson in culture and in mental sincerity, perhaps the highest of any that have been produced among us. Justo Sierra lived in a perennial ascending evolution, in a tireless progressive effort, from the time when in his youth he wrote his verses and his romantic tales, until, in the fullness of his longing, he founded—in order to free teaching from the clumsy interference of public power, and in spite of "the sophists of all tunics" and of all chapels—the greatest monument our nation raised on completing its first century of independent life, the National University: "a group of students of all ages summed up into one, the age of full intellectual aptitude, forming a real personality by dint of solidarity and of consciousness of its mission, and which, resorting to every source of culture, spring it from where it might, provided the water be pure, should propose to itself to acquire the means of nationalizing science, of Mexicanizing knowledge."

He was a true son of his epoch (so restless and revolutionary in the order of ideas and philosophical beliefs), notwithstanding the anathemas—which served him as comfort and encouragement—of those who, undeviating, that is to say absurd, disciples of decrepit and ephemeral systems, have known only how to substitute one fanaticism for another and one idol for another idol, in these most free days we have reached, when the prophetic lips of Zarathustra announce, with religious stupefaction, the twilight of all the idols. He was, like his century, not the follower of a dogma, but the pupil of his own disinterested dialectic, and in this too he was Platonic; he also, like the philosopher of Athens, had the courage to renew his doctrine when he found its foundations insufficient and its conclusions incoherent. For this reason now, when instead of his teaching accompanying us only his memory is with us, on feeling that we shall still have a long road to travel, we declare that we take inspiration from his example and accept without hesitation our fortune, thinking that we shall have to modify and perfect ourselves endlessly in the misty perspective of existence, bearing love as an imperative in order to attain the good and the truth, if they are to be attained, and irony as a shield in order to conquer evil, if evil and error combat us, as must inevitably happen, since, as Lucretius says, that great poet of the garden of Epicurus, "the gods, by virtue of the intimate privilege of their nature, enjoy a profound peace, their immortality, far from the sphere of our happenings, distant from our world, safe from pain and from danger, sufficing unto themselves, insensible inaccessible to anger." our virtues, THE MORAL PHILOSOPHY OF HOSTOS

"Conscientia propugnans pro virtute." ["Conscience fighting for virtue."]

In our American republics there appear from time to time in the course of History, despite the turbulence of the milieu and notwithstanding the intestine anarchy that so often lowers the dignity of nations, men who, reproducing what they received by secular inheritance, know how to preserve the purity of their spirit and heroically to reject the compromises exacted and won from the weak by life against morality, by reality against the incorruptible ideal. Of these men was the distinguished educator and moralist Don Eugenio María de Hostos, one of the highest and strongest symbolic representations of our Hispano-American race.

In these times of moral skepticism and exalted, truly anarchic individualism; when the most constant and patent fact in the philosophical speculations that reach us from Europe is the absence of faith in the rational progress of men; when the anti-intellectualist theories of a Nietzsche and a Stirner produce formidable ravages in clumsily restless spirits, in souls sick with the cult of vague and unformulable ideals; when ever more distant sounds the religious accent of the great systematic believers and all seems to dissolve in the radical imprecision of ideas, in the volubility of sentiments, in the desperate hypertrophy of characters, it may perhaps be opportune and consoling to recall the doctrine of the great moralist who knew how "to make his life equal to his thought"—according to the celebrated aphorism—thus converting the superiority of his spirit into a living and organic force, a stimulant of the slow perfecting of the American civilizations.

In going through the admirable chapters of Moral social, striving to follow in all its derivations the inflexibly systematic thought of the Antillean thinker; in traversing the most eloquent paragraphs that define and proclaim a harmonious ideal of justice and of benevolence, adducing for it all the arguments of the most laborious dialectic, all the inspirations of the most sincere and firmest belief, as well as the innumerable iniquities that indelibly stain the history of humanity; in receiving the true impression of finding myself in the presence of the regenerator of peoples and of spirits, repeating his teachings, I have thought that perhaps Ethics is to be founded only upon the ingenuous and disinterested faith in duty; upon the clearest intuition of the good, resplendent in the immaculate peace of the conscience of the just.

For Hostos the moral law is a natural order, as he himself says, and Ethics is constituted by the belief in the eternal harmony that binds man to nature and society to man; a harmony that reason discovers, in developing according to its own dynamic, in its endeavor to assimilate the manifestations of mutual dependence that exist between each of those relative terms, and which the moral conscience in its turn heeds and confesses, because it too is harmony between the interior and the exterior, between life and the environment of life, between society and the conditions of society, between being and the determinations of being, between the good and the attributes of the good itself.

"Even if the spontaneous logic of things did not establish a relation of means to end between each inhabitant of the world and that world," says Hostos, "the beneficent influence of the harmony of all things among themselves would suffice for the Ideal of good, the secret aspiration of great souls, to arise in the soul of all beings as a natural product of the environment."

The universe that Hostos presents as the archetype of the moral order (and which, within his radically optimistic soul, is nothing but a reflection of the real world in terms of the symbolic and aesthetic constructions of the intelligence), is governed, as by its essential law, by "irrepressible and irresistible progress." This fatally good universe is what engenders the basis of Ethics in the human conscience.

Every great rationalist is inclined, by an innate propensity of his spirit, to take the exceptional perfection of his congruous and synthetic mentality as a full copy of the world, which, unfortunately, is far from embodying, in its full and adequate expression, that hierarchization, that aesthetic subordination of the singular to the general, of the less general to the more general, and of the more general to the necessary universal: a subordination that constitutes the proper essence and the ineluctable law of the organic development of reason.

Hostos thinks that such a magnificent and prosperous world is capable of suggesting, by simple contemplation, the evidence of duty and the necessary triumph of justice.

The fundamental belief of the optimists who base the good upon rational beauty and upon the rigor of the concomitant syllogistic demonstrations, the comforting spectacle offered by apostolic souls seduced by their faith in the progressive moralization of men, the order which must finally submit, according to those believers, rebellious Humanity, in order to realize with it and through it the Good, are diverse expressions, according to the philosopher, of this essential truth: the necessary penetration of the rhythm of the world into the societies and the individuals of our species, the transcendental continuation of natural laws in the human conscience. The Morality of Hostos is built upon the affirmation of the perennial order that reason discovers in the intimate relations of things.

Mental progress has consisted in the elimination or attenuation of momentary and arbitrary perturbations and asymmetries; in the discovery of the invariable law; in the verification of the radical uniformity extensive to all phenomenal orders and to all spheres of knowledge.

The world is composed and combined by the modulus that the syntheses of analytical propositions of the geometers exhibit; everything exists subordinated to the rest by virtue of an inflexible deduction, and the universe reveals itself as a single infinite and perennial being:

"In the ocean of life and in the tempest of action (the spirit says to Faust), I rise and fall, I come and go! Birth and tomb! Eternal sea, shifting web, vigorous life which in the murmuring loom of time weave the seamless and animate vesture of God!"

And since human actions are modalities of universal existence or, as Taine says, prolonged resonances of the "eternal axiom"; since we ourselves are organic dependencies of the environment in which we live; since reason is nature itself changing its inertia into consciousness and illuminating itself in the spirit, reflecting itself in the transparency of its most clear mirror; the reflection of the eternal harmony in the human intelligence is an irrecusable guide of the Good. In defining and discovering truth, science discovers and defines, at one and the same time, duty and right. "Morality is founded upon nothing but natural realities, and it neither imposes itself upon nor governs conscience except insofar as its precepts are founded upon natural relations. We are bound by our bodily organism to the nature of which it is a part, and from that natural bond between whole and part are derived the relations of natural morality. What we call moral or human nature relates us in a more immaterial way to our intellective, volitional and affective organisms, and upon all the relations of that order individual morality is founded. And from a series of relations with social nature is born the branch of morality whose object is to make patent and to make lovable the duties that render the social good effective."

"Those relations of individual man with social man, of each individual with the group and of the group with society, are as manifest as those that bind each bodily organism to physical nature, and each conscience to the moral nature on which it depends."

"To be by nature related to society and to humanity suffices for man to be moral. Since that is a reality patent in the history of the ages, it suffices for science. Not only does it suffice, but it limits. Moral science, to be science, cannot go beyond that: its limits lie precisely within the relations that link the organic, psychic and social life of man with the cosmic, moral and social order. Therefore, if general morality is founded upon the relations of man with general nature, social morality is founded upon the particular relations of man with society."

"Every other conception of the foundation of morality seems to us an artifice unworthy of the degree of development to which human reason and conscience have attained. Neither the one nor the other requires, for the practice of duty and for the reflective search for the good, any other stimulus than the exalted dignity of the good and of duty. And if they should require others, it will be proof that they have not attained in their development that degree in which all morality is a consequence of the knowledge of our positive relations with nature, with our own being and with the social being."<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Hostos. Moral social; pp. 31 and 32.

Here, finally, is that decisive paragraph in which he presents science as supreme moralizer and causal origin of the "idea-forces" that the development of the Good imposes:

"Since the reflective knowledge of a law leads the one who acquires it, which is conscience, to submit to the precepts of the law, and duty is nothing but the submission of conscience to the

laws and principles, precepts and rules, mandates and ordinances of nature in any of its manifestations and in whatsoever ends and purposes of life, duty is almost a spontaneous deduction from each and every relation that binds us to the external world, to the internal world and to the social world."<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Moral Social; p. 23.

For Hostos, as for Spinoza, "truth is substantial to intelligence, being substantial to truth, and the good substantial to being." In the immense multiplicity of relations and modalities of existence, rationality attains with full perfection the essential end of all beings.

The intellectual love of God, the beatitude of disinterested meditation, the systematic abandonment of perishable enjoyments for imperishable happiness, participation in divinity, concomitant with the harmonious development of intelligence: that is the moral law.

!

The perfect life is the life founded upon reason, which logically keeps itself apart from remorse and does not fear death; it is a free and happy plenitude which, dominating bodily appetites and passions, rests upon the absolute power that knowledge affords.

The proper foundation of human nature is rational nature, supreme dispenser of unalterable peace amid the vicissitudes of existence.

For this reason Hostos declares that the basis of education must be the cultivation of intelligence; for this reason he says eloquently in that memorable address delivered in 1884 at the Normal School of Santo Domingo, when his first American disciples were graduated as teachers:

"What teaching was necessary to bring about the salutary revolution in this society already weary of murderous revolutions? True teaching: that which disregards historical purposes, partial methods, artificial procedures, and, attending exclusively to the subject of knowledge, which is human reason, and to the object of knowledge, which is nature, favors the union of the two and rests in the confidence that that happy union will bear truth as its fruit."

"Give me truth, and I give you the world. You, without truth, will shatter the world; and I, with truth, with truth alone, will rebuild the world as many times as you may have shattered it. And I shall give you not only the world of material organizations; I shall give you the organic world, together with the world of ideas, together with the world of affections, together with the world of labor, together with the world of liberty, together with the world of progress, together—to fire off the whole thought—with the world that reason fabricates enduringly above the natural world."

"And what should I be, wretched workman of nothingness, to have that virtue of the whole? What you all might be, what all men can be, what I have wished the generations that begin to rise to be, what, with all the devotion, with all the unction of a conscience that carries within it the foresight of a new moral and intellectual world, I would wish all beings of reason to be: a subject of knowledge fecundated by nature, eternal object of knowledge."

Such is the admirable, the sublime profession of faith of the great American rationalist who, as pedagogue and as apostle, lived seduced by the aesthetic proportions of his intellectualist synthesis, believing with all firmness, in the most intimate and hidden depths of his noble being, that it sufficed to open to intelligences darkened by prejudice or stupidity the fecund field of scientific speculations, placing the self and the world in intimate contact and tearing out by the root every intermediary obstacle, traditional or not, so that, on truth imposing itself in each conscience by the divine genesiac fiat of reason, there might spring forth, at the same time, as the most luxuriant and splendid fruit of scientific labor, the recognition of the good and the irresistible moralization of men.

The whole of the address mentioned, which is perhaps the masterpiece of independent moral thought in Spanish America—but especially the paragraphs in which Hostos reveals his profession of faith—may be compared with the finest classical passages in which the great thinkers of all ages have attained perfect literary expression, as a natural consequence of the logical perfection of the ideas.

Yet the universal harmony felt and confessed by Hostos when he declares that duty is "a spontaneous deduction from each and every relation that binds us to the external world, to the internal world and to the social world"; the constant and mobile accord, the permanently realized equilibrium, the total symphony in which the innumerable leitmotifs of existence mysteriously agree, is somewhat vitiated by the only perturbing element in the history of Humanity and in that of each concrete individual spirit, by the satanic power essentially rebellious against the cosmic autocracy of reason: the will.

"Perhaps, in instituting a supreme personification of evil facing the supreme personification of good, the positive religions have wished nothing else than to consecrate, as disorders of nature and of the spirit, of society and of universal law, the omnipotence of a will predominant toward evil. In this interior government of each man, which we call the soul, there are phenomena identical with the government of societies. In the latter, the predominance of the executive power infallibly determines despotism; despotism is a disorder of the laws of society. The will is the executive of the spirit: subordinated to reason and to sentiment, it produces good; unbound from sentiment and from reason, it produces evil."

"A great activity of passions, goaded by a will—that is crime. Some commit it upon themselves: they are suicides. Others commit it upon their brother, their kinsman, their friend, and they have a hundred names in the penal codes. Others commit it upon a people, and they are tyrants, despots and autocrats. Others commit it upon humanity, and they are called conquerors, heroes, demigods....

They are mere combinations of the same will, with different circumstances."<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Hamlet, p. 20.

The enemy of the necessary ordering of the moral world, the breaking of the cosmic rhythm, the contradictory dissonance of the eternal symphony, is, as you well see, the will, "an essentially perverse faculty." (Hamlet, p. 20)

The wicked, like Shakespeare's Claudius, in whom the will predominates over reason, are individual obstacles set against the final triumph of the idea.

But not only do individuals manifest by their perversion the irrationality of their conscience; the societies constituted by them also present, in the course of historical evolution, aspects irreducible to the principle of morality:

"Neither individual man nor the collective man of our time cherishes that ideal. There are those who hold it, of course, and those, in order to be at the height of their ideal, either live as its martyrs or are its victims. But those martyrs or victims of the human ideal, of the ideal destiny of the human being, of the true, of the supreme ideal—the one that consists in realizing or sustaining all the ends of being, harmonizing them—have been able to live and have existed in inferior

civilizations, and those who exist within the bosom of contemporary civilization, though more than their precursors, are very few. The rest, far from mortifying themselves in the eagerness for the ideal, accommodate themselves to the abnormal civilization which by their own abnormality they help to make more irregular and more incomplete, the more immorally they bequeath to the coming generations the task of improving, completing, harmonizing and moralizing the civilization to which they contribute." (Moral Social, p. 12.)

Hostos feels contingent reality as something that is set against his necessary ideal, but which, precisely for that reason, will have to be annihilated, the day coming when the very thought of evil will be erased from the intelligence; when no perturbation will any longer prevent men from gravitating toward the Good with all the powers of the soul, with all their intellectual faculties, because, thanks be to God, as Fichte says, "evil is not done for evil's own sake, but for the good that is expected from it."....

Like Zarathustra, Hostos believes in the mystical victory of the armies of Ahura-Mazda over the impious rabble of Angra Mainyu, and firmly hopes that today's optimistic ideal may be tomorrow's insuperable reality.

At last the harmonious equilibrium will be reestablished, and reason, extending its empire over the will, will manage to dissolve and reduce the negative elements by the conscious and most free affirmation of the good. And in unfathomable eternity, the symphony of the universe will never have ceased to vibrate!....

The logical basis of the morality of Hostos is the concept of universal eurhythmy constructed upon the notion of natural law. For Hostos, as for Montesquieu, every law is "the necessary expression of the relations of things"; and the moral law, the expression, necessary as well, of the relations of physical nature with the social and moral world. For this reason, as I have told you previously, the philosopher judges that the universal rhythm of the world prolongs itself into the inmost depths of the human soul; and civilization and moralization appear to him as superior aspects or results of progressive rationalization and conscification, as he himself says, making use of a vigorous and happy neologism.

In granting an absolute metaphysical value to the uniformities of coexistence and sequence that determine scientific knowledge, essentially relative; in sustaining, with Spinoza, Hegel and Taine, the logical concatenation between the attributes and modes of being, Hostos lays down, as an ineluctable consequence of his synthetic concept of natural laws, this fundamental postulate, which is wholly lacking in demonstration within his system: the essence of the world is rational, that is to say, adequate to the intellectual constitution of the human mind.

It is true that, obeying the concomitant inspirations of contemporary philosophical currents, experientialist and positivist, the moralist denies all ethics founded upon metaphysical postulates; but the principle he admits, according to which existence can fit and does in fact fit within fully logical enunciations, the idea of an eternal harmony between society and the physical environment in which it develops, the concept that erects duty into a "spontaneous deduction from each and every relation that binds us to the external world, to the internal world and to the social world," are, without any doubt, determinist and intellectualist metaphysical postulates, like those that served the illustrious systematizer of pantheism in the seventeenth century to knit together the diverse logical members of his Ethica more geometrico demonstrata [Ethics demonstrated in geometrical order].

The necessary spontaneity of duty as an adaptation of the moral nature of man to the harmonious and logical nature of the universe—a true deus ex machina [god from the machine] of Hostosism—shines forth in all its practical and speculative developments, and upon all of them it impresses a constant character of subordination of particular and personal elements to universal formulas, and of absolute dependence of the individual with respect to society.

Hence he conceives social groupings after the type of biological organisms and admits in all its consequences the notion, classic in sociology, of the reality of the social being. Hostosian philosophy and morality are exemplary of the most systematic and coherent rationalism.

Now then, present-day criticism, occupied in "thinking science," in order to unite the apriorisms of philosophy with the no less radical or essential apriorisms of the scientific disciplines, has accomplished a definitive, minute and transcendental labor with respect to the determinist conception of natural laws, which engenders as a corollary in the conscience the affirmation of the full intelligibility of the universe, the beautiful and dazzling pantheist conception of absolute harmonism.

Auguste Comte was the first philosopher of experientialist tradition who, contradicting the inheritance received from naturalism, affirmed the irreducibility of each order of natural laws to the next higher or lower order.

The great contemporary philosopher M. Émile Boutroux, in his treatises on The idea of natural law and the contingency of the laws of nature, has completed the labor brilliantly initiated by Comte, demonstrating that "logic, at least the real logic that comprises the theory of the concept, of judgment and of reasoning, supposes data irreducible to the rigorously analytical relation which is the sole title to perfect intelligibility." The uniformities of nature are methods found by man to adapt things to his intelligence, but they reveal to us, and can never reveal to us, anything but that the character of necessity attributed by determinism to the world resides in the essence of human reason, nature in itself being infinitely more complex and varied than logical pantheism supposed, infinitely more fecund and inexhaustible than it appears in the cosmic symphony engendered by the noble optimistic illusion in the conscience of Hostos.

No, the universe is not the monstrous geometric being that develops in the peace of its ineffable essence, infinitely unfolding its modes and its infinite attributes. No, life cannot be reduced to the logical proportions of an analysis which, at the moment of approaching it, destroys it with its apparent exactitude, when it believes it is reducing it, and denies it when it thinks it is comprehending it. No, the human soul is more than reason; it is what the history of the species exhibits in the symbolic forms of heroism and of love. The will is not a satanic faculty, essentially negative and perverse, as Hostos would have it, but a force victorious or vanquished, yet in extraordinary activity, which adapts itself to the good and realizes it, above the vicissitudes inherent in existence, thus founding the prepotent mainspring of the evolution of peoples and of individuals.

From metaphysical liberty, "immediate datum of consciousness," unanimous confession of the common sense of Humanity which no determinism will ever be able to destroy; from the faculties capable of harmonizing with the imperative prescriptions of reason in concordances more heterogeneous than those that pantheist monism feigns—thence proceed the moral necessities, collective and personal aspirations that constantly stir, seeking to be, in the depths of consciousness, only to appear later as a synthesis of life and of the ideal, arising with incalculable beauty in the actions of men, in the relations of peoples, in the dreams of utopians, in the vindications of the oppressed, in the apostolate of the saints, in the anticipatory creations of poets and of seers.

A world that fastens itself, as by its root, to the world that is, and flowers like an immense tree beneath whose solemn boughs the astonished eyes of men contemplate the plenitude of heaven!....

The optimism of Hostos encounters in its development, as an insuperable obstacle, the very notion of the will which, in subordinating itself to the physical order, does nothing but follow the suggestions of the flesh, as the theologians say, of the biological organism with which coexists the intelligent principle, the free basis of true morality.

If we were to govern our actions only by the reflection of the fatal laws of the inert world in our own consciousness, we should be completely immoral, or rather amoral; good and evil would lack all significance for us, and the law of absolute hedonism would logically be the ethical norm of our spiritual life, just as it is the superior law of our organic activity.

When, despite his most deeply rooted belief in the geometric harmony of the world, and by virtue of a manifest inconsistency with himself, Hostos confesses the "eternity of efforts that the simple purpose of making rational the only inhabitant of the earth endowed with reason has cost," we cannot help thinking that the cosmic order is impotent to engender in the consciousness of men and in the life of nations, not merely the moral law in all its imperative amplitude, but the smallest particle of good; and hence the real origin of morality is not physical and biological determinism, but the ideal and synthetic construction carried out by reason and based upon free will as the metaphysical element of its effective attainment.

One must not be seduced by those who think to build morality upon scientific bases, however venerable and conscientious their purposes may be: science can offer us only relative results, never necessary norms of action; and only by virtue of necessary principles can beings of reason such as men be obliged.

It is to misunderstand the proper essence of scientific speculation to ask of it canons for the elaboration of moral theories. Hostos fails to recognize the contingent value of cosmic laws: for this reason he built upon friable foundations his organic system of social morality, for this reason he fell into the contradictions I have tried to draw out in impartially analyzing the theories he fostered. His "preference granted to thinking over feeling and willing" led him to simplify the real picture of existence and to prevent the true harmony of the universe from being conceived in all its integrity by his luminous apostolic spirit.

But there is something of his labor that in a certain way endures and that, resisting the outrages of oblivion, will hearten and accompany us in our indefinite pilgrimage; there is something more fundamental than his perishable books and his philosophical beliefs; something still more immaterial and exalted: the consciousness he harbored of the adequate fulfillment of his mission, the example of his existence consecrated to the "harsh religion of duty," the mysterious energy of his soul which consciously opposed itself to the succession of external events and forced them to modify themselves, in exercising upon them the power of his energy, of his inner virtue, the more invincible in that it was inviolable and divine in its origin and essence. What remains to us of him, here it is expressed in these words that one hears spoken with unction in the serenity of intimate recollection, like an easy detachment and mystical ascension of the incorruptible soul toward the infinite:

"Only he is worthy of having done good, or of having contributed to a good, who has divested himself of himself to the point of having no consciousness of his personality save in the exact proportion in which it functions as the representative of a benefit desired or realized."

"He who in that impersonal manner has set himself to the work of the good, from no one, absolutely no one, has been able to receive evil. What worm, what viper, what slander, what calumny, what Judas, what Iago could have reached him? Is he a worm? Is he an excrescence clothed in human form?"

"No, gentlemen: he is the highest and the saddest thing in creation. He is the desert rock that sovereign efforts have most slowly heaved up above the sea of tribulations, and which suffers without breaking the foam of rage, the assault of fury, the desperate horror of the mortal waves that beset it. He is conscience, sad as the rock, but high as the desert rock of the ocean."

## ESTUDIOS INDOSTANICOS — HINDUSTANI STUDIES

By José Vasconcelos.

### I.

The book with which José Vasconcelos has enriched our poor and scanty philosophical production is one of the most notable of all those consecrated in Mexico to metaphysical and religious erudition. Let the distinguished thinker receive, in these few lines, the cordial congratulations of his old and constant friend, who like him believes in the ideal significance of life; who like him is a student of the arcane "beyond," which forms the favored object of meditation and the sole recompense of inveterate philosophical doubts. Taine's is the heroic and consoling phrase: "the absolute cannot fail to be found." The work of Vasconcelos is a sincere effort to find that which we cannot help seeking without rest, because we sense that it is rooted everywhere, and is all things—blind force or empty extension as much as divine and hermetic personality and consciousness.

And the author deceives himself. His modesty deceives him. His book is a complete book. That is to say, a coherent body of doctrine; an organism and not a repertory; a synthesis and not a florilegium. Clearly, as an exposition of secular ideas, it is not to be original with that absurd inventiveness which ignorant simplicity demands of sincere thinkers. Who is original in Philosophy, save the Vedas in India and the pre-Socratics in Greece.... Plato proves inconceivable without Pythagoras; Aristotle without Plato; Plotinus without Plato and Aristotle; the Fathers of the Church without Plotinus; Saint Thomas without the Patristics, and Descartes without Saint Augustine and Saint Anselm, as Leibnitz and Spinoza without Descartes. Let us leave to the illiterates of thought their guileless and childish madness of the "original idea," and let us rest our free and respectful mind upon the inspired phalanx of the centuries. To think is a collective work; a work of veneration, erudition and personality. New ideas are daughters of old ideas and adversaries only of the thoughts defeated in the dialectic of history. Let a distinction always be drawn between a fecund OLD IDEA and an IDEA GROWN OLD, a useless piece of lumber which, like those that accumulate in dwellings passing from fathers to sons and grandsons, hinders the easy removal of their owners and is relegated to abandoned cellars where it grows rusty or is eaten by moths.

The principal praise that the labor of Vasconcelos deserves is this, to my mind. He knew how to order the most fertile Oriental thought. To order, that is to say, to unify. India is everything at once: dazzling invention, intellectual passion, mystical and ascetic spirit, great and human poetry, subtle theology, inspired ecstasy, generous and sublime morality. Yes; but she lacks the eminent quality that has given European thought its constant superiority. India is neither dialectic nor science nor a congruous and exact system. It is neither the geometry of Euclid nor the Aristotelian demonstration. The goddess of the gray-green pupils before whom Renán prayed upon the ruins of the Acropolis, liberator from all barbarism, enemy of all obscurity, would have to strike with the bronze point of her lance the motley and sibylline swarm of Vedas, Puranas and Upanishads, in order to convert their eternal teachings into the common good of men. And our Mexican philosopher, in employing long years of activity in the ordering of archaic texts and doctrines, genuinely represents his Western culture; he is a son of Minerva in setting a clean frame of European logic about the disconcerting multitude of the Hindustani writers.

Thus he formed and drafted the Manual which, from today on, will surely not be missing from the shelves of students of philosophy. But he represents his role of "Western critic" not only by his constant eagerness for definition and ordering; he represents it, above all, because he brought to the meditation of the religious problems of Orientalism his Christian spirit. Vasconcelos does not for a moment lose sight of the enormous, the unquestionable moral superiority of the Gospel. Buddha is only a precursor, a remote and exotic John the Baptist; and Jesus is the center of history. Every baptism other than his is a "baptism of penitence," as the Scripture says. Buddhist rationalism lacked the divine passion of giving; the Christian charity that never calculates. Socrates calculated. Sakya Muni calculated. Jesus gave without reserve; as the sole victim that he was of the divine inspiration that guided him. For this reason, while the East drowses with the henbane of its enormous lethargic scriptures, the West, as Höffding says, still stirs and is animated with the "Excelsior" of the Cross.

Let this moving page of "Hindustani Studies" be read; one would call it the most authentic commentary upon the beautiful phrase of Maurice Barrès: "il y a des lieux où souffle l'esprit"..... ["there are places where the spirit breathes"]—a religious page, but not for the faithful of a church (however illustrious it might be conceived) but for the religious of the world, that is to say, for the sublime religion that we men have been elaborating, from the initial fetishism and totemism to the great contemporary and future forms of the most illustrious religions...., "but the soul spares itself all this effort and turns it to account for pure meditation if, instead of having to create the atmosphere, if, instead of having to overcome the hostile atmosphere, it sets itself to think or to pray in those easy places where it seems that the ascent to the heavens is already prepared, as on a sublime ladder that others have already climbed. Such an atmosphere is found in the interior of the old temples, in those temples in which the air has been cleared and refined by the music of the liturgical chants, and by creation and incense. Such an atmosphere, I imagine, must be found in all the sacred cities, but particularly in Benares and in Jerusalem. I do not include Rome, because Rome has always been a political capital, the center of a rude and odious empire, the Roman Empire, and afterward the seat of the temporal power of the Church; a power that has so greatly harmed the purity of dogma and the sanctity of practices. Benares, yes, because in Benares great sincere religions have gone to raise temples, choosing the place because something in it seemed to them propitious, and because from ancient times great sages had made it the site of their meditations and their prayers. Jerusalem, yes, and more than Jerusalem, Judea, because there, notwithstanding its being the center of the ancient law, the Pharisaic law, which is so odious to us, it is also the soil where Jesus walked, and this suffices to redeem it and sanctify it above all the places of the earth."

### II.

Having noted the attributes of the critic and expositor of the "Hindustani Studies," I shall point out the most estimable parts of his work. In the third, besides the diligent enumeration of Brahmanism, there stand out the excellent reflections upon Hindustani art and, above all, the chapter entitled "The Idea of Caste," a true embryo (together with the book's preliminary rectifications of the one-sided criterion of the European anthroposociologists and anthropogeographers) of a mystical sociology that would never neglect ETHICAL VALUE in the critical estimation of the diverse historical civilizations.

It is in the fourth part that the synthetic power of the critic is best appreciated. He does not say everything; but he knows how to choose so discreetly what he happens to transcribe for us that the philosophical systems appear delineated to the reader as the antediluvian animals surely appeared to Cuvier, who reconstructed them by analyzing merely a vertebra of their enormous skeletons. Vedantic, Sankhya, Yogi and Nyaya schools, like formidable megatheria and corpulent fossil forests, revive and stir before our astonished and somewhat skeptical attention.

In the fifth part, the reform of Sakya Muni is set forth. Nothing more often repeated nor that turns out to be more new. The topics of the Buddhist scriptures, from the birth and vocation of the Saint to his preaching, death, brotherhood and monasticism, all is related with pious unction, with sincere and profound fervor; but without religious enthusiasm depressing the critical vigor. Let paragraphs 20 and 21 be read attentively, on the influence of Buddhist doctrines upon European Philosophy and the ingenious theory of the prophetic relations of Buddha Gotama with Our Lord Jesus Christ:

"According to the Budivansa or Buddhavansa, the Buddha Sakya Muni or Gotama predicted that his doctrine would flourish for some time; but that afterward it was to fall, and would end by being forgotten; so forgotten and corrupted that five hundred years after his own preaching there would appear a new Buddha charged with reviving it, charged with encouraging men once more in the enterprise of their salvation; and that this new Buddha would be called Buddha Maitreya, the Kindly Buddha, the Merciful Buddha, we should say."

"The prediction needs no commentary. It suffices to hear it to feel oneself profoundly moved, and like one before whom the heavens suddenly opened to disclose some eternal truth. I firmly believe that the Buddha predicted the appearance of Our Lord Jesus Christ, and carried his prophecy to that degree of exactitude which permitted him to give the name of his divine successor. Buddha Maitreya, he said his successor would be called. And never had any Buddha, any saint, any sage, any seer given to love and to kindness the importance that Christ gave them for assuring salvation. Let the whole of human history be run through and not a single personage will be found who can dispute with Jesus the title of Buddha Maitreya. Nor is there any doctrine, apart from that of Christ, that makes salvation depend upon divine Mercy."

But the most interesting and the best of the volume is, perhaps, the commentary on the Atharva Veda. The ingenuity displayed in the magical and eschatological interpretation is captivating. Certain paragraphs consecrated to demonology produce the sharp and violent impression that a page of Strindberg or of Swedenborg engenders in the mind. Let whoever reads Vasconcelos read them with his own experience of life, of that singular store of the marvelous that the years knead in each consciousness. Between the author and his public, every indiscreet reflection of the interpreter is superfluous.

## EDUCAR, ARTE DE FILÓSOFOS — TO EDUCATE: AN ART OF PHILOSOPHERS

I judge that the supreme law of education is respect for the personality of the one being educated. I think that its ultimate end is the development of that personality itself. In sum, for me, education does not "form" its object, but simply "informs" it.

The human soul—irreducible, independent, "sui generis," unique—ought not to go to school in order to be "deformed," but in order to be "informed." Schools, so conceived, ought not to educate directly for the family, for the fatherland, for humanity, for God, but for the individuality of the one who receives education.

To produce or cultivate the greatest number of irreducible individualities, of men who have their own soul well set in its casing—which will engender, in the most complex relations of social life, the greatest heterogeneity of ends and of works, the richest commerce of spirits, the most constant and profound struggle of aspirations, the noblest conflicts of characters—such must be the lodestar of human education. To wish to pass a uniform leveling rod over men is the most stupid of collectivist aspirations and the most useless of all.

The world must yield its greatest return; it must not remain with hidden virtualities, must not lie "in potentia," but take shape "in actu" [in act]. Humanity is inconceivable save as action, as development, as a centrifugal movement of science, energy and goodness. Art and the good are the disinterested excursion of the spirit through existence; its creative overflowing. The most illustrious intuition of the Gospel compendiates this truth in the word of life that teaches: "My Father worketh hitherto."

If in other peoples education tends today frankly toward the development of individuality, in Mexico the need for individual development in the school is pressing. In other peoples distinct individualities, characters of exception, abound. In Mexico there are those who, distinct by reason of their essence, submit themselves thereafter to social discipline, to everybody's ideas, to the odious rod of "Celui-qui-ne-comprend-pas" ["He-who-does-not-understand"], to the disagreeable prejudices of every kind that are the exclusive directors of our psychological life. Truly one would say that, among us, individuality is a crime and a shame.

The timorous positivists are frightened by the idea of "mental anarchy." I am not. I believe that we ought to tend toward liberating ourselves, unbinding ourselves, freeing ourselves from prejudice, individualizing ourselves, in sum. The more diverse the mental life of Mexicans may be, the more each one of us lives more and more from himself, from his "deep self," as Bergson would say, and ever less from the heavy accumulation of secular political and religious prejudices, from the sad misoneism of our society, the happier Mexico will be; above all, it will be richer and nobler, which is what in the last analysis and in the first place matters to us, since the nobility of spirit of our own existence is fully exigible from us, and not happiness, that supreme and irregular gift of the gods.

I conceive, then, clearly, the institutions of education as sites of systematic intellectual and moral information, as repertories or libraries that offer good data and useful premises to the spirit, and I believe firmly that whatever does not conform to this conception corrupts the essence of the school in order to convert it into a penitentiary, barracks, convent or laboratory; at the very least into a place of vulgarization and not of individualization. The school is none of that, and differs profoundly from all of it. Its essence as a necessary element of information has already been noted.

In the year 1839, Nicholas I, Emperor of Russia, on visiting the University of Kiev, said, among other things, to the students of the institution these words, as absurd as they are revealing: "I see that you know how to study and that you are wont to do so with profit; but this alone does not suffice. Science, by itself, does not engender good results. I need subjects faithful to the throne. I demand of you unlimited devotion, submission and obedience."

Next, the Monarch addressed the professors of the University, and spoke to them in this wise: "As for you, it is well that you take care of the culture and the science of your disciples; but if you do not develop the notions of 'my morality' in the students, if you do not intervene and modify in the 'right sense' their political convictions, I shall keep you well in hand."

The despot ingenuously demanded of his subjects, university teachers and students, the cult of "his morality," in the school. All civil or religious power, all force that deforms and does not inform, are the most nefarious corrupters of the population of a republic.

Far be it from me, of course, to deny that the fatherland and religion may justly claim their empire over each man. But no one can be religious, charitable and disinterested if he has not first formed himself. What can the incomplete and vulgar individual give to Mexico—the spiritual pauper who needs to be succored? He, rather than give, will ask, and will have to be given to. What can the weakling give to humanity or to God—he who compromises with evil and violence, the idler, the prejudiced, the egoist? He will keep everything for himself. From him we ought to expect neither aid nor help nor collaboration. The weak will be bad citizens, bad religious men, bad men. Since they did not form themselves, they will not be able to render their greatest effort. Their spirit will never express itself completely. They will pass through life without ever really having been. Education, like life, like existence entire, is enunciated thus: to be is to be individual.

Respect for individuality must be practiced in each of the grades of education; but it is more essential when it refers to children; and then, precisely, is when it is least practiced, because it proves easier to trample heedlessly upon the charters of the human personality.

A child is a mysterious and avid being who goes to school in order to help himself to be a man, in order to express himself as an individuality. The virtualities of childhood are immense. The realities of mature age are, most often, poor—more than poor, wretched. The school is an accomplice in this painful insignificance of human fruits. Instead of aiding the inflorescence of the nascent personality, the school undoes it. There was something unique and strong, a singular soul that time would have developed; the teachers, in exchange for some clear notions—and many others confused, obscure or absurd—after involving in the child's spirit disagreeable prejudices and ignoble sentiments (a cult of bloodthirsty men, of inhuman heroes, love of extrinsic goods, disdain for the intimate and spiritual, love, too, of emulation and pride), kill the personal element they might have found in childhood, corrupt it systematically, and for corrupting it collect money that the State pays them punctually at the end of each ten days of their constant evil action. When it has been contrived to make of each child an impersonal receptacle of facts and notions, a body without initiative, a machine without enthusiasm, duty has been fulfilled, the effort is judged already perfect. The labor of poisoners, of public narcotizers, of authors of clockwork mechanisms, earns the good will of society and the recompense of the citizens.

One cannot educate unless one educates each individual; just as one cannot cure unless one cures each patient; just as one cannot sentence unless one sentences each delinquent. But children do not know how to defend their intimate treasure, their little soul that is going to germinate. They go ingenuously, urged by their parents or guardians, to offer their own individuality to the schoolmasters. Then the servants of primary education receive the divine and tender being whom fortune has bestowed upon them, apply to him the common mold, compressing and destroying him in carrying out such an application, in order to obtain from "one" what they have obtained from "all"; and the child, who went to school to individualize himself, will feel all his life the pain and the sorrow of having been "educated."

A child is the natural friend of all children. For them there are no differences of sex, as there are none for the angels. The teacher must, then, call all to his teaching. He is not to select, for a reason as unprofound as the disposition of the genital organs, the souls that are to be formed in the free commerce of his constant dealings. The boys and the girls of a primary school, far from dividing themselves into biological groups, must collaborate in the reciprocal information of their respective natures. It is proved that there are in the masculine spirit differential properties that complete differential aspects, likewise, of the feminine soul.

A people in which persons of different sex come together not only in the carnal act, but in the spiritual union of daily and assiduous intercourse (as happens in the United States), is more moral than a shy and prudish people that isolates man from woman from childhood on, and only demands that the sin be hidden which, if the conditions of social life were changed, would perhaps not be committed, because no one would think of committing it. Besides, childish predilection, unconsciously amorous, is one of the purest and noblest of human incentives; a holy enthusiasm among them all.

In Mexico little work is done. In the schools of Mexico almost no work is done. In the primary colleges an assemblage of literary or scientific subjects is taught, but knowledge of none of them is acquired. In general, one might say that the primary school is the greatest of national failures.

The "savory occupation of doing nothing," as Gautier wittily said—idleness—is the anti-Christian vice par excellence, the best solvent of character, the perfect narcotic of personality.

The child must be constantly impelled to "act," to "do," to spend his psychic and animal energy, without the puerile fear of overburdening and undoing him.

It ought to be said to the teachers: do not make of childhood, which is the seed of humanity, the promise of heroism, the germ of sanctity, a fetish of idleness. Project it upon action, impel it to creative movement, to disinterested labor, without fear of premature exhaustion or of ill-starred sterility. The human soul is not easily exhausted; and, on the other hand, it easily drowses and poisons itself with the tropical henbane of idleness. The virtualities of childhood can do more, much more than the very little that is demanded of them in Mexico; and, in diminishing the knowledge and the will of Mexican children, one corrupts in advance the future population, the virile race of tomorrow.

Education is a most important social factor or element, whose end is the individual's use of the wealth of society, but without that use killing or dissolving the personality of the one who is educated; because the evil of evils is to leave the soul emptied of its virtue in the formulas and the prejudices and the lies of everyone.

Education is the philosophical art par excellence. It is referred to general philosophy as to the science upon which it indispensably rests. Only philosophers can be educators. It is necessary to have opted for one of the possible solutions of the philosophical problems, in order to propose to oneself, with fruit, the essentially artistic problem of education.

When I affirm that one must previously have opted for one of the solutions to the problem of the "value of existence," it is clear that I do not discard as possible solutions either the flat denial of the value of life or systematic doubt. All are philosophical resolutions, if they are accepted by virtue of ordered and sincere meditation; but whoever has not analyzed the significance of existence, the value of knowledge, the ends of art and of moral conduct; whoever does not possess an epistemology, an aesthetics and a morality of his own, philosophically personal, will never successfully resolve the artistic problems of education. One must have made the great ideas "one's own" in order to be able afterward to use them in action.

Philosophy embraces in its totality, as an object of knowledge, the human spirit, its forms and activities, in terms of the universality of non-spiritual things. It is cosmology and noology at one and the same time. Education too, from the artistic point of view, embraces the totality of existence, the relations of the spirit with the world, with action. Both are philosophy, the one pure and the other practical. For this reason, when some scientific specialist claims for himself and for the science he cultivates the prerogative of giving the rational, biological or psychological foundations of education, he is irremediably mistaken, and engenders a one-sided, inhuman, absurd philosophy of education.

Ever since, following the order prescribed for our schools, I had to pass through so-called secondary education, I conceived a holy horror of pedagogy, that presumptuous science of creating teachers. A knowledge or science that would only have importance if those who take themselves for pedagogues were obliged to read for themselves, and not in wretched epitomes, a few classic books of philosophical education, which are the ones that deserve

: to be read: the "Dialogues" of Plato—which no normal-school professor reads;—the "Essays" of Montaigne—which I find it very hard to think have been read by normal-school professors;—the "Emile" of Rousseau, the "Pedagogy" of Herbart, the "Essay" of Spencer, the marvelous books of Ellen Key. If Pedagogy exists, it can only be a philosophical art. Classical culture is not a mere aesthetic ornament, as our ingenuous Mexican positivists believed, but a great pedagogical impulse toward disinterestedness and heroism. The Preparatory School had for its mission to nourish in science; to form the judgment of the young by the work and grace of science; in a word, to "make them discreet." And one must "make them heroic" as well.

A people that educates itself in science alone is a people without enthusiasm, without ideal. Science is pure egoism, an interest of knowledge, an ever-reiterated purpose of thinking, with the fewest possible notions, the greatest number of diverse phenomena. Art educates the spirit in freedom from preoccupation with oneself, in the "projection" of the soul outward, in the disinterested contemplation of existence.

History, ethically interpreted, educates the moral sense, incites to the sacrifice of what is one's own, to the renunciation of personal well-being. Young men who, like the young Rousseau, should read at night in their Plutarch, until the first songs of the birds announced the dawn, the examples of Caesar and of Marius, the virile and vigorous acts of ancient culture, would be capable, later on, of accomplishing the very exploits of those sublime characters; they would become heroic themselves; they would unify the discretion of their culture with the heroism of their will.

The fundamental defect of a purely scientific education, from the moral point of view, is that it implies an assiduously egoistic and utilitarian practice, not only incomplete but dangerous. Science is an economy of vital effort, a fruit of biological life—let the pleonasm be tolerated—like human intelligence itself. Young men who receive only a scientific education must become, "a fortiori" [all the more], petty egoistic calculators, systematically egoistic.

Let the whole heaven of the sciences be traversed, and one will never have emerged from the sphere of egoism. Dialectic is human egoism itself, distilled and learned. The end of science is industry, useful action, exploitation. But the end of human life is this and another or others besides, and surely nobler ones. Man has in art the symbol and the form of his renunciation of the economic ends of existence. Artistic genius is the highest disinterestedness with regard to one's own animal ends. For this reason art must represent, together with charity, with the supreme disinterestedness of the good, with the centrifugal life of the just man, an indispensable element of education.

Let us make use, in good time, and let us teach in our schools the best use of existence; but let us constantly remind the young that there is something superior to existence as economy, and it is existence as disinterestedness and as charity.

But, in order to know it and to remember it, the teacher implies the philosopher.

War, for the majority of men of good will, is an undoubted evil. Nevertheless, grave thinkers defend it. The fact is that it is not absolutely bad. It is wont to engender good effects. In human life, save cruelty for cruelty's sake, nothing is absolutely bad. Renán used to say that error and truth, evil and good, are transformed imperceptibly into one another like the changing iridescent shades on the neck of doves. The great physicist of Vienna—who is also a great philosopher—Ernst Mach, assures us that the same faculty that creates error engenders truth, and that only the efficacy of an idea, its practical utility, is capable of determining the degree of truth it contains.

War is not good for what it destroys, but for what it creates. But can war create anything? Yes: the warrior creates out of himself a human type that unquestionably possesses certain intrinsic gifts of great moral value.

The warrior intensifies his will, sets it above danger, tinges it with heroism. This is his virtue: to be superior to fatality, to conquer it with the boldness of enthusiasm.

A rebel against destiny, he represents, in one of his aspects, human autonomy. Prometheus, the hero of rebellion through thought, and Hercules, the demigod of virile force, are the same personification of the human genius that does not fear, in its total virtue, the cowardly force of the gods. One cannot love Prometheus and be an enemy of Hercules. Humanity is semi-divine. Neither pure spirit nor mere matter; but will and intelligence indissolubly joined; body and reason.

You say that you despise the warrior, but in the depths of your heart you love him as women and children do. It is impossible to abhor him. Without him, human society would have been as unstable as those flabby and shapeless mollusks that have not the shelter of protective shells; as the invertebrate animals which, without the firmness of a skeleton, cannot unify themselves for the securing of further vital efforts of better quality. Without warriors to defend them, peoples crumble like friable matter.

Nevertheless, cursed be whoever, without restrictions, makes an apotheosis of war! No, "bloody glory," as Hugo said, does not exist. War causes and will always cause sorrows. All morality and all philosophy are condensed in this simple formula: to avoid pain. Sakyamuni, the saint, taught his disciples: Do you see all the leaves of this immense forest? So numerous are—said the inspired one—the truths that it is possible to ascertain. But those that it is urgent to know are as few as these that my hand has plucked from the foliage of a single tree; and all are summed up in the one law: "to avoid pain."

War is good because it infuses into the spirit effort, boldness, enthusiasm. It is bad because it moves to cruelty, because it engenders pain. Might one find the means of turning to account what is good in it, while sparing ourselves, at the same time, its disastrous consequences?.... William James, the great Yankee thinker, saw in athletic games "the moral equivalent of war."

When the Persians of Xerxes arrived before Thermopylae, they had to learn that the Greeks had gathered for the international solemnity of the Olympic Games; and what astonished the Barbarians, says Herodotus, was not so much the strange occupation of playing to which the enemy gave himself over, nor even that they should play while the fatherland was in peril, but that they should contend in the Games to obtain, as the sole prize, a simple wild crown.

Let us moralists strive to substitute for the bloody trophies of war the wild crown formed of the very foliage of the tree consecrated to the God of Victory. Let us give our youth the glory of struggling for the sake of struggling, without the hope of better recompense than the unanimous acclamation of the people and the symbolic crown. Athletics, like war, can invigorate men without harming them. Would that some day the Christian nations, far from taking part in the horror of an "international exposition of barbarism" such as the one we are witnessing, might gather peacefully to honor the athletes triumphant in the noble exercises of the body, which so greatly dispose the spirit to the effort of sanctity and of heroism!

The Christian is not, as some believe, an enemy of life. He loves it, succors it, exalts it. He is an enemy of pain and of death. His supreme and only virtue is charity. The greatest saints were the strongest men, the most vigorous and active, the happiest.

The spirit of Christianity reproves war, condemns it because it is an accomplice of death. If in play the body is disposed to be the fit sanctuary of the soul, the Christian moralist will applaud the athlete as a precursor of the saint and will take him to his heart.

Plato defined man by saying that he is "a soul that makes use of a body." In vain would one strive to rectify the enunciation of so illustrious a truth. The first and fundamental thing is the soul; but the body, its necessary means of expression, must always be unhampered, must be an efficacious instrument.

Let us make of Mexican youth, thanks to athletic games, by means of the "moral equivalent of war," the efficacious instrument of the spirit.

## NUESTRA MISION HUMANA — OUR HUMAN MISSION

The world has not finished being made. This is the fundamental reason of existence and of humanity. In the valley of Jehoshaphat we shall know whether the world was bad or good. Let the optimists and the pessimists wait until the Lord's work is finished in order to judge it. Neither the ones nor the others conceived it; it seems, then, prudent to wait.

All life is an impetus, an effort. Morality is the effort toward the realization of human nature. There is no virtue that is weak. A weak virtue implies a contradiction. The virtuous man is the strong man. Fortitude is not a special virtue, but the condition of every virtue.

No one can surpass himself, aggrandize himself, realize himself without exerting himself. Life is a surpassing, an aggrandizement, a realization. The virtuous man is par excellence the man of effort.

Religions have always idealized those who strove by systematic effort. Like Achilles, the Christian ascetic is virtuous. The fakir, the martyr, the cenobite are virtuous. All undertook, in diverse senses—akin, contrary or contradictory—one and the same work: the realization of human nature, that is to say, the actualization of the hidden virtualities of humanity. Their conduct was moral insofar as they brought their life into accord with their purpose.

The good will does not want pleasure, but the pleasure of being good, of being valiant. Every act that is fulfilled engenders pleasure, Aristotle taught.

There is the pleasure of realizing oneself as a man, of fulfilling the human destiny, and the pleasure of being bestial, that is, of realizing oneself as a beast. The virtuous man does not deny pleasure, but he accepts it only as the immediate result of his effort, of his virtue.

There coexist in man the animal nature and the human. If a life is subordinated to animality, it will be pleasurable, but not happy, not moral, because it will not have been fecund for the realization of humanity, but retroactive, frustrating of its potentiality.

If conduct were subordinated to pleasure, movement would stop "ipso facto." The greatest biological happiness would be attained, but not the greatest human happiness, because one would constantly lack the bliss of being good, that is to say, of being a man of effort.

Morality founded upon the pleasure of the greatest number, as the disciples of Bentham say, does not tend to the elevation of life, to its realization, to its triumph, but to its drowsiness, to its inevitable defeat.

In seeking pleasure for pleasure's sake, one seeks, in sum, nothing but the defeat of life, the annihilation of action. Each species has fixed itself by its torpor, by its softness, by its laxity, by its egoism, by seeking "its" pleasure. But life is what creates the species and afterward transforms them; what takes shape and what dares.

Effort is a longing for creation, for diversity, for fecundity. Pleasure is an index of monotony, of peace, of sterility. The virtuous man inserts himself into the creative current of the world; he is one of its accomplices. The epicurean dozes upon his incomplete bliss, falls asleep, at times profoundly, upon his being.

If one seeks peace and not action, one will arrive at inertia; but then all pleasure will have disappeared. Pleasure supposes action as its cause, but it invites to inaction. It is good by the law from which it derives; it is not good as a law; the conduct it can inspire is not good. a

The only moral pleasures are those that derive from effort, not those that enervate it. Duty is not a dead rule nor a Judaic commandment, but an invitation, a persuasion to act in the direction of the greatest intensity and fecundity of existence. Egoism is the instinct that defends what has been created; altruism, the intuition that proposes what does not yet exist, but which can be.

Every moral formula must extol the condemnation of egoism, insofar as it halts the enduring work, not insofar as it makes it possible as a datum from which one must inevitably start.

The moral subject understands that he is not the ultimate end of existence, but only an instant of its development. Others were before he was. Other peoples have been previously as well. One life, many individual lives, all living beings are nothing but diverse conditions of the systematic exaltation of life.

Pleasure is a fruit. If you are good you will enjoy. If you are bad, you will also enjoy. If you are egoists without being bad, you will enjoy. If you sacrifice yourselves, you will enjoy. If you love, you will enjoy. If you hate, you will enjoy. If you wish only to enjoy, act as you please; do not ask for a morality, but for a theory of pleasure. The good man has good enjoyments; the bad man bad pleasures; but both enjoy.

Life is tension and relief, impetus and rest, egoism and sacrifice. If life dares, it is strong, original, disinterested, heroic, good. If it drowses, if it wishes above all to drowse, if it materializes itself, if it stagnates, it is bad.

Idleness is the evil. Diligence is the good. Idleness is to act for "oneself" who already exists, and not for "another" who will come. Virtue is to act not in order to be what one is, but in order to become another and better.

"My Father worketh hitherto," said Jesus. The virtuous work with Him.

Contemporary thought offers to the activity of man a vaster field for its manifestation than that which the determinism of the nineteenth century offered him. The world was then conceived as a concatenation of necessary laws that crossed one another, forming the subtle web of existence. Each inextricable point of interference of the eternal laws was a fact, a being, a soul. Reality was constrained within the threads of a net of diamond.

In a world so disposed, the action of humanity lacked originality, substantive reality. Conduct was the inevitable corollary of the action of cosmic energies conjugated at a point. The possible combinations and permutations of the invariable laws created the opulent variety of aspects. Everything dissolved into impersonal generality, into empty abstraction. Nothing remained as definitive save reason; nothing was durable and enduring save everlasting movement. Personality, individuality, liberty ceased to have any possible place in the perpetual passage from one form to another, from one being to another being, from one fact to another.

Today we do not deny the perennity of movement, the generality of law, nor the efficacy of abstraction; but it is urgent for us to find a fixed point for our most irrefragable immediate intuitions. Today, as before, we profoundly respect the data of science; but we should not be capable of offering up to them, in superstitious holocaust, the revelations, likewise essential, of our intimate feeling. We harbor the hope of being able to approach more and better the true reality of the world; because if we heed what our reason disentangles from the labyrinth of forms of life, we do not on that account disdain the unanimous dictates of sentiment and the arcane value of faith.

Natural laws—we think—do not constitute the essence of things; but, simply, the procedures we use to understand them. Our reason is determinist by its own nature. Its intrinsic mode of manifesting itself is the rigorous principle of contradiction. But the reality to which we apply the inflexibility of our understanding is not susceptible of bending to that reason. It is an experience that does not fit into the molds of our logic, of our algebra, of our geometry. It is a world that overflows, breaking many times the rigid frame in which we should like to enclose it. By no syllogism would universal gravitation or the transformism of living species have been obtained "a priori." There was needed the creative intuition, the genius of Newton and of Darwin, to apply fruitfully to the solar system and to universal life the organic determinism of reason.

Thus, in the measure that meditation moves further from the rigid schemes of logical and mathematical determinism; in the measure that from the astronomical law we pass to the physical and from the physical to the biological and the psychological, the poorer the false grid of determinism seems to us; the richer and more unforeseeable does multiform reality throb; the more and more do we reconcile ourselves with our own consciousness, which does not cease to warn us inwardly that we are free, personal and autonomous; that the world exterior to us determines us only partially, and that perhaps those "eternal laws" are only the ever-perishable and progressive symbol of our scientific and practical conception.

Then existence takes on a new sense for the ends of our action. It is no longer the world that governs us; it is we who shall be able to govern it. It is no longer the external environment that determines us; it is we who can determine it. The law no longer suffocates us, mere rational symbolism. Our own reality is worth more. Before, we were the fleeting episodes of an incoercible evolution; now we are the authors of the life we live, the artists of conduct, the masters of our own reality.

Then the sense of our activity appears clearly. The world, too, has not finished being made. It will have to be formed. Before, fatality vanquished us. Now we shall vanquish it. Perfect means finished making, fulfilled, realized. We shall perfect what is human. We shall realize our nature. We shall fulfill our program. The ideas of today will bear fruit later. The purposes of this instant will be the world to come. The work of humanity will consist in not letting rot, as mere barren virtualities, the energies that can take shape in acts. We shall turn the dream into life and the design into truth.

Such is our charge. To select in ourselves what must die, what it is fitting to abandon to the matter that falls, to perishable animal life, and to preserve and increase what is our own. To give to the time that flows what it does not matter to us to keep and to polish: the resplendent diamond of our essence: the love that pardons, the will that dares and the genius that invents.

Let the first vice be called idleness and the first virtue enthusiasm. Perhaps Something exists which, like us, is summed up in an impetus of creation, invention and heroism. Let us work. Let us fulfill beneath the stars our human mission.

To command and to obey are two profoundly disagreeable actions. To command is proper to tyrants. To obey is the attribute of the irresponsible. To command is to outrage others. To obey is to outrage oneself. Man, in what he has that is properly human, must neither submit nor make others submit. Persuade him. Substitute persuasion for the commandment. Set, in place of dominion, the example.

When a sentence is formulated with an imperative accent, one risks obtaining as a reply an adverse and categorical answer, at least as justified as the commandment itself. "Act as I command you"—says he who rules. "It does not please me to do so"—the one obliged will answer.... And both will occupy impregnable positions.

Force does not resolve the problem; because if the more powerful were the one who orders, he will certainly not conquer by ordering, but by being strong; and if he who receives the order triumphs on account of his greater power, his disobedience will not add a jot of fortitude to his rebellion. Morality and force are as incommensurable as body and spirit, as space and time.

The Kantian notion of duty was wholly foreign to the noble moral philosophy of the Greeks. They taught the good and virtue; but not duty. They strove for happiness. They submitted to no moral geometry.

The whole ethical effort of the ancient world is summed up in the rival theses of the Porch and the Garden. Both, despite their contrary attitudes, sought the same thing. The Stoic, happiness through virtue, through contemplation and disdain; the Epicurean, bliss through pleasure; but the ones and the others, like the skeptics, engage themselves in the ascendancy of a homogeneous spiritual movement. They longed to combine human dignity with their own satisfaction. For them "morality for morality's sake" bore no sense whatever; but morality for life, for the good that is attained by living. They would have condemned as a dialectical monstrosity the search for the moral law for its own sake. The Greeks always had the "sense of the earth" of which Nietzsche speaks. They always put lead on the wings of free metaphysical speculation.

Neither did Jesus Christ teach dead and exact rules like algebraic theorems; but gentle motives of action. The whole Law and the prophets—says the Gospel—are compendiated in the love of God and of one's neighbor. Christian charity is an enthusiasm, an act of supreme persuasion.

Man is the only creature capable of morality, because he is the sole being in all Creation in whom the animal and the rational natures coexist; the beast and the angel. For pure spirits, the moral law is superfluous. For pure beasts it is superfluous as well. Only we men can sacrifice what we drag along of the zoological in our hybrid nature to whatever of the angelic there may be in us. Morality is an invitation, a persuasion to act as spiritual beings and not as beasts.

Nevertheless, not all of us men are capable of producing ourselves as exclusive agents of the spirit. The just man sins seven times a day. Rights, governments, commandments, laws and prisons are sad necessities of the common weal; forms of the accursed inheritance of Cain.

To persuade is the most urgent and the highest of human occupations. How shall we persuade morally?....

To the people one cannot offer as norms of action abstruse treatises of philosophy; but it is indeed possible to show it that superior men are those who have best realized human nature. Heroes, martyrs, saints are more men than the rest. If you wish to realize what is intrinsic to humanity, act as they did. Be, in your turn, saints, martyrs and heroes.

Nothing is perfect yet. Man has not finished being made. There are superior and inferior men. There are good and bad, wise and ignorant, intrepid and cowardly, great and small. But the fact is that we all ought to be superior, good, wise, intrepid and great. Be, then, as those have been whom you admire. In this consists the whole of morality.

Imitate the wise in prudence; the heroes in fortitude; the saints in virtue. Imitate exceptional men. Be exceptional; in this way no one will be so any longer, since we shall all be so. The day virtue becomes vulgar, all human morality will be useless.

Imitate, above all, Jesus. "Imitation of Christ" was the name of the ascetic book that consoled the most souls in the West; but know that the imitation of Jesus Christ is truer in the life of Saint Francis of Assisi than in the inhuman pages of Kempis.

The only duty is to be superior like the superior. "Would you return to the beast," says Nietzsche, "instead of surpassing man?"

Nietzsche's error lies in thinking that one must surpass what is not yet realized. Man has not yet been realized. When we have finished forming him it will be time to speak of the superman. Meanwhile, the generous message of Zarathustra is only an unintelligible word. Let it suffice us, for now, to long for our species' own perfect advent. Let us turn into individual, concrete and living reality what we have achieved only by exception. This is the only attainable sense of human destiny.

Pessimism and optimism—superlative oppositions—merge at one point like all

+ extremes: the forced negation of moral action. If everything is irresistibly disposed toward evil, it is superfluous to trouble oneself with the impossible search for the good. If we are in the best of possible worlds, all preoccupation is likewise superfluous.

An optimal or a pessimal existence reduces human effort to nothing. Only in a world imperfect, but perfectible by the will, does action have sense. The universe of Leibnitz and that of Schopenhauer exclude all notion of activity; but optimism is more immoral than pessimism. Inaction without bitterness, without disillusion, is the worst. All true idealism implies a movement of displeasure with reality. Bitterness invigorates. Satisfaction paralyzes. This is the sole difference. The pessimist can savor the generous liquor of disdain.

Life has a double aspect. It is exoteric and esoteric, trivial and profound, egoistic and disinterested, stoic and epicurean, biological and moral. There are those who see only its exterior aspect. They are the moralists of pleasure, of happiness without virtue. There are, on the other hand, those who attend only to its interior and profound aspect. They are the theorists of disinterestedness, of charity, of virtue. Their error consists in disdaining pleasure; and, as Spencer says with admirable sagacity, pleasure is an "a priori" form of moral intuition as space is of sensible intuition.

Life seeks pleasure, but it exerts itself before finding it. Bliss is not the fruit of idleness, but the patrimony of valor.

The virtuous man is called the man of effort. Without the longing for struggle, for betterment, no life is possible. A secret movement, an interminable palpitation, a perpetual advent is universal existence. How conceive struggle without advantage and advantage without effort? Heroism too is law. Let your will declare: "Heroism too is law."

The most plausible conception of life consists in imagining it as an ever-active impetus which once hurled itself upon the assault of cosmic matter to model it at its pleasure:

"Hail, creating flame of the world, burning tongue of eternal knowing; pure germ, fecund principle that chainest death at thy feet."

The verses of vertiginous romantic inspiration that Espronceda consecrated to life reflect its esoteric sense. To live is, intrinsically, to strive for triumph, notwithstanding blind formless matter. Life is a longing for new forms, pure and triumphant.

But the "struggle for life" of the naturalists; the egoism that always wants its own advantage; the pleasure that relieves and procures rest, is also true. It would be impossible to deny its reality.

The organic species have fixed and defined themselves by seeking their pleasure; and they were transmuted by carrying out their effort, by attempting and daring. The mother who sacrifices herself for her offspring is the vulgar and profound sign of the heroic action that constitutes the hidden essence of living. Altruism is the longing that awakens constantly from the egoistic being who settles indolently upon his individual reality.

When the moralists of pleasure counsel placing the whole law of action in the constant search for and capture of one's own delight, they ignore that an action that ceases to renew itself engenders no pleasure. Vice varies or tends to vary the conditions of pleasure. Every vicious man needs to exert himself to find new delights. If the monotony of egoism is affirmed, pleasure disappears. Only by inventing new forms of enjoyment can one always enjoy. The greatest pleasure in the world, a thousand times repeated, is worth less as pleasure than the small new satisfactions that spring up by chance on the laborious path of the just man.

If you wish to be happy, model without rest the human clay at your disposal. Make it speak all tongues. Give it arcane sonorities, like the morning wind that made the statue of Memnon vibrate. Transform yourselves into incessant activity. To enjoy, one must suffer. If pleasure is a good, pain, effort, labor cannot be absolutely bad. At the end of the difficult road—via crucis [way of the cross]—lies the land of promise.

See, then, how to a world that is not yet realized there corresponds a morality that will realize it. Life has come daring from the first instant in which it throbbed. Egoism defends what exists. Altruism intimates what will come. If interest were completely subordinated to disinterestedness; if heroes never thought of themselves; if sacrifice alone held sway, life would suffer, in the end, its evident defeat. If, on the contrary, pleasure and egoism were to conquer, life would fold and refold upon its own essence; it would perish, at last, annihilated beneath the ponderous material yoke.

But, joined together, altruism and egoism, virtue and pleasure, present reality and the project of future realities, the fact and the design, the present and the virtual, complete and aid one another. Thanks to the individuals who seek their pleasure, there is sustained the impetuous current that feeds effort and sanctifies virtue.

Humanity is the army of the Good. It is will illumined by reason. Life dared without knowing what it wanted. Man intuits toward where the vital impetus turns.

An intrepid archer aimed at the star. Like the Imperial Sagittarius of the Aztec legend, he drew the bow and the quivering arrow leapt whistling. Human longing will fix it in the Sun.
