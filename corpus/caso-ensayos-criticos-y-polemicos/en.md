## PROLOGO. — PROLOGUE.

The Government of the Republic, victorious over Maximilian's ephemeral Empire, founded an institution important in the history of Mexican culture: the Preparatory School, a broad, encyclopedic establishment, harmonious in its curriculum. The soul of that school was a fervent admirer and devoted disciple of Auguste Comte, Mr. Gabino Barreda. Positivism supplanted Catholicism as the official religion. From then on, everything would be weighed, measured, and counted. The most rigorous methods of scientific investigation were established in its laboratories. The windows that had once shown the spirit those limitless vistas made of clarity, sky, and gold dust of the sun were shut. Telescopes, in their place, pierced the rooftops... An end was made of the vague poetry of incomprehensible and eternal things. A gust of materialism, of egoism, of down-to-earth [terre à terre] intellectualism snapped off the "fair flowers of belief," as one of the schoolmasters proclaimed. Latin and Greek were banished from the classrooms; Roman Law retreated in the Faculty of Jurisprudence; philosophy, reduced to logic, experimental psychology, and Spencerian ethics, vegetated in the lecture halls with its noble wings — which had served it to soar so high — now clipped. Integral and humanistic education lost its equilibrium. The humanistic tradition of the Pontifical University — founded in the year 1553 and a seminary of scholars throughout the Viceroyalty — which stemmed from the very marrow of Spanish university history, from Salamanca, Alcalá, and Valladolid, was severed; and professors and students alike devoted themselves exclusively to cultivating science, disdaining the other disciplines that give the spirit the breath to triumph. The indisputable axiom prevailed over the audacious interrogation of the unknown things of heaven and earth. The Preparatory School "prepared" practical men, skilled planners of affairs, shrewd financiers, eminent mathematicians. Through it passed all those who most distinguished themselves by their talent and culture in the last forty years. From it emerged, in particular, all those who, gathered about General Díaz as counselors, as officials, or as friends, crossed the country with railways, raised stately buildings, introduced magnificent material improvements without concerning themselves with the intellectual and social betterment of the multitudes who "hungered and thirsted after justice." There was no idealism...

Ambitious, youthful spirits ill accommodated themselves to the gilded prison in which their masters had enclosed them. Beyond the axiom and mere experimental confirmation, they glimpsed the widest and most virgin horizons for intellects accustomed to scouring the earth. A vague restlessness, a powerful desire to break free from shackles, and doctrines brought by new books arriving from Europe were the prelude to a renewal of methods and doctrines whose theater would be the Preparatory School itself.

The moment was propitious. A nervous, agile, and intelligent youth — a homogeneous group, perhaps unique in the literary history of Mexico — was at that instant leaving the classrooms and preparing to found first the Society of Lectures, and afterward the Ateneo de la Juventud. They were not only poets and writers such as those who had once gathered around Gutiérrez Nájera's "Revista Azul," or "La Revista Moderna" of Valenzuela and Nervo, illustrated by the tormented genius of that great draftsman named Julio Ruelas. In the Ateneo group there were even those writers who typically do not write, as Remy de Gourmont observed, but who exert a powerful influence on their contemporaries. Thinkers abounded, however: Antonio Caso, the master of today's youth as Ignacio M. Altamirano and Justo Sierra had once been; José Vasconcelos, one of the most powerful intellects that Spanish America has ever produced; Alfonso Reyes, a master in doctrine and in its expression, now reaping well-deserved triumphs in Madrid; Pedro and Max Henríquez Ureña — the former, perhaps the finest professor of literature the University of Mexico has had, profoundly influenced the ideas and culture of those of us who were his students; Alfonso Cravioto, an intelligent and perceptive art critic; Alberto J. Pani and Alfonso Pruneda, the one an engineer, the other a physician, who brought to the Ateneo a wealth of learning patiently acquired and augmented through their own studies and observations; Carlos González Peña, the novelist of the generation; Jesús Acevedo, an admirable observer; Luis Castillo Ledón; Julio Torri, Mariano Silva, Martín Luis Guzmán — all contributed to the common endeavor, which was one of seriousness, study, and daring. Joining them were those who, though of the preceding generation, had preserved in their souls that perpetual youth which always opens distant vistas on the horizon: Jesús Urueta, an extraordinary tribune who electrified crowds with his warm and surprising eloquence; Luis Urbina, a poet of silken softness and crepuscular nuance...

Soon the one who claimed all the enthusiasm of the youth, all its love, came to be Don Antonio Caso. Don Antonio Caso, now a guest of Argentina, is a man as physically robust as his thought. He is easily recognized by his pronounced and strong-willed jaw, and by his mane — generally disheveled — which floats in the lecture room like a glorious plume. He possesses a courtly and chivalrous courtesy, that courtesy which is the cherished patrimony of Mexicans and which Vicente Espinel had already remarked in the Golden Age. His habits are simple and modest; his time is consecrated to study, the lecture hall, his students, and his family. His life is one of exemplary austerity and inflexible rectitude. He subordinates every personal convenience to his duties as a friend, and has been seen more than once to decline enviable positions so as not to displace a friend. He is an indisputable master in every regard: by the vigor of his thought, by the warm eloquence he offers in his lectures and conferences, by the depth — not devoid of grace or of gentle irony, or of a joyful fluttering — that he imparts to his lessons, by the love he professes toward his students. His work as a teacher has been an apostolate. He combated positivism, barely out of the schoolroom, aided effectively by José Vasconcelos. The campaign has been fruitful, and the field of philosophy in Mexico has been enlarged. He advocates for the independence of the National University, and its autonomy is soon to be granted. In his life there is a devotion and a love which day by day he deepens further in study and in remembrance. This devotion and this love are bound up in the figure of a master, Don Justo Sierra, of great significance in the history of Mexican thought. Caso and Sierra have been thinkers and artists at once. Caso holds lectures in the Faculty of Advanced Studies on Philosophy and Aesthetics, and in the Faculty of Jurisprudence, on Sociology. He is clear in his exposition, convincing in his reasoning, vivid in his imagery, elegant in his bearing. He is the most remarkable lecturer Mexico possesses today. After Urueta, he is the noblest orator we have had. He knows, moreover, how to listen and to transmit all the world's insinuations — murmurs, sounds, and ideas. A curious meditator upon all the problems that agitate the universal consciousness, he thinks, works, and instructs.

He has gathered his work into several volumes, which Mexican youth read and study with noble earnestness. He is the most serious expositor of philosophical systems the Mexican University has had. Bergson and Boutroux have congratulated him on his labors and endorsed his exegeses. Beginning with the "Philosophy of Intuition" — a synthetic exposition of the Bergsonian viewpoint — he has published solid volumes in which, alongside the force and vigor of reasoning, there prevails an agreeable and attractive readability that makes them a pleasure to read. "Philosophical Problems, Philosophers, and Moral Doctrines" — juicy essays on men and ideas of Europe and America, this book incorporating into the history of universal thought the figures of Eugenio María Hostos and Justo Sierra; "Existence as Economy, as Disinterest, and as Charity," an original Christian interpretation of life; "Doctrines and Ideas," commentaries on the problems of the present hour; "Dramma per musica," comprising beautiful monographs on Beethoven, Wagner, Verdi, and Debussy; and he is preparing "The Concept of Universal History," which, judging by the chapters already known to us, will be a fine glory for Spanish-American thought.

As an exemplary mentor, his students are already following in his footsteps, and when — educated in the most absolute honesty and the most rigorous discipline — they come to govern the country, the ideal so gloriously championed by Rodó will be possible: a democracy of selection, an Athenian Republic in a land hitherto convulsed and maligned.

JULIO JIMÉNEZ RUEDA

Buenos Aires, September 1921.

## EL PROBLEMA FILOSOFICO DE LA EDUCACION<sup>∗</sup> — THE PHILOSOPHICAL PROBLEM OF EDUCATION<sup>∗</sup>

<sup>∗</sup> Lecture delivered at the Faculty of Humanities of the University of La Plata, Argentine Republic.

ADDRESS BY DEAN DOCTOR LEVENE. The dean, Doctor Levene, introduced Doctor Caso, saying: Ladies,

Mr. President,

Ambassador of Mexico,

Ladies and gentlemen, professors and students:

I have the honor of presenting to the youth of La Plata the ambassador of our sister republic, Doctor Antonio Caso — humanist, publicist, and professor at the historic Spanish-American University — who has graciously acceded to our request to occupy the chair in the Faculty of Educational Sciences.

He honors us with his presence and generously offers us a lesson on a subject as momentous as the philosophical problem of education — which preoccupies every superior spirit, which engrosses the attention of every man of study, inciting to

meditate, with gravity, upon the destiny of children and youth, upon the responsibility of teachers who mold the sensitive souls entrusted to their care.

In truth, beyond the economic crisis that lashes all peoples and draws forth improbable resources from the alchemy of their financiers — the philosophical system of education is the arduous problem that afflicts the world: before the great war, as evidenced through the modern pedagogical literature of ethical and scientific content; and after the catastrophe, as confirmed by the anguished necessity of posing it in its exact terms, clarifying it, and drawing closer to its rational solution.

Your word, esteemed master, will therefore be heard with respect by the professors and students of this house, for whom that subject is an essential part of their studies.

Gentlemen:

As you know, Mexico — which has just celebrated the centenary of its emancipation — is a most free democracy like our own; it has suffered, as we have, the misfortunes of civil war engendered by its unshakable passion for the Republic and Liberty; it has given to history brilliant poets who have sung, in imperishable verses, beauty and love; eminent historians who exercise their ingenuity in research; statesmen, representative of action and character; masters who teach youth the path of truth and the ideal of life.

We share with Mexico, apart from other spiritual bonds, a common origin of solidarity and fraternity, sealed in the realization of an enterprise at once magnanimous and epic.

In 1810, Caracas, Buenos Aires, Santiago, and Mexico City vibrated in unison in rebellion against the dominion of the Metropolis, our common mother, who was good as a mother ought to be, transmitting to us all that she possessed and much more in a generous and perhaps impractical idealism; and by virtue of supreme natural reasons — historical and biological — the children emancipated themselves from tutelage when the hour of physical and intellectual maturity struck.

At that dawn of our existence, a civilian figure of the Buenos Aires revolution — Mariano Moreno — made evident the impossibility of constituting a confederation of American states, yet uttered these bitter words, which I wish to repeat, for the time has come to put an end to the policy of isolation we have been living: "Who would reconcile our movements — wrote the spiritual director of the Argentine revolution — with those of Mexico, when with that people we have no more relations than with Russia or Tartary?"

At this very moment, students from all of America — among them Argentines from this University of La Plata and from Buenos Aires — are fraternizing in Mexico, celebrating its historical anniversary; and Don Antonio Caso, ambassador of his government and distinguished intellectual representative of the University, is teaching Argentine youth.

These constitute unmistakable proofs that a new historical moment is being inaugurated in the destiny of America. I have finished.

Ladies,

Gentlemen:

My first word is one of deep gratitude for the heartfelt words that have just sprung from the lips of the dean of this Faculty — most meritorious of all, for it is the faculty of education, of the formation of peoples; the most important of the university faculties, in countries that are still being made in civilization, that are still crystallizing into spiritual states, and that must one day spread in the realization, perhaps, of the highest destinies the human being has ever achieved on this planet. America offers new possibilities of development to European culture, and Europe — which always ends by forming trading posts and colonies in other continents — in America leaves children; in America it begets new peoples. My own was called New Spain, and near it there is another, powerful, called New England.

European civilization has secured for itself in America an interminable path of development; in our countries and in our regions there will probably unfold a new manner of fraternizing among human beings, building cities that may perhaps yield rich fruits like those borne by the venerable lands of Europe.

When a Mexican has the honor of addressing you in an Argentine university, he can only return to the same ideas and the same thoughts you have just heard from the lips of the dean.

It is true; we live very much apart, very far away. Geography has isolated us, but the spirit unites us. And precisely because we are far apart, that is a reason to work at drawing closer. Difficult works are those that most incite men's endeavor; easy works achieve and fulfill themselves of their own accord.

Mr. Dean: I again express my gratitude for the words so honorable to me that you were pleased to utter moments ago, and I proceed, with your leave, to take up the study of the subject that was duly announced.

The subject is, in sum, the philosophical problem of education. I shall say at once that, for me, education stands in relation to philosophy as art stands in relation to science; that is to say, one cannot be a good educator without being a philosopher. To educate is to conduct, to lead. But whither to conduct? Where shall we lead men? How shall we shape their destinies, if we do not first take pains to discover what those destinies are? Education cannot be claimed, as a proper domain, by the psychologist, nor by the biologist, nor by the historian, nor by the sociologist. A broader vantage point is indispensable. It is indispensable to contemplate the problems of education from a higher standpoint; one must subordinate all of those problems — which I would venture to call secondary problems of education, from the biological, physiological, and psychological points of view — to the supreme point of education from the philosophical point of view. For only practical philosophy embraces in its totality, as a synthesis, the ordered meditation of the things of the world in relation to the things of the spirit; and only by means of this ordered meditation of the things of the world in relation to the things of the spirit is it possible to ascertain how one ought to guide men, how one ought to form their character, how one ought to integrate their personality. Consequently, education belongs to philosophy as an art belongs to a fundamental discipline. The fundamental discipline is philosophy.

From the outset, it is indispensable to ask ourselves: what is it we wish to achieve when we educate? Why do we educate? With what purpose do we summon people to fill the schools? The great fear of the educator is that he may cause men to waste their time by calling them to school. He would then be the most criminal of all offenders; he would have called them to squander the best hours of their lives by filling the classrooms, from which the people would have drawn nothing but confused and incomplete notions. It would perhaps be better, then, to abandon each spirit to its own personal discipline, rather than to summon it and gather it in schools with other spirits.

Consequently, it is urgent to ask ourselves: what are we going to do by educating? What are we educators going to achieve by educating? We are going to integrate personalities, to integrate human beings, to form men. What is a man? What ought a man to be?... And who else can answer but the philosopher? Who else can know what a man is but the philosopher? If the aim is to form him, one must first of all ask the secret of his formation from whoever can gather together, in synthesis, the data provided individually by each of the various scientific and artistic disciplines in which humanity has exercised itself throughout the course of history.

All things in the universe are individual; none of the things or beings that exist fails to be individual; there are no general things, no general beings. Reality is always individual. General ideas and general words are manners of thinking about things. What exists in the world is nothing but a set of concrete beings, whose investigation falls to the scholar and the philosopher. Therefore, the greatest error for one who seeks to educate may consist in remaining fixed upon the general synthesis of things and having formed nothing but formulas, having constructed nothing but definitions, when what is at stake is precisely something very different: the forming of spirits, the defining of individualities, the refining of vocations, and the crystallizing of souls.

If this were the case, the worst of the men educated in the world would be the one whose sole purpose was to proceed in accordance with the formula he learned, or with the physical or chemical law he recalls from the pages of his schoolbooks. It is an error to think that the conic sections truly exist in reality; that all the intellectual forms by which we understand the universe truly exist in reality. To think that God, like Descartes or Pascal, arranged the world according to a treatise on conics is simply to misunderstand the value of the treatise on conics, besides being disrespectful toward religion — with which I do not wish to concern myself at this moment. Reality is given to us pro indiviso, as a totality, in consciousness; and we have traced paths through it by which we progressively disentangle it, order it, and reconstruct it with the data we take from outside and inside ourselves. These are the various syntheses that little by little draw closer to reality itself and tend to imprison it in all its aspects — never managing, alas, to make the vessel resistant and supple enough to capture the small details and manifestations of its exquisite individuality, which is what truly constitutes the subtle interior and exterior of things. The formulas seem bare. The definitions, hollow, incongruous with life.

Let us endeavor to penetrate the individuality of things; to become thoroughly acquainted with the personality of things; let us tend to see in the sui generis — always sui generis — reality of the world, the manner in which to educate ourselves and to form ourselves in accordance with that world, which always bears an individual, personal, and characteristic value or significance.

Imagine a human being worth no more than a vademecum, signifying no more than a booklet of formulas, who leaves school with a series of general propositions in his head; on going out into life he would be disappointed every day, for the formulas relating to life are far removed, after all, from life itself; and for any man, no problem is ever identical to another problem, and in two different men one may say that life never offers them an identical situation. Each person encounters at every hour of his life grave problems to solve. There will be two opposing ways to form the spirit: either to dispose it in such a fashion that it always has a general precept from which to derive a special, adequate, and perfect solution; or else to integrate the spirit's initiative, to dispose it in such proportion, to free it under such conditions, that the spirit — always autonomous, always intuitive, always agile — can exchange signals with the reality of things and prevail at the opportune moment. The first way is a mistaken way; the second is a generous and true way. It is the one I would urge you, if I could, to follow as educators.

Let the soul be integrated, let individuality be fortified, let personality be defined, let the spirit be crystallized — and then there is nothing to fear.

If one has managed to free each soul from preoccupations, to free it from the negative elements and causes that impede its own development, if one has compelled it to take contact with itself, if at all times the pupil is truly himself in every circumstance of life, the life the subject will live will be much happier, and far more efficacious in the end for action — which is the only thing we can ask of men, since happiness, as the ancients said, is the patrimony of the gods.

Let us integrate, then; let us form individualities, determine consciousnesses, crystallize souls — and we shall thereby spare ourselves many practical failures, and more than one theoretical failure.

There is no way to form a perfect man with booklets and formulas, but there is indeed a way to integrate the spirit through sympathy and conviction; and thus armed, spirits go out into life and find in it the means to exercise themselves and to triumph. For the things of the world — I repeat — as with the things of the spirit, are never general; reality is always individual. Two drops of water are positively not two identical beings; when we examine them under the microscope we find essential differences between one drop and another — they become for us two distinct universes, each with its own characteristic flora and fauna. Two leaves of the same tree are not identical; they are similar, but each has its own genuine personality and individual character. And if we say this of two drops of water or two leaves of the same tree, what shall we say of two human souls?

The preoccupation with running a uniform rule over all spirits is absurd, criminal, and odious.

I consider that schools are places where souls are informed. The mission of education is not to deform but to inform; the mission of education is not to bend but to discuss; the mission of education is not to bore but to teach; the mission of education is not to compel but to liberate; the mission of education, as I conceive it, is a school — not a workshop, not a laboratory, not a prison — but a place of free discussion, where the spirit attains its fullest development through the friendly, sincere, and constant contact with other noble spirits.

But individuality, one might say — is it the individuality of a criminal, or the individuality of a saint? Is it the personality of a hero or of a martyr? What personality do we seek to awaken or to call forth by educating? What human personality? And I answer: the human personality. But, after all, is not a criminal a man? Shall we seek to awaken the criminal instinct in schools? Is not a martyr a man? Shall we endeavor in schools to increase the martyrology? Is not a hero a man? Shall we endeavor in schools to increase the number of heroes? I answer: we must form and integrate human personalities. In sum, the difficulty, the obscurity, lies in the meaning of this word: what is the human? And I ask: at the Faculty of Humanities of La Plata, what is the human? Wherein does the human consist? What is characteristic and distinctive about humanity?

Man has many points of contact with the beast, but has, fortunately, many points of difference from it. What is characteristically human is not that in which the human race merges with animal activity, but that in which the human race is distinguished from animal activity.

Now then; the animal is a constant point of power and acquisition; the beast — which is, as Schopenhauer says, a will that has not become conscious of itself, that is, a will that does not know that it wills, that does not even know what it wills; an unconscious will that goes about with one constant, fundamental organic mission: to acquire, to nourish itself, to reproduce. To what end? In order to reproduce, acquiring and nourishing. To what end? In order to nourish itself, reproducing and acquiring.

When it comes to man, things are different. Our obligation consists in being good animals, but not in order to confine ourselves within the vicious circle of animality — rather, to take that characteristic as the basis and support of an activity which our consciousness declares to us to be nobler and better. That is, in the scale of values we possess, we place it at a higher grade, and the other, animal activity, at an inferior grade.

Man is endowed with thought. To think — as you have probably studied in some of the nineteenth-century philosophers that your masters teach you in the history of philosophy course — to think, is also an economic form of arranging reality.

When I say that in the auditorium of the University there are men gathered, I have spoken economically: I refer to all the beings who populate this space. Yet each one enjoys a characteristic individuality; I could not have described in any time the beings present in the room had I not said: there are men. But in reality, what there is is Doctor So-and-So, Miss Such-and-Such, Gentleman X or Y. These are those who truly surround me — and in that I take great pride — and I with them, who likewise possess my soul and my singular and genuine individuality; yet I, nonetheless, have made a synthesis, saying that there are men in the great hall.

A genus, a general idea, is always a synthesis of certain attributes that beings possess — leaving outside its scope other attributes that those beings also possess, and to which other general words may refer.

With the least possible effort, thought achieves the greatest stable profit; with the smallest number of ideas we endeavor to think the greatest number of things. The progress of science has occurred when the more numerous and less broad laws have come to be reduced to the fewer and broader laws. At last comes a moment when — if human thought is capable of so much — it manages to reduce a province of knowledge to a single generalization. The other generalizations and the other laws are then conceived as particular cases or derivations of the fundamental law.

The man educated intellectually — solely intellectually, solely for pure thought — is a profound egotist. To think, to think is the most extraordinary, most lucid, and most excellent form of egoism, because intelligence is the finest weapon that the Creator has given to living beings.

Intelligence is the finest manner of realizing the desiderata of life, the way of achieving life's purposes most exactly and most fully. If someone were to place me before a menagerie and ask me to choose what I desire — "Do you want the lion's claws? Do you want the agility of this species? Do you want such and such an offensive or defensive weapon of such and such an order of animal?" — I would answer: I prefer this weapon, intelligence, which sees before seeing, which hears before hearing, which anticipates reality and constructs it in great syntheses of ideas and thereby makes it more practical and more useful.

Consequently, the individual educated for pure intelligence, for pure reason, is a purely rational egotist; he will be constantly striving to encompass the greatest number of things with the smallest number of thoughts, and will achieve great success in action; but it does not seem to us, in our consciousness, that he is the type of complete man — for alongside the beast and the pure subject of knowledge there exist other gifts in the human soul that define what is proper and exclusively human.

What are these other gifts? Man is capable of aesthetic contemplation and aesthetic intuition. In the world as economy, everything that exists is a motive of acquisition and power; in the world as intuition, the universe is not something we wish to enjoy, not something we are about to use, not something on which we mean to impose our own seal — rather, we throw wide open the doors of our spirit and allow the world to people it.

When the sea is agitated, when the wind strongly stirs it, the beings cannot be reflected on its surface; the image of those beings on the water's surface is always false, distorted by the very movement of the wave. When the water is calm, it copies and repaints reality within its depths. So too with human consciousness: when man is agitated by ideas, when he is in the process of attaining something, he does not see where he is, does not know where he finds himself; he goes with his purpose: I shall achieve this, I shall seek such another thing. And there are many important things in the world, but he pays no heed; he goes in pursuit of the things that matter to him; the world then grows dark around him, the world passes at his side winking at him, but he is not disposed to see the signals the Universe is sending; the only images he manages to obtain are distorted images of reality. The consciousness of the artist, on the other hand, reflects a vision — but in a form such that it is no longer the Universe desirable for use, but for sight. We no longer wish to dominate it, to conquer it, to incorporate it into our personality. When we seek to enjoy it with the aim of imposing our seal on it, it rebels against us — hence the difficulties encountered in industry — whereas when the Universe offers itself to us as an object of contemplation, we are its friends, and it comes solicitously to our consciousness and is realized in works of art — be they called a sonata, a celebrated canvas, an immortal poem. But at bottom, what has occurred is that the world has been duplicated in human consciousness and has been combined in the imagination of man, modifying its attributes in the artist's conception.

This is a great good that man possesses; this is another great pleasure the spirit enjoys: the pleasure of hearing for the sake of hearing, of seeing for the sake of seeing — which Kant called disinterested pleasure, purposiveness without purpose, in which he perceived the ultimate object of art. The end was the disinterestedness of the human spirit before life.

If we are to form men, we shall make them, then, good animals, vigorous animals — but not with the purpose of enclosing or cloistering them in the less noble part of their being: we shall make them thus so that they may serve as the support for other activities of the spirit.

An animal plays because it has sufficient energies to burn or spend them in the activity of play, having already satisfied its other essential activities. That is why the higher animals play. Well, man has another way of playing — a transcendent way of playing, an extraordinary way of playing — which cannot be reduced to mere play, as the positivist philosophers have wished, because that surplus, that excess, that overflow of animal activity constitutes in man an energy sufficient to spend on other activities besides those proper to the beast.

Then, that activity is the seat of other superior activities: the activity of thought and the disinterested activity of art.

If we wish to form complete men, we shall make, in addition to vigorous and energetic men, in addition to good human animals, good thinkers out of these good human animals, diligent thinkers.

Thinking is a duty. If you believe that not all of you can think at a high level, it is because you do not exercise yourselves in thinking. An energetic will, disposed to make the spirit more intelligent and more agile, can achieve all things.

If we apply ourselves to thinking constantly, we shall think better. And who among you does not know the person who in school seemed to be worth nothing for life, and who, through exemplary tenacity, has managed to develop his own spirit and to be as intelligent — or to appear as intelligent — as the most intelligent of his school companions?

It is indispensable to work constantly toward the realization of man in the two fundamental activities already defined. But another characteristic is lacking. Man is also a being who sacrifices himself, an extraordinary being who, at a given moment, feels greater pleasure in the activity that causes him the greatest pain, yet benefits his fellow.

There is no one who has not at some time felt — however great an egotist he may be — there is no one who has not on some occasion felt this impulse to give, this desire to realize himself as selflessness, as providence.

We have all at some time had the impression that our perennial activity of acquisition is not the only one that constitutes the foundation of our reality, and we have asked, as the philosopher does, for hands that extend toward us so that we may fill them with good.

When man considers that the world is as if a gift; and places himself in tune with the infinite; and in the depths of his consciousness realizes that the life that flows in this instant is a present he has received from some unknown hand; when we do not consider ourselves the owners of our own activity but think ourselves capable of receiving in this good, this grace of being — for in the depths there is no necessary reason for each one of us to exist; when we see that the very life we spend and carry, more or less painfully, upon the world may be considered a gift — then we can also imagine that we are the constant givers to those who surround us.

The pleasure of giving is a great pleasure; it is a pleasure that an egotist might compare to the pleasure of receiving. Life is always asking; life says: give me, give me of this, give me of that, give me constantly; and goodness constantly says: take, take more, take more. Life asks, and goodness gives; life has not wearied of asking, but I do not yet recall a fact that demonstrates to me that goodness has wearied of giving. There have always been good people in the world; there will always be. Evil is no argument against goodness; egoism is no argument against charity; if there is the covetous one who constantly asks, there is also the charitable one who constantly offers. And reflect: which pleasure is greater — the pleasure of acquiring, or the pleasure that consists in overcoming all the resistances of all the cosmic laws and thereby producing individuality in all its forces and in all its energy, giving without expecting to receive benefits, giving for the sake of giving, with the purpose of educating, with the purpose of serving as providence to one's fellow — that is, with no profit for oneself, with no reason relating to one's own individuality?

The most individual and most personal of all beings would be the one who acts without subjection to any of the laws that determine activity in one direction or another. For if I act in conformity with a law, I am not autonomous but heteronomous — I subordinate myself to the very principle, I am an instance of a generalization, I am a moment in an already foreseen succession; whereas if I act in opposition to all other realizations, I am then something original — so original that I obey no principle, that I depart from myself. The autonomy of the will, the fullness of personality, the august and human personality, is attained by man at the moment when he overcomes all the suggestions of reason — which says: be prudent, do not give what is yours, to give it is foolish — and yet answers: I give, because there is a great pleasure in giving; because no reason would convince me that I ought not in this moment to be generous and serve my fellow.

Reason, prudence, discretion, measure, proportion, economy — small reasons that for a moment vanquish another, superior reason, a supreme reason — and above all those small reasons, which are small passions, is realized the great reason of giving for the sake of giving, of integrating for the sake of integrating; and in that very instant one believes, and in that very instant one hopes.

The activity of giving engenders in the soul the conviction that one is strong enough to perform it, and the hope that one will be constantly capable of performing an act one has performed once. This is what is characteristic of man; here is a man; a man is one who knows how to give under these conditions. If we wish, then, to make men in the schools — returning to the point of this discourse — let us form individual souls, let us make good animals, let us eugenicize the races, let us make them beautiful and fit for action; but at the same time let us instill in them this subtle egoism of thinking, this incomparable pleasure of seeing, of contemplating, of hearing, this magnificent and sui generis activity of giving for the sake of giving — which has a classical and Christian name. Let us make man charitable; let us make him an artist; let us oblige him to be intelligent, ever more intelligent in his action in the things of the world and of the school — and then we shall have fulfilled the ends of education, having integrated souls powerful in themselves, which in the various emergencies of life will know how to realize human action in the proportion and measure in which that action must be realized in order to secure happiness, or at least — since not happiness — the opportunity for the action of a man truly worthy of that name.

Once Napoleon and Goethe found themselves face to face. Napoleon had this single laudatory expression for the incomparable German poet: "Monsieur Goethe: you are a man [Vous êtes un homme]."

I would wish that upon completing their labors as educators, each of you might say to each one of those you have educated — and have reason to utter Napoleon's words: "My young lady, or my young gentleman: I have formed you; you truly deserve this fine name: you are a man."

## LA FILOSOFIA FRANCESA CONTEMPORANEA<sup>∗</sup> — CONTEMPORARY FRENCH PHILOSOPHY<sup>∗</sup>

<sup>∗</sup> Lecture delivered under the auspices of the "Alliance Française."

Ladies,

Gentlemen:

The object of this lecture is, as you will have read, contemporary French philosophy. In so vast and complex a subject, the lecturer will select only some of the characteristic attributes of French thought — those most revelatory, perhaps, of something more important than all philosophies — always hypothetical, in the profound words of the skeptic — namely, the moral character of a great people, which inspired in one of its most illustrious minds this maxim: "there is beneath the sky but one thing before which one ought to bow, genius; and but one thing before which one ought to kneel, goodness" [il n'y a sous le ciel qu'une chose devant laquelle on doive s'incliner, le génie; et qu'une chose devant laquelle on doive s'agenouiller, la bonté]. Contemporary French philos-

-ophy is precisely genius put at the service of virtue.

A great anonymous poet declared in a celebrated Spanish poem — worthy of occupying a prominent place in the Universal Anthology — that the moral law consists in aligning life with thought. The noble hendecasyllable says, word for word:

Match thought to life. [Iguala con la vida el pensamiento.]

Would that we might be convinced, at the close of this lecture, that France today endeavors to fulfill the moral dictum, and does in fact match thought to life!

France possesses a French philosophy — that is, a philosophical tradition of its own, vernacular. Philosophy in France is not the same thing as French philosophy. Very few peoples could truthfully declare of themselves that they have engendered their own genuine and vernacular philosophy. In all nations, almost as soon as they reach the degree of development at which the intellect's meditation upon its own fruits becomes inevitable, philosophical manifestations arise; but very few peoples have managed to elaborate their own philosophy — a direct reflection of their genius, an ideal prolongation of their personality.

France could affirm without boasting that it possesses not merely a metaphysics elaborated on its own soil and in French, but a French philosophy — that is, a tradition, several centuries old, of great thinkers who, through the individual divergences that enrich and nuance the common fund, reproduce the collective individuality, the soul of the race.

When men pool their lives and their longings, their incoercible feelings of justice, their designs of freedom, the ineffable prodigy is little by little engendered. In the collective soul of the people, the generations are continued, the heroisms are unified, the hopes become a single hope. The miracle then arises. The multanimous being acquires its own relief.

It becomes unmistakable, most real — as real as a human person. And philosophy, art, religion, and customs then reflect the single spirit that engendered them. The character of French thought consists in its innate spiritualism and its moral idealism.

Descartes, Pascal, Malebranche, Rousseau, Maine de Biran, Cousin, Ravaisson, Vacherot, Renouvier, Lachelier, Boutroux, Bergson... — the names of the most illustrious philosophers of France are also the names of the champions of systematic spiritualism. From René Descartes — whom the English and Germans alike, Hegel no less than Huxley, called the father of modern philosophy, a spiritualist defender of the difference that separates matter, whose essence lies in extension, from the spirit symbolized in thought — down to Henri Bergson, the most profound intellect of the contemporary age, the most original champion of the theses of spiritualism against the positivist and materialist denials of the philosophy that prevailed in the most cultured nations of Europe in the final years of the last century — the French genius has always been able to inspire some great defender of the vernacular, ethical, and metaphysical tradition.

Even in the times when the tide of materialism was greatest and most powerful, France produced magnificent specimens of systematic spiritualists. In the eighteenth century, when the prevailing philosophy was the sensualism of Condillac and the classical materialism of Helvétius and Diderot, France gave to posterity a great solitary spiritualist — one of Carlyle's heroes, a precursor of the new literary schools, a rebel against the atheistic conception of the Encyclopédie, a romantic and a mystic — Jean-Jacques Rousseau, whose work has never ceased to bear fruit. His Émile created modern pedagogy; his Confessions foreshadowed all the subsequent literary elaboration of De Maistre, Chateaubriand, and Lamartine, the individualist poetry of the early years of the last century; while the eloquent and utopian dialectic of the Social Contract demolished the crumbling edifice of the Ancien Régime and formulated the militant and triumphant theory of political liberty in the apocalyptic assemblies of the Revolution.

Political liberty, grounded in respect for individuality and human personality, is based on an immediate datum of consciousness — on the affirmation of metaphysical freedom, which in God is creation and in man is free will. French philosophy, throughout the last century and what has elapsed of the present, is the systematic defense of liberty.

In his book entitled Science and Consciousness [La Science et la Conscience], published in the year 1870 — retain the memorable date — Vacherot formulated, in these clear and concise terms, the metaphysical problem of liberty: "All the moral sciences are at this moment undergoing a crisis whose characteristic sign may be summed up in this formula: the antinomy between the theories of science and the principles of consciousness. None escapes this contradiction — history no more than psychology, aesthetics no more than metaphysics, morality no more than politics. If liberty follows from the teachings of consciousness, then determinism, which suppresses it, is the conclusion of all the explanations of science" [Toutes les sciences morales subissent en ce moment une crise dont le signe caractéristique peut se résumer dans cette formule; antinomie des théories de la science et des principes de la conscience. Nulle n'échappe à cette contradiction, l'histoire pas plus que la psychologie, l'esthétique pas plus que la métaphysique, la moral pas plus que la politique. Si la liberté ressort des enseignements de la conscience, le déterminisme qui la supprime est la conclusion de toutes les explications de la science].

Long has been the dramatic secular struggle between the partisans of determinism and the adherents of human free will. It fills the entire history of philosophy, beginning with the speculations of the Stoics — the first determinists conscious of their own systematic determinism.

Liberty has on its side, for its eternal defense, as Vacherot says, the testimony of consciousness. No man ceases to think that he is free to act or not to act, according to his own singular will. But determinism shields itself with the perpetual testimony of reason, which says: a free act is an act without a cause — that is, an impossibility. Metaphysical liberty, impossible for reason and evident for consciousness — is it real or is it not? Here is the tremendous philosophical interrogation, one of the most pressing puzzles for the philosophical thought of humanity.

Reason affirms that each being is bound to all others by an infinite dialectical relation; so at least the Hegelians maintained. All things refer to the Whole, along the threads of a diamond net which makes all of existence, as Schopenhauer ironically said, a crystallized syllogism.

You will understand that in a world thus bound together, man can be nothing but a transitory, ephemeral phenomenon. In this world as monotonous as a yawn, as Tarde said, there is no room for liberty. The individual disappears as an insignificant accident — becomes a vain shadow, less than that even. Only movement and its iron discipline, perpetual transformation, are real.

With personality suppressed, individuality denied, metaphysical liberty eliminated without remedy, right is suppressed, moral responsibility ends — and, in the image of the great autocratic empire of the universe, human society organizes itself autocratically. The State is then the Leviathan of Hobbes, the heavy Prussian machine that constrains liberties and peoples.

The determinist says: the liberty affirmed by consciousness is an illusion born of ignorance. Already the great philosopher Spinoza, father and master of all German philosophy, taught: "If the stones that, obedient to gravity, come rolling down the slope of a mountain, had consciousness, they would think: we fall because we wish to fall." Ignorance of the cause produces the illusion of believing it possible to suppress that cause. If we knew the causes of the complicated problems of history, we would see how no man has ever acted freely — how heroes, saints, apostles, and martyrs have been, like Spinoza's stones, objects obedient to the eternal necessity that governs with an iron hand all that exists, the star no less than the saint, the body no less than the soul. All things bind together by virtue of perennial reasons, by the inflexible laws of nature.

Contemporary French philosophy has understood that every attempt to demonstrate or define metaphysical liberty must be fruitless, because one demonstrates by reasoning, and reason is essentially deterministic. Bergson interprets, against rationalist determinism, the immediate data of consciousness [les données immédiates de la conscience]. He departs from the spiritual foundation rather than from the external data provided by the sciences. He resembles Socrates. Like Socrates, like Descartes, like Kant, like all the great renewers of philosophical thought, he penetrates to the depths of consciousness, takes the oracle's "know thyself" [nosce te ipsum] as the theme of his meditations — because, in the end, it is not the world that poses the problem of discovering what man is, but man who poses the problem of discovering what the world is. The common denominator of science, the treasury of metaphysical invention, we carry within our own spirit. We must constantly Socratize, questioning our memory, our consciousness, digging into the psychic recesses, in order to emerge afterward into the world as agile divers emerge from the sea with hands brimming with pearls.

This is Bergson's procedure for vindicating free will. Hear what the philosopher teaches: "Every request for clarification, where liberty is concerned, reduces unawares to the following question: can time be adequately represented by space? To which we answer: yes, if it is a question of elapsed time; no, if you speak of time as it is elapsing, and not of time already elapsed. Freedom is therefore a fact, and among the facts one may observe there is none more clear. All the difficulties of the problem, and the problem itself, arise from the desire to find in duration the same attributes as in extension, to interpret a succession by a simultaneity, and to express the idea of freedom in a language where it is evidently untranslatable" [Toute demande d'éclaircissement, en ce qui concerne la liberté, revient sans qu'on s'en doute à la question suivante: Le temps peut-il se représenter adéquatement par de l'espace? — À quoi nous répondons: oui, s'il s'agit du temps écoulé; non, si vous parlez du temps qui s'écoule, et non pas dans le temps écoulé. La liberté est donc un fait, et parmi les faits que l'on constate il n'en est de plus clair. Toutes les difficultés du problème, et le problème lui-même, naissent de ce qu'on veut trouver à la durée les mêmes attributs qu'a l'étendue, interpréter une succession par une simultanéité, et rendre l'idée de liberté dans une langue où elle est évidemment intraduisible].<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness [Essai sur les Données immédiates de la Conscience].

When we reason and speak, we attempt to turn into space what is time; and that is why we do not understand liberty. Free will is pure time; but unconsciously we project pure time — real duration — unconsciously we spatialize, by virtue of the tradition of life — "first is to live" [primum est vivere] — which moves constantly in the direction of satisfying needs. If we return to ourselves, everything becomes clear. There is then nothing to translate, because the clearest language is the interior language, the music of the soul, the testimony of consciousness. The problem has disappeared.

Reason must acknowledge that by itself it founds nothing. It must be grounded in evidence, in the intuition that gives it its point of departure. Reason is like a machine. If you remove from a machine the raw material it works upon, it becomes useless — it moves comically in a void, however perfectly constructed and disposed for productive movement it may be. So too with reason. Its raw material is consciousness, which offers it its postulates, its intimate and supreme reasons — just as steam provides the machine with its motive force. And intuition says to each one: "you are free when you act with your whole personality. Within you resides an essential principle of existence. You resemble God, who creates. He made the world freely, by the omnipotence of his will. You perform good through the nobility of your heart. Nothing could ever obscure this truth for you."

Contemporary French philosophy has proved that the laws of Nature are not so completely objective as German determinism had supposed. M. Émile Boutroux — "one of the most enlightened intellects in Europe," as his Spanish translator, the philosopher Diego Ruiz, declares — has managed to demon-

-strate that logical necessity is lamentably confused with scientific determinism, and that the more complex and determined a thing is, the less it may be called necessary, to the point that mere necessity, absolute identity, the principle A is A, determines nothing — because nothing, not even in syllogistic logic itself, can be applied to it without alteration.

Boutroux asks himself in what sense and to what extent the natural laws are intelligible, whether they are the principles of things or merely the symbols of reality, whether determinism represents no more than a simple rational concatenation or is intrinsic to existence; and he resolves that the uniformities of nature are methods found by man for adapting things to his intelligence, but that they reveal to us only that the character of necessity attributed to the world resides in the essence of human reason, while existence in itself is infinitely more complex and varied, infinitely more fertile and contingent.

If the contingency of natural laws is true, there remains a foundation of existence for the Spirit and the Good, while at the same time their efficacy in the material world is assured: "In the beginning, man saw everywhere only caprice and arbitrariness. As a result, the freedom he attributed to himself had no grip on anything. Modern science made him see law everywhere, and he thought he saw his freedom sink into universal determinism. But a just notion of natural laws restores to him possession of himself, while at the same time showing him that his freedom can be efficacious and can direct phenomena. Of external and internal things, the latter alone depend on us, said Epictetus; and he was right in his time. The mechanical laws of nature, revealed by modern science, are the chain that links the outer to the inner. Far from being a necessity, they set us free and allow us to add an active science to the contemplation in which the ancients had confined themselves" [A l'origine, l'homme ne voyait partout que caprice et arbitraire. Par suite, la liberté qu'il s'attribuait n'avait de prise sur rien. La science moderne lui fit voir partout la loi, et il crut voir sa liberté s'abîmer dans le déterminisme universel. Mais une juste notion des lois naturelles lui rend la possession de lui-même, en même temps qu'elle lui montre que sa liberté peut être eficace et diriger les phénoménes. Des choses extérieures et intérieures, les secondes seules dépendent de nous, disait Epictete; et il avait raison au temps où il parlait. Les lois mécaniques de la nature, révélées par la science moderne, sont la chaîne qui lie le dehors au dedans. Loin d'être une nécessité elles nous affranchissent, et nous permettent d'ajouter une science active á la contemplation où les anciens s'étaient renfermés].<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> The Idea of Natural Law in Contemporary Science and Philosophy [L'idée de Loi naturelle dans la Science et la Philosophie contemporaines].

In sum, Boutroux and Bergson have together elaborated — through the logical analysis of science and the metaphysical intuition of what is properly spiritual — the finest demonstration of the cosmological possibility and the psychological reality of human free will.

Reason accounts only for the world that has already been made, for the reality that was, for the past. Consciousness, intuition, is capable of witnessing the constant and present formation of reality. We believe in freedom because we witness in our own inner forum the creation of reality, because we are collaborators of God, and we know it. Our reason, by itself, does not know this, because, in order to begin its lucubrations, it requires that reality should have been made, that it should already have crystallized. For this reason — which reason does not know — consciousness will always say to man: "you are free." And ignorant reason will always say: "do not believe it."

Germany and its philosophers denied psychological freedom. Kant affirmed it, but as an impenetrable mystery — which is, in the end, another ingenious way of denying it. And perhaps that is why the German people constrict themselves within the iron determinism of Prussian discipline. Determinist philosophy and a constraining military regime perhaps obey the same genius of the race. Since free will is judged an illusion, the individual and personality disappear entirely from the organization of the German State: might takes precedence over right [la force prime le droit].

Belgium lived free and happy, strengthening its individuality, developing its own culture, deploying its energies, fortifying its intellectual and moral personality. It was a small people defended by international law, sustained by the European balance of power. Belgium was fulfilling its destiny in the bosom of European peace, which seemed unalterable. A creation of human right, it was a living example of the morality of the world — like Switzerland, like Holland, like Denmark — peoples born of justice sanctioned by civilization, small and strong, more cultured than the great military powers surrounding them. Belgium had faith in itself. Rich, industrious, intelligent, religious, attached to its tradition, confident in its future, it was fulfilling its destiny, blessed of God.

But Germany, as Hasse writes in his Weltpolitik, "needed lands even when they were inhabited by foreigners, in order to determine its future according to German needs." The Emperor said in his patriotic speeches: "The world shall see what Germany is" — and the politicians, the priests, the thinkers, the artists, and the apostles of Pan-Germanism placed themselves at the service of the imperial orator, and they too spoke of the predestination of Germany, of the superior and terrible race, of the fair-haired dolichocephalics who represent the future of humanity, of the agile and powerful supermen, unscrupulous and violent, anti-Christian... Belgium saw the armies of the Caesar arrive in the guise of war — bombarding universities as they laid siege to fortresses and subjugated opulent cities. The historical relics were covered with new and immortal scars; the dead cities — as a dreamy and subtle Belgian poet, Rodenbach, had so beautifully named them — suffered the consternation of war; and the king and queen, the people and the primate bishop of Flanders, were deposed from their thrones, their houses, their episcopal palaces. Barbarossa resurrected on his return from the Crusade. The heroic legend of the thirteenth century was fulfilled miraculously in the twentieth. The Emperor was going on Crusade again — not against unbelievers holding the Holy Sepulcher, but against most Christian Flemish burghers. One day Germany grew hungry, and Belgium was immolated — but around the Homeric citadel of Liège the dragon sowed his teeth.

Everything had been foreseen by Bismarck's nation in order to humiliate France. Its cannons were the most powerful, its aircraft the swiftest, its submarines supreme, its generals most sapient, its politicians as utilitarian and rational as could be. Germany had done everything on its part that reason counsels. Victory had been calculated geometrically.

But heroism — this intuition of consciousness, this intimate datum that reason does not understand, this apotheosis of human liberty — placed God on the side of the armies of France; and the unforeseen element of history, the genius of Joffre, defeated the enemy hosts between the strongholds of Paris and Verdun. It is as Hamlet says to Horatio: "there are more things in the world than are dreamt of in philosophy" — above all, in Germanic philosophy. It is that the will to sacrifice overcomes all things, and would be capable, for example, of proving the efficacy of French thought by scattering the armies of the great Empire of the Hohenzollerns in a miracle of heroism and faith.

The philosophy of imperialist determinism, stripped of mystical disguises and sublime rhetoric, is the apotheosis of life without right, of power without moral purpose. Nietzsche formulated it for all time in his Critical Essay on Christian Belief. It reads as follows:

"What is good? — Everything that heightens in man the feeling of power, the will to power, power itself." "What is bad? — Everything that proceeds from weakness." "What is happiness? — The feeling by which power grows, by which a resistance is overcome."

"Not contentment, but more power; not peace above all, but war; not virtue, but valor (virtue in the sense of the Renaissance; virtù, virtue stripped of prudishness)."

"Let the weak and the failures perish: the first principle of our love of man. And one must also help them to disappear."

But one must ask, as Socrates asked the sophists: who is the strong one? The just man or the covetous, the charitable one who gives or the egotist who always asks or wants more; Belgium or Germany; Our Lord Jesus Christ or Nietzsche.

The strong one is he who has right on his side, because — as Rousseau profoundly observed — he who has power will tomorrow be overcome by another more powerful, and he who has justice on his side has it for eternity. No one will be stronger than he, for there are not two justices. He is master of the kingdom of heaven, according to the evangelical beatitude. How profound is the strength of the just man before the greed of the covetous!

Let the sentence of the great German sophist be modified, then. Let it read: not contentment, but more eating. This is less sublime, but it is the truth.

Words — those subtle symbolic beings of an eminently social nature — frequently change their meaning as they are polished and mingle among themselves, like the pebbles of rivers in their bed. Thus words are transformed ideologically and imply their new signification within the impetuous current of social life, and sometimes, with resolute and firm movement, they invert their ancient sign. What was subjective, for example, for the medieval doctors, was the subject or matter of knowledge. What is subjective for the moderns is the spirit and its forms, the interior world, whether logical or fantastic. The inversion of meaning has in this case been absolute.

Something similar befell that illustrious word idealism, which for the common run of minds signifies the innate tendency toward the immaterial in life, the effort to bend things to our designs, the movement of disgust at an imperfect reality condemnable on more than one count. In sum, a systematic longing for reform (new form); a preference always accorded to the idea over matter. But for the modern Germanic philosophers, the ideal is identical to the real. Hegel was the author of the tremendous transmutation of the values of that word, when he formulated his celebrated apothegm, pregnant with dangerous metaphysical and moral corollaries: all that is real is rational, all that is rational is real.

One can then easily conceive what the attitude of a people will be when it has convinced itself that the ideal is immanent in the things of the world. It will believe that history, by itself, is gradually accomplishing the triumph of the good. That what occurs fulfills itself in the service of God, necessarily, fatally, as the premises of a syllogism are linked together by their own intrinsic logical necessity. Crimes and virtues, evils and goods, errors and truths — all is, at bottom, virtue or preparation for virtue, good or preparation for the good, truth or element of truth. A Hegelian will say, like Schelling, Hegel's master: all is one and the same. A German will say, interpreting Hegel and Nietzsche: Germany above all, because Germany is strong and Belgium is not. Germany above all, because force is reason and reason is force; because the ideal is the real taking consciousness of its reality and projecting itself upon the future. Here, in its most chilling form, is the transmutation of intellectual and moral values, the formula of German idealism.

France professes another idealism, less dialectical, less inhuman; but truer above all, and more moral — far more so. How different the cause of the French Revolution from the cause of Germanic Imperialism! How diverse and contrary the moral purposes of the mystics of force and the theorists of bourgeois emancipation!

Today men fight from hunger, from an immoderate desire for domination, for military hegemony, for the preponderance of races, for the acquisition of markets, for the primacy of violence over liberty. Then they fought to strip the privileged of their privileges, to overthrow tyrants, to impose with noble spirit broadly human ideals — those which, had they not been fulfilled at that moment, would have triumphed in the end, in spite of negative causes, in despite of all contrary conditions. For they were necessary ideals, capable of overcoming the contingencies of history by the mere passage of time and by virtue of their own intrinsic worth.

Bonaparte fought for something eternal. Wilhelm II fights for something ephemeral, as ephemeral as his own existence. Power, success, triumph are things that each instant raises and each instant also casts down. Every hegemony that establishes itself begins forthwith to succumb. Had France disappeared in the Napoleonic odyssey, the eagles of Caesar, wing-clipped and battered, would have fallen upon the imperial disaster, proclaiming the purity of their intention. The eagles of Prussia would fly over their impossible victory, possessing nothing more than the shame of extermination and the misery of power.

France is the creator of great moral movements conceived to impose upon human matter, upon history, the incoercible mold of the idea. France has faith in metaphysical free will [libre albedrío], in the effective, social, Christian collaboration of man with God. The world is not, for her, an immanent idealism, but an object submissive to the will [voluntad] that realizes the ideal. Not many days ago, M. René Viviani, on a solemn occasion, exclaimed: "We have risen for justice and for justice alone. We shall remain standing for justice; we shall remain standing to avenge our wounded, our orphans, our sick, our dead. From the depths of their sepulchres, where an eternal patience nonetheless dwells, these dead would start with alarm if France were to make some premature gesture that let fall beside her the sword that was drawn from its scabbard under aggression, and drawn solely to defend the right."

In the Middle Ages France was the Soldier of God. A people of heroic crusaders who fought without respite to reclaim the empty sepulchre of Jesus Christ. In the eighteenth century it created the Revolution. In the twentieth it imposed upon the invading Germanism — we may speak in the past tense already, without fear of error or risk of being judged as boasting of prophecy — its natural limits, raising above the astonishment of the earth the citadel of Verdun. The old coveted river, the River of Legend, reviews at this moment its French grammar, which it had forgotten since 1870, as Heine would say.

Conception for conception, force for force, virtue for virtue — and let human consciousness decide in the historical conflict between the heroic idealism of Peter the Hermit, Joan of Arc, and Rousseau, and the dialectical idealism of Hegel, Bismarck, and Wilhelm II.

Let us believe in the unforeseeable quality of the human heart, in heroism, in metaphysical liberty, in the intimate data [datos íntimos] of consciousness [conciencia]. "La justesse dans l'esprit," said Hugo, "devient la justice dans le cœur" [Soundness in the mind becomes justice in the heart]. France has equaled its thought with its life. It has been worthy of its philosophy, and worthy, too, of the enthusiasm of History.

## EL CONCEPTO DE LEY NATURAL.<sup>∗</sup> — THE CONCEPT OF NATURAL LAW<sup>∗</sup>

<sup>∗</sup> Introduction to the Castilian version of the celebrated book by M. Émile Boutroux.

M. Émile Boutroux is one of the great representatives of contemporary thought, one of its classics. His work, of broad scientific and philosophical learning, is eclectic not in its fundamental inspiration (no profound work has ever been, essentially, eclectic); but rather in its perspicacious historical sense, in its universal character, in its tendency toward an alliance between empiricism and idealism, between Religion and Science — a work that does not combine diverse data and values by sustaining the sacrifice or diminution of some in order to exalt others easily but fruitlessly, but rather strives to achieve a true synthesis: that is to say, one capable of uniting, within a superior construction, what the spirit [espíritu] engenders as related within itself (however little this may appear so without the powerful intellectual effort that explains such congruence and unity); his work, at once historical and philosophical, reflects the idea-forces of the contemporary spirit [espíritu]; and in De l'idée de loi naturelle and De la Contingence des lois de la nature, books already classical for the energy of their thought and their ideological precision, as are the books of the great thinkers of the seventeenth century, it rises to the rank of an author of capital works belonging to a period in the evolution of philosophy through history.

Taine and Renan, the masters of French thought in its most universally known and admired forms or aspects, were certainly not, even in the time when they flourished, the most original philosophers of France, however much the intellectual public of Spanish America may believe otherwise. Taine with his empirical idealism derived from Hegel and Comte; Renan, not very important on the whole as a synthetic philosopher, however important he may have been in the history of French and human letters — these two literary thinkers so widely read and admired among us do not surely represent the effort of speculative construction that forms the glory of the French spirit [espíritu]. There stands, for example, beside the brilliant labors of Taine and Renan, the imposing synthesis of the great Renouvier, as William James calls it — a labor of pure philosophy, disdainful of easy and ephemeral applause, independent and severe, pursued without respite for nearly a century and developed around the central idea of liberty. There stands the probabilism of Augustin Cournot, Comte refined and condensed, as Tarde said — a most subtle spirit profoundly unknown to Latin American philosophical culture. There, too, remains Comte himself, the greatest realist philosopher of the nineteenth century, as Rudolf Eucken expresses it — studied through his commentators, known through his impugners, extolled or distorted by fanatical devotees or bitter enemies, but little read, in sum.

The spiritualist tradition of Maine de Biran, Ravaisson, Lachelier, Boutroux, and Bergson, which forms the fundamental originality of French thought in the last century and the present, is nevertheless the philosophical current most urgently in need of dissemination in certain Spanish-speaking peoples of America who, like Mexico, have lived consecrated for many decades now to the assiduous practice of a kind of positivist scholasticism that some limited spirits judge to be the very expression of Science, the final end of humanity's philosophical elaboration.

The philosophy of Boutroux will inevitably clash against the tendency of those who have made of Science — or rather, of the sciences (without a capital letter and in the plural) — a new absolute, a new superstition, a new dogma which, by dint of being so extolled and distorted by its adherents, is already growing old. Science-as-ontology seems today a fraud unworthy of the critical spirit of the century.

For the majority of Hispanic American thinkers who do not profess Catholicism, the notions of personality, finality, and liberty are mere anthropomorphic phantoms, mere philosophical errors, idola theatri, as Bacon said. For Boutroux, finality and liberty are not, like scientific laws, contingent uniformities, but principles of the explanation of the universe; and such principles in no way contradict the conclusions of the sciences, but explain them by assigning their true relativity.

When projected upon the life of the spirit [espíritu], scientific determinism created a false metaphysics, a philosophy in which man and his works, far from being perceived in their characteristic individuality, were conceived as epiphenomena or developments of non-human forces. Such was, for example, the celebrated doctrine of milieu, race, and historical moment which, eloquently expounded by Taine, served so many Latin American positivist sociologists and politicians to derive from it pessimistic conclusions about the future of these peoples. According to its conclusions, the psychological element — what is properly human, personal, and free — was denied in the absolute affirmation of the physical and biological causes of social evolution.

Someone may think, perhaps, on reading these lines, that the illustrious author of La Contingence des Lois de la Nature perhaps intends a return to some antiquated and false anthropomorphism. This is not the case. "I admit," Boutroux has said, "<sup>1</sup> that the human spirit [espíritu], in the progress of its reflection, begins by judging things ex analogia hominis [after the analogy of man], and dogmatic philosophy then flourishes; next it judges them ex analogia universi [after the analogy of the universe], and Science then takes its turn; and finally, ex analogia hominis [after the analogy of man] once more, but now availing itself of the lessons of Science — and then the philosophy of contingency and of liberty begins."

<sup>1</sup> See: "Boutroux," by Paul Archambault — In the collection: "Les Grands Philosophes français et étrangers."

Anthropomorphism lies at the very root of every philosophical effort. Man — as Protagoras already said, as Plato notes in the Theaetetus — is the measure of all things, of those that exist as much as those that do not. Philosophy would never have begun to be without the initial anthropomorphic datum [dato]. But this anthropomorphism is dogmatic, as Boutroux says, whereas the anthropomorphism of the philosophy of contingency is critical. The intellectualist theory, by making pure reason and its speculative fruit, Science, the sole principle of explanation, far from clarifying the nature of things, engendered a monotonous and somber universe, the resonance of the eternal axiom, as Taine said — a false schematism of multiform, protean reality. The initial anthropomorphism is false; but determinism is also false. The world does not conform to the primitive intuition [intuición]; but neither does it fit within the frame of abstract intellectualism. Science is still more relative than the positivist philosophers thought,

than those think today who clumsily make an apotheosis of its conclusions and methods.

With reference to the philosophy of contingency, its adversary, the philosopher Alfred Fouillée, spoke wittily of the pulverization of the unknowable. It seemed to him that the irreducibility of the diverse natural orders, the impossibility of the universal transmutation of things, the critique of monism, ended, at last, in the apotheosis of ignorance, in the triumph of chance. Boutroux teaches that at the base of evolution, at the origin of history, there exists a true activity, like our own human activity; that the essence of the world is a psychological activity. Such is the result of Boutroux's critical anthropomorphism, the explanation of existence ex analogia hominis [after the analogy of man], as the philosopher says.

Every philosophical system is, strictly speaking, humanism. Intellectualist humanism if one prefers thinking to willing as the basis of explanation; anti-intellectualist, if one prefers willing to thinking; but always humanism. The materialist or naturalist systems are diverse forms of intellectualism — that is, preferences that opt for reason and its intrinsic determinism as the principle of explanation. Our century has founded its philosophical originality on this, its first truth: to prefer the will [voluntad] to the intelligence as the metaphysical principle of the explanation of the universe; that is, as the essential principle of psychological life; but without claiming to exclude intelligence from synthetic labor, without incurring in the limitations of the mystical thought peculiar to other centuries.

"Notre siècle," says Boutroux, "est las d'une philosophie qui pretend se suffire et se nourrir exclusivement de sa propre substance. Mais il appelle, en revanche, une philosophie cherchant dans l'expérience elle-même, dans le réel universellement admis comme tel, dans les sciences positives, dans la vie de l'individu et des sociétés, les éléments de ses réponses aux incoercibles questions de l'esprit humain: qu'est-ce que le monde? que sommes nous? comment devons-nous agir pour faire de notre mieux notre métier d'hommes?" [Our century is weary of a philosophy that pretends to be self-sufficient and to nourish itself exclusively from its own substance. But it calls, in return, for a philosophy seeking in experience itself, in the real universally admitted as such, in the positive sciences, in the life of the individual and of societies, the elements of its answers to the incoercible questions of the human spirit: what is the world? what are we? how must we act to do our best at the trade of being men?]<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Avant-Propos of the book: Les Grands courants de la pensée contemporaine by Rudolf Eucken.

Such is the philosophical attitude of Boutroux as declared by himself. The alliance of metaphysical speculation with the data of science and of moral and historical experience. A broadly human synthesis, an ordered meditation upon total experience. This is to philosophize within the spirit [espíritu] of the century, and not to cloister oneself sterility in pure psychological introspection or in the pure dialectical deduction of abstract concepts. To cite all witnesses before the philosophical consciousness [conciencia] and to make philosophy with all useful testimonies, orienting speculative reason toward life — such is the effort of the great contemporary thinkers. From this current of thought were born the two celebrated books: De la contingence des lois de la nature and De l'Idée de loi naturelle dans la science et la philosophie contemporaines.

We shall not follow the great philosopher into his speculative construction of Leibnizian and Aristotelian inspiration, developed especially in the fundamental book De la contingence des lois de la nature; but, confining ourselves simply to the critical effort carried out in the work whose translation we have the honor of offering to the public, we shall endeavor simply to offer a conception of the design which, in our opinion, presided over the elaboration of the thesis — a thesis clear, methodical, and conclusive, with that demonstrative clarity that forms the highest merit of the French mentality, the seal of its unmistakable personality, from Descartes to Boutroux.

What would the American positivists say — given as they generally are to thinking that positivism rejects all community of relation with metaphysical spiritualism — if they were assured that the famous serial, logical, historical, and didactic classification of the

abstract sciences, due to Comte, implies and expresses the contingency of natural laws?

For, despite the fact that some might regard the acceptance of the foregoing proposition as a subtly paradoxical affirmation, it may be affirmed — following in this not only M. Boutroux (who has studied Comte profoundly in a special course), but also the historian of modern philosophy Harald Höffding, who, in his book entitled Contemporary Philosophers, says: "The fundamental laws of the more concrete sciences cannot be reduced to those of the more abstract sciences. Every new empirical domain demands new and special principles not contained in those of the preceding domain. Boutroux thus finds himself in astonishing accord [étonnamment d'accord] with Comte, who vigorously accentuated the discontinuity of principles in passing from one science to another, and prevented the temptation of deriving from a more abstract science the principles of more concrete sciences."<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup> See page (89) of the French translation of the cited book.

The discontinuity, as Höffding says — the contingency, as Boutroux says — is unquestionable in passing from logic to mathematics, from mathematics to mechanics, to physics, to biology, to psychology, to sociology, according to the logical criterion of the celebrated classification of the abstract sciences of positivism.

Boutroux asks: In what sense and to what extent are natural laws intelligible? Do the differences of generality and complexity, of which Comte speaks, imply the operation of irreducible philosophical principles? Do laws form the substance of things? Are they, by chance, the elements or merely the symbols of reality? Is determinism a simple concatenation of things or does it constitute their intrinsic nature?

These profound perplexities — antinomies which, resolved in a purely intellectualist sense, make impossible the originality of spiritual life as concomitant with the world, by preventing its individuality and its liberty, and which, resolved according to the philosophy of contingency, bring thought into accord with will [voluntad], and Morality and Religion with Science — constitute the deep problem discussed in the pages of the book we are translating.

A great English philosopher has said that ideas which serve us to explain things, and which have no other purpose, are retained by us nonetheless when they fail to fulfill that purpose, even when they render things unintelligible to us; because the spirit [espíritu] of man seems more faithful to its preconceptions than to its noble destiny of elaborating knowledge. Thus determinism is judged necessary. It serves to understand a part of reality; within certain limits it is its symbol, as Boutroux says; but when it is opposed to the immediate reality of the consciousness [conciencia] that affirms in an irrefutable manner the unity, the liberty, and the human personality, we still declare it true, by reason of our economic, biological attitude of thinking what is diverse by reducing it to the simple. Formerly it explained the world to us; it was therefore philosophically admissible within its own sphere of action, which is matter. Now it conceals the world from us and, despite this, we declare it acceptable; because we prefer our intellectual convenience to the truth that escapes us through our own egoism.

If the contingency of natural laws is true, a foundation of possible existence remains for the Spirit [Espíritu] and the Good. If mathematical and physical laws are universal and necessary, there is not a crevice in the world for the Spirit [Espíritu] and Liberty; the Good becomes a contradiction in the face of economics, in the face of the utilitarianism of existence. Such is the moral and philosophical importance of this small and perfect book — "what is good, if brief, is twice good" [lo bueno si poco dos veces bueno], said Gracián — compact as a thesis by Leibniz or an essay by Hume.

May this translation, by diffusing the work in our intellectual milieu, serve to destroy in many a consciousness [conciencia] the empire of the rationalist superstition of determinism, establishing the profound distinction Boutroux desires between scientific determinism and logical necessity. May it also yield, as a philosophical corollary, a more disinterested morality and a less absurd politics than those proclaimed by our Mexican positivists in their books filled with disheartening distrust of the human will [voluntad] — that prepotent spring of the evolution of peoples and men, the supreme originality of existence.

## ANALISIS DE LA NOCION DE CAUSA. — ANALYSIS OF THE NOTION OF CAUSE

The thought of Spinoza possesses such force and congruence that, if one were to admit as evident the definitions and axioms from which he deduces his system with geometric rigor, it becomes impossible to impugn any of his conclusions. Substance is "that which is in itself and is conceived through itself"; or that which "requires no other thing in order to exist and no other notion in order to be conceived." Being and Cause of all that exists, it is in itself: the sole real existence. But what is this mystical Substance? That which is absolutely indeterminate and real — pure being — seems, contrary to what Spinoza says, to be precisely what requires all things in order to exist and all notions in order to be conceived. It is necessary, therefore, to discuss the principle set forth as indisputable and to explain it. What does Substance mean? Substance means being everything without being any of the individual existences, and being also the sole cause. In the idea of Substance Spinoza envelops the idea of Cause, comprehending both in one and the same definition. Now then: is it the same thing? It is necessary to distinguish the notion of cause from that of substance.

Cause in the sciences is a different thing from cause in Metaphysics. In the sciences, certainly, one speaks of "causes"; but the laws that condition facts by seeking "causes" refer to a mere concatenation of phenomena, of appearances — to a simple external relation between two facts discovered through the general method of concomitant variations. In an ontological sense, "cause" is something else. It is a matter of something more than a mere useful correlation of phenomena. It means something else, and indeed other things still; because the notion implies two diverse ideas, according to whether one speaks of efficient or final causality. Aristotle considered as many as four causes: the efficient (the artisan); the final (the design); the formal and the material (the work). We shall consider only two: the efficient and the final. Efficient cause implies a succession; it is a rigorous relation of antecedence of the cause with respect to the effect. The type of final causation is the psychological phenomena, the facts of consciousness [conciencia], whose fundamental characteristic is the tendency toward an end. We act from motives; the acts of our inner life are better explained by motives than by anything else. To investigate this species of causation is to investigate whether we are free.

It is necessary, therefore, to perform an analysis of the notion of cause, in order to distinguish it clearly from the notion of substance. Spinoza takes as resolved the problem that we here propose to ourselves, when he admits in the third definition of his Ethics the universal transmutation of everything into everything. "Substance is that which is in itself and is conceived through itself..." and "all that is, is in itself or in another thing," hence all things are one unique thing: Substance, which is the only thing that is. By this conception of the unity of substance Spinoza is the father of the fundamental idea that informs contemporary science. Joule, Helmholtz, Clausius seem to think that all forces are one Force; they believe in the transmutation of everything into everything, in the transformation of a unique thing which preserves itself identical to itself through all its variations, through all its modes, as Spinoza would say. The idea of the unity of substance lamentably confuses the notion of efficient cause with that of substance — that is, it confuses the concept of the permanence of energy with that of its unity. Now then: in the first place, a relation of causation is a relation of succession. Every causation is a succession; but not every succession is causal. The genus succession is vaster than the genus causation, because the succession of things does not necessarily imply a causal relation, though one may exist. In the second place, Spinoza admits that everything derives necessarily from one and the same thing, that what is everything transmutes itself through a rigorous causation into the infinite variety of distinct things. This transformation, which he admits without more ado, must be demonstrated. For him the things that appear to us original and diverse are the same thing; effects are identical to their causes, they are the cause itself transmuted into effect. In the relation of cause is thus contained the idea of the transformation of substance, so that not only does E follow C, but E is C. Is this true? Common sense tells us that the effect is diverse from its cause. It is not the cause. It is something new, distinct, original — it is the effect. If it were the same thing as the cause, there would be but one, and then it would be pointless to speak of effects. Hydrogen and Oxygen form water. They are the cause. Water is the effect. But water is a new thing, diverse from Hydrogen and Oxygen. It is water. It is a distinct and new being, with diverse properties and qualities, that quenches thirst, that is clear, that is liquid, that does not serve the purposes for which the bodies that composed it were employed. Two gases have been transformed into a liquid. It is water. How did this new and distinct being emerge from those two? Chemists do not know. They know that in such and such a way the effect can be produced; but they do not know what passes within the intimacy of the reaction. Will it be true that the cause passes intact into the effect and that the latter is nothing but a transformation of the former and, therefore, the same thing as it? Is everything one and the same? Will it be true that "one and the same manner of world is all"? If we apply Leibniz's theory of indiscernibles, we shall have to conclude that two things which are the same thing are one thing at two distinct moments of consciousness [conciencia]. There are no two things that are the same thing. Not even in Fichte's A is A. A is A, we say, and the predicate is already something diverse from the subject. If we admit that A is A (which is to admit that time does not act upon things), if we admit that A is A (which it is not, because A is always becoming B), we are already admitting two diverse things because A, as predicate, implies something else — the understanding that affirms A in two diverse positions, when it affirms A and when it affirms an A that is equal to A. Every effect is something discernible from its cause, something new in the universe. Those who do not agree with this confuse the notion of universal causation with that of the conservation of energy. They confuse two diverse things. If we affirm that all is the same, we affirm that Substance — which is what is everything, call it what one will — had to be C in order to be E. The universal transformation of substance implies the necessary, unconditional passage from cause to effect, the conversion of Cause into Effect, the absolute transformation of one thing into another. The definition of Spinoza to which we have referred is an enunciation of what a cause is in terms of substance. That something be the cause of something means that one unique thing which is cause makes itself into effect, and that in this transformation it does not differ from itself, but is itself. To be a cause then means not only to be a prior condition of something, but to be that very something itself. And the truth is that the notion of causation implies antecedence, rigorous antecedence, necessary antecedence; but not the transformation of the cause into the effect. For the partisans of unity, Substance unconditionally must have been in state C before finding itself in state E; it had to be cause before being effect. Substance transforms, transmutes, modifies itself — it is one, and it is always itself, always the same. An effect is nothing new, nothing distinct — it is what the cause was: it is the cause.

I contest this notion of cause. I affirm that the concept "causation" is diminished, degraded, limited, lessened, if it is fused with the concept "conservation of energy." The law of causation implies solely a relation of necessary succession: it teaches that the cause must precede the effect, but it does not imply the substantial identity of cause and effect. There is, certainly, a necessary relation between C and E, but not a transformation of C into E. What is indispensable is that C exist if E is to exist; but not that E be C. On the contrary: E exists precisely because it is not C. The notion of cause is vaster than that of conservation of energy; it encompasses or can encompass other things. The effect is diverse from its cause; so that the cause does not transform itself or pour itself as it is into the effect, but creates — gives of itself something new, something that was not, that was not contained whole in what it was. Something diverse, original, unmistakable, unforeseeable from its causal antecedents alone — this is what is called an effect. The cause is creative. In Spinoza we find an absolute identification between effect and cause. All is the same: the world is identified with its cause. But the notion of causation is richer and broader than it first appears. It would subsist as an intrinsic law in a world like Spinoza's, in which all is transformation; but even if C were not rigorously converted into E, it would subsist as antecedence in a world that were like a creative impulse always new and always diverse. Causation is a necessary relation; but it is urgent (I was about to say necessary) to admit alongside necessity the contingency. The necessary is what cannot cease to be without contradiction. The contingent is what can cease to be. Causation is necessary; but there is a contingency between cause and effect. The cause does not predetermine the effect, because the only necessary thing is that it precede it. There is a contingency. E is not necessary with respect to C. It is something else, something new, diverse, unique, unforeseeable — something that has an unmistakable originality. Consider how impoverished the world becomes within the pantheist hypothesis: there is but one thing from which everything springs. From one, only one can come. For pantheism one thing is all things and all is the same, the same. I see, on the contrary, diversity — the richest diversity at the foundation of existence, the most luxuriant diversity in the essence of all that exists — not unity, but plurality. Everything is new, everything is different, nothing is like another thing, nothing repeats itself. What repeats itself is monotonous and miserable. The universe is rich and fecund: at the bottom of everything, in the impulse that hurls forth the things that are, there is an unspeakable luxury of diversity. It is not one thing that exists: it is many things, all unique. The essence is new, always new — and the phrase of the Gospel, "My Father worketh hitherto," is truth. He works still, He works always, He creates always unexpected things. He does not tire, His faculty of giving does not exhaust itself, His longing to give does not wane. All things are not one thing. Existence is original and free, creative. That is why the world is always beautiful and marvelous, because it is diverse. Were it one and the same thing, it would be insupportable, since, as Tarde says, the monotonous allied to the unique can yield nothing but tedium. There are no equal things. In nature nothing repeats itself; all is unique and there is no equality, nor unity, nor uniformity. Unity is degeneration because it is repetition. The creative impulse is unique and therefore creative. And all that has worth in the world is unique — think of inventions. The whole of existence is like a burst of incalculable, inexhaustible, supremely fecund force. And essence is richer than existence: the cause is creative. It does not exhaust itself in the innumerable manifestations of what exists; it does not tire, it does not turn back upon itself. It is creation without laws, without limitations, without shackles. It does not surge forth subjecting itself to immutable laws. Laws are our limitations, accommodations, manners of understanding — by limiting it — that astonishing fecundity that exceeds our intelligence and overwhelms it. The creative impulse is not determined. There are no determinations in the original and fluid reality. Our laws, as Mach says, are the limits of hope that our reason imposes upon things. The diversity and fecundity of existence are infinite, incommensurable. What is about to be realized, what is being realized, is better than what has already been accomplished. Life always opens unexpected perspectives, new horizons: it is always new and unexpected. The pantheists and the intellectualists wish to explain rich and fecund reality through one of its projections, which is perhaps the poorest. They wish to explain all by uniformity — but if uniformity does not exist, if the equal exists nowhere. We say "star," "tree," "man," and in this way we name most diverse beings. We constrain life, which strains to escape and which disconcerts — bright and magnificent — our myopic reason at every instant. The uniform and convenient beings of our intelligence do not exist except ideally; but they are very useful — so useful that we can scarcely think without them. Concepts are not realities; laws are not realities either; they do not deliver things as they are, they do not give us their essence; but they are forms of action, paths opened in advance that channel our strength by diverting it from useless deviations and greatly facilitating our life. They distract us from certain aspects and show us others with clairvoyance; that is why scientific laws serve industry so greatly and impel it without limit. But is existence itself what scientific uniformities teach? Existence is original, indeterminate. Laws are determination itself. And this contingency of all things, which I admit, is not contrary to the principle of causation. The rigorous idea it contains, as has been seen above, does not impede contingency. The effect follows the cause, yes, but it can be discerned from it. It is distinct. And this is observed in all cosmic orders: the mathematical, the astronomical, the physical, the chemical, the biological, the psychological, the moral, the supernatural. If we observe in them the relation of causality, we shall notice that in each order, as the complexity and reality of being [ser] increases, the effect is ever more diverse from its cause, more clearly discernible from it, more free. There is a rich — an unspeakably rich — diversity in what exists. The world is not that Spinozistic or Hindoo thing. It is not always the same and the same. It is surprise, fecund burst, unexpected and marvelous creation, astonishment, miracle. Thus I understand the world; thus I understand it and love it. The man who acts today as yesterday bores me. We men must be, like the universe, a creative and free impulse. A world that were thus is, to be sure, difficult to think; but in exchange the attitude of humanity within it is more worthy; because it is a world in which human action has meaning.

## EL HEROISMO FILOSOFICO I — PHILOSOPHICAL HEROISM I

Lacking in Carlyle's celebrated book devoted to the cult of heroes and the heroic in history is one more type of exception: that of "philosophical heroism" — a silent archetype of discrete and passionate attitude. The maxims that follow aim to indicate how and why, alongside the warrior, the king, the seer, the poet, and the god, the philosopher must stand with his heroism "sui generis" [of its own kind] — an attitude not diminished for being silent, which is expressed with rare perfection by the beautiful name that, according to Iamblichus, Pythagoras invented: "lover of wisdom."

A condensation of probabilities is science. The only truth that is not probable but certain is of signal insignificance: A is A. This is absolutely clear, but of little value as knowledge. The estimable truths are those that show something new in the second member of the symbolic equation; those that teach: A is B.

One must take risks in order to think, as in order to act. Descartes taught that the pure understanding neither affirms nor denies. The only thing it does is conceive things that can be denied or affirmed. It is the will [voluntad] that says: I believe — the same in science as in religion.

To find truth immediately, without approximations or gropings, would be excellent; but to investigate it constantly without ever succeeding in reaching it is perhaps our greatest good.

Human truth is something dynamic and evolutionary; the same is true of dogma as of law. If you suppress the flowing time, you will suppress in turn the possibility of remedying error with error, conjecture with conjecture, and approximation with approximation.

The philosophical spirit [espíritu] is a constant and incorruptible disposition toward adventure that has much of the heroic in it. The enchantment of philosophy consists, more than in success — always problematic — of affirmation, in the effort deployed in meditating. Whoever covets the interior quietism of the mind, the solid stability, the easy and indolent repose that corrupts thought as it corrupts psychic activity in general, ought not to concern himself with the study of philosophical questions.

The equilibrium of truth is not resolved like that of the pyramid resting upon its base, but like that of the spinning top which revolves without rest in order not to fall, and inevitably falls the instant the propulsive movement ceases. Metaphysical truth is a desideratum [desideratum] that is never fully achieved, and yet never ceases to be achieved more and more.

That is why the law of thought is at once a logical and a moral imperative. It might be formulated thus: let your sole preoccupation be the preoccupation of freeing yourself from preoccupation. Or else: do not form your personal conviction except in the moment immediately prior to your death. Conviction is the most intimate and difficult spiritual movement; but in the history of intelligence, a clever and fecund error — like the hypothesis of "phlogiston" or Descartes' "vortices" — is worth more than a trivial truth.

The Greeks — who campaigned with such brilliance and freedom in the exercise of thought — seemed to satisfy themselves better with the search for truth than with its most glorious discovery. "In all things," says Gaston Boissier, "the pleasure they experienced along the way made them less impatient to arrive at the end." One must have had recourse to the Platonic dialogues in order to know the spirit of heroic adventure that animates the Socratic discussions. "It is a good thing to discuss, without often arriving at a definitive conclusion," Plato seems to say in several of his colloquia, whose final affirmation was never formulated.

The evil of agnostic philosophy is not its thesis about knowledge, but its immoral corollary. If the agnostic were to say: we do not possess truth nor shall we ever possess it, but we must strive to reach it every day, because we shall always draw closer to its hermetic circle — far from damaging the will [voluntad], this would fortify it with bitterness, serve it with caution, nuance it with prudence; but the agnostic disappoints, paralyzes, suppresses the heroicity of philosophy. That is why it is harmful; because it is imprudent in its denial; because it does not know that truth, at least human truth, is neither definitive nor static, any more than the world to which it refers is static or definitive. Truth "is in the making," and so is the world.

All changes. The only thing that does not vary is the longing to vary. All is altered and transformed; what remains invariable is movement and transformation. Repose, truth, dogma are illusions, momentary crystallizations of our spiritual movement. Live at rest, yes! — but like the flame that seems not to move outwardly, and yet vibrates in the whole intimacy of its being. This is the only possible quietude for the flaming intrepidity of thought — a quietude that, the more firmly it reveals itself, floats the higher, like birds with motionless wings upon the subtle atmosphere.

"Not the possession but the pursuit of truth extends human faculties," says Lessing in his "Duplik" [Rejoinder]. "In this alone consists ever-growing perfection. If God held in His right hand all truth, and in His left hand the sole and ever-active striving for truth (though with the condition of erring eternally), and said to me: Choose! — I would humbly throw myself at His feet and say: 'Give me, Father! — for pure truth is for Thee alone.'"

### II

It is easy to distinguish, in the history of philosophical thought, two lineages of genius which, to use Gracián's gallant technical term, might be called: heroic and discreet.

At first glance, one would say that, without discretion, there can be no philosophy. Without the temperance of judgment — this weighing of reasons, assaying of arguments, and deciding of violent or subtle antitheses — philosophy is impossible. And what else, pray, is philosophizing but the determination of the classic "golden mean" between extremes?

Philosophy — Discretion — Discretion — Philosophy — "All is one and the same," as Schelling said.

Let us tolerate the disheveled and magnificent genius, the incoherent impetuosity, the brusque and revelatory movement that, if it does not captivate, conquers and subjugates — in the lyric poet of broad effort, like Victor Hugo; in the dramatist who scorns, like Shakespeare, by the vigor of his most spontaneous inspiration, the useless rhetoric and the consecrated canons. Let us reserve, on the other hand, for the genuine thinker the maturity of judgment, the mental repose, the serenity of doctrine, the purity of intention.

Yet just as the philosopher seems inconceivable without discretion, he seems equally inconceivable without heroism. He always possesses, like the poets, his temperament, his unique character. Only because he is "himself" does he endure. Spirits without personality are neither philosophers nor artists. A pure dialectician will be a great disputer, like Euthydemus or Dionysodorus, but not a great philosopher. Reason "inserts itself" in genius and distributes and orders it, but does not function "a priori." The raw material of philosophy is an intuition [intuición], a heroic act. Genius is heroism; ingenuity, discretion.

Hence, even though every great thinker synthesizes the heroic and the discreet, in some profound spirits genius and intrepidity predominate, while in others the "reasoning reason," the dialectical and organizing spirit [espíritu], prevails. Only in a few supermen are both cardinal virtues combined in equivalent proportions.

It has been said that in every philosopher there is something of the Platonic and something of the Aristotelian. This is true. Plato, an indisputable superman, and Aristotle, "the eternal prince of true thinkers," as Comte called him, are archetypes of the two great philosophical lineages. Plato is heroic — "the heroic" par excellence [por antonomasia]. Aristotle, heroic as well, is nevertheless the exemplar of the discreet.

The heroic has as essential characteristics the power of invention, which is called "philosophical intuition [intuición]," and, a direct corollary of the former, intrepidity — that is, the systematic subordination of data to the thesis he professes.

The discreet is characterized not only by his equanimity but by his greater objectivity. Since he is not a victim of the enthusiasm of invention, the ideas he professes do not wrest from him, as from the heroic, shreds of his very consciousness [conciencia]. He is a relentless dispenser of justice. He trims opposing theses, makes them negate each other, obliges them to make common cause with their contraries, communicates and arranges them in organic synthesis, and thus approaches fuller objectivity — or at least the common sense of men, the metric system of objectivity — more nearly than the heroic.

The philosophical progress of history, the very rhythmic pulsation of philosophy, is owed to the concurrence of heroic and discreet thinkers. If only the heroic were to philosophize, metaphysics would be a magnificent swarm of genial thoughts without mutual relations, without traditional concatenation, "without windows," as Leibniz would say.

If only the discreet were to philosophize, philosophical thought — patient, exact, meticulous, scholastic — would lack the supreme quality of invention, the supreme power of the intrepidity that verges on the absurd at times, but at times also suspects or discovers unforeseeable approximations and surprising analogies.

Socrates represents the alliance of heroicity and discre-

The notable author of the "Griechische Denker" [Greek Thinkers], Theodor Gomperz, an exceptionally delightful and exceptional historian, has recourse to an ingenious comparison to explain the Socratic spirit [espíritu]. He says of the Athenian that he was like those machines driven by the expansive force of steam which, despite their extremely high temperature, produce ice. A source of heat that forms frozen masses; that is, a great power of philosophical intuition [intuición] developing in impeccable reasonings, a vigorous personality objectifying itself in conclusive demonstrations, a divine and frenzied passion pouring itself out in discreet and subtle conversations. Such was Socrates: force in the core and grace in expression; discourse and genius.

"To encompass all perfection is granted only to the first being who, receiving it from no other, suffers no limitations. Some gifts heaven bestows, others it leaves to industry; one or two do not suffice to exalt a subject; whatever heaven withheld of natural gifts, let diligence supply in the acquired."

Thus sentences, in his peculiar elliptical style, the profound Gracián, a good friend of the discreet. The same conclusion may be drawn with respect to philosophical heroism and discretion: "Thinkers," the muse of that pure writer seems to say, "be Socratic insofar as you are able. In this way you will come to be, at one and the same time, heroic and discreet. Upon the wings of metaphysical invention, place, I charge you, the sure ballast of logic. Bold and firm, you will then fly over the varied regions of thought, in the ethereal and distant spheres or in the recondite darknesses of consciousness [conciencia]."

### III

Metaphysical invention has been throughout human history a most rare gift.

Greek philosophy before Socrates, far from showing itself meager (for being incipient) in the original elaboration of philosophical systems, was perhaps the preferred period of speculative creation. Parmenides, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, Democritus, Empedocles, and Anaxagoras created the cosmological ideas of thinking humanity.

Parmenides founded pantheist idealism. Heraclitus the doctrine of evolution. Pythagoras the mathematical conception of the world. Empedocles synthesized the idealism of Parmenides and the evolution of Heraclitus. Democritus created atomistic materialism and Anaxagoras initiated monadology — that is, spiritualist atomism. It seems that in those remote days the Greek genius offered to coming centuries all the ideas that were afterward discussed and assayed through the effort of generations.

Socrates refined the critical spirit [espíritu] of his race; he ordered systematic dialectic, together with the great sophists Zeno, Gorgias, and Protagoras. He himself was also a sophist. His greatest work, however, was called Moral Science. He created Ethics as a discipline independent of Religion. This shall be his eternal glory.

Perhaps to Plato should be reserved the title of the most profound intelligence in the evolution of thought. His work — of incalculable transcendence — is one of the cornerstones of Western civilization. Champion of philosophical paganism and precursor of Christianity; "Father of the Fathers of the Church."

Aristotle fused the Platonic ideas with life, with experience. The world of Plato was divine, but unreal. The Stagirite preserved its august divinity, but brought it closer to earth. The Idea became Form and could be separated from Matter only by abstraction. The Aristotelian Encyclopaedia synthesized the final teaching of Greece. Thereafter, Stoics, Epicureans, and Skeptics would divide the immortal inheritance as Alexander's lieutenants divided his ephemeral Empire.

In Alexandria — the great midden of the ancient world — Plotinus of Lycopolis, at the age of fifty, composed his "Enneads"; fifty-four mystical treatises in which the East and West were fused. The prophecy of the Apostle was about to be fulfilled: "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new."

Saint Augustine is the greatest philosopher of Christianity. In his work the Gospels are joined with the Platonic Dialogues in intimate and immortal communion. The Church owes him so much that it owes more only to Saint Paul. Protestantism and Cartesianism are distant resonances of his work. He was the Doctor of Luther and the Master of Descartes.

Descartes is the first modern philosopher. He reversed for ever the terms of human certitude, the sign of truth. He made the "I" the origin of philosophy: "Dubito, ergo cogito, ergo sum" [I doubt, therefore I think, therefore I am]. France, Hegel said to Cousin, had already done enough for philosophy by giving it Descartes.

The saintly Baruch Spinoza, intellectual lover of God, is the honor of the modern world. The systematic pantheism of the "Ethics demonstrated after the manner of geometers" is still today one of the boundary points of speculation. Parmenides reborn in the seventeenth century of the Christian Era.

Leibniz represents the vindication of spiritual individuality against the vast synthesis of Cartesian and pantheist rationalism. His work initiates all contemporary psychological and metaphysical ideas: evolution, the unconscious, the ideality of time and space. He is the Aristotle of the Cartesian cycle.

Kant. One says: Socrates and Kant. They are the two Socrates; the two great founders of history. The "nosce te ipsum" [know thyself] is transformed in the eighteenth century into the "Kritik der reinen Vernunft" [Critique of Pure Reason]. Socrates is the hero of Virtue; Kant the theorist of Duty.

Hegel realizes the example of the most systematic philosopher in history. His work attracts and repels, maddens and subjugates. His "new organon," his imperturbable, constrictive, subtle dialectic, came near to magically syllogizing existence. Are you certain you have vanquished Hegel? How can one destroy a system that is constructed at the points of intersection of all systems?

Schopenhauer "was born posthumous," as Nietzsche would say. He served us the bitter liquor of pessimism. He invented for us anti-intellectualism, pragmatism, the philosophy of the will [voluntad], the "élan vital" [vital impulse]. Wagner, Renan, Tolstoy, Ibsen, Nietzsche are his poetic and musical descendants.

M. Henri Bergson has the floor today.

Parmenides, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, Empedocles, Democritus, Anaxagoras, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus, Saint Augustine, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant, Hegel, Schopenhauer, Bergson. Add, if you please, two more names — Bacon and Comte, for example. Only twenty great philosophers of the first rank in the course of twenty-five or twenty-six centuries of history.

Can there be, perchance, anything more extraordinary and magnificent, and that honors humanity more, than philosophical genius?

## MI CONVICCION FILOSOFICA — MY PHILOSOPHICAL CONVICTION

1. We are in the world to act. The essence of our activity we do not know and probably never shall; but the meaning of our effort is intelligible.

2. The positive sciences and history are perfectible forms of knowledge that will, with each passing period, render our effort upon the world and upon ourselves more intelligible.

3. By contrast, there is almost no metaphysical progress. None of the great cosmological hypotheses of the pre-Socratic Greeks has been vanquished or thereby excluded from the group of contemporary metaphysical ideas. Hylozoism is still a way of conceiving the ultimate reality of being [ser]. And it was with this ingenuous assimilation of spirit [espíritu] and matter that the philosophical thought of Greece began.

4. Logic, ethics, and aesthetics — that is, practical, canonical philosophy, as the Stoics called it; the rule of human life, the philosophy of value, as we moderns say — has entered fully into an evident progress. Logic is economic, biological, utilitarian in essence. It is the wise and refined form of egoism. Mach, W. James, Bergson, Poincaré, etc., are the epistemologists who have resolved the problem of reason, insofar as reason can resolve itself — that is, they have resolved the problem of the law of rational functioning, whose forms were fixed for ever by the Greeks and, above all by them, by the eternal prince of true thinkers, as Comte calls Aristotle of Stagira. For the nature of thought will always be an enigma to thought itself, as Höffding wisely and eloquently teaches at the conclusion of his Compendium of Psychology. We know, in sum, how reason functions and why it functions as it does; but we shall never know what it is. Docta ignorantia [learned ignorance].

5. Aesthetics was founded by Kant — the greatest philosopher after Aristotle. To him we owe the impeccable definitions: "beauty is a disinterested pleasure"; "a finality without end." Schopenhauer and Bergson perfected the theory by completing its de-intellectualization, if one may so speak; they finished off what it had of the Platonic. Plato was the best philosopher of the poets and the greatest poet of the philosophers.

6. Ethics was founded by Our Lord Jesus Christ. So long as no man had sacrificed himself for the ideal of sacrifice, ethics was a theory — that is to say, a clever and profound but merely theoretical, dialectical, and unreal exercise in metaphysics. Socrates died so as not to be inconsistent with himself: Jesus died from spontaneous inspiration. He did not make syllogisms like the Greek. He lived. Christian morality is life, not hypothesis; act, not law; conduct, not imperative. I would wish that Jesus had never existed, or that if He had existed He had not sacrificed Himself, or that He had manifested Himself in His century as an angel, a genius, or a supernatural apparition. But He was a man — and then, if we are men, we must be as He was. There is no help for it. By clinging to the Cross He committed us all. If we do not sacrifice ourselves we shall not be saved. This is the truth.

The other great religious founder, together with Socrates and Jesus — Shakyamuni the inspired — died of an indigestion from truffles. If the moral world is will [voluntad], the most false of moral hypotheses is the negation of the will [voluntad] to live.

7. Through education we resolve the realization of a complete man. That is to say, we constitute an ideal being that has not been fully realized in History. To imitate Christ — not as the celebrated medieval book teaches, where the contempt [contemptu] of the world is enjoined, but as Francis of Assisi imitated Him. This is the human destiny. To create men who are good and lucid spirits [espíritus], apt and living centers of artistic disinterestedness and moral abnegation (Charity) — this is the Law and the Prophets summed up in the love of one's neighbor as oneself.

The rest — cosmology and ontology — are vanities of human reason, which exists not to understand the essence or the substance of anything, but to serve action:

1st. Through scientific knowledge, creating Industry — always progressive, unlike Metaphysics, always unprogressive.

2nd. Through ethical knowledge, realizing Man as the sole accessible meaning of the world and of history.

3rd. Through aesthetic knowledge, making us rest from the pain of living with the pleasure of seeing and hearing for the sake of hearing and seeing.

8. At the foundation of all things Something exists — I do not know whether Omnipotent, but noble, loyal, and human — which makes possible the heroism and the sacrifice needed to fulfill a design which I can perhaps only glimpse if before that Being [Ser] I humble myself and understand that I cannot understand it.

9. Our Father, who art in heaven. Hallowed be Thy name, Thy will [voluntad] be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread, and forgive us our debts as we forgive our debtors; lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from all evil. Amen.

## INTUICION Y EXPRESION — INTUITION AND EXPRESSION

"Estetica come scienza dell'espressione."

Benedetto CROCE

Mental states do not engender themselves alone. Spirit [espíritu] and body are linked to each other in a mysterious way, to such a degree that by the physical manifestations of the soul we know that it exists; just as, without a force, without an impulse, without an inner spontaneity, material substance is reduced in our view to mere extension. Perhaps what we call spirit [espíritu] and what we call body are nothing but diverse aspects of one and the same reality that it is impossible for us even to begin to know unless we inevitably bifurcate it into two eternal and irreducible symbols.

The problem of the relations of spirit [espíritu] with matter is one of the most abstract puzzles of philosophy. Their sempiternal communication is the most trivial phenomenon and the deepest arcanum of existence.

With this arcane and trivial fact are intimately bound the question of the origin of language and the mystery of Art. Language is inseparable from thought. In Greek as in Spanish, "discourse" means indistinctly reason and speech.

Some philosophers judge that human thought created language; but for such a creation, thought would have had to have already at its disposal what it was about to form. Besides, many animals express their psychic states with sounds. Expression seems to be a concomitant aspect of internal psychic effort. The reflex act is already the immediate response to an exterior excitation. It possesses a certain intrinsic fatality that is truly exemplary.

Artistic language, like the humblest reflex, is a fatality, an inevitable expression. Great artists are the human mouthpiece of the mystery of spirit [espíritu]. They create because such is their law: to create with the mysterious spontaneity of instinct.

As the "peaceful bees" construct their geometric dwellings ignorant of the end they achieve by means of their magical somnambulism — so unconscious and so vital — so do great poetic geniuses engender their stupendous works. The proceedings of inventors and poets seem like an act of demonic possession, of aesthetic hypnotism. Their intuition [intuición] prolongs itself into their expression. Their work is their spirit [espíritu] materialized, externalized, corporized in symbols.

To Nietzsche belongs this immortal page that tells us how "figurative language," of which the rhetoricians speak, is the most natural, the least impregnated with rational motives or arguments: "What strikes one profoundly," says the philosopher, "is the character of necessity with which the metaphor imposes itself. The most natural, the most just, the simplest expression seems to be the one that offers itself. In truth, one would say — in keeping with the word of Zarathustra — that things themselves come to the spirit [espíritu] desirous of becoming symbols. And all comes with tender longing to find a place in the word, and smiles winningly, wishing to be carried away enraptured by it. Upon the wing of every symbol you fly toward every truth. For you all the treasures of the word open of themselves. Every movement wishes to learn to speak from you. Such is my experience of inspiration" — Nietzsche adds — "and I believe one would need to go back thousands of years to find a man who had the right to say: That is also mine."

Nietzsche deceives himself in this last exclusive affirmation. Every true artist has felt how the millennial inspiration bursts from his unconscious — the trail of light, the work intuited [intuida] and expressed at once, in the inaccessible miracle, in the arcanum that humbles thought.

Nevertheless, the despair of artists has often consisted in the rebelliousness of the word to bend itself to the urgency of the interior vision. The mystics of all times declare they have seen in their raptures things ineffable and most subtle, for which the vain murmur of the word was not made. "One must raise altars to silence," wrote Carlyle.

There are three kinds of silence — according to Miguel de Molinos in his celebrated "Guía Espiritual" [Spiritual Guide]: "The first is of words, the second of desires, and the third of thoughts. In the first, of words, virtue is attained; in the second, of desires, quietude is achieved; in the third, of thoughts, interior recollection. By not speaking, not desiring, not thinking one arrives at the true and perfect mystical silence, in which God speaks to the soul, communicates with it, and teaches it in its most intimate depths the most perfect and lofty wisdom."

Is intuition [intuición], perchance, diverse from expression, which seems to accompany it and follow it consubstantially? Is silence the altar of the most exalted work of art? Does the soul, pierced by its purest contemplation, fly without sound in an impalpable and recondite atmosphere? Or is spiritual quietism a clumsy negation of spirit [espíritu], of expression, of art, of thought itself?

A grave aesthetic and linguistic problem, intimately related to the old hypotheses of the communication of the spiritual and corporeal substances. To resolve it would be almost to resolve, at a single stroke, all the metaphysical problems proposed — by the divine discovery of a supreme truth. Psychology, Linguistics, and

Aesthetics enclose at bottom the same question.

## LA CRITICA ARTISTICA — ARTISTIC CRITICISM

Have you found in your life anything more sterile than rhetorical criticism? I have; the so-called "scientific criticism" of works of art. The rhetorician who, with the "figures of diction" scrupulously defined, sought to judge the merit of genius, and held it up to the light with all the candor of his inoffensive and tiresome secondary activity — the rhetorician like don José Gómez Hermosilla, whom our fathers and, still more, our grandfathers knew and feared; that rancid personage whom we laboriously exhume from our childhood memories — understood the proper nature of Art better than the scientific critics, paragons of aesthetic unintelligence.

Rhetoricians and learned psychologists and physicians who thrust their sickle into another's field; who decide, according to logical canons, upon the mastery of a work of art, and define its style, its value, and its form by virtue of purely rational data — these are the same personage at different times of his action, the same pedantic schoolmaster who speaks of what he does not understand.

Rhetoric, like Science, or what passes for "scientific criticism," are invasions of pure reason into the sphere of intuition [intuición], which is the exclusive domain of Art.

The human spirit has two distinct forms of knowledge: knowledge through reason and knowledge through intuition. The scholar, the philosopher, the mathematician know through genera and species, through general and abstract relations. The artist, the poet, the musician think intuitively.

When something is known because it is seen directly in itself, any other kind of knowledge is superfluous. No one will ever prefer a description — such as those travel books offer — to the direct vision of the countries described. The artist is like the traveler who directly intuits the countries he traverses. The reasoner is like those who, without stirring from their corner — "in a little corner with a little book" [in angello cum libello] — reconstruct panoramas and, through sheer reading, imagine the attributes of countries they have never seen.

The rhetoricians and physicians who, in recent times, have presumed, in their own estimation, to exercise the high magisterium of criticism, never knew how to see the reality before their eyes as the artists whose merits they assess know how to see it; rather, when the inspired ones discover that reality, these critics hasten to declare, if they are rhetoricians: "this is a 'metonymy,' a 'trope,' a 'preterition'"; or, if they are physicians: "this is a consequence of the author's being mad, or epileptic, or mystical." Mysticism, like genius — it should be said in passing — is a disease, according to some contemporary psychologists.

It is the same defect in both, the same myopia, the identical limitation. Because they cannot see, they think. Thinking is only tolerable when seeing is impossible. If men could contemplate all that exists in a single synthetic glance, in a most luminous intuition — as God can, according to the theologians — we would already have abandoned this tiresome artifice of reasoning, which, after prodigious effort, so often leads us to absurdity. Dr. Max Nordau is the type of the physician turned art critic, just as don Josef Mamerto Gómez Hermosilla was, for Spanish-speaking readers, the archetype of the rhetorician with his ferule and doctoral cap.

Nordau has, among his most delightful inventions, one most precious for assaying the works of human genius. It consists in discussing the private lives of great artists, with the aim of uncovering the secret of their magnificent productions.

Does the sublime duet of Tristan and Isolde move you to delirium? Does the prelude to Lohengrin fill you with enthusiasm — remember that enthusiasm means being seized by the god? Do you ride Olympically with the Valkyries to the sound of Wagner's magical orchestra? That is because Wagner was mad, mystical, degenerate, and you are, too, somewhat — just enough to enjoy his morbid and seductive art.

Is this not an easy way to judge without leaving the pharmacy laboratory or the clinic?

The rhetoricians were foolish, but the physicians have surpassed them. How can they not understand that the man and the work are two different things? Let us suppose — without granting it, as the scholastics say — let us suppose they are right in asserting the morbidity of genius. The work is not the man. A poem by Verlaine is not sick. The music of Wagner suffers from no secret ailment. The novels of Tolstoy are imperishable works of art. If genius is a delirium, let us all rave with genius.

Furthermore, there are no normal men. We are all somewhat deranged — physicians and the sane and the mad alike. Each of us has his measure of morbidity, abnormality, and personality. If we were all identical in terms of mental health, no one in the spiritual realm would have the right to exist. Perhaps personality is the most commonplace form of madness.

And if neither rhetoric nor medicine can determine the proper nature of artistic criticism, who shall know how criticism ought to be?

The poets know. They have always been good critics, perhaps somewhat imperious, precisely because the critic cannot be a reasoner but must be an intuitive. Read these lines by Oscar Wilde; they teach more about the craft of the critic than all the rhetoric books in the world: "You say that the highest criticism treats of Art not as expression, but as impression only, and so is creative and free; in fact, it is an art in itself, which stands in the same relation to the creative work as the creative work does to the visible world of form and color or the invisible world of passion and thought."

## LA MORALIDAD DEL ARTE — THE MORALITY OF ART

The fundamental law of thought is the principle of contradiction, which states: a thing cannot be and cease to be at the same time. If, then, it can be demonstrated that every true work of art consists in the disinterested intuition or expression of individual reality, no work of art can be immoral, because it is precisely immoral to act or think with self-interest; and since nothing can be and not be at the same time, in conformity with the principle of contradiction, the work of art — whose essence is disinterestedness — can never move self-interest; that is, it will never be immoral.

Now, how can a work of art be disinterested? Because the artist, if he truly is one, intuits and expresses in his creation not things in their relations to our desires, but things in themselves; not an individual, a being, an animal, or a given situation for some purpose, but the individual, the being, the animal, or the situation considered in themselves.

Most of us do not know how to intuit reality except in relation to some purpose; we take note of something only insofar as it can satisfy some preconceived end; and for this very reason we do not understand existence in its individuality. Our egotism prevents it.

Behold that "quidam" absorbed in his habitual activity by which he earns his keep. He takes from the world only what can serve the end of his development, his sustenance, his action in the world. He wishes to obtain, with the least effort on his part, the greatest possible benefit. For this reason he does not understand what surrounds him. He sees it, certainly, but he does not look at it, does not scrutinize it, does not penetrate it. Because he covets it, because he wishes to subject it to his dominion, he does not understand it. A man who passes beside him is no more than a false but useful representative sign — he is, for example, simply this: a silk hat, a white beard, a gold-handled cane, and a pair of patent-leather shoes. The quidam who sees him needs no further data. But the artist looks, scrutinizes, perceives — that is, intuits — and expresses his intuition.

If the quidam were to perceive or intuit everything, he could not live. He would spend in the mere disinterested contemplation of a single person as much time as a painter spends in studying and observing a subject he is about to portray. He would stand transfixed each evening watching how the sun slowly sinks in the miracle of the twilight; and a human cry or lament would change for him, in an instant, what Carlyle calls the silver of word and time into the gold of eternity.

The artist, as described by Schopenhauer and Bergson, possesses an innate disinterestedness of mind that enables him to intuit or contemplate each thing or being in its characteristic individuality; and so, in a flock, he will know — like the Good Shepherd — which is one sheep among the others; which is the particular lamb that, to other men, seems as trivial as any other ram. The artist places his disinterestedness in the work of art. He makes of the work a mirror of reality.

Suppose an abominable act of hatred and desolation. No matter! The artist will see it in its characteristic individuality, will present it without human motive of wickedness, will display it like a clean and pure mirror. The dramas of Shakespeare are full of intuitions of perverted scenes, and they do not impel to crime but, on the contrary, lead away from it. They are cathartic of passion, purgative of sin. Oedipus is founded on incest, yet no one will wish to be incestuous like Oedipus. The genius of Sophocles displays it with the clear purity of art, in an expression that does not stain desire, that does not contaminate the will. "The painter," said Leonardo da Vinci, "must be universal and a lover of solitude; he must consider what he sees, and reason with himself, choosing the most excellent parts of all the things he sees; making himself like a mirror that is transformed into as many colors as are placed before it; and in this way he will seem a second nature." Leonardo's "second nature" is the true one; the other is a fiction of egotism.

If, then, for there to be a true work of art there must also be disinterestedness, no pure work will be sinful, even when it depicts the most horrendous human actions. "Being," said Saint Thomas Aquinas, "is good in itself." Every true ontology begins in aesthetics, Novalis adds.

Does this mean that immoral acts cannot be committed by immoral men through the reading or contemplation of works of art? No, unfortunately. But this is not reason enough to declare art immoral. The Venus de Milo may inspire in some wretched madman loathsome carnal desires; but those desires would not be sufficient reason for us to do away with the most chaste sculpture that exhibits feminine nudity in the holy Attic innocence of marble. One would rather say to the perverse man: "If thy eye offend thee, pluck it out."

Would you behead a beautiful woman because she provoked the wickedness of many by exhibiting, without ostentation, her beauty?

Sometimes artists, far from being completely objective and disinterested in their creation, mingle in the work of art their aesthetic intuition with a certain carnal interest, as in the celebrated Saint Teresa of Bernini. But the "Saint Teresa" is inesthetic precisely in what is impure about it — that is, it is immoral in what is not artistic. There is no intrinsic contradiction, nor can there ever be, between beauty and the good, between art and morality, which are the two highest forms or "values" of existence.

## BELGICA EN LA PAZ — BELGIUM IN PEACE

Belgium in Peace is the second oblation of the spirit of Francisco Orozco Muñoz to the martyr nation that has just emerged reborn from an onerous captivity. The young Mexican writer has put all his gallant energy at the service of an ideal vivified by the fragrance of a pious remembrance. This book is life, truth, and poetry. Its pages possess the mysterious power of whatever was once ours, bearing the proprietary mark of unmistakable personality. They are states of the soul, fragments of a spirit that spreads indiscernibly into a beloved landscape; music that one could not say whether it is exterior or interior. "In Mechelen, Ypres, Bruges, or Ghent..." So one of the many descriptive spiritual moments is titled, all of which together form the admirable oblation.

The artistic nature of Orozco Muñoz does not disdain, in displaying the adornments of the imagination, the flavor of the land, the sanction of holy reality. It is an idealistic realism of an enthusiastic and devoted lover. He writes of what he loves and has seen. He reflects for us the poetic country: its marvelous women and churches, its Levitical or revolutionary universities, its ports fragrant with oil and tar, its kitchens with their black-and-white tile floors, and the trees that, in Belgium as everywhere, "grow old in peace beneath the moon." The universal vibration of love and death — the only great things under the stars, as Leopardi said — lends a soft and discreet hue to this evocative book.

The love is frank, youthful, jovial. Neither prayer nor psalm, but flesh and passion. It is the love of a subtle student who knows how to undertake several noble pursuits more worthwhile than studying. Besides, "the wine, the excesses, the bite, the grotesque fondling, all the sensuality of the race, exist in Leuven among the churches and scientific institutions:

"Calm houses, ancient passions!" [Calmes maisons, anciennes passions!]

Thus the faithful narrator intimates. The joy of living still throbs and endures in the opulent homeland of Rubens and Van Dyck.

I imagine him in his Flemish surroundings, surprised at first, then devout. Each lived moment would release its particle of soul; for the mission of the one who writes is not only to tell but to express. For this very reason, one need not say everything, but only the essential. Expression is art; the mere civil communication through language is without nerve or value. Orozco Muñoz knows how to fix the moment of possession of the object. He moves toward it with all his sympathetic impulse; he renews it with his lucid cordiality. In this way he sifts his memories and selects them capriciously. He seems to say to us: "What matters is what I did not forget. Behold it as I saw it." And these scattered movements have more unity than others that might more readily conform to the unperturbed regularity of geometric prose. Their unity is the spiritual nucleus from which, in the mystery of intuition, they proceeded without effort. Als ik kan (as best I can), such is the author's motto.

In another brief and dramatic book, this sincere poet recounted the horror of systematic devastation. In this one, the pointed helmet of Prussia barely appears at the end of his stories. It interrupts a sacred idyll. (How many more must it have interrupted throughout the world, to its eternal damnation!) Jeanne, "a white lump of sugar in the golden atmosphere of the afternoon," a child without sin, like Goethe's Dorothea, bows before fate, bids farewell to her beloved in chaste silence, and weeps... Let us not renew the infamy of the nameless crime by recalling it. Like all crimes, great or small, it deserves oblivion. May it await forgiveness in vain! Let us turn the page of violence, then, and feel the harmony of the life of Belgium, great in peace and war, in industry and in school, in church and in history, in the present and in the future. Hans Memling, angelic painter — so Orozco Muñoz would say — "touches with his brush the skies of the Brabantine dawn."

## DON ENRIQUE JOSE VARONA — DON ENRIQUE JOSÉ VARONA

"In other times the difficulty was to find a book; today the difficulty is to be free of books — I mean mediocre books, insubstantial books. It has been necessary to erect warning posts and organize rescue brigades. The posts, as on bathing beaches, declare in large letters: danger. Understand: danger of being hypnotized or made an idiot. The brigades are composed of that most useful class of literary critics, whose calling is to taste mental nourishment and declare whether it is nourishing, and sometimes even to peptonize it, in order to facilitate digestion."

The foregoing words are those of don Enrique José Varona, the author of the articles in this selection: a literary thinker, an ingenious essayist, a politician and statesman as well, who brings to the exercise of his social calling the criterion of measure and clarity belonging to a man of meditation. Don Enrique José Varona is, above all, a moralist prepared always to challenge every contact with injustice.

Prologues and preliminaries signify acts of courtesy, written introductions placed at the front of books. My good fortune has placed me in the position of telling Mexican readers who this amiable Cuban psychologist is. I should wish to possess the exquisite and traditional urbanity of French writers in order to compose a few brief and perfect lines in which, as in the prologues to books written by those nationals, the finest praise of Varona's work and the discreet incentive that artistically suggests the reading of what follows after the prologue might both be contained. But I am not French; I am Spanish American, and perhaps that is worth more for appreciating — if not with absolute discretion, at least with predilection — the intellectual effort of the Antillean writer.

I shall not write his biography, which I do not know, nor shall I classify his psychological thinking in any of the divisions or subdivisions of the abstract chart of contemporary systems, nor shall I arrange his ideas methodically and in a compendium of economical synthetic apprehension. No. Let such labor await another occasion. "In other times the difficulty was to find a book..."; yes, and in these times the same; but here is one gallant and subtle — a book of meditation on life, of perspicacious accentuation of life.

The moralist is, precisely, an impartial observer of existence, not an impertinent counselor. The difficult thing is to ground morality, said Schopenhauer; to preach it is simple. True; but to live it is still more difficult than to ground it; and moralists like Varona help us to live it, because they discover — and this seems to me the highest praise that can be accorded them — that evil is not only bad, but stupid and ugly, and they instinctively lead us away from its company.

The moral life of man is the supreme work of art. Varona knows this and impels us toward its realization, employing against the wicked that instinct of fine and easy irony which Socrates had to oppose to the disputants who were corrupting Athens.

Like a true and genuine moralist, he begins from himself, traces the personal spiral of his own mental transformations, and — by reason of the fact that all one knows well about one's own person is indirect knowledge of the human soul — comes to know others by the intuition of a skilled intellectual player, by a refined philosophical carom; especially when he applies to the study of his own individuality the combined hesitation and ardor of the disinterested investigator, as, for instance, when he sketches the lean and disagreeable silhouette of Father Juanín. Varona, in these articles, is a specialist in psychological specialties, as distinguished from the philosopher who ambitiously defined his discipline as a specialty in generalities. That is why he is so interesting and timely. Thus always proceeded that most human of skeptics, Michel de Montaigne, author of a book of incalculable consequence.

Moreover, he is a gentleman; his assiduous reading of English books, his nuanced, diverse, and undulating culture, leads him to convey to us — without tragic bitterness, yet with sincere emotion — his revulsion toward evil and stupidity; but he offers it to us without excessive gesticulation, without gasps or unacceptable contortions, as a gentleman.

Read his brief, ephemeral notes. The author never declaims. His style ought to be "prescribed" for the nervous Latin American writers who have not managed to achieve, except in rare cases, the secret of genuine prose, of European prose. His smile, like Voltaire's, sometimes reaches outright laughter, but — as Hugo said of the smile of the author of Candide — philosophical sadness succeeds in tempering it. It seems to me an apt decision to include Varona among the American writers popularized by "Cultura."

## VELAZQUEZ EN MEXICO — VELÁZQUEZ IN MEXICO

Our Mexican artists Pina, Herrera, and Izaguirre have revealed to us, each within his personal instinct, the distinguished work of Velázquez — the painter par excellence of Spain, and perhaps par excellence the painter.

Not long ago I visited the galleries of our "Academia de San Carlos." The great Spanish masters — Zurbarán, Carreño, Ribera, Murillo, and Morales — made me reflect, as they stood out vigorously above the talents of other schools and nations, that Spain, despite shining so brightly in mystical and humanist letters, is yet more glorious in the arts. The maternity of the Romancero, the tragi-comedy of La Celestina, and Don Quixote de la Mancha confers on Spain an immortal place in Parnassus; yet while the realism of Cervantes is merely a transcript of life, the world of Velázquez is life itself — creation reborn, enclosed and formulated, through the miracles of perspective and coloring, within but two of the three dimensions of space.

My visit to the "Academia" took place on a fine sunny morning. The master don Mateo Herrera — learned art critic and exemplary copyist — introduced me into the broad and venerable Assembly Hall of the School of Fine Arts. In a corner of the vast chamber, covered in dust and perhaps unknown to many visitors and curious passersby, the two stupendous works of Velázquez's "abbreviated manner" fixed my attention. Las Meninas and Las Hilanderas offered the enchantment of their everlasting vigor, faithfully rendered by Herrera's brush.

Both creations suggested to me the effort of a single audacity: the luminous patch that breaks the background of each celebrated painting with decisive force and impressive energy. In Las Meninas it is, simple and sober, the opening of a doorway through which a steward of the court of don Philip IV is about to pass freely. In Las Hilanderas it is a most joyful ray of sunlight blazing over the multicolored tapestries of a room adjoining the workshop.

The two paintings of Velázquez's accomplished and definitive "impressionism" — of which Paul Lefort speaks in his Peinture Espagnole — might seem at first glance to be photographic reproductions of trivial human scenes. But what delicious overall movement in the figures! What harmonious and intentional placement of beings and things! What exemplary expression of a single throbbing reality through the singular expressions of many diverse realities! Velázquez too had his rhetoric; but it was so insinuating, so entirely his own, so consonant with the subjects he painted, that nature accepted it as the symbol of its ennoblement by human genius.

See, in Las Meninas, how the gallant figure of the little male dwarf, beside the sturdy and utterly adorable female dwarf — so ugly, so vital, like the Quasimodo of Notre-Dame de Paris — infuses animation into the left side of the painting. See, in Las Hilanderas, that incomparable working woman who, like the goddesses of classical mythology, seems to spin human destinies with unconcern. Her foot, her body, her head, her gesture are for the spectator; but her enigmatic face is turned toward the luminous festival of the background.

"This method of painting," says Lefort, "spontaneous, abbreviated, subtle, is in the Artist the required, premeditated, certain result of consummate knowledge, of sovereign virtuosity."

Velázquez adorned the center of his Meninas with the perfection of a little royal infanta, who is like a pearl without a setting. The child displays her golden hair over her tender shoulders. Dressed all in white and gold, she shows at her wrists only some red and black ribbons. Beside her, to the right of the canvas, the softest human creature my eyes have ever seen seems to be attentively indicating to her mistress something of no consequence, accompanying and clarifying it with a single gesture. The hand of that gesture is ineffable. It irresistibly brings to my memory a certain celebrated madrigal by a poet friend, whose are these verses:

"The kiss, that flew in pursuit of the hand, Breaking the air, turned into a sigh..."

And the artist, with a few strokes, achieved the ineffable marvel! O Velázquez's "impressionism"! Take one square foot of oil painting from any of the supreme works of a great Dutch or Venetian artist and compare it, in the absolute perfection of its technique, with the same unit of surface that you previously find placed at random upon a canvas by Velázquez. The experiment will inspire in you this evident conclusion: if Velázquez is not the greatest painter in the world, he is an unsurpassable painter.

The first law of the artist is to paint — to paint solely for the sake of painting; for the joy of performing an activity that has no end other than itself. Art, of course, never quarrels with moral or philosophical purposes; but its distinctive essence does not require or presuppose them. To revive on canvas the singular nature of things, to perceive it in its characteristic individuality, without ever generalizing, without dogmatizing, without serving — whether vaguely or consciously — hypothetical ideologies: this is to be a painter of genius. This alone is enough to endure.

For this reason the dwarves and monsters of Velázquez enchant us, while we justly disdain the tiresome or grotesque academicism of the literary painting that Lessing censured. It is because Velázquez knew — by the most genuine intuition, not by deliberate intention — to make us penetrate existence itself in its powerful individuality, in its marvelous subtlety.

## UN PINTOR DEL TIEMPO — A PAINTER OF TIME

Let us today interrupt the metaphysical conversation to draw near a painter's studio. The pretext is laudable. Gilberto Chávez humbly exhibits the work of an entire silent, constant, passionate lifetime.

Humility is a great Christian virtue that consists in placing all human pride in the work, reserving within the consciousness no useful seat for vanity. As the Christian expends everything in effective labor, he keeps nothing inactive, idle, or inert for himself. He can feel pride only through his spiritual expression.

Gilberto Chávez, whose name, according to the illustrious poet Villaespesa, seems to belong to some pious artist of the Middle Ages, has practiced, during his years of endeavor, the noble precept of living for others, of burning in reiterated, patient, sempiternal activity. God grant him glory, as the simple of heart say!

In contemplative silence, the purest attribute of the soul is memory, and the supreme form of universal existence is time. It is not surprising that a silent, humble, and disinterested painter should paint, by his infallible vocation, time.

Do you know how to paint everything? One must paint what is genuine in the hour that slides lightly by. One must capture forever the flavor of the passing instant. Other painters, like Velázquez, painted space — the "pure intuition of sensibility," as a disciple of Kant would say. One must paint the other supreme intuition of consciousness: time. But can time be painted? Everything can be painted, above all what is most real, as time is.

Why should the discreet hour and even the fleeting instant not be reflected? Time passes in things and in consciousness. Nature is diverse in the long drama of the day — diverse and unique, harmonious in its disconcerting, luxuriant diversity. In everything that endures, time inscribes itself somewhere.

If you attune yourself to the real duration of the landscape, you will express it with more depth than those conventional mechanisms that know only how to spread comically, over their geometric faces, their insubstantial chronometric lie.

Gilberto Chávez paints our hour, our Mexican time, our instants in the Valley of Mexico. For him not all moments of the day are equal; some are privileged. "The hours of our life," says Emerson, "are diverse in the authority they exercise over us and in their effects. Our faith comes in moments; our vice is habitual." Our painter thinks as the mystical Yankee philosopher thinks.

Whether the overcast morning persists or the sultry siesta scorches; whether it is at the fainting of the afternoon or at the breaking of the Lord's dawn, Chávez knows it, intuits it with disinterestedness, and communicates it to you. He has a hidden and instinctive sympathy with the hour. He paints it in the reflections of the water, in the silver of the snow, in the purple — sometimes black — earth. The rocks, with their ochres and siennas that are friends of the sun, tell the hour. The grass, so subtle, humbly suggests it. Only for the most pure blue is there no time that endures. Above the cones of the volcanoes, in the region of luminous ether, it is God's hour. Eternal midday!

The landscape painter must have a just and pantheistic soul. He cannot prefer one datum to all others, one color to other colors, one being to other beings. Let the scattered individuals be joined in friendship, let them feel the community of their origin, let them rest gently upon one another — the painter will paint only the discreet ensemble, the friendship realized, the platonic love of the system. His justice, like that of humanitarian utopias, will give to each what he deserves according to his capacity, and to each capacity or tonality, according to its works.

When this point of perfection in charity toward the things of the world is attained, nothing can be concealed. Human error is overcome. Things duplicate themselves docilely in the soul, as in the sincere stillness of a pond. The realism is in the work — as Bergson says — because the idealist is in the spirit.

Let the most discreet artist take pride in the unctuous propriety of his accomplished work. His name begins to be spoken, with praise or censure, but always with interest. This aristocratic Mexican nature is reflected in his paintings which, like Turner's — as a painter friend declared — captivate, at times, by the delicious velvety softness of their nuances in major white and minor green.

An intuitive of time as "thing in itself" must place his romantic memory, his melancholic spirituality, in the landscape. Gilberto Chávez is a romantic of our climate. Autumnal spring, autumnal summer, autumnal winter — eternal Mexican autumn!

To conclude these rambling thoughts gracefully, here is a classical quotation from one who, besides being a stupendous artist, was and will always be a profound moralist. In addressing the "proper judgment" in his Treatise on Painting, Leonardo advises those of his craft with this maxim, which must first be placed upon the head and then upon the heart: "There is nothing that deceives us so much as our own judgment when we evaluate our own work; and in this case the criticisms of enemies are more useful than the praises of friends, because the friends, being like ourselves, can delude us just as much as our own judgment."

## RAPSODIAS BIBLICAS — BIBLICAL RHAPSODIES

From the Sanctuary of Our Lady of Carmel in the poetic city of Teziutlán, in the state of Puebla, where he lives "very contentedly, satisfied and happy, far from business" [procul negotiis, as the Venusine would say], the Presbyter don Federico Escobedo has enriched the national literature with a new and admirable volume entitled Biblical Rhapsodies, poems of noble impulse and generous religious inspiration, cast in the impeccable mold of the most serene classicism.

The poet Escobedo continues the series — today, unfortunately, much depleted — of our most beloved humanists. He belongs to the race of Bishop Montes de Oca, of the distinguished Joaquín Arcadio Pagaza, of the glorious Manuel José Othón. His earlier productions have elevated him to the Mexican Academy of Language, Corresponding Member of the Royal Spanish Academy, and he likewise holds a most honorable place among the Arcadians of Rome. And from his happy Arcadia he ascends, in this new book, to the heights of a religious art that, grounded in the faith of Israel transformed by Christianity into the universal faith of mankind, revives Jewish heroes and heroines and has them converse in dialogues worthy of the Luises of the Golden Age of Spanish letters.

I can easily imagine him evoking in the beautiful city of Puebla the majestic dark silhouettes of Elijah and Elisha, Deborah and Moses, outlined against the bright backdrop of the mountain mist, while silently his inspiration, springing from the depths of his soul, causes them to utter the sacred words of patriotic indignation or redemptive enthusiasm.

Elijah, archetype of the biblical wonder-workers, is a volcano, "snow-capped yet ardent":

His skeletal frame is wrapped in a mantle of rough skins; his gaze pierces like a thunderbolt; he is an oak that, despite its weariness, does not cast down the majesty of its crown.

Elisha converses with the Illumined Man and takes shelter beside him with affection, as though he sensed in his fervor that the fire which purified the lips of the Master would leave an inspired spark upon his own lips, yearning for hidden and divine wisdom.

David, beside Saul, completes the formidable choir. He bears the youthful bearing that the genius of Buonarotti lent him; he is not, like Elisha, a rosebush at the foot of the old oak of Prophecy, but a magnanimous and terrible Ephebe, capable of shattering the Giant's head. Meanwhile Saul, furious in his kingship:

...fears that David might scale the royal throne, and from Bethlehem already looks upon the little shepherd with a dark countenance.

And beside the Jewish Ephebe, beautiful as father of the race of God, and beside the wrathful Monarch brought low by the misfortune of not always being noble, Deborah advances with a light step: the bright deeds of her pure history are recounted garrulously by resounding Fame; and heaven and earth, like the sea, all in chorus, cry: Deborah!

Naomi, "the lovely mourner," as the poet calls her, speaks of the subtle beauty and harmony of her inner and outer being in this beautiful romance that might well, without diminishment, take its place among the most prized of Góngora's:

Her face is a perfect oval, encircled round by floating curls the color of the vines of En-Gedi.
Like a pond at rest is her peaceful brow, though on it the clear mark of sorrow may be perceived.
Her eyes, the color of sky, clouded by the tears they shed, have grown dim and resemble the eclipse of two suns.
The fields of her cheeks no longer display the hues that Jericho's roses lent them; now they show... the tints of pale lilies, and seem sad...
very sad!
The neck that in grace left the swan's behind now... droops languidly, as a willow branch droops...

Who, upon reading such gentle and flowing verses — which glide like the water of a stream among smooth pebbles — does not bring to his lips the concise Latin of the Scripture that serves as epigraph to "The Lovely Mourner": Haec est illa. Noemí! [This is she. Naomi!]

And amid evocations of the Bible and figures of Hebrew heroes conceived in the magical disproportion of Oriental history, pass and come alive the reminiscences of Greece, which the poet unconsciously fuses with the Hebrew landscape, softening and adorning it with the refinement of classical nuances:

Spring is in its fullness!
From her flowering footstep her traces may be seen everywhere; and the mountain and the meadow rejoice, displaying opulent life.

Her lascivious tendrils spread the vines of En-Gedi across the elms that serve as their anchorage; and in passing, the languid breezes gather up the singing of the cicadas.
Among pebbles the streams bound away, resonant, and at the gentle breath of mild Favonius, the palm trees fan themselves while the royal sycamores sway.

Sincerely, without any bias that might be excused by the reverent friendship I bear him, I believe that, excepting always the ever-respectable name of the measured and gentle artist that was don José Joaquín Pesado, there is in the entire history of Mexican lyric poetry no religious poet more worthy of homage than don Federico Escobedo.<sup>1</sup> At what enormous distance from his

<sup>1</sup> I do not refer, of course, to the Latinists Alegre and Abad, illustrious Mexican Jesuits.

inspired verses stand the copious and insubstantial antipoetic paraphrases of the worthy don Manuel Carpio!

The second part of the volume is entitled "HORACIANAS." The poet now enjoys greater freedom, without thereby ceasing at times to strike the note of sublimity that persists in the "BIBLICAL RHAPSODIES."

The section opens with a gallant poem which, also inspired by Scripture, says to the Coryphaeuses of the literary chapels and schools: "if the water is good, drink it without asking about the source":

No schools — away with "Cenacles"!
Art is one, lived, resplendent!
To those who love and dignify it, Apollo pays tribute and crowns with garlands.
It rests upon God. Like Him, it is Unique!
Of three loves it draws the divine formula and carries it triumphant everywhere:
"I am beautiful, I am true, I am good!"

But the very invocation of Apollo makes it plain that while the poet renews ideas, exalts contemporary life, and is a child of his century, in casting his poetic intuition into the mold of expression he resorts to classical meters, follows the good counsel to put new wine into old wineskins, and sings, for instance, the marvels of aviation and the exploits of aircraft in verses that Horace himself would have measured within the strict proportions of his impeccable technique:

Who are these who fly like birds and pierce the cirrocumuli of the clouds that in the deep sky pile up in rebellious masses?
Who are these who with metallic blades produce exotic songs by which they waken from their eternal calm the stars that revolve in silence?
Ah! they are the new eagles of the air who above the vault of the heavens go to inscribe their famous names with the dust they gather as they pass from the stars that, astonished, watch how the new Icaruses invade them, who, scorning death, audaciously hold their place firm in the ether!

The four sonnets to Ipandro Acaico are a perfect work of art. No homage received by the illustrious humanist could surpass the filial offering of the Presbyter Escobedo. Ipandro Acaico, from his tomb, will know that the cult of ancient beauty remains inviolate in the soul of his illustrious emulator.

Now, savor with exquisite delight — as though you were witnessing the most beautiful scene of the Colonial period, as though you were attending the court of the Marquis of Mancera, as though you were drawing near to the very being of the illustrious Hieronymite Nun and kissing the hem of her miraculous habit — this sonnet, which delivers to us Juana de Asbaje reborn in the full splendor of her literary glory:

God, who is the sovereign beauty's most skilled painter and architect, wished to accumulate in you all that is most select when He fashioned your body, Sister Juana.
He blended snow with scarlet on the perfect oval of your face; and gave your bust, from its erect volcano, the fine curve that takes pride in its ascent.
And the tumultuous silken curls He let spread over your pure brow, seeing them desirous of kissing it.
He refined the sweetness of your lips; and bestowed upon you the greatest of enchantments:

the spark of your genius that still blazes!

The "HORACIANAS" conclude with another sonnet so fitting, so pure, so inspired, that it is better not to speak of it but to appreciate it in its genuine beauty; for more than the literary expression of a state of the soul, it seems the perfect complicity of a woman's face with the mountain mist of the Puebla highlands and the subtle spirit of a contemplative who, with the greatest disinterestedness of art, ponders the beauty of the notes that are born in his soul with the same naturalness with which the poetic mist wraps its shawls around the rugged crags of the mountain or irises them miraculously in a ray of sunlight...

I love the mist because it circles round the roof that holds you prisoner within its walls, and thus withholds from the profane eye that beholds you the thousand graces of your person.
I love the mist, because in a vague spiral, transparent, subtle, and playful, it fastens a bright crown upon your temples and wraps you in a mantle of glass beads.
I love the mist, because in it I see the imprint of our chaste love and the unceasing turning of our soul.
And I love it, above all, because it captures, to carry to you, the sweet sigh with which my absent heart kisses you!

"From my dark solitudes I come and to my sad solitudes I shall return"...

Thus spoke our classical poet. Thus will the Presbyter don Federico Escobedo speak when he launches the lyric swarm of his verses into the tumult of the City, before returning, piously, to take shelter at the feet of Our Lady of Carmel, in the secluded Sanctuary of Teziutlán. We will not let him return to the gentle life that Horace and Fray Luis celebrated, without telling the Church and the Mexican State that a man of such merit is not meant to live in his hermitage, without contact with the pressing need for culture that our people have. Why not seat him fittingly in a chair of Humanities at the National University or at the venerable Pontifical University, which I imagine must be much in need of men of the intellectual and moral worth of don Federico Escobedo?

Mexico is not a nation that ought to squander the efforts of such illustrious sons; but if the Church and the State do not summon the humanist to their halls, the poet will remain on the beautiful crag of his beloved highlands, praising God with the effusion of his generous feeling and honoring the national letters with the prestige of his song.

## ESTETICA SPENCERIANA — SPENCERIAN AESTHETICS

If Spencer's grave assertions in An Autobiography regarding Art and great artists had been designed to provoke in the spirit of his readers that mordant and vehement feeling of British "humor" — which consists in pretending to believe good or praiseworthy what is inept and censurable, or little worthy of acceptance what in itself deserves the unanimous and devoted applause of all — then the celebrated philosopher's book, despite its unusual volume (more than a thousand pages of text), ought to be placed beside the ingenious pamphlets of Swift, which are eternal masterpieces of the art of laughing in the English manner.

But the critical reflections of An Autobiography cannot be taken as ingenious "boutades" [bons mots]; rather, they exhibit the entire naive and painful mental limitation of one who, after reading the Iliad and the Odyssey "with sociological intent," found Homer to be, on the whole, a disagreeable author.

The evolutionary thinker, preoccupied with the supreme formula of his own philosophy — according to which the heterogeneous always implies an advance over the homogeneous — detested with heartfelt cordiality the "childish habit" of employing without restraint the Homeric epithets, so gallant and uniform, in phrases such as these: "the well-greaved Greeks"; "the long-haired Achaeans"; "the horse-taming Trojans." He found it intolerable to specify the pedigree of a celebrated horse at the very moment of describing a battle, as well as the constant accumulation of "bloody and brutal" episodes. "Homer," comments his reader, "is insufferably childish"...

Dante too proves monotonous and "homogeneous" — that is, inferior. Upon reading him one would wish for a poetic substance less uniformly opulent. His tercets would seem to be, as it were, sumptuous garments ill arrayed. Decidedly, Herbert Spencer was not disposed toward the Epic. England, in opening for his mortal remains the glorious doors of Westminster Abbey, might have the singular honor of enclosing in the grave of the author of First Principles the greatest adversary of the Apollonian Genius. Nothing so well as sincerity — even when it borders on barbarism — serves to cover with the admiration of a great people the tomb of one of its heroes. May the intrepid thinker rest in peace.

And lyric poetry kept pace with the Epic in Spencer's "scale of values."

Wordsworth, for instance, is not intense. He does not move one to leap for joy. "He should have written in prose." Meter was made for the emotional outburst. The poetry of the subtle and intimate poet is "rather beer than wine." Is there anything as decisive as these autobiographical confessions? One must thus disdain, without respect for genius or scruples of conscience. Herbert Spencer had every right to abominate monotony — he above all others, who was assuredly one of the least monotonous authors in the world...

Now read this page devoted to Plato. It deserves to be engraved in the depths of your memory. It is an example — as Nietzsche would say — of the "transvaluation of values" in literature: "Several times I attempted to read one or another Platonic dialogue, and always closed the book with impatience, on account of the imprecision of the thought and the habit of convincing by verbal arguments. I was also displeased by the rambling form of the discourse. On one occasion I communicated my impressions to an erudite acquaintance, who replied: 'Indeed, but the Platonic colloquia deserve to be read as works of art.' I returned to them, but was compelled to set them aside at once, more exasperated than before. To call a dialogue the dialectical exchange between a thinker and his own shadow — which says precisely what is necessary for him to reply to — is something absurd. There is more dramatic truth in the conversations of our third-rate novelists. A book like Rameau's Nephew [Le Neveu de Rameau] by Diderot contains more accurate observations than all the dialogues of Plato." O candor of autobiographical confidences! What a touching attitude it is that esteemed as "more dramatic" the novel of Diderot or The Last Days of Pompeii by Sir Bulwer Lytton than the Apology, the Phaedo, or the Symposium! A declaration of this kind would reconcile the most hardened misanthrope with the intrinsic goodness of human nature.

Music, in its turn, kept pace with Lyric poetry.

Let the last specimen of Spencer's artistic taste be furnished by his judgment on Meyerbeer and Wagner. In Facts and Comments, Meyerbeer, an unquestionable type of false grandeur, of purely theatrical and decorative grandiosity, is honored as a "great dramatic musician." Wagner, on the other hand, is the author of "phrases that can be foreseen" and — in accordance with the unassailable judgment of George Eliot, which Spencer naturally adopts — "lacked dramatic sense"...

Our most beloved Alfonso Reyes tells of Ventura de la Vega that, "in articulo mortis" [at the point of death], he gathered about him his family and intimate friends, prepared to die as a Christian, and said to them: "My friends, before bidding you farewell forever, suffer one last confession of my literary conscience: I cannot stand Dante!" And he lay back to sleep with seraphic beatitude the eternal sleep. The trouble with Mr. Herbert Spencer's case is that it was not only Dante — as with Ventura de la Vega — but Plato and Homer as well that he "couldn't stand."

## LAS NUEVAS IDEAS Y LAS VIEJAS IDEAS — NEW IDEAS AND OLD IDEAS

The struggle for life is a universal law, far more universal than Darwin supposed. Not only do living beings struggle; languages struggle, laws struggle, inventions struggle, ideas struggle. In the immaterial world of the spirit as in the world of life, that which does not struggle neither breathes nor triumphs. Repose is a rational illusion, an "Egypticism" — as Nietzsche would say — a mummification.

But the notion of struggle is inseparable from that of advantage. One struggles in order to acquire a new property, an exclusive domain. The struggle between old and new ideas is more dramatic than the vicissitudes of action; the blows, the charges, and the victories are silent in the world of the spirit, but not on that account dispassionate or any less vehement. Perhaps it is true to say that spiritual movement is more intense and heroic than that of vital and organic action. A language is an organism of ideas and longings; a religion synthesizes hopes and profound redemptions; a philosophy is an invasive and rebellious being that strives to dissolve the enemy it finds before it, with the corrosive and stinging force of persuasion and dialectic. To think is a drama superior, at times, in cruelty and energy to the most tragic scenes of Hamlet or Lear.

The whole world is strife and drama — that is, creation and evolution, "creative evolution," as Bergson says, or rather "evolutionary creation"; invention and imitation, novelty and repetition, ascending life and descending life, genius and custom. One might say that at the heart of existence there is a power of interminable flowering, a source of perpetual transformation; but if every dawn announces an advent and every dusk carries down into non-being something that will never again appear upon the stage of time, it is equally true that life channels itself and orders itself, once created, into a repetition that tends to become identity and monotony, an undiscriminating and absurd reproduction.

Nothing is absolutely new and nothing entirely old. The most gifted inventor has antecedents, and the most ordinary man has original moments. Each person possesses, if not precisely genius, at least a personal and singular flair, individuality, personality. The common crowd exists only for the commonplace. Humanity, like nature, is at once variety and uniformity. Each day the work of God is new, even if not absolutely new.

Old ideas maintain on their side the merit of having served to order the life that was already lived, the world that was already formed, the time that has already passed. For this reason they are respectable — because life was lived under their dominion. Existence adopted them for itself; they shaped it according to their will. They were its venerable teachers; they were successful, and above all, they were once new. They ought not, therefore, to be so austere and dogmatic. Their exclusivism kills them. When something loses the divine plasticity of the spirit and clothes itself in a stony carapace, it is about to die. The dead are rigid, inextensible, solid as matter itself. Thought is the most agile thing in existence, the most unexpected and elegant thing in history. To make use of hidden paths, of unforeseeable resources, of magical sudden adaptations — to think victoriously — is to fly without obstacles, to glide without mishap, like the waters of a stream over the pebbles that the current smoothed in its passage.

Old ideas are often venerable encumbrances. A conservative is a burdensome and absurd being. He does not live in the present but in the past; his head is turned perpetually backward, like Lot's wife who turned to stone on the road from Sodom. An incorrigible romantic, he lives lamenting the passage of time that carries him farther from his dearest dream. Yet he forms the weight of history, the lead that regulates the flight, the ballast that makes action possible. Without him, life would be a catastrophe...

The "snob" is the other trivial aspect of human energy. His attitude is delirious, enthusiastic, "futuristic." He would like to exchange signals with the world yet to come, but he loses contact with the earth; he does not know how to wait, does not know that the good may never arrive and yet be truly good. He lives by repudiating existence and wanders, at times, into the groves of the madhouse. Prudence and reason are obstacles to him. He is a romantic of the future, of what has not yet been born, of what will perhaps never be born — another absurd being.

The misoneist is a lunatic of the past. The "snob" is a madman of the future. Both are charlatans — perhaps tragic charlatans in some endeavors, but charlatans always. Both constitute two charming false directions; they console one for life and strengthen it because they are incapable of understanding it. They are not wise; they are ingenuous lovers of Our Lady Tradition or Our Lady Fashion...

There are old ideas like those useless objects that accumulate in houses and impede easy, vital movement. These ought to be killed definitively, as primitive peoples do with their decadent and inept elders. On the other hand, there are new ideas with the dangerous instability of explosives. They must be handled with prudence, with knowledge, with human dignity. Auguste Comte conceived the law of social life as an alliance of order and liberty. Liberty is order, not disorder. Nothing is absolutely disordered. When one order disappears, a new one emerges.

"Wings and lead," as Bacon said; boldness and habit, invention and imitation, creation and evolution — this is history. If genius were to prevail over custom, we would all be mad. If custom were to impose itself over genius, we would all degrade ourselves until we became instinctive, purely organic beasts. Might Leibniz have been right? Might we be in the best of all possible worlds?...
