[p. 9] Poetic intuition, or artistic creation, is the resultant of two forces that are certainly not mutually exclusive but are opposed: the CONATIVE movement of ideas, and the obstacle that ordinary life experience always sets against the EMPATHIC PROJECTION of the empirical self.

## Preliminar — Preliminary Note

[p. 13] The object we set ourselves in writing these Principles of Aesthetics is, preferably, to bring to converge upon a synthetic point, obtained through the application of a firm criterion, the body of fundamental doctrines and ideas that have succeeded one another in the elucidation of the problems of art and beauty, and that, without a break in continuity, stretch back to classical antiquity and are refined and perfected, at times, in contemporary speculation.

Philosophical ideas possess a certain form of immortality that is peculiar to them. A given concept, older than Plato, is renewed and defined in the dialogues of the great philosopher, to be refined and strengthened still further in the work of Aristotle and Plotinus, and to flourish in the mystical psychologism of *Einfühlung*. The idea of beauty as disinterestedness appears in Kant, is elaborated and combined with the voluntarist metaphysics of Schopenhauer, and inspires the most definitive pages of Bergson's famous book entitled *LE RIRE*. The thought of assimilating art and play already exists in Kant; it is developed [p. 14] afterward in Schiller's Letters, inspires what is fundamental in Spencerian aesthetics, and finally becomes, in the scientific literature of our time, one of the commonplaces of Aesthetics.

There are sciences, such as Sociology and Aesthetics itself, that today call above all for a critical spirit of weighing and organization; for authors frequently develop their own thought, discreetly and boldly, at times with genius, but take no care to relate their constructive work to other theses arising from the analysis of aesthetic problems from a different point of view.

All the essential aesthetic ideas seem to us to be summed up in the theory of intuition, or creative projection, which the reader will find set forth in the eighth chapter of this book.

Our work will take care, above all, to prove that there is now available, in our day, a sufficient stock of aesthetic ideas which, suitably related to one another, make up not a repertory more or less original, interesting and copious, but a body of theories on the problems of art, most worthy of standing beside Logic and Ethics.

If we are to declare our whole opinion, we would venture to affirm that moral science has not yet attained, in what is fundamental, the precision and harmony of aesthetic knowledge.

The reader will find in what follows no essays of [p. 15] artistic or literary criticism, nor chapters of reasoned history of literature, music, and the visual arts. This book aspires to be solely one of philosophy. It is a pure investigation of aesthetic ideas and values. Whoever seeks in it anything different may suffer disappointments, which we spare him, of course, by means of this prior warning<sup>1</sup>.

## Capítulo I. La demasía vital o potencia superflua — Chapter I. Vital Excess or Superfluous Power

[p. 19] Trivial is the experiment recommended in elementary physics treatises to prove the porosity of matter. A small brass sphere, attached to a tube of the same metal, both filled with water, is subjected to the action of a hydraulic press. At the start of the experiment the sphere is dry on the outside. If the pressure increases, the water passes through the pores of the brass and covers the metallic vessel with a slight but perceptible dew. What has happened? Surely a sufficient increase in the energy transformed into pressure produced a new fact. Before, the sphere was dry on its outer surface. The pressure increased, and now it shows moisture; yet not every increase in the force pressing on the wall of the vessel engenders the new phenomenon that demonstrates the porosity of brass; only a certain pressure is capable of engendering it. That is, a quantitative increment of the cause has visibly engendered a qualitative differentiation.

Take a bar of tin between both hands and try to bend it; the tin will creak as it submits to the force that diffuses through its molecules [p. 20]. (This is already a new phenomenon. The tin, which was silent, now impresses our consciousness through the sense of hearing.) Continue bending the creaking bar; its creaking will grow more intense, and there will come a moment when the rupture of the solid mass will have occurred. Once again, the quantitative increment of the cause determined the qualitative variation.

Now it is a bladder being inflated. If the tension of the gas inside increases, the new effect is determined, *ipso facto*. The burst is an unheard-of event in the history of a bladder that gradually increases in volume. What a mysterious relation between the two irreducible categories, quantity and quality...!

Often, in the world, homogeneous phenomena become more complex. Such is the case with supercooling. Phosphorus melts at forty degrees. One can lower the temperature gradually, well below forty degrees, without the phosphorus ceasing to remain liquid; but if a fragment of solid phosphorus is thrown in, the whole mass immediately solidifies. If a violin string vibrates and a harp stands nearby, the harp's strings will vibrate in unison with those of the violin. Fire a dynamite cartridge, and you will provoke the explosion of other cartridges close by.

Another interesting example. When calcium carbonate is heated in a closed vessel, the solid decomposes into lime and carbon dioxide, until this latter [p. 21] gas acquires, in the container, a certain pressure that depends on the temperature, and depends on it alone. It is what chemists call dissociation tension. If the carbon dioxide is removed, it forms anew through the decomposition of the carbonate; but if it is introduced again into the container, it combines with the free lime until the dissociation tension is restored.

Probably all the phenomena of Nature are produced by vibration. The universe is rhythmic. Science has discovered it step by step, beginning with the famous experiment of Pythagoras. Art intuited it from the first day. Sound, light, heat, electricity are vibratory movements. Sound vibrations vary from 32 to 73,000 per second. Heat vibrations begin on reaching the figure of one hundred thirty-four trillion (134.000,000.000,000.000,000) per second, and come to be luminous, that is, visible, at 483 trillion per second. And the noblest of the luminous vibrations of the rainbow, the color violet, rises to the figure of 708 trillion per second. If the vibration increases, our imperfect senses do not perceive it; but by means of certain reagents we can perceive it.

We are then dealing with chemical phenomena. Hence, in the physical world, the quantitative increment of the cause produces not only the correlative multiplication of effects but their qualitative differentiation. That is, the Universe [p. 22] is rich and varied only because it signifies, constantly, a prodigious congestion of energies.

And History, like Nature, confirms the principle in all its parts. The division of social labor is, if not the fundamental fact of historical evolution, one of its preponderant factors. Now, the cause of the division of labor is, as Durkheim<sup>1</sup> clearly set forth, the increase of population. If the population grows, the needs of the many differ qualitatively from the needs of the few; that is, not only are the effects increased, but they are multiplied and diversified in an extraordinary manner.

<sup>1</sup> *De la Division du travail social*. Alcan. París.

Population density determines a double action; for it compels the search for new modes of production, and demands objects and services that previously would have had no reason to exist. With the increase of material and moral density, the field of rivalry and struggle widens. More things are desired, and of better quality. Luxury (a phenomenon homogeneous with art and play) consists in creating habits that are imitated and end by being indispensable to all. Moreover, the division of labor, determined by the pressure of the collective mass upon the individual, organizes law and the other forms of subjective solidarity. On the other hand, inventions and discoveries flourish [p. 23], as Tarde<sup>1</sup> proved, where there is most imitation; since invention is a process of complex imitation. Where there is most imitation there is most invention; and there is more imitation where there are more individuals to imitate. That is, the increase of population makes social groups more intelligent. The congestion of the human mass in the great cities is the characteristic fact of modern civilization.

<sup>1</sup> *Les Lois de l'imitation*. Alcan. París.

At the beginning of the Revolution, France had twenty-five million inhabitants; today it has forty. Germany, in the sixteenth century, had twelve million; today it reaches the figure of sixty. England possessed two million in the Renaissance, and today reaches forty-three; and the United States, the most vigorous and stupendous of the national organizations of the world, has risen, in a single century, from five million to a hundred. The law verified in the physical world, as we saw above, is verified equally for the moral world. It is a true cosmological principle, whose enunciation has a full scope: The quantitative increment of the cause produces not only the correlative multiplication of effects but their qualitative differentiation.

The vital force, said Claude Bernard, "directs phenomena that it does not produce. Physical agents produce phenomena that they do not direct"<sup>2</sup>. Life is an essential purpose of appropriation.

<sup>2</sup> *Leçons sur les phénomènes de la vie*. I, 51.

[p. 24] To nourish oneself is to work the physical environment into exclusive property; to impose one's own constitution on the surroundings, making them internal and homogeneous, from the external and heterogeneous they were. In a living being, function is the only thing that is new. Form and matter are two cosmic facts, more or less complex. From the chemical point of view, the organism of a superior man lies, wholly, in the materials and forces that surround him. Nevertheless, the supreme novelty of this exquisite structure lies in what Bernard called the vital force, director of phenomena that it does not produce. In a beehive, three or four days after laying, the eggs break and the whitish larva emerges, which the nurse bees will tend according to the sex of the individual that is to be produced. The nurses engender queens or workers. When a swarm loses its queen and there is no female larva in the hive, the workers hasten to demolish the partitions of several cells in order to form the royal chamber, and the larva, abundantly nourished, becomes a queen instead of an ordinary worker. An increment of the cause produced, as always, the qualitative differentiation. Materially, we human beings are an indiscernible part of the great whole. Vitally and psychologically, we are an individual, that is, something unique and unmistakable; and, still more, a consciousness, that is, an individual that knows itself as an individual being. It is the case to repeat Pascal's profound expression: "Man is a feeble reed; but a reed that [p. 25] thinks." Admirable vital excess; strange superfluous power!...

Strictly speaking, for life to be, it would have sufficed to carry out the two elementary functions in their primitive or primordial forms: nutrition and reproduction; but life, through the external action of the environment, has had to adapt to the most varied and dissimilar conditions or perish; and, since it has not perished, it must have possessed within itself a surplus, a plastic directing force, sufficient to accomplish, with ever greater perfection, its imperialist and invasive end.

Will it be impossible to discover the origin of life? Science should never pronounce the word impossible. Only what implies contradiction is impossible; but, among possible things, there are the improbable, and one of these is the fruitful search for vital energy by the means at the disposal of Chemistry. By contrast, according to spiritualism, it is relatively simple to perceive the nature of life.

Function, which the materialists think they create, is first for the spiritualist: the will to live of which Schopenhauer speaks. The organ is a product of function, of the energetic congestion that brings about the prosperity of each biological individual; of fattening, of superfluous power, of the economic capitalization found in every living thing.

Thus vital energy is not summed up in a simple transmutation of physico-chemical forces, but in a new unstable, superabundant, [p. 26] explosive power, which directs, without producing them, the physical and chemical phenomena of the organism.

Suppose you succeed in removing from your personal egoism the awareness that you are egoists, and you will have the notion of the blind impetus that one day fell upon matter, and has spurred it ever since, without ceasing.

And why does it spur it? Because it always gives itself in excess; because no living being expends itself strictly in being alive, without excreting from itself another individual who, in living, will give from itself yet another. Which already proves, in the elementary function of reproduction, the intrinsic superabundance of life. Every obstacle accumulates upon itself the propulsive force that seeks to overcome it, and is in the end its belated ally.

Each organ, each apparatus or system, digestive or circulatory, respiratory, muscular, nervous; each organism, animal or vegetable, unicellular or multicellular, is the promise of another better adapted. The vital process is one of successive and dramatic triumph, shaded episodically with constant partial defeats: illness and death. In the long course of beings, evolution is purely organic, vegetative or animal, until man is reached. Progress in plants and beasts is of a biological kind. Each new species of beings signifies the employment of the accumulated capital in the more efficient refinement of organs and the economic complication of functions.

When universal life succeeded in forming the human being, progress took a different trajectory. [p. 27] Goethe devised the vertebral theory of the skull; he deduced, as he himself says, the bones of the skull from the vertebra itself, explaining thereby, at once, their form and their function. And he added reflections of great scientific restraint concerning the fact of his discovery. This vertebra, hypertrophied and enormous, represents the best apparatus, the most precious weapon, the most powerful powder magazine of life. Progress is no longer organic or biological, but immaterial and psychic. From many points of view man is inferior to the mammals, the fishes, and the birds. He cannot, like vessels of scales, furrow the bosom of the seas; nor, like the birds, beat the atmosphere with his oars. He has not the lynx's sight, nor the lion's claws, nor the bulk of the sperm whale or the elephant, nor the dog's sense of smell, nor the agility of the monkey or the squirrel, nor the instinctive infallibility of insects, nor the perfect sense of direction of homing pigeons; but he has something better than all that: the power of his intelligence. With it he has made up for the wings of birds by creating the airplane, for the mobility of fish in the watery depths by inventing the submarine, for the mass of the sperm whale and the elephant by imagining the tanks of war; the telescope and spectral analysis, to witness from his dwelling the spectacle of the composition of the stars and decipher their luminous messages; conquests all of which are nothing but the most imperfect prelude, the infantile babbling, of the conquests of tomorrow. Man assumes over the animals no more than a spiritual [p. 28] superiority; but so great, so immeasurable and sovereign, that it alone proves to us the new meaning of the universal progress of life: On reaching our species, energy, vital excess, the superfluous power of which Schiller speaks, after attempting innumerable unsuccessful trials, only relatively unsuccessful, each time less deficient, abandoned the road it had followed for so many hundreds of centuries, set the human animal upright on his two feet, made him look at the sky, and prepared his daring, to launch it like an enchanted arrow into the infinite. Man is the true superman, the only enchantment. Human life might be called: the unfailingly progressive.

From this energy accumulated in the course of the ages, from this excessive, luxurious, most brilliant force; from this unprecedented congestion, unforeseeable and most luxuriant fruits have sprung; and among them two that are the most extraordinary, and form the supreme distinction of the lineage: Art and the Spirit of Sacrifice.

Let us leave it to the moralists to elaborate the difficult philosophy of self-denial (which for us implies the mystery of the world), and let us undertake, on the basis of this concept of the superabundance of life, the study of aesthetic problems. Already Hemsterhuis, in the eighteenth century, declared: "Beauty is what affords the most pleasure, because it gives us the greatest number of ideas in the least time. The enjoyment of the beautiful surpasses all others, because it gives us the greatest quantity [p. 29] of thoughts in the smallest space of time." Beauty would be, in this way, a supreme mental conglobation, engendering delightful plenitudes; an ecstatic fruition by superabundance. In our opinion, opulence or vital excess is an irrefutable condition of artistic production; but aesthetic intuition proves irreducible to this one principle. Complex facts frequently arise from the joint action of numerous causes, and Art is, perhaps, the most individual and complex of the great forms of the collective thought of humanity. Its condition is rich and triumphant life, but not its sole cause. Architecture, sculpture, painting, poetry, and music are luxury and ease in human living; but not only that.

[p. 31]

## Capítulo II. El juego y el arte — Chapter II. Play and Art

[p. 33] The preceding chapter is only the cosmological introduction to Aesthetics. It demonstrates, as we believe, the mere possibility of artistic activity, as a corollary of the energetic superabundance of the world. Art is possible from the moment that life manifests itself as a force capable of new creations, being constantly urged by external obstacles that it cannot at once overcome, but which it finally surmounts by accumulating and organizing itself internally. Every considerable, persistent hindrance acts as a stimulant that in the end stamps its seal upon the organism, modifying its composition and its form, and contributing to its future victory. It would be impossible to understand organization without obstacles; but neither without the plastic power, primary and fundamental, of every living being. Life invents new victorious arrangements; but it is slow to invent them. The root, the hand, the tongue, and thought are stages of one and the same effort. All is one and the same. Daring and more daring! Those who tire, die; but life goes on working.

It would be difficult to find in the history of literature a poetic nature more devoted to the feeling [p. 34] of freedom, and more jealous of the distinguishing attributes of humanity, than the poet Schiller. A great thinker as well as a great poet. He has bequeathed to us in his book *Ueber die asthetische Erziehung des Menschen*, above all in the fourteenth and twenty-seventh letters, the principle of a theory of Art that grows ever more important in contemporary philosophical literature.

Schiller sets out in the following terms the dilemma formed, on the one hand, by man's animal condition and his consequent submission to natural laws, and, on the other, by the moral dictates of reason, imperative and absolute: "Our nature, equally incapable of remaining animal any longer and of pursuing the subtle labors of reason, demands an intermediate state that reconciles both contradictory states, transforming harsh tension into gentle harmony and easing the passage from one to the other. Only the aesthetic sense, or feeling for the beautiful, is able to render us this service..." "The play instinct, in which the instinct of the senses and the instinct of creation are united, will impose itself, morally and physically, upon the soul; and, by suppressing all chance, it will suppress, in turn, all necessity, giving man moral and physical freedom..." "Language signifies, by the name of play, everything that is neither subjectively nor objectively accidental, neither externally nor internally necessary." In sum, Schiller's thought could be formulated by saying that the soul floats, in playing, between [p. 35] rational law and organic necessity. By dividing itself between the two, it frees itself from their double dominion, and creates, from itself and its freedom, a new world.

This position of the sphere of Art, intermediate between the material and the moral world, Schiller surely derived from Kantian thought. The aesthetic sense is a bond between intelligence and will. It has neither theoretical nor practical reach. The *Critique of Judgment* defines the middle point between the theory of reason, which in Kant's work is called the *Critique of Pure Reason*, and the theory of will, which is called the *Critique of Practical Reason*.

But the very idea of relating Art to Play, which Schiller, as we have said, took from Kant and developed amply, appears for the first time in these concise terms of the book cited above: "Free play of the imagination, in accord with the laws of the understanding." In this phrase one of the principal characteristics of aesthetic pleasure is surely enclosed. Man, declares Schiller, is fully man only when he plays. "Wherever we find the signs of a free and disinterested appreciation of pure appearance, we may affirm that a total revolution has taken place in the nature of man; and that humanity has properly begun in him..." "When man does not harass the lion, nor does another beast challenge him to combat, idle strength creates for itself an object of its own. The animal fills the echoes of the desert with its valiant [p. 36] roar; superfluous power enjoys itself, spending itself in efforts without purpose." Kant had already given as a characteristic of aesthetic judgment, in one of his celebrated definitions, that of being a freely performed action, purposiveness without purpose, or, what at first sight seems entirely the same: play, an activity carried out for the sake of carrying it out, only to accomplish it, with no ulterior design.

In the same excellent twenty-seventh letter, Schiller contrasts the attitude of the animal that works with that of the animal that plays. If it works, it is because something is lacking to it and it tends to procure it; if it plays, it is because it is moved by richness, by abundance of forces, or, as the poet says: "Life, superfluously swollen, spurs itself on to action."

The ideas of Kant and Schiller were at once developed and mutilated in the philosophical work of Herbert Spencer. According to the English thinker, the acts we call play and those we call aesthetic are intimately linked by this fundamental trait: neither the one nor the other serves, by any direct means, the useful processes of life.

The higher animals are not exclusively devoted to the demands of their immediate needs. The mental powers are subject to the law by virtue of which their organs, after having rested longer than they usually do, awaken exceptionally fit for action; and it then happens that they readily engage in a [p. 37] simulated activity, if circumstances provoke it, instead of being incited to a real one. Hence arise games of every kind, and the tendency to the superfluous and useless exercise of activities that have remained at rest. Hence also arises the fact that purposeless movements are manifested, most often, by the faculties that play a dominant role in the animal's life.

The same principle governs the combination of sensations that arouses ideas and feelings of beauty. The movements of the body, agreeable to him who performs them, are of such a kind that they awaken many muscles to moderate action without violently fatiguing any of them. Therefore, one may believe that those formal arrangements are beautiful which effectively exercise the greatest number of nervous elements collaborating in perception, and overburden only the smallest number of those same elements. The same holds for the complex visual wholes presented by real objects, or for representations of objects with their light, their shadows, and their particular coloring. The highest aesthetic feeling will be that which has the greatest volume, engendered by the normal exercise of the greatest number of energies, without any of them being exercised abnormally; or else, the feeling resulting from the full and not excessive exercise of the most complete emotional faculty<sup>1</sup>. At the bottom of the Spencerian theses lies the theory [p. 38] of the animal that works and plays, which we have seen developed in the "Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man;" but, as we set forth earlier, in being developed the idea was mutilated; for the English thinker, faithful to his agnostic principles, had to strip it of that transcendental sense of creation and freedom, of that joy in being a cause, of the supreme enjoyment of being able to do (*Freude am Können*), which stands out so prominently in Schiller's pages. It is always the English spirit, trimming German ideas from its exclusive, insular, materialist point of view; but it cannot be denied that, in failing to understand a certain part of the Kant-Schiller theory, the Briton ingeniously combined the practical portion accessible to his positivism with the indefectible thought of cosmic evolution, which has explained so many things satisfactorily in the books of Spencer and the other evolutionist disciples of his, and has disguised or obscured so many others in claiming to elucidate them.

<sup>1</sup> *Principles of Psychologie*. Part VIII. Ch. IX.

Schiller's generous theory is reduced, in sum, for Spencer, to these bare terms in which Tolstoy formulates it, in his pamphlet entitled "What Is Art?:" "In the lower animals, all vital energy is employed in securing individual life and that of the race; but in man, when his instincts are satisfied, there remains an excess that is consumed in games, first, and afterward in art." What value has the hypothesis?

To begin with, language itself and common sense [p. 39] corroborate the theory of the arts as games, as diversions. It may be said of every great aesthetic theory—as we shall verify in due course—that common opinion confirms the point of view of speculation.

It is obvious that neither art nor play can be conceived without a surplus of energies (*over-flow*). Both are the result of the superabundance of life; but many other accessory formations could be likened to them by reason of the same origin. Play and art would represent—and this is the true point of their positive intimacy—a kind of purposiveness without purpose, as Kant would say; that is, an action that transcends only indirectly, and that would carry its object implicit within itself. The true player would play for the sake of playing, not to win, which would be to admit a spurious interpretation of the spirit that guides those who devote themselves to this exercise. The true artist, too, would carry out his action by the very fatality of his nature, not to fulfill other extrinsic ends. In this respect, both fruits of life would agree.

But if the modality of art and play seems, at first sight, the same, their quality differs. All play, or at least most of it, is an imitation of combat, in animals as in man; or it is an act with a sexual background, or a simulation of defense and attack: in sum, of the great biological law that affirms: "To be is to struggle; to live is to conquer."

[p. 40] An animal, superior or inferior, human or non-human, obeys, by reason of its own vital economy, the principle that makes every living thing a fighter. If there is an excess, a *surplus*, the remaining force is spent in the same habitual way of burning and expending it. In the animal no principle of disinterestedness can reside; and what has been formed instead is an instinct of play, as Karl Groos maintains. At bottom, the theory of Groos and that of Spéncer do not seem to us opposed but mutually supportive; and this is the opinion of the French psychologist Théodule Ribot<sup>1</sup>. To speak of disinterested play, says Souriau, is not to know what one means: when we play, we are always concerned with the result of the activity. Finally, Grosse<sup>2</sup> teaches: "Between practical and aesthetic activity there stands, as a form of transition, play. Play is distinguished from art because, like practical activity, it always has an external end; and it is distinguished from practical activity because its emotional value, like that of art, does not depend on the external end, which is rather insignificant, but on the activity itself. The relations that practical activity, play, and art bear to one another can be formulated graphically by representing the first term as a straight line, the second as an undulating line, and the third as a circle."

<sup>1</sup> See: Karl Groos. Opus cit.

<sup>2</sup> "Los comienzos del Arte." Spanish translation by Pedro Umbert. Barcelona, 1906.

[p. 41] In our opinion, practical activity, play, and art are three different things; but, as Grosse very well expresses it, there are more resemblances between art and play than between art and practical activity. By reason of their origin, art and play proceed from organic excess; yet this excess is not the sole cause of art. The opinions of Souriau and Grosse make clear how in play a certain purposiveness does not cease to subsist, latent; which would lead us to correct the absolute expressions we formulated before. Art, by contrast, is disinterested; it is founded not on an instinct of play but on an intuition that delights in seeing for the sake of seeing and hearing for the sake of hearing.

Play betrays its biological nature in its forms; art, in its own, speaks to us clearly of the contingency of the biological law and of triumphant spirituality. The artist, if he truly is one, intuits and expresses in his creation not things in their relations to our desires but in themselves; not an individual, a being, an animal, or any situation whatever for an end, but the individual, the being, the animal, or the situation considered in themselves.

Most of us human beings know how to intuit reality only in relation to a purpose; we pay attention to something only insofar as it can satisfy some end. For this very reason we do not understand existence in its individuality. Our egoism prevents us.

To sum up: Play, in the evolution of life, is a principle of liberation, as Schiller saw; [p. 42] but it betrays its animal and biological origin. How? Because it is neither fully disinterested (even though it signifies, through the superabundance of energy that sustains it, a forgetting, or at least a disregard, of the egoistic and economic end), nor does it cease to recall constantly, in its episodes, the sexual struggle or the harsh combat for existence. The instinct of play that leads the little girl to lull her doll, or that delights her in putting her little brother to sleep; the laborious game of chess that two international champions play before the expectation of the civilized world, are two imitations, two simulations of the biological struggle; and in both, to perceive the external end of which Grosse speaks is neither to corrupt their nature nor to make it spurious. "The plastic force is directed toward the ideal;" but it cannot yet act, in its productive quality, by laws of its own<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Schiller, Opus cit. Letter XVII.

Art signifies full disinterestedness, absolute purposiveness without purpose, the breaking of the animal law, through the transformation of the instinct of play into a new faculty: aesthetic intuition. The plastic force has reached the ideal and now moves in its own world. Beauty fills us with joy without our coveting the things that delight us. Pure art—sonata, poem, statue, temple, dance, etc.—never has a demonstrative or practical purpose. It suffices unto itself.

## Capítulo III. Teoría de la intuición estética — Chapter III. Theory of Aesthetic Intuition

[p. 45]

### Primera fase de su desarrollo.—Kant — First Phase of Its Development.—Kant

The "Critique of Pure Reason" comprises: I. A critique of sensibility (time and space are *a priori* forms of sensibility); II. A critique of the understanding (categories of quantity, quality, relation, and modality; or *a priori* principles of the understanding); III. The critique of reason (ideas of the thinking Self, the World, and God).

Nature, the object of the sciences, is intelligible only by virtue of the categories of the understanding. In other words, the principles of nature are those of the understanding.

The foundation of morality is the rational concept of freedom.

Hence between the "Critique of Pure Reason" and that of Practical Reason there seems to lie an impassable abyss.

Nevertheless, it is only by its use, speculative or practical, that the position of the subject of rational knowledge varies. The very same pure reason of speculative knowledge is the pure practical reason of morality [p. 46]. Or, what comes to the same thing: by reason of the unity of the subject of knowledge, Kant had to try to throw a bridge across both Critiques; but, because of ethical action itself, given that morality must act upon existence by modifying it, a bridge is also required, objectively, to link the world of nature with the world of consciousness.

If the world of causes is to bend to the world of the ideal, the "Critique of Judgment" will fill the void in the Kantian work, logical and ethical, by studying, first, the agreement of the aspects of nature with our own faculties (Aesthetics), and the agreement, also, of the objective ends of existence (Teleology). Hence the two parts of the "Critique of Judgment," namely: the critique of aesthetic judgment and the critique of teleological judgment.

Beauty asserts a contentment, a satisfaction. What contentment? What satisfaction? If something is asserted, according to Kant, it is judged, a judgment is made; and since every judgment implies the categories of the understanding, its *a priori* forms, in order to define beauty let us define its quality, its quantity, its relation, and its modality, and we shall have defined it.

The agreeable, the useful, the good, and the beautiful are satisfactions, contentments; what, then, will be the specific difference of beauty? The useful implies an end, an interest. (It will be useful if it fulfills its end; useless, if it does not. The key to a cupboard is useful if it opens the cupboard, useless if it fails in its end of opening it). The [p. 47] good has value in itself. A good conscience is absolutely good.

The agreeable is a pure subjective pleasure. The agreeable, the useful, and the good imply an interest. In all three, "the concept of an end is always enclosed, hence the relation of reason to willing (at least possible willing) and, consequently, a satisfaction in the existence of an object or of an action, that is, a certain interest<sup>1</sup>. The agreeable refers to inclination, the good to esteem, and the beautiful to contemplation. Hence, "an object that satisfies without any interest whatever, is beautiful."

<sup>1</sup> "Crítica del Juicio." p. 64. Trad. de Manuel G. Morente. Madrid.

"One must not concern oneself in the least with the existence of the thing, and must remain indifferent in its regard, in order to play the role of judge in matters of taste"<sup>2</sup>. The difference between the useful, the good, and the beautiful lies in the fact that the useful and the good are not desired for their own sake, but for the good they bring about. Disinterestedly we delight in the works of God. As beautiful can be the subtle cloud-glow that we could never reach as the gentle wave that kisses the shore and dies at our feet. We are, if we know how to appreciate its beauty, and while we endure in its contemplation, its docile friends, never its masters. The spirit ceases to will and to covet when the eye or the ear sees for the sake of looking or hears for the sake of listening. What a great pleasure this aesthetic pleasure is! The solitary wanderer who gazes at the stars in the [p. 48] peace of the night enjoys them as much as if they were all his. And if an earthquake or a hurricane frightens the common man, the artist takes them as a matter of mere contemplation. Everything is beautiful if one knows how to see or hear. Even the most disagreeable rhythm pleases, and Leonardo advises the painter to observe the capricious stains that dampness forms on walls. At the sight of those shapeless stains, the painter is spurred to create. Satisfaction or displeasure without any interest whatever: here is the secret of taste, as Kant says; of aesthetic intuition, as we prefer to say.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 65.

From the standpoint of quantity, the beautiful is that which, without a concept, pleases universally. No man of taste can wish that a thing be beautiful for him alone.

In this respect Kant himself recognizes the limit of intellectualism and the contingency of its law, when he speaks to us of the universal without a concept. How can something be universal without a concept, since the concept itself is the expression of universality? Because there exists a harmony between sensibility and understanding, a spontaneous accord, a relation that does not have the concept for its foundation, a free play of imagination and reason, without any determinate idea constraining them to a particular form of knowledge. The universal pleasure of beauty proceeds from that accord of the spirit with things; from that unification, we would say, essentially different from the logical act. Since all of us men are endowed with imagination, [p. 49] with understanding, we accord "freely and universally" with the poet, who writes:

"I have felt myself to be the drop of some hidden spring; in the throat of some bird I have been a note / and even perfume in the effluvia of the rosebush." / "But in my subjective realms, / Where I alone can enter, / A soul stirs with its exclusive joys, / Its own impulse and its particular grief"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> González Martínez. "La Muerte del Cisne." México, 1917.

And, although the poet's individuality is unique, all of us, if we have taste, as Kant would say, enjoy the pleasure of feeling ourselves a drop of some hidden spring, a note in the throat of some bird, and perfume in the emanations of a rose. Our faculties have played freely in the mystery of intuition. Hence the deep pleasure, *sui generis*, that we experience. This power of sympathy with existence is profoundly different from the logical relation between the subject and the object of knowledge. Aesthetic anti-intellectualism was born in the immortal book of the philosopher of Königsberg. Aesthetics now exists, differentiated from Logic. It remains only to break the scholastic molds of the "Critique of Judgment" in order to realize it fully. Schopenhauer will take the second step, sheltered beneath the august shade [p. 50] of Plato; and, in our own days, the great psychologist M. Henri Bergson, rejecting the Platonic myth, will accomplish the apotheosis of aesthetic intuition, freed from all logical formalism. Science, logic, industry are economic instrumentalisms. Art interrupts the universal law; it marks its limit in the human. It is another law of existence.

We consider it unnecessary to insist on the definition of the beautiful from the standpoint of relation; for we have already dwelt on the character of purposiveness without purpose, which assimilates play to art, in the immediately preceding chapter. Kant formulates the definition of the beautiful drawn from this third moment in the following terms: "Beauty is the form of the purposiveness of an object, insofar as it is perceived in it without the representation of an end." And the philosopher adds this clear illustration: "A flower, for example a tulip, is considered beautiful, because in its perception a certain purposiveness is found which, as we judge it, does not refer to any end"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 114.

Beauty, then, pleases not *materiáliter* but *formáliter*. Only by its form does it answer to the idea of purposiveness, but it depends on no determinate end, not even that of perfection. "The judgment of taste is entirely independent of the concept of perfection"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 98.

The character of necessity that Kant attributes to beauty does not require us to dwell on laborious [p. 51] commentaries, after what has been said above regarding the universality of aesthetic judgment. It is a matter of a subjective necessity, like universality itself. A *consensus* is presupposed which makes possible the commerce of aesthetic intuitions. Whence this fourth definition of the beautiful: "It is that which, without a concept, is known as the object of a necessary satisfaction." Kant's aesthetics rests, taken as a whole, on the subjectivity of the formal accord of the faculty of judging with the imagination. Beauty in itself is as unknowable as all noumenal existence.

To understand, in all its fullness, the enormous step Kant took in the discussion of aesthetic problems, it suffices to consider the profoundly economic nature of conceptual knowledge. Science is economy of action, an aspect of life, the most intelligent form of interest, a corollary of the law of the greatest gain obtained with the least effort. Art is disinterestedness. Before Kant, the logical categories had monopolized aesthetics. After him, the science of the beautiful assumes its autonomy.

The modern epistemological theory, which makes of reason an instrument of action, no less in James's pragmatism than in the speculations of the physicist Mach and the psychologist Bergson (to cite only a few of the most illustrious upholders of the tendency), brings out still better the [p. 52] principle of aesthetic disinterestedness, irreducible to the postulates of logic.

Moreover, play, after what has been said in this chapter, is relegated to the role of a mere activity intermediate between the purposiveness that governs life and pure purposiveness without purpose, at most when it aspires to attain it; and the character of disinterestedness, of contemplation, indifferent to good and evil, to truth, and even to the very existence of things (from which one must detach oneself "in order to play the role of judge in matters of taste," as Kant says), consecrates the ample and genuine sphere of beauty as a private region, as a world sufficient unto itself. This point will be seen more clearly confirmed still, as we proceed to the study of art as disinterestedness in the work of Schopenhauer, who poses in these simple and profound terms the problem of the metaphysics of the beautiful: "How is it possible for us to take pleasure in an object that has no relation to our will?"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Parerga y Paralipomena. Metafísica y Estética.

[p. 53]

## Capítulo IV. Teoría de la intuición estética (continuación) — Chapter IV. Theory of Aesthetic Intuition (Continued)

[p. 55]

### Segunda fase de su desarrollo.—Schopenhauer — Second Phase of Its Development.—Schopenhauer

No one better than Schopenhauer could, by reason of his own metaphysical system, have granted its full value to Kantian aesthetics. In the philosopher of the will, Kant's brilliant intuition, which sees in art the assertion of an innate disinterestedness, the realization of a purposiveness without purpose, had to attain its greatest scope; for nothing is more antithetical to the essential principle of pessimistic “etelismo” [sic; word uncertain in the source] than this indifference of contemplation, a kind of neutral point in the headlong course of the will, which constantly seeks its good and does not find it, except by denying itself.

Moreover, Schopenhauer was a great artist, not a dialectician of genius, like his incomparable master. The prose in which his thoughts on the metaphysics of the beautiful are written, both in the fundamental book entitled "The World as Will and Representation," and in the admirable fragments he called "Parerga and Paralipomena" (which are his true *cogitata et visa*), will last [p. 56] as long as German lasts, and will make its author one of the greatest thinkers of all time.

For Schopenhauer, the World, Universal Existence, psychic or not, is will. In man, the essential will is illumined with consciousness and reason. In animals it does not yet receive the full illumination that signifies the liberation of knowledge. In plants, the will to live acts dull and larval; and in the lowest degrees of being, in the mineral world, it ceases to receive the illumination that intelligence lends it. But in all beings, from the lowest to the highest, conscious or unconscious, the will to be is the primordial principle, Kant's thing-in-itself, the universal nucleus, constantly avid and, therefore, perennially consecrated to pain. The world is evil, because its law is always the unsatisfied will, which tries out all forms in order to convince itself, at last, as it is reflected in human representation, that all of them are bad and must be abolished in the purifying renunciation of the just man.

Art signifies a brief respite, sporadic and incomplete, in the tragic course of willing. Why?

The arts are, in the philosopher's words, "contemplation of things independently of the principle of reason"<sup>1</sup>, as opposed to that other contemplation, subject to the said principle, which is that of experience and of the sciences. Contemplation [p. 57] subject to the principle of reason has authority and is useful in practical life and the sciences; that which abstracts from the principle has authority and is useful in art. "The first is like an impetuous hurricane that runs without beginning or end, bending, shaking, and sweeping everything along with it; the second is the tranquil ray of sun that cuts across the hurricane's course, without being stirred by it. The first is like the drops of the cataract, which fall with violence and which, continually replaced by others, never stop; the second like the rainbow, which unfolds peacefully above all that tumult"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "El Mundo como Voluntad y como Representación." Book III. p. 29 of the Spanish translation.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 30.

The genius, subject of art, is distinguished by a preponderant aptitude for contemplation, which demands a complete forgetting of one's own person and of its practical relations with the world. In other words, it is the "objective direction of the spirit, as opposed to the subjective direction aimed at one's own person"<sup>2</sup>. Or, to say it all at once, the genius is the pure knowing subject, the limpid mirror of the world.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 30.

Imagination is an essential element of genius; but the two cannot be identified without error. Without imagination there is no genius; but neither is there without a fully objective intuition of reality. Intuition leaps over the things of the world perceived by the senses, which are always becoming, [p. 58] and never are; things whose existence might equally be called non-existence; it leaps over the perception of the shadows of real things that are drawn in the cave of the Platonic myth, and is unified with the eternal Ideas, without beginning or end, prototypes or archetypes of all their ephemeral copies.

Imagination enlarges the visual field of genius; it extends it beyond the objects that surround it, quantitatively and qualitatively; but, without objective intuition, it does not lead to the true work of art, expression of the Idea; "but to forging chimeras that flatter egoism or caprice, momentarily deluding, or amusing, the mass of the public, who put themselves in the dreamer's place, confusing true reality with the images that entertain them in their solitude"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 33.

The common man—a product of Nature's wholesale manufacture, which creates him by the thousands every day—is incapable of rising to the disinterested apperception that constitutes true contemplation. "He can direct his attention to things only insofar as they have some relation, however indirect, to his will"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 33.

This psychology of the common man is the most profound interpretation that can be given of the Kantian definition. For Schopenhauer, the aesthetics of disinterested intuition sets its truth, brilliant [p. 59] and lucid, against the dark and uniform background of the metaphysical principle of the will. Man ordinarily sees in order to accomplish some end; he hears in order to escape a danger or obtain an advantage; he does not look for the sake of looking, nor hear for the sake of listening. What matters to him is to relate the datum of the world, exterior or interior, to the trajectory of his own conduct, to the purpose of his egoism, to the aim of his decision. Hence, at most, he comes to be satisfied with scientific knowledge, which is always knowledge of relations, an extension of common knowledge. "He does not fix his gaze for long on an object, but quickly seeks the concept under which he could include all that is offered to him, as the lazy man seeks a chair, after which he does not trouble himself about it again"...

"He stops at nothing, he seeks his way through life, or, at most, the one that might someday be his way, and gathers topographical information, in the broadest sense of the word. As for the contemplation of life itself, he does not waste his time on it"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 30 of vol. II of the Spanish translation.

How different is the spiritual position of the artist! He, if he is truly one, will intuit and express in his creation, not things in their relations to our desires, but in themselves; not an individual, a being, an animal, or some situation or other [p. 60] for an end; but the individual, the being, the animal, or the situation, considered in themselves.

Most men, who are common, to be sure (but not so common as Schopenhauer thinks, because at least we enjoy, as a public, the work of artists, which argues against the irremediable vulgarity the pessimistic ideologue assigns us); we do not know how to intuit reality except in relation to a purpose; we pay no heed to anything except insofar as it can satisfy some preconceived end; for this very reason, precisely, we do not understand existence in its individuality. Our subjectivity, our egoism, prevents us.

Behold that *quídam* busy in his habitual action, which must provide him with sustenance. He takes from his surroundings only what can serve him for the end of his development, of his action in the world. He wants to obtain, with the least effort on his part, the greatest possible gain. He sees, to be sure, but he does not look, does not peer, does not penetrate. Because he covets, because he wants to subject reality to his dominion, he does not understand it. A man who passes by his side is nothing but a false, though useful, representative sign. He is, for example, simply this: a silk hat, a white beard, a gold-handled cane, and patent-leather shoes. The *quídam* who sees needs no more data. But the artist looks, peers, perceives, that is, intuits and expresses his intuition.

[p. 61] If the *quídam* perceived or intuited everything, he could not live. He would spend in the simple disinterested contemplation of a person as much time as a painter spends in studying and observing a subject he is about to portray. He would be left perplexed every evening watching the sun slowly sink in the miracle of twilight, and a human cry or lament would change for him, suddenly, the silver of speech and time, as Carlyle says, into the gold of eternity.

Suppose some abominable act of hatred and desolation. What does it matter! The artist will see it in its characteristic individuality, will present it without human interest in evil, will exhibit it like a clean and pure mirror. Shakespeare's dramas are full of intuitions of perverse ideas, and they do not move to crime but, on the contrary, turn away from it. They are cathartics of passion, purgatives of sin. Oedipus is based on incest, yet no one will wish to be incestuous like Oedipus. Sophocles' genius exhibits it in the limpid purity of art, in an expression that desire does not stain, that the will does not contaminate. "The painter," said Leonardo da Vinci, "must be universal and a lover of solitude; he must consider what he sees and reason with himself, choosing the most excellent parts of all the things he sees; acting like the mirror, which is transmuted into as many colors as are set before it; and in this way he will seem a second nature." Leonardo's second nature [p. 62] is the true one; the other is a fiction of egoism. If, then, for there to be a true work of art there must likewise be disinterestedness, no pure work will be sinful, even when it shows the most horrendous human actions.

Being, said Saint Thomas, is good in itself. Novalis adds: All true ontology begins in Aesthetics.

Next Schopenhauer explains the enjoyment we find in the contemplation of the beautiful. The will is the perennially needy, the insatiably afflicted, by its very essence. It can never cease to will, precisely because it is will; and for every desire that is satisfied, ten will spring up that will find no satisfaction. Moreover, we suffer not only from desiring pleasure; also from fearing misfortune. We are afflicted not only by present pain; we are also assailed by the past in memory and the future in fear.

"The subject of the will is always bound to the wheel of Ixion; he is condemned to fill the barrel of the Danaids; he is Tantalus, eternally thirsty;" but, in the moments of aesthetic contemplation, when we reflect the world without coveting or craving it, and approach the position of the pure subject of knowledge, we are no longer the agitated and opaque wave, with its plume of impotent rage, spitting at the sky its incomprehension and its egoism; but the smooth and uniform sheet of the still lake, which a bird barely ruffles with the subtle tip of its [p. 63] wing, and which doubles in its depths the luminous diversity of the day or the secret twinkling of the stars at night:

"For to be in love / Is like being in long communion; / A taste of pleasure, a taste of pain, / The Universe shines all around me / And I welcome its light in my heart." / "For my love is fire / And the whole world is consumed in it: / I understand pleasure, I understand pain, / To all things I give my fervor / And all of them yield me their drop of honey"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> E. Marquina. Elegias. Barcelona.

Or, as Schopenhauer splendidly says: "While contemplation lasts, we are free of the humiliating yoke of the will; forced laborers of the will, we celebrate a day of rest. The wheel of Ixion has stopped."

Aesthetic pleasure is a rest in the bustle of daily living; a merciful dittany that makes us see our own wounds as if through a dream. For an instant, we were not the afflicted actors of the tragedy, but its happy spectators. Pain is returning and will return!; but consciousness has been appeased, for an instant, in the serene contemplation of art. A transitory, insecure peace, but peace after all!...

[p. 65]

## Capítulo V. Teoría de la intuición estética (conclusión) — Chapter V. Theory of Aesthetic Intuition (Conclusion)

[p. 67] In his essay on the significance of the comic, the French psychologist M. Henri Bergson has sketched the foundations of an Aesthetics which, unless we are greatly mistaken, reproduces, in its fundamental thought, the theory of intuition that we have seen develop in the work of Kant and of Schopenhauer.

Strictly speaking, the essay cited, "on the significance of the comic," does not set out to construct a theory of Art, but only to define the essence of Comedy by opposing it to Tragedy, to Drama, and to the other forms of Art. But, as the author himself very well suggests<sup>1</sup>, in order to prove that the essence of comedy is the representation of general types, he must rise to the definition of the object of art itself, in general; that is, to formulating the foundations or principles of an Aesthetics.

<sup>1</sup> "Le Rire." p. 153.

M. Bergson is an intuitive nature of the first order and, at the same time, one of the great metaphysicians of our century. His style keeps pace with his psychological talent, and will hold, like that of Schopenhauer [p. 68], one of the loftiest places that impartial criticism may grant, in contemporary philosophical literature, to the vivid and lucid prose that expresses, with astonishing ease, the deepest, subtlest, and most varied shades of the spirit.

If reality, declares Bergson, were to impress our senses and our consciousness directly, and if we could enter into immediate communication with the external world and with ourselves, we should all be artists. But between Nature and our person; more than that, between ourselves and our own consciousness, a veil is interposed. This obstacle is thick for ordinary people; slight, on the other hand, almost transparent, for the artist and the poet. Why?

Because to live is to take things in the relation they bear to our needs. Life implies action; or, what is the same, the acceptance, and no more, of the useful impressions that objects offer us. The other impressions reach us only confusedly. "I look and think I see, I listen and think I hear, I study myself and think I read in the depths of my heart. But what I hear and see of the external world is simply what my senses extract from it to guide my conduct; what I know of myself is what rises to the surface, what takes part in action."<sup>1</sup> "My senses and my consciousness deliver to me, of reality, only a practical simplification..." "Things have been classified with a view to the advantage that can be drawn from them." "And it is this [p. 69] classification that I perceive, rather than the color and form of the things themselves..." "The individuality of things and beings escapes us whenever it is not materially useful to us to perceive it"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Le Rire." p. 156.

<sup>2</sup> El Mundo como Voluntad y como Representación. Book III p. 33.

Schopenhauer, as we have seen earlier, has recourse to the Platonic Ideas as objects of the pure subject of knowledge. That is, he believes in the Platonic myth as the object of aesthetic intuition. The phenomenal world of experience does not suffice him to explain beauty. This world is subject to the fourfold root of the principle of reason; but the artist and the poet, in the expression of the work of art, correct and select nature in its own defective realizations, and rise to the contemplation of the paradigms, of the archetypes, imperfectly copied in the economy of the world.

Bergson abandons, definitively, all Platonism, and explains to us, without leaving the field of experience, the object of contemplation. That world of concepts, which the common man seeks "as the lazy man seeks a chair," is the world of ordinary action, according to the French psychologist; a world that has been formed in our spirit to meet the urgent needs of action; and the sphere of art is constituted, not by the supernatural things and beings of Platonism; not by the inhabitants of the arcane and higher region of [p. 70] the Ideas, but by the very beings that experience offers; though seen without pragmatic preoccupation, without tending to prepare the imminent and future act of our conduct, of our will. Let us avail ourselves of an example to explain the French psychologist's profound epistemological and aesthetic thought. Suppose that a passer-by crosses the square of a great city. To the North rises a sumptuous palace of beautiful and noble architecture; to the South the cathedral displays its towers and domes; to the East, magnificent commercial buildings exhibit, in their shop windows, rich and varied products of industry; to the West a public garden offers its winding little paths, adorned here and there with some beautiful statues. If the passer-by were to resolve, like the artist or the poet, to see for the sake of seeing, with no further practical purpose, he would not take another step. He would stand perplexed before the individuality of the things around him, which nevertheless serve him only as practical signposts for continuing on his way and carrying out his purposes.

In other words, reality is one and the same: shadows of the cave and objects of aesthetic representation; incorruptible forms in art and transitory phenomena in life; paradigms and their copies. All Platonism is posterior to aesthetic intuition and not its cause. The Ideas that, to Schopenhauer, seem to precede life are merely stylizations formed *a posteriori*; artistic creations. One and the same world exists in the [p. 71] poem, the painting, or the sonata, and in trivial experience. The needs of action oblige us to fail to recognize it in its characteristic individuality.

Language effectively aids our failure to recognize what is genuine in individuals; "for words, with the exception of proper names, always designate kinds." "The word notes of the thing only its most common function and its banal aspect; it slips in between the thing and ourselves, and would hide its form from our sight, if that form were not already concealed behind the needs that created the word itself. And not only external objects: our own states of soul, too, are hidden from our consciousness in what they have of the intimate, the personal, the originally lived"<sup>4</sup>.

<sup>4</sup> Opus cit. p. 157.

Language, as the fruit of reason, is a great crystallizer of concepts and generalizations. That is why it is so difficult to express, and not merely to say. Croce has gone so far as to identify aesthetic intuition and its expression. If we say that we harbor or feel hatred, we do not express our passion. The brilliant poet, on the other hand, expresses it in its own individuality, as Shakespeare does in conceiving the figure of Iago. Merely civil communication is the work of everyone. The expression of a unique datum is the work of the artist, of the poet.

"From time to time, through absent-mindedness, Nature raises up souls more detached from life... If the detachment were complete and the soul no [p. 72] longer referred to action through any of its perceptions, it would be the soul of an artist such as the world has not yet managed to see. It would excel in all the arts at once, or, rather, it would fuse them into one, it would perceive all the colors and sounds of the material world, as well as the subtlest movements of the inner life. But that is asking too much of Nature. Even for the artists themselves it was by accident, and from one side only, that she lifted the veil. In one direction alone she forgot to tie perception to need. And since each direction corresponds to what is called a sense, it is through one of the senses, and through it alone, that the artist devotes himself to Art. Hence the diversity of the arts. Hence, too, the specialization of aptitudes"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 159.

In sum, art is a rupture grounded in the innate disinterestedness of the senses or of consciousness; a rupture with vulgar life, which enables us to grasp the proper nature of individuals in a new language, that of painting, sculpture, poetry, or music. The celebrated Kantian definition is corroborated in the aesthetics of Bergson, and given new vigor and strength thanks to the epistemological labors of our century. Idealism and realism in art conclude their perpetual peace; for only by withdrawing from trivial life do we arrive at the aesthetic intuition of true life. The human spirit has two different forms of [p. 73] knowledge: knowledge by reason and knowledge by intuition. The scientist, the philosopher, the mathematician know by genera and species, by general and abstract relations. The artist, the poet, the musician think intuitively.

When something is known because it is seen in itself, another kind of knowledge is superfluous. No one will ever prefer a description such as travel books offer to the direct vision of the countries described. The artist is like the traveler who intuits directly the countries he traverses. The reasoner is like those who, without moving from their corner, reconstruct panoramas and, by dint of reading, imagine the attributes of countries they have not seen. Artists know that, by an innate disposition of their nature, they are able to surprise the arcane singularity of the beings and forms they express in their productions. A painter does not know, intellectually, the attributes of his model. If he waited to attain external and intellectual knowledge before carrying out his work, he would never accomplish it. His action is instinctive. (Like that of the subtle arthropods which, by an innate sympathy, communicate with the life of their victims and subordinate it to their action)<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> See: F. H. Fabre. Moeurs des insectes. Delagrave. París.

We have more resources for communicating with existence than our false intellectual humility supposes; but false modesty-like all sophisms-soon turns into sin, [p. 74] into undoubted arrogance, if it declares that what is not obtained intellectually will not be obtained by any means. "Philosophy," Schopenhauer teaches, "has as much in common with art as with science." And Aesthetics, we shall add, found its fundamental truth in Kantian intuition, which Schopenhauer and Bergson, in concert, corroborate: art is disinterestedness. Yes, but is it disinterestedness only? This is what we shall resolve when we discuss the aesthetic ideas of Platonic mysticism, and of that other contemporary mysticism which makes of *Einfühlung*, of empathic projection, the essence of man's mythic and aesthetic activity. "Systems," Leibniz said profoundly, "are often true in what they affirm and false in what they deny." For, in exalting a single principle, one of the concurrent causes in a complex fact, they ignore the action of the others and, by affirming absolutely, deny. Aesthetic contemplation, grounded in disinterestedness, implies the superfluous power that makes it possible. It sufficiently distinguishes art from play; but without *Einfühlung* it does not suffice to explain that powerful total movement of the spirit which ends, victoriously, in the expression of aesthetic intuition, in the work of art as a being distinct from the creating artist; a fruit, now ripe, that detaches itself from the tree that bore it, to live its own life, objectively and not subjectively.

[p. 75]

## Capítulo VI. Teoría de la proyección sentimental (*Einfühlung*) — Chapter VI. Theory of Empathic Projection (*Einfühlung*)

[p. 77] We have said in the preceding chapters, in treating of the differentiation of aesthetic intuition as disinterested contemplation, that each great theory among those which have sought to elaborate a concept of art has on its side the affirmation of common sense. Thus the assimilation of art and play rests on the interpretation of this indubitable fact: play, as diversion, does not bear impressed upon it the character of purposiveness of the other acts of ordinary life. Only a later analysis, subtler and more perfect, is able to disentangle, in the mind of the player, the interest, latent and hidden but real, that distinguishes him from the artist; without this consideration, art and play are, for common sense, two diversions different in their species but unified in their genus.

The theory of disinterested intuition likewise has in its favor the corroboration of common sense. The poet, the artist, the scholar have always enjoyed, in everyone's opinion, the reputation of disinterested persons, of men to whom life does not matter with the pressing eagerness that spurs most of their fellows; rather, devoted to the undertaking that [p. 78] guides them, they live absorbed in their dream, fascinated by their speculation or their art, detached from the world and consecrated to the ideal.

The other great aesthetic idea that now occupies us has a long tradition in the history of human thought, beginning with Plato. In Plotinus's "Treatise on the Beautiful"<sup>1</sup> there are magnificent examples of this outpouring of the soul upon the things of the world, which is what constitutes *Einfühlung* or empathic projection. Meumann<sup>2</sup> finds the idea of *Einfühlung* in Herbart, for whom all beauty is expressive of an inner life and not pure external form. The fullness of fitness of the parts for the common end of the life of the whole is, according to Herbart, the essential content of beautiful forms; a fullness already realized in the highest manner in the human organism. Hence in aesthetic impression we interpret the visible forms of things by analogy with our own organism.

<sup>1</sup> Enéada I, book VI.

<sup>2</sup> "Introducción a la Estética Actual." Spanish translation by José J. Urríes Azara. Chapter V. p. 44.

The very name of empathic projection (*Einfühlung*) is owed to the German Romantics. To what Meumann has said we shall add that the French psychologist Jouffroy maintained, under the name of "aesthetics of sympathy," ideas close to the thesis of *Einfühlung*. "Inevitably, says Jouffroy, *vous êtes jeté* into the same state as the external objects..."" "However much we may hate, sympathetically [p. 79], the immorality of hatred does not fall upon us. If we love, the evil consequences of love do not concern us. We have the pleasure of all intellectual movements, without any of their bad results. We are not disturbed as in the personal state. It is a great happiness to feel oneself thus in a sympathetic state, without fear, responsibility, or disturbance..." "On seeing a tree rocked by the wind, the movement that is produced of itself in us, thanks to the contemplation of the expressive object, this *laisser aller*, this impersonality, pleases us deeply"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Cours d'Esthétique." Lección 35, pp. 358 and 359.

The mystic Novalis put forward the idea that man can be drawn so deeply by the pleasure of nature that he comes to feel himself in complete unity with it, the opposition between the self and the non-self disappearing, and he himself changing into the object contemplated: "In such a case the latter seems to us humanized, animated, and becomes a symbol of our inner life"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>2</sup> Meumann. Opus cit. p. 48.

"The end of man in art," said Hegel, "is to find in external objects his own self."

But the true introducer of the idea of empathic projection into the philosophical vocabulary is Robert Vischer, who made use of certain terms that shade, variously and subtly, his own thought: *Einfühlung*, *Anfühlung*, *Nachfühlung*, *Zufühlung*. (Diverse states of the outpouring of the soul and its [p. 80] interpenetration with the objects of art, designated by the synthesis of different prepositions-*an*, *nach*, *zu*-and the verbal element of the expression *sich einfühlen*, which indicates the projection or immersion of oneself in external beings or objects).

We shall consider, in particular, the aesthetic theory of *Einfühlung* in the work of Lipps<sup>1</sup>, which has just been translated into Spanish, from the third German edition, by the distinguished professor of the Universidad Central of Madrid, don Eduardo Ovejero y Maury.

<sup>1</sup> "Aesthetik."

What is *Einfühlung*? asks V. Basch<sup>2</sup>, and he answers: "To interpret another's self according to our own self; to live his movements, his gestures, his feelings and his thoughts; to vivify, animate, personify objects devoid of personality, from the simplest formal elements to the most sublime manifestations of Nature and Art; to straighten ourselves with a vertical, to extend ourselves with a horizontal, to coil ourselves with a circumference, to leap with a broken rhythm, to lull ourselves with a slow cadence, to tense ourselves with a sharp sound and relax with a veiled timbre, to darken with a cloud, to moan with the wind, to stiffen with a rock, to spill ourselves out with a brook; to lend and give ourselves to what is not ourselves, with such generosity [p. 81] and fervor that, during aesthetic contemplation, we are no longer conscious of our gift, and truly believe that we have turned into line, rhythm, sound, cloud, wind, rock, and brook." This is *Einfühlung*.

<sup>2</sup> "La Philosophie Allemande au XIXe siècle," pp. 84 and 85.

In the following admirable example, which we take from the poet Tagore<sup>1</sup>, there will be seen confirmed not only the thesis of empathic projection but also its conjunction with the initial thought of this book, which we have described under the names of vital excess or superfluous power; there will also be discovered, as we believe, the relation of *Einfühlung* to mysticism: "When a feeling awakens in our hearts that exceeds by far the quantity of it that can be absorbed by the object that inspired it, it returns to us and makes us aware of ourselves with its backwash. If we find ourselves in poverty, all our attention is concentrated outside ourselves, on the objects we must acquire to satisfy our need; but when wealth far surpasses our needs, its light is reflected upon us and we feel the exultation of being rich persons. This is the reason why, among all creatures, man alone knows himself, because his eagerness for knowledge returns to him in its excess. He feels his personality more intensely than other beings, because his [p. 82] faculty of feeling is greater than the objects of his feeling could consume. This outpouring of the consciousness of his personality demands an outlet of expression. Hence, in art, man discovers himself and not the things with which he deals."

<sup>1</sup> "¿Qué es el arte?" Translation by the Departamento Editorial of the Universidad Nacional de México, p. 171.

Theodor Lipps, in his "Fundamentals of Aesthetics," is today the most distinguished representative of the theory of empathic projection. His merit lies in that he has not only developed his aesthetic thought systematically, but pursues his fundamental idea through the diverse worlds of art: the beautiful, the sublime, the ugly, the comic, the tragic, etc., and through the diverse arts of sight and hearing, with an intrepidity that disdains criticisms and differing points of view, in order to hold to his own conception and to disentangle-this would be, at least, his ambition-the nature of the beautiful as a pleasurable, aesthetic feeling capable of signifying a value, peculiar and characteristic of universal existence, in the moral consciousness of humanity. "All artistic and aesthetic enjoyment, in general, is by itself an enjoyment of something that has an ethical value, not as an element of a whole, but as an object of aesthetic intuition"<sup>1</sup>. And this is because, for Lipps, the value of human personality is an ethical value, and in *Einfühlung* personality itself is found, the positively human, objective, pure, and free of all the real interests [p. 83] that remain outside the work of art. Croce holds that, in this way, "the aesthetic fact is deprived of all value of its own and receives only a reflected significance from morality"<sup>1</sup>. We think, in agreement with Lipps and against what the Italian philosopher asserts, that the world of art always bears a moral significance, without, nevertheless, proposing an ethical end; and that every purely beautiful work is incapable of being contaminated by evil, and signifies the apotheosis of the human person, accomplished with the forms and aspects of reality, thanks to the very act of empathic projection; therefore, we affirm with Lipps that the value of personality is an ethical value, and that no other definition can be given of the possibility and the field of Morality. In other words, the act of *Einfühlung* is neither logical nor ethical, it is aesthetic, more still, it is the aesthetic; but, since it implies the full activity of the human person, projecting itself upon the world, it transcends into the moral, inasmuch as morality defines its own field, as Lipps says, in the value of personality. Hamlet's monologue, or rather Hamlet's monologues, in the Shakespearean tragedy, are pure works of art, admirable outpourings of the soul and overflowings of human personality upon the world, yet they bear a moral meaning; not by deliberate purpose (then they would not be aesthetic), but by their transcendence, because the exultation of the human person [p. 84] will always interest Ethics. Don Quixote of La Mancha, with his sublime madness of destroying enchantments and wickednesses, is another brilliant projection of the soul upon the elements that life offers it. And the "Quixote" acquires great moral value although its conception is purely aesthetic. Were it a panegyric of the good and not the life of hallucination of a poetic character, it would annul, at once, its ethical significance and its artistic value.

<sup>1</sup> See: Croce, Estética. Segunda parte, chapter XVII.

<sup>1</sup> Croce. Opus cit.

Having established, then, that art has *eines ethisch Werthvollen*, as Lipps says, let us go on to set forth the beautiful as a feeling of pleasure, first, and then to expand by setting forth the properly aesthetic considerations of the German thinker.

The following proposition<sup>1</sup> may be formulated, says Lipps: "We find the reason for pleasure in the degree to which certain psychic processes-or complexes of psychic processes-that is, sensations, perceptions, representations, thoughts, and relations among these elements, are natural to the soul. Pleasure accompanies psychic facts to the extent that these are self-affirmations of the soul. Pleasure is the immediate expression or symptom of this fact, its reflection or repercussion in consciousness. In the same way, displeasure is the immediate symptom in consciousness of the contrary fact, that is, of the fact that certain psychic events involve for the soul a certain [p. 85] violence or discomfort, and that their production finds, in them or in their nature, less favorable or wholly unfavorable conditions."

<sup>1</sup> T. Lipps. Opus cit. p. 11.

In sum, an object will be pleasurable if it is perceived and apperceived; if the spirit assimilates it completely. A psychic process arises in the soul; if there are conditions that facilitate its apperception, there is pleasure; displeasure in the contrary case.

Next, Lipps formulates the foundations of Aesthetics, namely: the principle of unity in variety; that of monarchic subordination (both formal)<sup>1</sup>, and *Einfühlung* proper.

<sup>1</sup> Dice Meumann en su "Introducción a la Estética Actual," p. 56 de la Spanish translation by Urries y Azara: "Hay un defecto en la estética de Lipps: que el agrado estético no se refiere sólo a la proyección sentimental, sino que a su lado propone el autor cierto número de principios formales, generales, estéticos (v. gr., el de la unidad en la variedad), que saca él de la naturaleza del alma. Es verdad que después ha explicado que por naturaleza del alma no entiende sino la regularidad de los hechos psíquicos; pero con eso no queda más aclarada la posición de dichos principios en la Estética, pues no se ve qué relación guardan los tales con la proyección sentimental; si ésta es, verdaderamente, la base de todo placer estético, esas leyes formales han de ser, también, leyes de la proyección sentimental; mas no las considera así el propio Lipps y no pueden ser entendidas de ningún modo como tales."

Meumann's criticism proceeds from the idea of excluding from the aesthetic field everything not reducible to the single principle of *Einfühlung*; but one might argue against Meumann by asking: Why must everything aesthetic be reducible to the very principle of *Einfühlung*?

[p. 86] Meumann rejects every formal law of Aesthetics, in order to make of this branch of knowledge something purely psychological and experimental. According to Lipps, on the other hand, besides *Einfühlung*, to explain pleasure, if we are dealing not with simple objects (colors, sounds, etc.) but with composite objects, of parts that refer to a whole, there is need of unity in variety, of the differentiation and organization of divergent elements in the fundamental identical element. This is the principle that makes its impression in the pleasure a colonnade or a rhythm of ornamentation or of music causes us.

But aesthetic harmony requires, besides, according to Lipps, another principle, that of monarchic subordination, which implies a still closer coordination. According to this law, the parts of the complex whole submit to one of them, which is concentrated, in a privileged way, at one or several points: if C corresponds to 200 vibrations per second, and G to 300, there is a common element of monarchic subordination that unites them, the rhythm of 100 per second. In Gothic cathedrals, the monarch, the part of the whole that achieves the concentration, is the tower, aspiration of the soul toward the infinite. It is clearly seen in this example how monarchic subordination is not identical with unity in variety, but functions and expresses itself automatically within it.

Lastly, empathic projection and its diverse [p. 87] forms: The movement of *Einfühlung* is not a pure gratuitous endowment of the soul but, rather, according to Lipps, an internal activity of the spirit provoked by the collaboration of the peculiar form of the objects upon which the empathic projection is effected. The self and the world are unified, by sympathy, in every act of *Einfühlung*.

Lipps distinguishes several species of *Einfühlung*. First, apperceptive *Einfühlung*. Next, empirical *Einfühlung*, or *Einfühlung* into nature upon which we project our intimate feelings: pride, audacity, tenacity, subtlety, well-being, etc. All these moral attributes are granted, indissolubly, to the object of intuition. So too the empathic projection of our states of soul upon our own states of soul, each of which comes to attain a privileged and superior situation: as if, upon the fear we experience, we projected our total consciousness. Lastly, *Einfühlung* into the sensible aspect of the living being. By which psychological process the physiognomy of our fellows leads us, imperiously, to manifest ourselves in the same sense. The sensible becomes symbolic of feeling. Beauty brings together all these aesthetic elements "in a free affirmation of life, felt in the contemplation of an object, and bound, in a sensible way, to the contemplation itself."

[p. 89]

## Capítulo VII. El misticismo platónico y la *Einfühlung* — Chapter VII. Platonic Mysticism and *Einfühlung*

[p. 91] The riddle of art, how to solve it, is a question debated since the days of Plato. The Attic Bee himself denied artists the rights of citizenship in the Republic. Because artistic and poetic activity does not reproduce the characteristics of pure rational knowledge, that subtle dialectician, in love with reason, peremptorily rejected artistic activity as a virtue of the senses alone, unworthy of dwelling on the summit where pure intelligence rules in its divine world of eternal and incorruptible types, which are the models of all transitory reality. What a paradox, worthy of meditation, to see the greatest poet among the philosophers standing on the walls of the ancient city, driving out the sons of Apollo like an archangel with his keen sword, and pointing out to them the road of perpetual exile! For Plato, only reasoning can give us truth; dialectic alone can lead us to virtue. The free play of fantasy is incapable of realizing the human good, the supreme good. But humanity, more just in its decisions than Plato himself, a greater friend of truth [p. 92] than the immortal philosopher, cannot conceive of republics or cities without poets and artists; for art is not merely the exclusive invention of an extraordinary man and the delight of a discerning public, but a differential form of our own lineage, which has guided it from savagery and barbarism to contemporary culture; from the primal dance and the picturesque tattoo to the higher forms of tragedy and drama; forms symbolized, in the annals of history, by a few superhuman men, equals in intelligence of Plato himself: Aeschylus and Euripides in Athens, Shakespeare in England, Lope and Calderón in the Spanish court of the Philips.

There was in the theory of the disciple of Socrates, alongside the denial just noted, a psychological element of great worth, and it is love (the philosophy of Plato is the philosophy of love, as he himself declares); intuition of beauty identified with truth and the good. This love represents, in the greatest of the Platonists, the philosopher Plotinus, a mystical transport which, as he himself says, "does not refer to the color or to the magnitude of things, but to the soul which, itself without color, possesses the full splendor of all the virtues..." "This beauty, the most beautiful of all, because it is beautiful in itself, this primal loveliness, makes its lovers lovely and fills them with love"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Enéada primera, book VI.

It is interesting to observe how the German aesthetics [p. 93] of our day has interpreted and reproduced, in some sense at least, the mystical anthropomorphism of Plato and Plotinus in the theory of empathic projection, another ecstatic transport.

Not, of course, that we pretend to reduce *Einfühlung* to the mysticism of Plato; but we do see, in the works of the new experimental aesthetics, the reflection of what is true in that flight of the soul upon the things it admires; a spiritual movement that constantly appears and reappears in the work of the Platonists. All mysticism is individualist. Without personality there is only civil tradition. The mystic is a rebel who believes only in himself, in the fundamental experience of his own action. When this species of mysticism (a lesser mysticism, *Einfühlung*), which is art, is joined to the energetic tendency to live outside oneself, as in a unique world sufficient unto itself (a propensity reflected in the rapture of contemplation), poetry, music, or the visual arts sum up, in a juicy cluster of intuitions, something practical and concrete, different from pure logical thought and from metaphysical ideality. Nothing is so different from a dialectician as a believer. Nothing is so unlike a reasoner as a poet.

The difference between art and mysticism is only one of degree, not of essence, because their efficient cause is the same psychological attitude. Pictorial or poetic creation, like faith, is a vehement flight of one's own heart, or of some very principal part of it [p. 94], toward the things that are believed, adored, or sung: *Einfühlung*, *Anfühlung*, *Nachfühlung*, *Zufühlung*. When the palpitating heart pours itself out upon objects apt to arouse admiration, how could one fail to believe in the reality that *ipso facto* takes shape and comes to be? How could he who escapes from himself and roots his soul in the object of his contemplation not believe in his demon, in his god, in his work of art? It would be as much as not believing in one's own consciousness, in transcendental apperception itself, as Kant would say, launched with its psychological train upon the world. The artist alienated himself from it by transmuting it into lines or color, poem or song.

When a tree, a willow, bends its foliage over the surface of a lake, the poet's soul flees from itself, becomes one with the languid foliage of the willow, and weeps with it in silence over the lake. He has sympathetically poured his sorrow into a tree that bends. If the cypress points to the sky, poetic intuition climbs up the ascending branches, and makes of the erect tree an episode of its infinite longing. Man consoles his beloved dead with the sign of the cypress pointing to heaven. That is to say, he consoles himself. The rectilinear vigor of the sacred tree demonstrates, constantly, within consciousness, that region, true or chimerical but in the end most human, in which nothing is forgotten because nothing perishes.

All creation proceeds from an externalization of the [p. 95] soul, which applies itself insistently to what incites or moves it. Primitive man, who in his ingenuousness admires the attributes of fierce beasts or the stars that glitter in the night, makes of the bright star an extension of his person, and of the wild beast another, casting upon the stars that amaze him or the brutes that overwhelm and fascinate him his stupefied soul.

Our ancestors projected upon the haughty eagle and the subtle serpent their admiration, their poetic intuition. The national coat of arms was born of this aesthetic anthropomorphism, just as the cock won for itself and its fierceness the patriotism of the Gauls, and the Muscovite bear, which today has filled the frozen steppe with its red blood, harbors beneath its hide Russian nationalism. What an artistic union of the noblest human sentiments with the most interesting beasts!

Modern man no longer intuits or believes in those beautiful idols, but he has others. He will never cease to beget some, and on the day he no longer admires, following the counsel of Horace, he will have changed so much that he will no longer be a man.

But this artistic alienation of the spirit does not have only sublime aspects. When you follow, hardly breathing, the daring movements of the acrobat who leaps from one trapeze to another, you are no longer yourselves; you do not remain comfortably settled in your seats, you go through the air leaping from trapeze to trapeze, and, applauding the tragic flight [p. 96] of the artist, you delight in yourselves. We can all leap in magnificent capers, if the acrobat leaps for us; German aesthetics says: with us!

In sum, the mystical attitude in art is an eternal datum, because instead of complicated ideologies it hands us trivial experiences of life.

Tagore says admirably, in his essay "What Is Art?," glossing, as the great poet he is, the eternal theme of empathic projection: "The things that awaken our emotions arouse in us the feeling of ourselves. It is as when we touch the strings of the harp: if we barely brush them, we are aware only of the contact; but if we strike them forcefully, our action returns to us in the form of sound and our consciousness is intensified."

"There is the world of science, from which the elements of personality have been carefully removed. It is a world that we must not touch with our feeling. But there is also the vast world that is personal to us. We must not only know it in order to set it aside afterwards, but we must feel it, because in feeling it we feel ourselves."

[p. 97]

## Capítulo VIII. Crítica de la *Einfühlung* y teoría de la intuición poética (creadora) propiamente dicha — Chapter VIII. Critique of *Einfühlung* and the Theory of Poetic (Creative) Intuition Properly So Called

[p. 99] The first question that arises with respect to *Einfühlung* or empathic projection is whether we are dealing with an original and irreducible psychological process, or whether we are in the presence of a psychic complex reducible to more elementary forms or aspects.

The psychology of association, which reacted, first in England and then on the Continent, against the old spiritualist psychology of the faculties of the soul, undertook to apply the procedures of Chemistry, at least the general method of its investigation, to psychological questions. Association was the magic word that could explain everything, especially when the magic of evolutionism was added to that of associationism. Between the two ideas, evolution and association, the field of psychic life was divided up, and what one of these concepts did not explain, the other explained, or else it no longer withstood the combined action of both psychological reagents. But the possibilities on which [p. 100] the psychologists who were partisans of associationism relied (pure association, evolution, or a synthesis of association and evolution) are logically completed by one more term, which would be the impossibility of explaining a psychological fact by association and evolution, whether separate or combined.

To begin with, consciousness is not an epiphenomenon, but the very condition of psychological activity. To say that consciousness was formed by association is absurd, because association is unintelligible without consciousness itself.

To declare that evolution formed consciousness is likewise absurd, if one does not wish to turn perpetually in a vicious circle; for to make the idea of evolution intelligible, one will always have to have recourse to consciousness. In its prison we are enclosed, even in order to study the life of the psychic unconscious and subconscious; but this time the prison is of glass, of the finest glass in the world. Against all psychological evolutionism and associationism one can always raise the peremptory exception of Kant, in his excellent notion of transcendental apperception. (Which does not mean, of course, that the value of the laws of association and evolution in the facts of mental life is denied: but this value is subordinate and secondary, relative to the supreme unity, to the primitive synthesis of consciousness.) Perception means mere representation. Apperception means conscious representation; transcendental apperception is [p. 101] the pure consciousness of oneself. This principle of the synthetic unity of apperception is the foundation of understanding, because it not only delivers to us the subject of knowledge, but makes possible the unity in the qualitative diversity of the object. In other words, every object is a diversity of attributes or qualities; but a coherent, synthetic diversity. This which synthesizes and connects is no longer objective, even though it is the irrefutable condition of every object; it is subjective, in the Kantian sense of the word, or, what is the same, universal for all subjects; the supreme synthetic principle of all understanding; primitive synthesis or transcendental apperception.

To strive to obtain the I think by association or evolution is to lack a philosophical head. The time would be better spent on less fruitless things. Associationism and evolutionism are scientific theories, applications of the categories of the understanding; and the understanding itself, as a faculty of knowing, is annihilated the instant one pretends to explain the eternal luminous unknown that makes possible the subject of knowledge, the object of knowledge, and knowledge itself, as a relation between the two terms.

By contrast, the theory of the projection of the subject upon the object has in transcendental apperception a brilliant foundation; for it will no longer seem unusual that the empirical self should be projected, with all the richness of a concrete personality, upon the world, when Logic itself was born of a [p. 102] first, irrefutable projection of pure consciousness into the object of knowledge. With reason "Fichte started, as Falckenberg says<sup>1</sup>, from this pure consciousness of one's own self, the highest point to which Kant rises, in order to develop, from the primitive fact of the spirit, synthetically, the system of the pure acts of the understanding." Without projection of the pure self, there is no Logic. Without projection of the empirical self, there is no Aesthetics. Transcendental apperception makes Science possible. Empathic projection makes Art possible. At the base of human spiritual life is the mystical union of subject and object. Or, what is the same, empathic projection is a primary act of the spirit, irreducible and constant.

<sup>1</sup> "La Filosofía Alemana desde Kant." p. 19 of the Spanish translation by F. Giner.

Strictly speaking, the most universal principle is not transcendental apperception, which we shall call, if we may, the logical projection of the subject; but total projection, empirical projection or empathic projection, of which the creation of the object by the I think is a part; a creation motivated, perhaps, like the aesthetic one, by something exterior and impenetrable, the thing in itself, the Kantian noumenon. Logic was born of stylizing and selecting the primitive, mystical act of the projection of the empirical self, for reasons of mere utility. The world of primitive man must have been a surprising world [p. 103], fully aesthetic, like that of children<sup>1</sup>, without principles, or laws, or imperatives of conduct. And the ideal of action that we would present for the superman (the richest empirical self in history) would be that of a being who has achieved so high an externalization of his mental life, and so just and exact an appreciation of scientific laws and the norms of social conduct, that he returns once again to the ingenuousness of vision, to the spiritual virginity, as M. Bergson would say, of the humble worshiper of a fetish (a god fashioned by the believer's own hands, as the Portuguese word expresses it).

<sup>1</sup> A little girl, to show me her affection, said to me: "I love you blue," unconsciously projecting her little soul upon the affection she bore me and the color she preferred.

But if *Einfühlung* is a primary and irreducible fact of the spirit, superior to all association, it is fair to admit that not every *Einfühlung* has aesthetic significance. To begin with, transcendental apperception is not an aesthetic fact, nor is the effusion of the soul in religious myth.

According to Wundt<sup>2</sup>, the union of consciousness and object is the more intimate the deeper the feeling that binds them. When the emotion is intense and permanent, objects live the life of the subject. If the objectification determined by an intense feeling has as its only result to make it translate itself into sensible forms, aesthetic creation is produced (simple representation).

<sup>2</sup> "Volkerpsychologie" (Mythus und.. Religion).

[p. 104] When objectivity is complete and goes so far as to animate and personify the object, mythic creation results. The intervention of reality is greater in myth than in art, where subjective and individual factors have more freedom. Aesthetic creation, while more varied in its motives, is simpler. "In mythic creation the impression common to all, on the occasion of the same phenomenon, has more vitality; the projection of consciousness is more complete and the transformation of the object more permanent and stable, because only the images and affections common to all the members of the collectivity participate in it"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Sociología General," by M. H. Cornejo. pp. 104 and 105 of the French translation by E. Chauffard.

In our view, Señor Cornejo is quite right in affirming the eminently social character of myth, already defined in Wundt's work but not sufficiently brought into relief. We believe that the most characteristic difference between the mythic and the artistic process is founded on the action of the collectivity. It is clear that art, too, is the combined fruit of individual genius and the collective environment; but whereas in the dramatic or poetic work of Shakespeare and Dante the first and principal thing is the genius of the artist (let Taine say what he will in his celebrated theory, somewhat discredited by now, of race, milieu, and moment as the universal causes of the work of art), in the mythic process of the soul's effusion upon nature or [p. 105] upon the events of human life (principally dreams and death), almost as important as one's own individual empathic projection is the uniformity and homogeneity of fear and hope in the spirit of the believers. The mythic process is a more complete, more common detachment, and, what is very important, because it also constitutes another characteristic difference, more utilitarian. No religion has been, is, or will be disinterested. Even the highest and noblest, such as Buddhism, imply a utilitarianism of feeling. How different the attitude of one who prays, an attitude, in sum, all too human, full of fear and hope, compared with the divine attitude of the great Florentine sculptor who, after completing his unsurpassable work of art, struck his marble creature with his own chisel, uttering this single expression: "Speak!," which sums up and encapsulates the aesthetic phenomenon of creative projection!

It seems to us that what precedes has made clear, first, that *Einfühlung* is a process irreducible to association; and second, that not every *Einfühlung* is aesthetic.

But there is an obscure point in the theory of the German aestheticians, and it is, in our way of seeing, the most interesting of all, because it would explain, precisely (as far as the riddle can be explained), that passage from intuition to expression or, in other terms, the very creation of the work of art.

[p. 106] We believe we have plausibly defined this mysterious passage, by virtue of the ideas we shall now set forth, which constitute what we shall dare to call the theory of poetic intuition, taking this last word in its purest etymological meaning. Poet, in Greek, is the creator.

[p. 107] Leibniz considered thoughts to be nascent actions, which already sketch or outline in advance the subject's own later action. And the French psychologist Fouillée built upon this Leibnizian thought the philosophical system of idea-forces. His formula is equivalent to the one cited from Leibniz, and is, in fact, another statement of the principle: "every idea is conative." Every spiritual movement tends toward act. States of soul tend to secure the position and conduct of the living being in the world.

In truth, the world and the spirit are two abstractions that consciousness shelters. Mental states do not engender themselves. Spirit and body are bound to each other in a mysterious way, to the point that it is by the physical manifestations of the soul that we know it exists; just as, without a force, without an impetus, without an internal spontaneity, material substance is reduced for us to mere extension. Perhaps what we call spirit and what [p. 108] we call body are only diverse aspects of one and the same reality, which it is impossible for us to begin to know, and therefore to use, unless we unfailingly bifurcate it into two eternal, irreducible symbols.

The problem of the relations of spirit with matter is one of the most abstruse puzzles of philosophy. Their everlasting communication is the most trivial phenomenon and the deepest arcanum of existence. With this arcane and trivial fact are intimately linked the question of the origin of language and the mystery of poetic intuition, the riddle of art.

Now then, in this constant relation of the inner and the outer, which is the supreme reality, the ideas that serve to secure or to sketch our action upon things are externalized in the vulgar acts of life, which always imply a schematization, a practical stylization of reality. Then spiritual externalization obeys utilitarian ends, and would probably disappear if these ends disappeared. The world is selected because it must be attended to only in function of the individual life that is preparing itself. Transcendental apperception, or logical projection of the pure self, takes place, and the world is geometrized or mechanized for the practical ends of living.

But the other projection of the empirical self, empathic projection, is an introjection that the world [p. 109] does not consent to, that vulgar life does not admit, and which nevertheless must be externalized, because, like every idea, it is conative, and, like every spiritual movement, tends toward its expression. The two causes or propelling forces are added together in a new and magnificent result, unexpected and creative. Since ordinary life does not consent to disinterested intuition or to empathic projection, they forge their own world, their region, their universe, which seems to duplicate ordinary life and the habitual world. Statues arise in their marble and bronze realm; sonatas and symphonies in their hermetic region, sonorous and full of mysteries; architecture, painting, and poetry in their respective ideal territory. They are expressions, irrefutable, fated, most spontaneous externalizations of empathic projection, which find no room in the moments and situations of everyday life, and which, nevertheless, must be realized in some way and in some form, in order to fulfill their law; for, as we said before, "every spiritual movement tends toward act." Poetic intuition or artistic creation is the resultant of two forces, certainly not mutually exclusive, but opposed: the conative movement of ideas and the obstacle that ordinary life experience always places in the way of the empathic projection of the empirical self.

In the biography of all the great poets and artists one finds episodes that would corroborate our thought; but particularly interesting [p. 110] is what Romain Rolland relates in this connection in his essay on Berlioz:<sup>1</sup>. "At the time when his wife's health was imposing greater expenses on him, one night he had the idea of a symphony. He already possessed the whole first movement: an allegro in A minor. He got up, and was about to write, when he thought: if I begin the piece, I shall write the whole symphony. It will be considerable. I shall spend three or four months. I shall not write for the newspapers, and shall earn nothing. Moreover, if by chance I should finish, I shall not be able to resist the temptation to have it copied (which would amount to a thousand to twelve hundred francs); then the symphony will have to be heard. I shall give a concert whose costs will not be covered. I shall lose what I do not have: I shall lack what is necessary for the poor invalid, and there will be nothing with which to defray what is indispensable for myself and for my son who, shortly, is to go aboard a ship... These ideas sent a shiver through me. I threw down the pen and said to myself: 'Tomorrow I shall have forgotten the symphony!' The following night, I could hear the allegro clearly; I seemed to see it written. I was the victim of a feverish agitation; I hummed the theme, and was about to get up, when the reflections of the evening before restrained me. I overcame the temptation and abandoned myself to the hope of being able to forget. I managed to fall asleep, and the next day, on waking, every memory had vanished forever." Here is a tragic example of the struggle between the everyday world and the externalization [p. 111] of the poetic self! Happily, the tragedy of artistic expression does not always coincide with the tragedy of life. And, besides, an intense pleasure accompanies one who surpasses existence in the region of art. With reason everyone thinks that the poet is a fallen god who, nevertheless, like the other, creates!

<sup>1</sup> Musiciens d'aujourd'hui.

Art is not, therefore, an imitation of Nature. Nor is it its needless repudiation. It is a purely human world, in which exterior forces collaborate as stimuli. Suppose a contemplative, a creator, a poet, in vulgar life, and you will see him irremediably rejected. He then takes refuge in his empire. He creates unusual expressions, which Nature did no more than suggest. There he dwells, master and lord, autonomous. The heteronomy of humanity in ordinary life has ceased for an instant, as Schopenhauer would say. Sisyphus can stand erect on his own, letting the rock of his martyrdom rest at his feet. From his head springs a world of his own. If he could remain in it always, he would have dealt pain a mortal blow with the sword of song.

[p. 113]

## Capítulo IX. La intuición poética y su expresión — Chapter IX. Poetic Intuition and Its Expression

[p. 115] Every complete process of poetic intuition ends in its expression. That is to say, just as language is inseparable from thought, to such a degree that we cannot decide whether we speak because we think or think because we speak; just as spirit and body are related in an indissoluble fellowship, creative intuition is such only because it expresses. Artistic language, like the humblest reflex, is a fatality, an inevitable expression. Psychology, Linguistics and Aesthetics involve, at bottom, the same question.

Some philosophers judge that human thought created language; but for such a creation it would have been indispensable that thought already have at its disposal what it was about to form. An intelligence that did not understand (because in order to understand it needed language) showed itself nonetheless so intelligent that it understood why it did not understand, and then devised the invention of language in order to be able to understand. The mere statement of this absurdity obliges us to reject it. That is, the formation of language is not a teleological process but something spontaneous, [p. 116] concomitant with reason. Not for nothing did the Greeks say with the same word, *logos*, intelligence and word. To "discurrir" is the same, in Castilian, as to "discursar." In Italian, discourse is the mental act and its expression. Many animals express their states of mind with sounds. Expression seems to be a state concomitant with internal psychic effort. The reflex act is already the immediate response to an external stimulus. It possesses a certain intrinsic fatality, truly exemplary. Every energy capable of stirring our nervous organization ends, even without rising to the higher centers, in an expressive discharge. Diderot, speaking of the gesture, said this profound word: "every gesture is a metaphor." Expression is no longer regarded today, says Fouillée<sup>1</sup>, as a more or less remote sign that could be detached from the fact expressed; it is an integral part of the fact or of its history, a fatal prolongation of the very changes that constitute it, as the roar of thunder is the prolongation of the collision between stormy clouds. When Darwin asked a child of less than four years what he understood by being happy, the child answered: that means laughing, chatting and kissing. The young psychologist did not separate the feeling from its expression." For Fouillée, the psychological and sociological law of sympathy governs and explains all the phenomena of expression<sup>2</sup>. [p. 117] Darwin explains the expression of the emotions by the biological principle of the inheritance of habits. Useful at first for the maintenance or defense of life, certain movements have been preserved even when they no longer present any immediate utility<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "La Psychologie des Idées-Forces." p. 141.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. pp. 144 ff.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 143.

Physiologically, the law that relates or joins emotion with its outward signs is that of the equivalence of movements. If the nervous excitation is not simply transformed into cerebral movements, or into internal movements of the viscera, it follows the motor nerves and is transformed into movements of the muscles, which then become visible, outward signals of the emotion<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. pp. 147 ff.

In our view, a further step, very grave and momentous, has been imposed upon the theory of the relations of spirit and body, and consequently upon all linguistic, aesthetic and psychological theories, by the beautiful hypothesis of emotion due to the great psychologist W. James. He himself formulates it, synthetically, in the following terms, in chapter XXIV of his book *The Principles of Psychology*: "Bodily changes follow immediately upon the perception of the exciting fact; and our feeling of these changes, as they occur, is the emotion."

Let us suppose it were to be demonstrated that not every [p. 118] emotion originates, in the first place, from what was judged to be its expressive train. In spite of this, it will remain undeniable that, in many cases, the real history of the psychic phenomenon has been traced following James's formula; and it will always have been proved, by means of the American psychologist's brilliant analyses, that the intimacy between expression and emotion is closer still than was believed, given that, in many cases at least, if not in all, the cause of the emotion is the bodily change consequent upon the perception of the exciting phenomenon.

When aesthetic facts are at issue, we think that artistic expression proves inexplicable without bringing in empathic projection. Darwin's purely biological explanation; the physiological explanation by the equivalence of movements, or the psychological one of the perception of the exciting fact, which causes the bodily changes as they occur and engenders the emotion, as James says, do not suffice. It is necessary to bring in *Einfühlung*, the total projection of the self upon the things or the aspects of reality that are expressed.

We have distinguished three different kinds of *Einfühlung*: a). Transcendental apperception, foundation of Logic and Linguistics; b). Aesthetic *Einfühlung* proper, or poetic (creative) intuition; and lastly, c). The projection of consciousness in the mythical or religious phenomenon. The formula of the first [p. 119] *Einfühlung*, condition of all spiritual life, was given by Fichte in these memorable words: "Let us assert any fact whatever of empirical consciousness, let us remove from it, one after another, all the empirical determinations, until nothing remains in its purity but that which thought can in no way exclude, that from which nothing more can be removed." "All concede the proposition: A is A. It is admitted without any reflection, as completely certain. The question at issue is in no way whether A exists or not. What is at stake here is not the content of the proposition, but only its form; not an object of which something is known, but what is known of every object, whatever it may be"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Principios Fundamentales de la Ciencia del Conocimiento." p. 3 of the French translation by Grimblot.

This necessary relation, which asserts the "proposition: A is A; this X, of which Fichte speaks, is Kant's transcendental apperception, whose expression, which is the minimal or limiting expression, constitutes, in our opinion, the basis of Logic and Linguistics.

Religious or mythical *Einfühlung*, with its differential characteristics of social solidarity and utilitarianism, to which reference was made in the preceding chapter, has as its expression or natural terminus of development the fetish, prayer, magic, from the simple amulet and the humblest practices of [p. 120] sorcery to the sublimity of the Vedic Hymns or the Our Father.

Aesthetic *Einfühlung* ends, naturally, as we also explained in the preceding chapter, given the hostile, or simply thwarting, conditions of the world of action, in poetic creation, in the work of art, in the affirmation of the purely human world, architectural, pictorial, sculptural, poetic and musical.

Great artists are the human mouthpiece of this inevitable extension of the spirit. They create because such is their law: to hurl themselves out of themselves, diffusing themselves over reality with the unrestrainable spontaneity of instinct. The conduct of poets seems an act of demonic possession, of hypnotism. Their intuition is prolonged in their expression. Their work is their spirit, materialized, exteriorized, embodied in symbol.

To Nietzsche we owe this immortal page, which tells us how "figurative language," of which the rhetoricians speak, is the most natural, the least impregnated with rational motives or fragments: "What strikes one profoundly—says the philosopher—is the character of necessity with which the metaphor imposes itself. It seems that the most natural, the most just, the simplest expression is the one that offers itself. One would truly say—in keeping with the word of Zarathustra—that the things themselves come to the spirit eager to become symbols. And everything comes with yearning tenderness to find room in the word and smiles flatteringly, [p. 121] wishing to fly away, carried off by it. On the wing of each symbol you fly toward each truth. For you all the treasures of the word open of themselves. Every movement wants to learn to speak of you. Such is my experience of inspiration—adds Nietzsche—, and I believe one would have to go back thousands of years to find a man who had the right to say: 'That is mine too...'"

Nietzsche is mistaken in his final exclusive assertion. Every true artist has felt how the age-old inspiration wells up from his unconscious, the stream of light, the work intuited and expressed at once, in the inaccessible miracle, in the arcanum that seems to humble thought.

The Italian thinker Benedetto Croce, who is not only an original philosopher but a most learned humanist in the history of Aesthetics and a literary critic of the first order, will forever have the merit of having understood and affirmed the intimate relation that exists between intuition and aesthetic expression. His thesis affirms in the most absolute and categorical manner the identity of intuition and expression. His book is entitled "Estética come Scienza dell' Expressione e Linguistica Generale." Undoubtedly the title of his work suffices to understand that the science of expression is, according to Croce, general linguistics.

Human knowledge takes two forms. It is intuitive or logical; imaginative or intellectual; individual [p. 122] or universal; of things or of their relations. In sum, it produces images or it produces concepts. Intuition has no need of an intellectual master or lord. No doubt many intuitions are mixed with concepts; but in many others there is no trace of such a mixture, which shows that it is not necessary. "A work of art may be filled with philosophical concepts and may even have them in greater quantity and more profound than a philosophical dissertation. The latter, in turn, may be enriched with and abound in intuitions and expressions; but, despite all these concepts, the resultant of the work of art is an intuition, and, despite all the intuitions, the resultant of the philosophical dissertation will be a concept"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Estética come Scienza dell'Espressione e linguistica Generale." Chapter I.

It is not enough, however, according to Croce, to recognize the independence of intuition relative to the concept in order to form a true and precise idea of it; one must go further and declare that intuition is the objectification of our impressions, an undifferentiated unity of the perception of the real and the simple image of the possible. In it we do not set ourselves against external reality as empirical beings. Nor is sensation, formed and ordered according to the categories of space and time, intuition. "A shade of the sky or of feeling, a cry of pain and an impulse of will objectified in consciousness are intuitions in which nothing has been formed within space and time" [p. 123]<sup>1</sup>. In other words, time and space may enter materially into intuition, but as ingredients, not as ordering principles. Nor is intuition sensation, nor psychological association, nor even representation, or complex sensation. "True intuition is, at the same time, expression." Only by acting, forming, expressing does the spirit intuit. If intuition and expression are separated, they can never be reunited. "Intuitive knowledge is expressive knowledge." "Intuition is expression, nothing else, nothing more and nothing less than expression"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. Chapter I.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. Chapter I.

In the preceding chapter we said that every spiritual movement tends toward action, and that, nevertheless, empathic projection cannot crystallize into acts of ordinary life, because only the ideas that serve the vital insertion of our being into the world accord with our ordinary conduct. Since the intuitive movement cannot be exteriorized (because of its uselessness) and yet, following the law of psychic life in general, tends to exteriorize itself, intuition becomes poetic, that is, creative, expressive, as Croce would say. On numerous occasions Croce's theory has been objected to, by pointing out that not every intuition is creative, and that, if it were, we should all be artists. To which the philosopher of [p. 124] expressive intuition has replied in advance that we can understand the works of art that geniuses create, or perceive and feel them, only because we too are capable of expressing when we intuit. If only a Schubert or a Chopin were capable of affirming, expressively, his personal intuition of melancholy or desire, no one, as audience, would care for a famous lied or prelude. These pieces of music move and enchant us because, in hearing them, we arrive, by an inverse route, at the spiritual intimacy of Chopin and of Schubert. The musical expression of the lied or the prelude makes us partakers in the poetic intuition that engendered them, within that "free play of our faculties" which, as Kant and Schiller saw, constitutes the universal condition of art.

Other small, slight and passing intuitions are expressed by means of a single word, and die and vanish once the word is pronounced or written; or they are exteriorized in a single interjection, whose pathetic energy is unique, almost untransmissible; or, lastly, they are so elementary that their expression is unified with the very murmur of the world that engenders them, or with the stony gesture of a rock which, in awakening them, as it were monopolizes and expresses them without need of further movements; or they are unified with the slenderness of a tree that rises or the discreet and luminous blinking of a star in the darkness. A friend of ours, if he hears at night the mysterious sound of the [p. 125] whistle of a locomotive, invariably feels detached from life and experiences, as he tells us, a deep, unformulable pleasure, whose expression is, perhaps, only for him, the motionless silence he keeps before us and the gesture, which always accompanies it, of closing his eyes and then opening them, slowly, as if returning from a remote, bewitched, celestial country; and he usually utters at once these words: It has passed!

It is true that there is a great distance between this simple intuition, passing and profound, and the complex intuitions that form the themes of Schubert's "Moment Musical" or the angelic andante of his marvelous Unfinished Symphony; but it is only distance that lies between one and the other kind of intuitions; that is, a quantitative, not a qualitative difference; and Philosophy, as Croce very well says, is *scientia qualitatum*.

It has also been said that, if intuition implied, *ipso facto*, its expression, artists would never feel the anguish they experience in trying to bend the word, all musical, pictorial or sculptural language (sounds, color or matter), to the imperious urgencies of their own aesthetic conception. To which one can answer, victoriously, in our view, that these difficulties do not arise from intuition's failing to adapt itself to its just expression, but rather from the fact that the expressive elements of language, of all language—colors, forms and sounds—are given to us for the performance [p. 126] of the utilitarian ends of action, and not to be harmonized with that violent victory over the ordinary laws of life and their attendant common ends which poetic *Einfühlung*, creative intuition, always implies. For this reason technique, in the training of the pianist's hand, the painter's eye or the acrobat's muscular sense, is nothing but the result of a slow, prolonged and rugged bodily and intellectual exercise, aimed at drawing the human organism out of the paths and directions along which it ordinarily proceeds, orienting it toward the new courses of artistic action. "Genius, it has been said, is a long patience;" and, we shall add, there is no talent without technique; but genius, intuition, is not technique.

Even in mystical intuition, when the author of the Areopagitic Books declares that the Supreme Being cannot be named or defined in any way, because none of the attributes of reality befits Him, he comes to attain the aesthetic expression of his negative thought in this magnificent expression: The luminous darkness of God; the ineffable no longer proves so ineffable. If man is exalted to the feeling of the divine and attains its true intuition, expression accompanies him; and what an expression!, perfect, absolute, of the kindly mystical arcanum: luminous darkness of God. Carlyle too said, in splendid fashion: "Altars must be raised to silence..."

In sum, poetic (creative) intuition is [p. 127] unified with its expression. It is its expression. The world of life and of intelligence serves as support for a new universe: the purely human region of Art.

[p. 129]

## Capítulo X. Lo bello y lo sublime — Chapter X. The Beautiful and the Sublime

[p. 131] In his Analytic of the Sublime, Kant identifies the beautiful with the sublime in three of their attributes, because both provoke in us a disinterested, universal and necessary satisfaction: "The method here will not therefore depart from that of the preceding section, unless it be borne in mind that, where the aesthetic judgment referred to the form of the object, we began with the investigation of quality, and here, on the other hand, because of the lack of form that there may be in what we call sublime, we shall begin with quantity as the first moment of the aesthetic judgment on the sublime"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Crítica del Juicio," Spanish translation by Morente, page 133.

Beauty refers to the form and definition of a given object; sublimity to the limitlessness of its object. Natural beauty contains a purposiveness of form by which the object seems to have been predetermined for our imagination. The sublime is the absolutely great; it surpasses and does violence to the imagination, it exceeds everything we can represent to ourselves mentally, "it demonstrates a faculty [p. 133] of the spirit that surpasses every measure of the senses"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Crítica del Juicio," Spanish translation by Morente, page 133.

The analysis of the sublime requires one division more than that of the beautiful required, namely: that of the mathematical sublime and the dynamical sublime. The simple estimation of magnitudes by concepts of numbers is mathematical; but that of mere intuition is aesthetic. "Sublime, then, is Nature, in those of its phenomena whose intuition carries with it the idea of its infinitude." Which cannot occur except after the greatest effort of imagination to estimate the magnitude of an object, and after having established, nevertheless, its inadequacy, its failure<sup>2</sup>. In the sublime, reason—in the Kantian sense of the word—, a faculty superior to the understanding, perceives its own impotence to fathom the infinite; and all the grandeurs of Nature then seem small. "A tree that we estimate by the height of a man gives us, to begin with, a measure for a mountain; and this, if it is something like a mile high, can serve as a unit for the number that expresses the diameter of the earth, and make the latter intuitable; the diameter of the earth for the planetary system known to us, and this for that of the Milky Way; but the immense multitude of similar systems of the Milky Way, under the name of nebulae, which in turn form among themselves a similar system, does not allow us here to expect any limit"<sup>1</sup>. The sublime in aesthetic judgment resides, then, according to Kant, not so much in the greatness of the number as in the fact that we always arrive at greater units as we advance. Every magnitude of Nature proves small; small our very imagination, in all its limitlessness, before the ideas of pure reason. Hence the feeling of the sublime involves a pleasure and a pain. Pain through the discord between imagination and reason; pleasure, because thus the greatness of our nature and our destiny is revealed to us. "Man, said Pascal, is a feeble reed, but a reed that thinks."

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 139.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 147.

Nature is also a force; but, in aesthetic judgment, "considered as a force that has no power over us, it is dynamically sublime"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. p. 156.

Here is this passage, twin of the previous one we cited with regard to the mathematical sublime, which describes, with undoubted vigor and grandeur, the sense of what Kant calls the dynamical sublime: "Bold, overhanging, as it were threatening rocks; storm clouds piling up in the sky and advancing with lightning and thunder; volcanoes in all their devastating power, hurricanes that leave desolation behind them, the boundless ocean roaring with rage, a deep waterfall on a [p. 134] mighty river, etc., reduce our faculty of resisting to an insignificant smallness compared with their force. But their aspect is all the more attractive the more fearful it is, provided that we find ourselves in a safe place; we gladly call these objects sublime, because they elevate the faculties of the soul above their ordinary mean, and make us discover in ourselves a faculty of resistance of a wholly different kind, which gives us courage to measure ourselves against the apparent omnipotence of Nature"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 150.

In sum, the incommensurability of Nature, like the irresistibility of its force, is far from provoking us to the placidity and balance of beauty. We are no longer the unexpected accomplices of peaceful and enchanting life, yet we remain its disinterested spectators, because, however great the world and however fertile our imagination, the infinite is greater, and our reason, without encompassing it, has another, non-sensible measure, before which everything in the universe is small. And however powerful the dynamism of the world may show itself, our human person remains undiminished before this force, because we are masters of a power of another kind, the moral power of holiness and heroism<sup>1</sup>. The sublime lifts us up before the world and lets us glimpse our destiny. Hence sublime is the saying of the just man: "Make me strong, Lord, against Thyself..."

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 158.

[p. 135] In Schopenhauer's theory, which is a pessimistic voluntarism, to the disinterestedness of the mathematical and dynamical sublime, which Kant sought to explain by means of moral considerations, a tragic flavor of opposition between aesthetic intuition and the will is added. Below we set out the eloquent descriptions of sublimity, as they are synthesized in the passages of "The World as Will and Representation." Nevertheless, the sublime is not the tragic, even though, for the pessimist philosopher, everything assumes the contours and proportions of tragedy, even the disinterested contemplation of the world.

"Before the sublime, the state of pure knowledge must be won beforehand by the individual, who tears himself away, violently and consciously, from the relations of the object he knows that are contrary to his will, and rises freely and deliberately above the willing and the knowledge of everything related to it... If the individual's actual anguish or the danger arising from without were to produce in consciousness a single real movement of the will, this, personally affected, would soon gain the upper hand, serene contemplation would become impossible and the impression of the sublime would be destroyed"(1).

Next Schopenhauer presents most brilliant examples of the mathematical and dynamical sublime: "Let us transport ourselves to a solitary region; the horizon [p. 136] is infinite, the sky appears cloudless; no breath of wind stirs the trees or the plants; there are no animals, no men, no running waters; the deepest silence reigns; such a landscape invites to seriousness, to contemplation, to the forgetting of all will and its miseries; but this also gives to that landscape, where solitude and silence dominate, a certain tinge of sublimity. For since the will, ever avid to desire and to acquire, finds no object either favorable or unfavorable, nothing remains but the state of pure contemplation, and he who is not capable of it will feel the emptiness of a will without employment and the torment of boredom. The aptitude for appreciating this kind of beauty, and, in general, the lesser or greater ease in bearing or loving solitude, are an excellent scale for measuring the intellectual worth of men. The region we have described gives us, then, an example of the sublime in a lower degree. Associated, by contrast, with pure knowledge in its calm and sobriety is the recollection of a will ever dependent, ever miserable, ever agitated by the need to move. This kind of sublimity is what characterizes the well-known beauty of the endless prairies of North America"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "El Mundo como Voluntad y como Representación." p. 55

"This impression becomes still more powerful when the struggle of the unleashed elements takes place before our eyes; when a cataract [p. 137] plunging from on high even prevents us, with its din, from hearing our own voice, or else on the shores of the immense sea, tossed by the tempest, when monstrous waves rise and fall again, break impetuously against the cliffs of the coast and throw their foam into the air, far off; the hurricane roars, the sea bellows, the lightning rends the black clouds and the thunder drowns the noise of the sea and the wind. It is at such moments that the intrepid spectator of such a scene recognizes with full evidence the double nature of his consciousness; he understands that he is an individual, a frail phenomenon of the will, whom the slightest blow of those forces could annihilate; a being impotent against powerful Nature; a dependent creature, plaything of fortune, an imperceptible atom before colossal powers; but at the same time he feels himself the immortal subject of pure knowledge; he is the bearer of the entire world; he feels that this terrifying struggle of Nature is nothing but his own representation, and that he himself, in the tranquil contemplation of the Ideas, is a free being, alien to all will and all misery. Such is the perfect impression of the sublime. What produces it is the contemplation of a power incomparably superior to man and which threatens to annihilate him"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. pp. 57 and 58.

In sum, according to Kant and Schopenhauer, the sublime always involves a conflict between the limited imagination [p. 138] and pure reason, whose ideal is the infinite; between the power of nature and the moral force of man; between the pure intuition of beauty and the will constantly preoccupied with its own ends. The first would be an ethical conception of the sublime; the second also, because Schopenhauer himself, who criticizes Kant's way of seeing, is obliged to present his whole aesthetics, not only his theory of the sublime, as a path toward liberation from the constant motives of desire<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. pp. 59 and 60.

The aestheticians of *Einfühlung* base the feeling of the sublime on the projection of the force of will and of action. "In the beautiful we find a will or free manifestation of life. This manifestation of life appears, in relation to the will or action, as something that has been attained, achieved, won; that is to say, that I have acquired something, that I possess, that I enjoy something"(1).

The domain of the sublime, according to Lipps, is that of the force that exists and acts; the field of the non-sublime beautiful—of the graceful, the charming, the delicate—is the field of free enjoyments. In the sublime, the force of life, labor and effort throb; in the beautiful there is tranquil enjoyment, satisfaction and contentment, not will or action.

"A field more important than any other for the feeling of force and of greatness, and [p. 139] consequently for the sublime, is the field of struggle, even of sterile struggle; but struggle is the opposite of the free manifestation of life, of the attaining and enjoying of that to which a need impels us"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Los Fundamentos de la Estética." p. 512.

The empathic projection of our own energy upon the world makes us diffuse ourselves easily and flatteringly over the cloud that glides weightless in the blue of the sky, or the wave that spreads and dies on the gentle expanse of the sandy beach; but it also becomes irritated at the obstacle of the rock against which the furious ocean beats, or rises up like a preponderant rock against that same sea. If the projection takes place upon the conflict of the cosmic forces and not upon the beings who act in it, the feeling of the sublime is likewise attained. We would say that the empathic projection that engenders the sublime is synergic. And when it takes place upon a conflict in which one of the parties or forces, or both, are human, the ethical character implicit in sublimity is revealed with all the greater reason. Such is, for example, the case of Guzmán el Bueno, hurling his steel to the besiegers of Tarifa for the slaughter of his own son, in the conflict between fatherland and filial love; or the hero who defends single-handed, before a numerous army, the autonomy of his people, like Leonidas at Thermopylae; but there is also conflict and empathic projection of one's own force in this famous [p. 140] phrase of Pascal: "The silence of the infinite spaces terrifies me;" or in the attitude of Socrates, who converses serenely with his disciples on the proofs of the immortality of the soul, moments before dying. The beautiful creates its fantastic world in the pure delight of contemplation. The sublime is the contemplation of the struggle, of the strife of cosmic, psychological and moral energies. The entire world is struggle, conflict, synergy; thus it is sublime; but it is also easy movement, cadenced rhythm, amiable and seductive turning; thus it is beautiful. Apollo, who bathes Olympus and Parnassus in light, is the mythological invocation of Beauty; Jupiter, who gathers the clouds and holds on his knees the future of peoples, the personification of sublimity<sup>1</sup>. But both myths sprang from the same root: the outpouring of the soul upon the things that incite, dismay or enchant it. In peace as in war, in the static as in the dynamic, in the rhythm that is prolonged as in those that collide with one another, poetic intuition finds its expression and exercises its power<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> The reader will find, on page 132 of E. Meumann's "System der Ästhetik," the reasons for his dissent from the theory of the sublime of Kant and Schopenhauer. The weak arguments adduced by Meumann seem to us to leave intact the ideas and considerations on the sublime that we have set forth in this chapter.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. p. 513.

## Capítulo XI. La división de las artes y los sentidos estéticos. El sistema de las artes de Hégel y su crítica. El problema del progreso en el arte. Ensayo de una clasificación objetiva. Artes puras e impuras — Chapter XI. The Division of the Arts and the Aesthetic Senses. Hegel's System of the Arts and Its Critique. The Problem of Progress in Art. Toward an Objective Classification. Pure and Impure Arts

[p. 143] The arts are commonly divided into arts of sight and arts of hearing. Those of sight are architecture, sculpture, and painting; those of hearing, poetry and music. This is the traditional division of humanity's artistic inheritance.

"Art, says Hegel, addresses itself to the senses as much as to the spirit; hence the division of the particular arts must rest upon the senses to which they are addressed and upon the sensible materials that correspond to them. It is easy to see that touch, taste, and smell must be excluded at once. If statues are distinguished by feeling them, by running one's own touch over the smooth and delicate surface of the marble, there is nothing in this in common with the perception of beauty and artistic enjoyment<sup>1</sup>. Nor can the work of art be tasted. Refinement of taste can be demanded only for the appreciation of foods and the chemical qualities of bodies. The work of art, on the contrary, must be considered in itself and perceived by man in pure contemplation. It has [p. 144] no relation to desire and will. Nor can smell be an organ suited to artistic enjoyment, because objects address it only by virtue of a chemical decomposition and insofar as they dissolve in the air. It is, then, once more, an action that is wholly physical."

<sup>1</sup> Would Hegel exclude a blind man from communicating through touch with beautiful things..? Apotheosis of the systematic spirit!

"Sight, on the contrary, is purely contemplative. It owes this in part to light, a matter in some fashion immaterial, which in no way assails objects or their freedom and independence; it merely makes them appear. Sight, the sense without desire, embraces the whole of material existences: bodies separated and distributed in space, unalterable in their integrity, exhibited only by form and color."

"The other intellectual sense is hearing. In it we have precisely the opposite of visible appearance. Instead of being related to form and color, the ear perceives sound, the vibrations of bodies, without any dissolution or alteration. The ideal movement in which, by means of sound, the inner principle, the soul of bodies, is revealed, the ear perceives in a manner as intellectual as the eye perceives form or color"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Estética." Part Three. System of the Particular Arts. Third section: On the Division of the Arts.

The page of Hegel which we have wished to cite in its full extent flatly denies to the unprivileged senses any aesthetic function or activity. On the one hand, the intellectual senses; on [p. 145] the other, touch, hearing, and smell, stripped of every intervention or part in the world of beauty. Only two broad spiritual windows from which to look out upon the ideal, and the other paths of communication would be mere skylights, permitting us to communicate and attend to the chores and pressing urgencies of vulgar life. We judge the doctrine false, however much it may seem to count on unanimous traditional opinion and be corroborated by the authority lent it by the great name of Hegel.

The senses, in the beginning, must have served, in all likelihood, ends neither aesthetic nor intellectual, but rather to secure the success of life in each living being, procuring its ever more effective and perfect insertion into the conditions of its environment. Sight and hearing, like smell and taste, like touch itself, are bound up in the act of living, and only by exception can they lend themselves to aesthetic ends. But superfluous power, accumulated vital excess, brings about the possibility that the organs consecrated and fashioned for the fulfillment of selfish and biological ends may nonetheless serve as seat and conduit of the disinterested contemplation of reality, of the empathic projection of the spirit, of poetic intuition.

Only that, whereas touch and taste free themselves from the original yoke merely by exception, associating themselves with visual and auditory representations, sight and hearing assume, as a rule, the [p. 146] autonomy of organs exclusive to the spirit. Taste relates to nutrition itself; it was born clinging to the breast of the world, like the tongue of the infant that fuses with the mother's nipple, and models it, with the delicacy of the lips, as beautiful as it is. So with smell, so fine in the dog, the chief instrument of his defense and his love. Touch, more fundamental than all the senses, and of which the others are probably only refinements, stylizations, and reminiscences, is the true sense of the earth, of which Nietzsche speaks, the first animation of the clay that Jehovah blessed. To feel, to feel is to live. The jellyfish feels, elegant and soft, in the delicious half-light of its watery world; and, as it moves, it raises or lowers its fleshy feeling parasol, a witness to the mystery of the sea. The serpent feels, and, along its long breast, each little clod of the earth's surface goes on telling its essential secret. The lover feels, and even the mystic lover feels the impalpable... Saint Thomas, Saint Teresa...! So many others besides...!

But hearing and sight, as Schopenhauer says, are more disencumbered of the real: "The pleasure we experience with light is in reality no other than that which the objective possibility of knowledge in its purest and most perfect mode makes us feel; whence it follows that this pure knowledge, free of the will, is a source of lively enjoyment, and that, in this respect, it already contributes considerably to aesthetic pleasure."

[p. 147] "This observation about light allows us to appreciate the ineffable richness we find in objects reflected in water. This mutual action of bodies upon one another, which is of the lightest, swiftest, and subtlest kind; this action, to which we owe the purest and most perfect perceptions, the action of the reflection of luminous rays, we see there, before our eyes, in its cause and its effect, exercised on a grand scale and in the clearest, most visible, and most perfect manner. Hence comes the aesthetic pleasure we experience, and this pleasure is grounded in the subjective principle of aesthetic enjoyment, and is nothing but the joy that pure knowledge and the paths that lead to it cause us"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "El Mundo como Voluntad y como Representación." pp. 52 and 53.

Sight and hearing are, at once, the lookout from which we gaze upon the infinite, the vents of the inner life, the laboratories of reverie, the magic lanterns of hope, the severe judges of error. They are never mistaken! It is we who, on occasion, misinterpret their data. We believe we see what we have not seen, or hear what we have not heard. Nevertheless, if their spirituality is greater because they are less implicated in the ends of animality, will it be impossible to receive aesthetic impressions through the ministry of taste, touch, and smell?

[p. 148] "To ask which are the aesthetic senses, says Croce, means, in effect, to inquire:

I. Which sensible impressions can enter into aesthetic expressions?

II. Which must necessarily enter? To which we shall answer at once that all impressions can enter into aesthetic expressions or formations, and that no impression must necessarily enter into them"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Estética." Chapter II. Critique of the Theory of the Aesthetic Senses.

"It is a curious illusion to think that a painting gives us only visual impressions. The softness of a cheek, the warmth of a young body, the delicacy and fragrance of a fruit, the edge of a sword, etc.: are these not, perhaps, impressions that we receive from a painting?; and what is there visual about them? What would a painting be for a hypothetical man who, deprived of all or a great part of his senses, were suddenly to acquire the sole organ of sight?"<sup>2</sup>.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. Chapter II.

When you go through the countryside, on a dawn fresh as a bouquet of flowers, and breathe in the happiness of daybreak in the newly drawn milk of a cow, whose udder guards the intrinsic mystery of life; when you taste the newly drawn milk, does it not provoke in you, besides a certain lower feeling, the very act of tasting it, an aesthetic impression, an artistic delight? Guyau assures us he has thus experienced [p. 149] an impression of beauty. Do you think as Guyau does?

Does perfumery, subtle in its diverse and harmonious nuances, not speak to you, with discreet and regal authority, of artistic intuitions? The French say: "other races feed, France dines." Does the magnificent culinary art not evoke in you the Platonic shadow of beauty? As you pass your hand over the smoothness of ivory and become aware of the precious quality of this substance, does a feeling of loveliness not accompany your physical delight? Is it not beautiful, the light brushing of Cleopatra's breast against the skins of the fierce beasts that enjoyed the supreme distinction of caressing it?

Let us meditate on the conjunction of all the senses, aesthetic and non-aesthetic; and further, on the complicity of the powers of life (in what they have of the intrinsic and fundamental), synthesized in verses such as those of Lugones, which would be unintelligible without the constant possibility that is ours of making the whole of the psychic self, our spiritual, emotional, and instinctive ego, and not the purely intellectual, collaborate in poetic intuition:

"The sea, full of masculine urgencies, / roared around your waist, / and like a colossal arm, the dark / shore sheltered you. In your retinas, / And in your hair, and in your astral whiteness, / there shimmered with opaline decadences, [p. 150] that light of the dying afternoons / that lingers on the peaceful water. / Throbbing to the rhythms of your breast, / the serene sea swelled into a wave, / to plunge you into its feline vertigoes. / Its voice spoke to you a vague caress, / and as it penetrated between your slender thighs / the wave sharpened like a dagger."

Even when the splendors of the divine enter as color, line, rhythm, and melody, straight into the soul, they also tend to stir, in the most illustrious mystery, the throbbing of flesh and matter, offering it in homage to the Ideal!

In sum, there are no aesthetic and non-aesthetic senses; but sight and hearing are, without doubt, more propitious to the economy of beauty. The division of the arts founded on this trivial, relatively false distinction cannot serve, by itself, as the basis of a good classification of the arts. It will have to be sought along other, different paths, and based on the very aesthetic principle of poetic intuition. What is the great objective fact that corresponds in the world to disinterested contemplation? On it the objective classification of the arts would rest without obstacle. But first we must study the "System of the Particular Arts" that Hegel conceived. To its analysis we shall devote the second part of this chapter, and we shall reserve [p. 151] for the end the investigation of the phenomenon that, objectively, will correspond to aesthetic intuition.

Hegel's system of the arts is built upon the general principles of his metaphysical synthesis: the identity of the real and the ideal, on the one hand, and the dialectic, on the other.

Kant declared the thing in itself unknowable. Through Fichte and Schelling, Hegel arrived at the identification of the real and the ideal. "The thought of existence is existence itself thinking in us." Or this other absolute formula: "the real is rational." Art can then be nothing but the representation of the ideal; and the various arts will be classified according to the manner they use to become capable of expressing it. Which already implies, of course, the establishment of a gradation, of a hierarchy of the arts, and, consequently, also, progress affirmed as the law of art; and furthermore, the transitory character of art itself, which, as it progresses according to its law, tends to turn, denaturing itself, into pure abstract metaphysics.

The dialectic, already formulated in the axiom of Heraclitus: "Everything exists and nothing exists," becomes, in the system of the identification of the real and the ideal, the law of the world. Existence is a perennial succession of events linked among themselves, which act upon one another, with nothing remaining constant or being what it was before; but where everything moves, is transformed, and perishes. Or as Hegel [p. 152] says: "To all things both being and non-being are applicable." A system of the arts whose sole mission is to express the ideal must be the constant dialectical application of being and non-being to architecture, sculpture, painting, music, and poetry. History is Logic and Aesthetics at once.

These preliminaries established, let us mark out the historical process of art according to Hegel, which will amount to an ordering or classification of the particular arts.

Now, art in its progress has passed through three principal forms: the symbolic, the classical, and the romantic. Architecture marks the beginnings of art, by virtue of its very nature. In its origin, the artistic representation of the spiritual element does not find the suitable materials and the form that corresponds to it; "it confines itself to attempts whose aim is to reach the true harmony between the two terms; it contents itself with a bond, still external, between the idea and its mode of representation. The materials of architecture are furnished by matter properly so called, not animated by spirit, but modeled according to the laws of gravity, by lines and forms of external nature, with regularity and symmetry. The architectural work of art offers a mere reflection of the spirit"<sup>1</sup>. Architecture is the symbolic art par excellence.

<sup>1</sup> "Estética." Part Three. System of the particular arts. Division of the Arts.

[p. 153] Next, sculpture. Its principle is the spiritual individuality that constitutes the classical ideal. It takes for its physical elements heavy matter and its three dimensions, like architecture, but without limiting itself to modeling it regularly, according to the laws of gravity. "The form determined by the very substance is, in sculpture, the vitality of the spirit, the human form and its living organism penetrated by the breath of the spirit"<sup>1</sup>. Sculpture is the classical art by antonomasia.

<sup>1</sup> Opus cit. Part Three.

Romantic art embraces painting, music, and poetry. Painting inaugurates the series. "Although contained within the limits of the external world, it does not represent only the ideal concentration of the absolute in itself; it manifests it also in its subjective personality, in its spiritual existence. Man, with his determinate character, his sensations, his volitions, his actions, his relations with other beings; in his pains, his sufferings, and his death; in the whole circle of the passions and affections, constitutes the subject of painting"<sup>2</sup>. But as to the means of expression of these ideas, painting does not employ heavy matter with its three dimensions; it spiritualizes it as it does with figures; it brings the physical element and the spirit closer, by arranging the real appearance and transforming it, for the sight, into a purely artistic appearance. In sculpture and architecture, forms become [p. 154] visible by external light. In painting, on the contrary, matter, dark in itself, manifests its inner element, its ideal: light.

<sup>2</sup> Opus cit. Part Three.

Within its own sphere, says Hegel, music is opposed to painting. "Its element is the soul itself, invisible or formless feeling, which cannot manifest itself outwardly except by means of a phenomenon that rapidly disappears and effaces itself." Its physical element is sound, its combinations and modes, its chords and divisions, its oppositions and dissonances, harmonized according to the relations of quantity and proportion that art ordains.

Poetry is the true art of the spirit; the richest of all. Its domain is unlimited. Yet what it gains from the ideological point of view it loses in sensible aspect; but this too implies a progress, because sound is the word, the sign. Poetry reproduces, in its own domain, those of all the other arts. As epic poetry, it gives its content the form of objectivity. As lyric poetry it is subjective, and is wont to bring in the aid of music. Finally, as dramatic poetry, it represents the whole man in a visible spectacle. Apotheosis of art in general and of romantic art!

Next, the great dialectician, for whom history is everything (provided it does not oppose his logic), like Olympian Jupiter (Jupiter would perhaps not have scorned them), disdains other fine arts, and among [p. 155] them, the dance; "It is an amphibian," a being of transition, "nothing that is perfect." Hegel passes it over. But, it occurs to us to ask, why this disdain for the amphibian dance, and not for the other amphibians such as dramatic art, for instance? Why, the whole world of art, and art itself, in general, is an immense amphibian that comes apart in the meshes of the dialectical net of Hegelianism...! Nothing that is perfect!

In symbolic art, the idea cannot yet be expressed and allows itself to be translated only by the ponderous means of its expression. In classical art, the harmony of form and content is found. But the idea dares still more, disregards the harmonious accord, and seeks the infinite and the unlimited. By seeking it and progressing without end, art will deny itself and turn into abstract metaphysics... Such is the aesthetic paradox of Hegelianism: Art denied by Art. Or, as Heraclitus would say: "Everything exists and nothing exists."

If we had to object to Hegel's system of the arts, we would oppose it with three reasons, which are summed up in the last, namely: It is not possible to establish hierarchies among the arts. Art is not a transitory and perishable fruit of culture. The artistic progress of humanity is very debatable, and can be affirmed only in certain epochs of history, if it can be affirmed at all; because what progresses rather in art is the technical procedure, [p. 156] the means of expression, not the intuition itself, not the creative imagination of genius.

Scientific works are elaborated by parts. Artistic works are the result, always synthetic, of empathic projection, of poetic (creative) intuition. Hence they do not admit, as an intrinsic law of their nature, progress (*progressus*), the march forward, which is what the word etymologically means.

In our opinion, an Egyptian temple is, in its powerful symbolic individuality, something unique, essentially unprogressive. Its sublimity and majesty are expressed, forever, within its colossal proportions. It may be built otherwise, in a more graceful and expressive form, but not better. The Parthenon will be more harmonious, within the classical ideal; but no more beautiful than its magnificent predecessor. When the Egyptian symbol disappeared, humanity left behind a unique, unmistakable, and perfect moment of its history. One may likewise conceive romantic art as an exaltation of the human soul to the infinite; but the Apollonian art of Greek architecture will remain, as something insuperable, representative of an instant likewise unique and definitive of the human ideal.

The same in sculpture as in architecture. The Scribe of the Louvre is a profound example of sober artistic expression. Its glorious impassivity is not inert and stony, but on the contrary something that breathes with the hidden and subtle life of the great creations [p. 157]. It lies massively settled upon itself; it rests as if it were immovable. Its attitude seems to have imprisoned the gravity of matter in the regular placidity of thought.

The Apollo of the British Museum conveys the dignified attitude of the Greek genius. The Egyptian symbol yields its place to the divine and perfect form of the adequacy of matter and spirit. The expression is much wiser, much more ingenious and varied, but not deeper. There was something in the hieratic symbol of the Egyptian that the Greek did not express. In expressing better, he expressed less; that is, he ceased to express the august and unfathomable mystery, the eternity objectified in sublime hypogea, in gigantic monoliths, which will always make of Egyptian art an immortal elaboration.

The Moses of the Chartreuse of Dijon is more spiritual still than the Greek Apollo. The Christian artist polished and kneaded the stone to wring a prayer from it; to destroy its ponderous gravity and make it the vehicle of triumphant spirituality. The imbalance shows itself anew; but now it is in favor of the spirit and not of matter. The "romantic genius," as Hegel would say, made of the arts and philosophy humble handmaids of faith.

One could continue the exemplification by placing beside the medieval Moses Rodin's Balzac. In the Balzac, little shows that is not the soul. The cyclopean figure lies in an intentional indecision, profoundly expressive. Only the face assumes [p. 158] its full value, its absolute spirituality. A Greek would have thought, on contemplating the celebrated sculpture, that its author had perhaps set out to produce a brilliant caricature. Will modern art be a caricaturesque art in which only what moves us as a revelation of the spirit is exalted? Will the evolution of romantic art end in a kind of abbreviated system of signs for the human passions, which would be equivalent, in the aesthetic realm, to the briefest shorthand of emotion?

Perhaps we are closing, with our inveterate symbolism, a great cycle of artistic evolution. The Egyptian, like ourselves, carved symbols. Artistic progress seems an absurd thesis.

The evolution of the symphony (sonata magna for orchestra), from Haydn to Mozart and Beethoven, likewise consisted in the search for more energetic, more ductile expression, which does not imply that the symphonic work of the creator of the "Pastoral" and the "Eroica" must be placed, on the perennial scale of works of genius, above the winged symphonies of Mozart or the noble creations of Haydn. In Beethoven there is more depth, but not more beauty. An all-too-human expression prevents the formation of works less dramatic, less impassioned, certainly, but purer, perhaps, more serene, more Apollonian. Who today will conceive, as Haydn did, those jocund themes, light and disinterested, without tangled metaphysics or esoteric intention...? [p. 159] Music, like painting and poetry, no longer knows the purest ingenuousness that formed the charm of our elders. Harmonious diaphanousness has disappeared, to yield its place to contemporary aesthetic expression, electrically charged with ideas as stormy clouds are with lightning.

And if, within a single art, progress does not prove evident, to equate the various particular arts with one another, so as to declare one of them progressive in relation to the others, seems to us impossible. Hegel places poetry at the head of the immortal sisters, as one that would sum up, in its three forms (lyric, epic, and dramatic), aesthetic evolution; Schopenhauer prefers music, "direct and immediate objectification of the will," and not mere representation of a Platonic idea, to architecture, sculpture, poetry, and painting. We believe the five Muses equal in dignity and sublimity, and we repeat, with respect to them, the supreme phrase of Victor Hugo: "the masterpiece is equal to the masterpiece." It would be impossible for us to set Apollo above Dionysus. The epic and sculpture are as absolute as drama and music; to begrudge them merit is, in our view, an error unworthy even of being discussed.

Finally, we harbor no fear that art will perish and turn into abstract metaphysics. From primitive civilizations, from tattooing and barbarous dances down to the [p. 160] musical works of a Wagner or a Mussorgsky, art has been and will be one of the differentiating elements of humanity, across the centuries. Against its victorious hegemony the Hegelian dialectic has been able to do nothing..., nor any other dialectic.

The brilliant reflections of M. Henri Bergson on the intimacy of pure reason and space, on the one hand, and intuition and time on the other, have come to prove clearly that reason is not capable of understanding movement except by resolving it into a series of static positions. Intuition, on the other hand, the hidden sympathy of the spirit with life, is always correlative and concomitant with a movement, interior or exterior, spiritual or material. The invincible difficulties of Zeno of Elea with respect to the rational conception of movement, which inaugurate, one may say, Greek philosophy in its period of critical reflection, prove the impotence of pure reason to resolve them; but they dissipate as soon as one resorts, as M. Bergson has done, to the immediate data of consciousness, to the intuition we have of movement.

There might be formulated, perhaps, in this single expression, the objective character of every aesthetic intuition, and even of every intuition in general: the expression of individuality in movement is the end of art.

Professor Alfonso Caso, in his "Thesis" for the Faculty of Higher Studies of the University of Mexico, entitled "Essay on a New Classification [p. 161] of the Arts," has made use of Bergson's theory to formulate the synthetic table of the arts. Here is his ingenious and profound classification:

"I. Arts of sight. (They represent the being that has moved): Architecture, Ornamentation.

II. (Or else they represent the being that moves): Sculpture, Painting."

"III. Arts of hearing. (They represent the movement of the being): Poetry, Music.

"IV. Arts of both senses: (sight and hearing). (They represent the being and its movement): Dance, Drama."

"As may be gathered from the classification we propose, adds its author, no art is declared superior to the others; it is simply indicated what each expresses in particular, without preference for any other aesthetic manifestation."

"Whereas the plastic arts are enjoyed visually, and Poetry and Music only through hearing, two further arts, which bear great resemblances to each other, Drama and Dance, are enjoyed through both senses."

We believe that the foregoing classification (whose principle is the expression of movement, of rhythm) is a true objective, and not subjective, ordering of the arts; hence, very superior, in our view, to Hegel's classification. Moreover, it is founded, subjectively, on the principle of aesthetic intuition, which is the basis of the psychological [p. 162] explanation of art and of the theory of *Einfühlung*. Finally, it establishes no impossible hierarchies among the arts. It leaves the immortal sisters in their blessed equality. The organism of art would be complete if to the pure arts, of which account has been taken in this chapter, consecrated to the sole expression of beauty, there were added the impure arts which, like Didactic Poetry, Oratory, History, Criticism, and Caricature, carry implicit an intellectual, practical end, which distinguishes them from the pure expression of disinterested intuition. These complex artistic activities signify the supreme victory of Art over Life. From its peculiar region, the world of poetic intuition pours itself out upon the actions and ends of man's ordinary activity, and dignifies it with the magic of its splendor...!

We shall devote the next chapter to the study of the impure arts.

## Capítulo XII. Las artes impuras. Caricatura, elocuencia e historia — Chapter XII. The Impure Arts. Caricature, Eloquence, and History

[p. 165] The pure arts, lyric poetry, music, painting, are the simple outward expression of poetic intuition:

"I listen, nothing more, and leave open / The doors of my curious spirit; / The verses enter without asking leave..."

So said our Manuel Gutiérrez Nájera, an artist of such select fancy and exclusive originality that one would search in vain in the history of Castilian letters for another who should be called his forebear.

As a rule, the author of works of art does not reflect or opine on life, he reflects it; he does not dogmatize, he sings; he does not criticize, he exhibits. The poet opens the doors of his soul and the verses enter him, that is, material and spiritual existence made poetry. The musician too offers his virginal senses, "like five vast and profound seas," to the universal rhythm. A pure musician, Haydn, Mozart, or Schubert, is the cosmic rhythm that invades a human spirit and makes it resonant, as the morning wind made the statue of Memnon sing.

[p. 166] The painter, to use Leonardo's phrase, "transmutes himself into as many colors as are placed before him."

The caricaturist differs from the painter in a single respect only; but a decisive, fundamental one. He not only sees, but opines about what he looks at. He is not impartial; he collaborates with his own intuition and says what he thinks of what he has seen. Hence he provokes, *ipso facto*, the desire to laugh.

Suppose a pretty girl before the pencil of a talented caricaturist. The model will not be so perfect as to epitomize the ideal. If it were so, the artist would insist, for example, on the meager dimensions of the eyes or on the smallness of the mouth. The caricature will exaggerate the defect. It will make the little eyes insignificant, and the mouth, if small in the model, will turn into a slight dot. And that likeness of Phryne will brazenly exhibit her gentle imperfections in its caricatured expression. The sharp and malevolent pencil has already opined that, despite being beautiful, the lips are a trifle too little to agree with the perfection of the whole, and that the eyes turn out to be two unreal smudges, incredibly veiled by the eyebrows and as if lost beneath the immoderate expanse of the forehead. The caricature insinuates slyly: "She is beautiful, indeed; but perhaps a less small mouth and eyes in harmony with the forehead would not have suited her ill." Hence we laugh on contemplating the work of art.

In literature too there are caricaturists [p. 167] of genius. Recall, among others, the subtle French ironist and humorist Jules Renard, one of the shrewdest auscultators of the silent life of things. Examine this brief, profound dialogue: "The Flowers. Will there be sun today?" "The Sunflower. Yes, if I so wish."

"The Watering Can. I beg your pardon, if I so wish, it will rain."

Or this other, stupendous in synthesis and intuition:

"The Wall: I don't know what shiver I feel in my back."

"The Lizard: It's me."

At times caricature reaches tragic proportions. Read the episode of Samson in the Bible. How terribly ridiculous is the blind lover before the perfidious Delilah! What deep pain; what strange laughter springs from our heart at his misfortunes! The laughter of Scripture intimidates and dazzles, like that of Shakespeare or Cervantes; but it leaves in the soul, along with sorrow, a sacred feeling, clean of all contamination.

Music too is wont to laugh. It is in the greatest, in the foremost of all musicians, that the noble art of unfathomable Love and Hope dares to laugh. We do not refer, of course, to the operettas of Offenbach; nor even to the brilliant [p. 168] humor of Schumann or Berlioz. We refer to the terrible "March of the Kings" in Parsifal (which irresistibly evokes in our memory the verses on Samson, by virtue of its firm clipped notes and its imperious leitmotif that limps, limps like a valetudinarian monster); or to Beethoven's eighth symphony, of which Weingartner says that it reproduces in his imagination the tale of Hoffmann in which, while some friendly after-dinner chat among intimates is under way, one of the guests' enormous nose grows before his companions until it nearly realizes Quevedo's line:

"There was a man stuck to a nose."

Art turns out more human with caricatures than without them. Rabelais—distinguished caricaturist of genius—confessed it so without circumlocution, when he exclaimed:

"*Par ce que rire est le propre de l'homme...*"

Of course, a difference is noticeable between the orator and the poet. Oratory triumphs momentarily, but so too it succumbs. Poetry perhaps is slow to conquer, but, if it conquers, it has triumphed. The orator is more vital than the poet, therefore he is less definitive. In him the world throbs as it takes shape. In the poet it becomes, as it were, eternal. One is the psychic current that adorns life; the other the spiritual [p. 169] movement that stylizes and purifies itself. The orator speaks in the present; the poet in the future. The muse of oratory devotes herself to the passion that overwhelms and exalts; that of the poet to the emotion that is contained and deepens. One is extension and the other intension. For the orator, the clamorous success of a second. For the poet the secular and definitive renown. "Time is gold; silence, eternity," as Carlyle said. And the philosopher added: "Altars must be raised to silence."

The poet is a spectator of life. The orator an actor. Poetry means creation and contemplation; eloquence, work. An oratory that does not tend toward action is pure inconsistent declamation, pure unreal verbalism. To invite us to reform the world, to model it, the orators speak; to overcome the pain of life and carry us to the higher plane in which life itself "is beautiful though it be cruel," the poets sing. An orator counsels, persuades, saves or corrupts. A poet neither saves nor corrupts. He was born to express the Platonic Idea that persists above the changes and vicissitudes of existence. Art knows neither how to be moral nor immoral. It looks upon all that is from the eternity of its destiny: "beyond good and evil." Eloquence is another impure art, another combination, like caricature, of aesthetic elements with the intellectual, rational purpose of persuading and moving to action. Hence oratory is always more contingent than poetry, more ephemeral and transitory.

Which of the great orators of the world has not [p. 170] already withered? Who reads Castelar? Where are the devoted followers of Gambetta or Macaulay? The tribune of Jaurès is still in mourning, and in its funereal crepes the formidable breath of the athlete was lost. Clemenceau has scarcely ceased to deliver speeches, and no one remembers him. Which of the great orators of the world overcame the indifference of posterity with the frenzied heat that subjugated the soul of his contemporaries? Cicero lives and will live among the friends of humane letters, for his undulating philosophy and his polished and pure style, which reflected the richest jewels of the Latin genius; but the orator would no longer drive Catiline from the Capitol. Demosthenes himself would not move us as when he upheld the "Case of the Crown" before an astonished Greece. In the words of his rival, Aeschines, he was a terrible lion; but what roar, what voice is not lost and extinguished in the passage of the centuries...? In contrast, the glory of Homer and Virgil is lit by every dawn, and as it were spiritualizes itself and surpasses itself...

Far from being reducible to mere erudition, history, taken in its fullness, is an organic, aesthetic effort to reconstruct the past; and only he who reconstructs the life that was and the world that was made and afterwards disintegrated in the everlasting evolution of things deserves the name of historian. That which happened once only in time and space and will never again be as it was, whether it concerns humanity, the [p. 171] animal or vegetable species, or, finally, the planet itself (the great historical being that made all history possible); such is its object; beings unique among their kin; men unique among men; peoples, races, and civilizations that are personal, always individual, always different.

The word biography has been reserved solely for the history of a human person. Biography is always history. That is, the faithful painting of a unity, whether it be a being or a nation. The great historians are those who, besides possessing the indispensable endowments of erudition and criticism, know how to restore, to revive the subject of their inquiries.

The historical work begins with a critical and scientific preface. The sources are discussed; the documents are assayed; the witnesses are purified before the tribunal of "pure reason." But this effort is not enough. One must go on in the historical work until the last end is reached.

When the facts have been discussed conscientiously and the documents speak clearly, the most admirable work has still not begun. One must complete it with an intuition of the whole.

Suppose, to use a metaphor that may perhaps illuminate the doctrine, that the historian is like one who had to construct a solid body from data which, scanty and scattered, lie in museums and ruins, in libraries and archives. Suppose that the solid to be constructed were a pyramid [p. 172]. But, at the same time, grant that, despite having computed and arranged the geometrical construction, one still did not have the swift and luminous intuition of the total pyramid; that one did not see in the mind the conjunction of the faces of the pyramid at a point. Despite all the effort of criticism, the geometrical solid would remain to be constructed, unconstructed.

So with the historian as with the geometer. One must intuit, project one's own consciousness toward an ideal point at which everything converges like the faces of the pyramid in the explanatory metaphor. If the point of convergence is divined; if one has artistic genius to sympathize mysteriously with the character of a people or of a man of genius, historical creation is achieved *ipso facto*. If one remains indefinitely in bare and incomplete criticism, one is not a historian.

Now, this last effort is essentially artistic. Only by intuition is it attained. Only by poetic genius is it accomplished.

Why does the history of Joan of Arc related by Michelet or the biography of Frederick the Great written by Carlyle captivate us? Why do we prefer to a repertory of news about the Roman republic a few pages of Livy, and to all the data on the crimes of the Caesars the severe and gallant style of Tacitus? Because criticism is not intuition, nor do voluminous repertories revive [p. 173] the past; because history is always art, profound art of evoking above the dust of the centuries the soul of the centuries. In sum, another synthesis of aesthetic elements with the intellectual purpose of investigating existence<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> See, for the development of the author's thought on the character and nature of History, his book entitled "El Concepto de la Historia Universal," from which these few paragraphs on history as a work of art have been drawn.

## Capítulo XIII. La crítica de arte y la significación del humanismo — Chapter XIII. Art Criticism and the Significance of Humanism

[p. 177] Have you found in your life anything more sterile than rhetorical criticism? The so-called "scientific criticism" of works of art. The rhetorician who, with the "figures of speech," scrupulously defined, sought to judge the merit of genius, and held up his sample to it with all the candor of his inoffensive, secondary and bothersome activity; the rhetorician such as Gómez Hermosilla, whom our fathers knew and feared and, more than our fathers, our grandfathers; a musty personage whom we laboriously exhume from our childhood memories, understood the proper nature of art better than the scientific critics, paradigms of aesthetic unintelligence.

Rhetoricians and learned psychologists and physicians, who put their sickle into another's field; who decide, according to the logical canons of mastery, on a work of art, and define its style, its value and its form, by virtue of purely rational data, are the same personage at different moments of his action, the very same pedantic schoolmaster who speaks of what he does not understand. Rhetoric and Science, or what is taken for "scientific criticism," are invasions of pure reason [p. 178] into the sphere of intuition, which is the exclusive empire of art.

The rhetoricians and physicians who, in these latter times, have exercised, as they see it, the high magisterium of criticism, never, as the artists whose merits they assay did, knew how to see the reality that lay before their eyes; rather, when others discover it, they hasten to say, if they are rhetoricians: "this is a metonymy, a trope, a preterition," or, if they are physicians: "this is the consequence of the author's having been mad, or epileptic, or mystical." Mysticism, like genius, be it said in passing, is a disease for some contemporary psychologists.

It is the same defect in one and in the others, the same myopia, an identical limitation. Since they do not see, they think. Thinking is tolerable only when one cannot see. If we human beings, like God, as the theologians affirm, could contemplate everything that exists in a single synthetic glance, in one most luminous intuition, we should already have abandoned this bothersome artifice of reasoning, which, after prodigious labors, often leads us to the absurd.

Max Nordau<sup>1</sup> was the type of the physicians turned critics of Art, as Gómez Hermosilla is, for readers of the Castilian tongue, the archetype of the rhetoricians.

<sup>1</sup> See, above all, his noisy book "Degeneration," in which he affronts all the great poets and writers of the century, Wagner, Ibsen, Tolstoy, Ruskin, etc.

Nordau has, among his most charming inventions, [p. 179] a most precious one for assaying the works of the human mind. It consists in discussing the private life of the great artists, in order to discover the secret of their magnificent productions.

Does the sublime duet of Tristan and Isolde move you to delirium? Does the prelude to Lohengrin fill you with enthusiasm (remember that enthusiasm means being the victim of the god)? Do you ride Olympian-wise with the Valkyries, to the sound of Wagner's magic orchestra? It is that Wagner was mad, mystical, degenerate, and you too are so a little, enough, at least, to enjoy his morbid and seductive art.

Is it not true that this is an easy way of judging, without leaving the pharmacy laboratory or the clinic?

The rhetoricians were fools, but the physicians have surpassed them. How do they not understand that the man is one thing and the work another? Let us suppose, without granting it (as one says speaking scholastically), let us suppose that they are right to affirm the morbidity of genius. The work is not the man. A poem of Verlaine is not sick. The music of Wagner suffers from no secret ailment. The novels of Tolstoy are imperishable works of art. If genius is a delirium, let us all be delirious with genius.

Besides, there are no normal men. We are all a little demented, physicians no less than the sane and the mad. Each one has his little bit of morbidity, of abnormality, of personality. If [p. 180] we were all identical, as regards mental health, no one in spiritual matters would have a right to be. Perhaps personality is the most trivial form of madness.

And if neither rhetoric nor medicine hits upon the proper nature of artistic criticism, who will know what criticism ought to be?

The poets know it. They have always been good critics, perhaps somewhat imperious. Read these lines of Oscar Wilde; they teach, more than all the rhetorics in the world, the office of the critic. "You say that the highest Criticism deals with art not as expression but as impression merely, and is therefore creative and free; in fact, an art in itself, standing in the same relation to the creative work as that work stands to the visible world of form and color or the invisible world of passion and thought." In short, Criticism is a work of art, but an impure one, like Caricature, History and Eloquence. Its end is not contemplation, the disinterested expression of beauty.

Art, the expression of intuition, manifests the reality of things, independently of every utilitarian end. Pure art (sonata, poem, statue, temple, dance, etc.) never has a demonstrative or practical purpose. Its purposiveness is without purpose, as Kant says, that is, it does not transcend the work of art itself, except for extrinsic, social and moral reasons. Art, far from implying some activity other than the purely aesthetic, suffices unto itself. [p. 181] This could not be said of any other human activity, not even of artistic criticism.

The foregoing does not amount to a confession of the thesis of "art for art's sake," a theory full of false romantic generosity, which once seduced the men of the generation of 1830, and afterwards turned into the "ivory tower" and other picturesque absurdities, as foolish as literary creeds always are when they result from carrying to an extreme, that is, from falsifying, a true thought. Style is the man and is the age, even in these days that run and these men we deal with today, of whom it might be said that they adorn themselves with the grand style of having none of their own. To pursue art for art's sake is false; but there is no great artist who has failed to carry out his work "beyond good and evil."

Criticism, which for Wilde is a work of art, we believe ought not for everyone to be merely a pure work of art. We recall a certain hermetic combination of the chatter of several friends with the movements of the clouds and the tercets of the Divine Comedy, which the curious reader can find without difficulty in the book Intentions. We recall, too, the exceptional interpretations of Beethoven and of Schumann, performed by spirits such as Godowsky's, or the fleetingness of Schubert's "Moment Musical," interpreted by Russian dancers, amid the lightest of veils and bodily attitudes (hands, arms, hips, feet) of a few [p. 182] beautiful women, who harmonized their beauty as in a Greek amphora that glided, musically, in prodigious balance, along a subtle edge. All this, pages of Wilde, the perfect and sure interpretation of Godowski and the delight of the exemplary dance, suggests to us the essence of criticism: a work of art which the intelligence approaches in order to combine with it, respecting it nonetheless as an organism that has full right to exist in its individuality, and which only thus, respected and obeyed, can offer to the most intelligent men, not its secret, because life and art have never offered their secret to reason, but yes the communication of its character and of its proportion with the other things of the world.

The critic will be deficient if he does not know how to find the intimacy existing among purely literary works and musical and artistic ones. Moreover, if he is not capable of perceiving, in the forming of reality, this same intimate consortium of the pictorial and the poetic. To speak more clearly: the best commentary on Berlioz's "Symphonie Fantastique" is a painting by Delacroix. The life of Spinoza is understood only in a Flemish interior, where the figures are exquisitely reflected in the burnished copper of the plates and dishes that adorn the meek bourgeoisie of the chimney-piece. El Greco painted refined figures, dressed in black, like the friends of Saint Ignatius who, gathering in Paris, founded the militia of the Society of Jesus. [p. 183] Or, to offer a contemporary example: what was Mallarmé but the synthesis of a French spirit with the philosophy of Hegel and the evocative and indefinite music of symbolism? Is not, perhaps, the best commentary on the music of Debussy a page of Bergson or a poem of Verlaine...?

Wundt distinguishes two kinds of art, the musical and the constructive. What is not music is architecture. What is not architecture is music; unless, as in the dance, music itself is constructed in a tangible rhythm, or architecture takes on tonality before the rapt eyes of the spectator. Aztec sculpture is farther from the statues of Phidias than the Venus de Milo is from the tragedies of Sophocles. There exists an inner concatenation among all the artistic fruits of a civilization and among all the productions of a great poet. But let us beware of exaggerating. Every civilization produces what destroys it; the difference, which will later be law, destroyed by another irreducible difference, which will also be law, whether it concern the production of a man or of an age. Góngora represents the irreducible difference of the second Spanish golden century; Victor Hugo's "The Satyr" created French modernism. Certain simple verses of Rubén Darío inspired the complicated simplicity of Amado Nervo. Just as there is always in a tree a branch unruly even to the gardener's shears, there is always in individual and collective [p. 184] culture the unruly branch, which tomorrow will be the docility of all and the expression or the seal of the moment. If the critic does not see this, he has seen nothing.

Our century, a twilight historical moment of unquestionable decadence, besides its painful moral attributes, distinguishes itself in the purely artistic and literary sphere by a certain lineage of wit that is spent on Byzantine trifles and works of affected acuteness.

Every decadent century is a kind of excessive stylization; a scholasticism rather ingenious but not profound; an excessive complication of the means of aesthetic expression. As the acanthus of Louis XV ornamentation, with its effeminate contortions, debases the noble Hellenic mold from which it sprang, so contemporary literature complicates itself uselessly in order to hide with its cosmetics the lack of true originality that defines it.

The Churrigueresque of the monuments of the Colonial period, like the Gothic of decadence, with its swarm of little columns and images that denote only imaginative incontinence, not rhythmic congruence or critical balance, are the artistic equivalent of many contemporary books, empty of human content, fasting of authentic beauty; ingenious, not inspired; subtle perhaps, not exact; somewhat perverse, deliberately malevolent. One would say that contemporary writers and essayists would renounce, if they could, firm and real talent, for the sake of a sterile preciosity. The poets show their [p. 185] disrespect for the noblest values of human life and scorn the differential sentiments of the soul: love, compassion, meekness, justice, in order to exalt hatred, cruelty, injustice and falsehood. The law appears to be, to talk nonsense gracefully; to overwhelm us with literary juggling and the autumnal japonaiseries of thought. Nothing that implies a congruent development; nothing that disposes and orders rationally the fruits of the imagination. As if superior art were not always intuition and reason at one and the same time:

"firmness and light, like rock crystal!"

Wit and grace, as things in themselves, are a sham. No one is more graceful than Aristophanes and no one stronger. No one is more witty than Cervantes or Shakespeare; but the pleasantries of the Quixote and the wedding of Titania and Oberon are the fruit of a sovereign intelligence placed at the service of the noblest ideal. Heine carried with him a deep human sorrow, and Voltaire defended the rights of humanity and justice, against the judges of Calas and La Barre, with the same diabolical pen that traced the adventures of Candide and Micromegas.

What contemporary art lacks is not invention but harmony, that is, reason. We forget that art is the mirror of the soul and the hermetic union of the faculties. So long as we depart from the course that [p. 186] Minerva marked with her bronze lance, our productions will live no longer than the fashion allots them. They will die with it. Classical works, on the other hand, have endured and will endure. To set out to speak or write ingeniously is to ignore the secret of literary production. The aphoristic style is proper to seers or to idiots. Only its creator himself could tell, in a few verses, the work of creation. We human beings must reason, develop and explain. A profound thought will give birth to many witty ones; a witty one can only have arisen at the expense of a profound one. Hence the witty impression of the concept pretends to imitate the mystic haze that surrounds every true intuition. Since we cannot be profound, we are obscure; since the clear sight of what we cannot see is forbidden to us, we substitute for it the deceit of expression and the verbalism of the concept. One must say obscure things clearly; for if the vague is expressed vaguely, nothing at all is expressed.

Firmness in the core is grace in expression. The torrent leaps like an apocalyptic bull over the age-old rocks; then scatters in fine threads of silver, and at last rolls over the soft grass. The sun cracks the bark of trees and scorches the crests of mountains, yet it knows how to gild with its rays an acorn, and to unbridle the pompon of an acacia. The ingenious Ulysses, of whom Homer tells, persuaded the Greeks with his subtle speech [p. 187], but his spirit had been tempered in combat.

When the energy that animates us to bring to a good end the enterprise we covet is given us in excess, it flowers and bears fruit in strokes of wit and of firmness. Nothing is more graceful than the infants and little angels of Donatello's Cantoria, and no one is wiser, after Greece, in the use of the chisel, than the marvelous Florentine sculptor. The girdle of Venus rested on the hips of the goddess and girt her weightless belly; but in her lap Mars reclined his victorious head, and only to the caresses of the mighty-armed did that Greek emblem of grace yield.

Criticism is more useless than it seems at first sight; but as inevitable as all useless actions, as all artistic activities. To such a point that today many prefer, to the recreation of the work of art, the reflection of criticism. Perhaps the case might be explained by the haste in which we live, by the lack of aesthetic sense in many people, or by a very natural desire to understand what art means. Works of art cannot be solved, fully, in their fundamental unknown, just as the unknown of no being is ever solved. The critic does what the physicist does, relating to one another the objects of his world; what the biologist does, harmonizing environment with life; what the historian does, who forgets the personage in order to fix on how his actions mesh with the actions [p. 188] of other men. Acts that are conjugated: this is the world; this is history and the soul; this, too, is artistic activity.

For that reason, Criticism and History are the clearest exponent of culture, if not the deepest. Aristotle, the man who knew everything, the first historian of the human spirit, was the founder of the critical disciplines in Greece. Petrarch and Erasmus, in the Renaissance, exercised a universal magisterium. What is Voltaire but the conscience of an age, which delights in the music of Rameau, the disaster of the Church, the physics of Newton and the politics of Frederick the Great...?

Nietzsche, "the last European event," as he himself called it, thinking of Wagner, referring to the wit of Renan, wrote this sorrowful phrase: "One more calamity for poor sick France, sick in will." France has healed. The formidable ascetic of the "Twilight of the Idols" would not pity her today. But what would Nietzsche think of the wit of Anatole France, twin of Renan's, and like him fond of ideological diversions? He would say, perhaps, disrespectfully, that it constitutes another calamity for the France of today. Nevertheless, Anatole France was, perhaps, the most illustrious of contemporary writers. Bernard Shaw, D'Annunzio, Maeterlinck, Anatole France... Behold the great names of Parnassus, the summits of human thought. Among them one sees only the witty and subtle masters [p. 189] of the literary moment; eminent no doubt, not supreme.

Where are the Ibsens, the Tolstoys, the Nietzsches, the Wagners?

Where is genius today...?

Art criticism, in its aesthetic and logical, artistic and speculative complexity, is an impure art, but one of very rich human content. It reflects humanism as the fruit of tradition and history, and constitutes the exponent of the cultural life "whole and full," as Otfried Müller says, in each century. Petrarch, Erasmus, Voltaire, Lessing, Sainte-Beuve, Carlyle, Emerson, Ruskin, Renan, Taine, Nietzsche, Menéndez y Pelayo, Benedetto Croce are its greatest heroes, from the Renaissance to our own days. Spirits curious about all the movements of the spirit; distinguished philologists, in the broadest and noblest sense of the word; historians of the human spirit.

## Capítulo XIV. La psicología del placer estético. Fechner y la experimentación estética. Límites de la teoría psicológica del arte — Chapter XIV. The Psychology of Aesthetic Pleasure. Fechner and Aesthetic Experimentation. Limits of the Psychological Theory of Art

[p. 193] The psychology of pleasure is less favored in the philosophical speculation of all times, not only the contemporary, than that of pain. The same holds when it concerns art. Painful and somber scenes abound in universal literature, difficult trials; in contrast, the artistic representation of pleasure is rarely formulated; rarely is a radiant instant of happiness defined.

The cause, assuredly, lies in the fact that pain is more interesting to consciousness, because it signifies the limit of spiritual action, whereas pleasure is like the natural atmosphere of the soul and the consequence of its action; at least, for its innate and consubstantial egoism, which believes it so. Certain dramas of Shakespeare are nothing but blood and pain (the historical ones). The music of Verdi, in its first romantic period, knows only the tones of red and black. How rare are the absolutely pleasant moments in literary art! To find them one would have to turn to the choruses of Sophocles, above all those of "Oedipus at Colonus," but without forgetting the instability of human happiness, which is formulated [p. 194] in "Oedipus Rex," transposing and adorning the maxim of Solon, dear to ancient wisdom, in the following terms: "Inhabitants of Thebes: Behold Oedipus, who deciphered the famous riddle and was a powerful hero, to such a point that no other citizen could see his prosperity without envy, into what terrible whirlwind of calamities he was plunged. Therefore, wait to see the last day of a mortal, and call no one happy except him who has reached the end of his existence without having fallen into misfortune!"

We, however, need to undertake a psychology of aesthetic pleasure. The principal obstacle that meets us is the complex nature of the sentiment itself, presumable *a priori* by the readers of these Principles of Aesthetics who have taken the trouble to follow the development of the various theories that explain different phases or aspects of poetic intuition (creative).

Of course, the plan for the development of this chapter is drawn in advance. We shall have to ask ourselves what phases of artistic enjoyment each of the ideas explains that have seemed to us adequate to the clarification of beauty and its different modalities.

As we set forth in the first chapter of this book, art, like luxury, like social life itself, like the growing complexity of the higher organisms, obeys an absolutely irrefutable cosmological condition: the excess of force, the *surplus* of energy, the superfluous power [p. 195] of which Schiller speaks. When something is given in abundance, the correlative action proves easy, and, as that ease of acting is reflected in consciousness, it engenders enjoyment, because even when we are not conscious of the cause of the enjoyment engendered, we are conscious of its effects. At this point, but only at this point, the pleasure of the player is identified with aesthetic satisfaction. To be able to spend what is one's own, without counting or sparing it, is a prodigality common to luxury, play and art.

Experimentation, as K. Groos says, the joy of being able to do (*Freude am Können*), is the mother of all the arts, common to beasts and to man. Monkeys already amuse themselves by striking hollow objects or by contemplating themselves assiduously and shamelessly in the surface of a mirror. We enjoy our own freedom in taking pleasure in the appearance we ourselves have created. Hence, as Groos says, the generating principle of self-display, ornamentation and imitation. Aesthetic pleasure is summed up, from this point of view, in the idea that only the strong, the rich, the one who enjoys, for acting, such a store that what he spends in his action is only a minimal part of what he could spend, is free. Gracián says in his Treatise on the Hero that he must always practice incomprehensibilities of means. Freedom is the force that is burned or thrown away, because it is surplus to him who has already satisfied the pressure of his need.

But in man, as Kant saw, aesthetic play [p. 196] is a free association of the mental faculties, which are no longer bound by the double fetter of logic and will. On hearing a certain piece of music, a waltz of Strauss, let us say, we go like the mythological nymphs and nereids, leaping over the surface of the stream, spurred by the rhythm of the piece; and, like the waves of the "Blue Danube," our imagination and our understanding play, with our memory and our desire. Our mental energies enjoy their own wealth and mobility.

Schopenhauer and Bergson have elucidated the other aspect of aesthetic enjoyment: liberation. To live is to attend, constantly, to the act that is being prepared; to be a slave of the will, which, constantly too, subordinates us to desire. At a given moment, the world is no longer good for seizing, but for seeing or hearing, for contemplating. We are the pure subject of knowledge. The veil that hid from us the individuality of things is rent. Liberation! Liberation! "The wheel of Ixion has stopped." Here is another phase of aesthetic enjoyment, well elucidated.

But none of the foregoing explanations can be compared with that which *Einfühlung* provides us. Erect upon our own excess of strength, free causes of our Apollonian dream, pure subjects of disinterested contemplation, we now leap, emancipated from the bonds of the will, over our own spirit; we flee [p. 197] with it and infuse it, with Dionysian delirium, as Nietzsche would say, upon the things of the world. From this effusion springs the purest torrent of aesthetic enjoyment. Things learn to speak of our own enthusiasm. It is the case to repeat the word of Zarathustra: "Man is no longer an artist, he has become a work of art." Poetic intuition (creative) has become its expression, is its expression, as Croce would say. Superfluous power, overflowing life, joy of being cause and principle, incomprehensibility of means, freedom, liberation, *Einfühlung*, expression: this is aesthetic satisfaction. Nothing more than this?

To Aesthetics conceived as a mystical theory of the beautiful or as the dialectical development of an absolute principle, in the manner of the Hegelians, "Aesthetics from above," as Fechner said, this thinker opposed his "Aesthetics from below," or empirical aesthetics, which has now developed in Germany and counts numerous adherents and followers<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> See: Meumann, "System of Aesthetics" and "Introduction to Present-Day Aesthetics." Spanish versions by F. Vela and J. de Urríes y Azara. Calpe, 1924.

In his "Introduction to Aesthetics," Fechner formulates six fundamental laws or principles of aesthetic pleasure. The first principle is that of the aesthetic threshold: "The first condition for things to please us is that the effect they produce possess a determinate strength." (Colors too weak and sounds too low cannot attain [p. 198] sentimental effect). The second principle of Fechner's Aesthetics is that of aid or reinforcement. It is expressed thus: "From the totality of conditions of aesthetic pleasure that are not opposed, and that in themselves are worth little, a pleasing resultant is formed, often much greater than what corresponds to the isolated conditions in themselves." For example, rhythm without sound or sounds without rhythm do not engender the satisfaction that is produced by enjoying rhythm and sounds at once. Then the pleasure is much greater. The third principle is the harmonious linking of the manifold. Unity pleases, variety gratifies; on the other hand, uniformity displeases, as does incoherence. The fourth principle teaches truth or absence of contradiction: "When we perceive the same thing in two or more different aspects, and it awakens in us different representations, pleasure arises if we recognize the lack of contradiction among them; but if they seem to us contradictory, displeasure is produced in us. But it is necessary that we expressly recognize the possibility of contradiction in the representation. Example: winged angels cannot be presented in such a way that the wings do not seem fit for flying, for otherwise contradictory representations are awakened that displease"<sup>1</sup>. The fifth principle is, as Fechner says, the supreme formal law, which organizes the quantitative principles of the aesthetic threshold [p. 199] and reinforcement with the qualitative ones of harmonious linking and absence of contradiction. It teaches that, "if the other principles are to take effect, they must be recognized with complete clarity"<sup>1</sup>. Fechner's sixth principle, the only material one, that is, which refers to the content of aesthetic pleasure, teaches that, when any thing pleases us, there operate jointly in our satisfaction the direct or objective factor (everything that is externally given with the thing), and the associative factor (series of earlier experiences, which are fused, immediately, into a whole, with the impressions given from without, or are assimilated with them).

<sup>1</sup> Meumann. "Introduction to Present-Day Aesthetics." p. 17

<sup>1</sup> *Opus cit.* p. 17.

In our opinion, the brilliant German psychologist, in formulating the laws or bases of aesthetic experimentation, opened as broad a field to the philosophy of art as that which he gave psychologists a glimpse of in laying the foundations of psychophysics; but, in the same way that psychophysiology cannot encompass the whole domain of Psychology, the studies of experimental aesthetics likewise do not suffice for the constitution of Aesthetics.

A very important legion of investigators has followed in Fechner's footsteps, and has developed, in complex laboratory work and most interesting inquiries, his essential ideas, confirming or correcting them. We are devoted partisans [p. 200] of experimental aesthetics or "Aesthetics from below," but we are also of "Aesthetics from above," because we think that speculation and experience, in concert, have formed modern science. Aesthetic ideas and facts, combined with one another, constitute the work of human thought.

The principal benefit we expect from experimental aesthetics is the study of the objective element of beauty. If it were to be confirmed that there are forms, rhythms and colors universally liked by humanity, throughout its prehistory and history; if it were possible to discover a unanimous *consensus*, in the simplest elements, *verbi gratia*, of ornamentation and music; this universally liked thing would constitute the objective beauty of reality, because, practically, there is no difference whatever between universal subjectivity (what is admitted without disagreement by all spirits) and absolute objectivity. At least, in our feeling, in accordance with our epistemological criterion, the two are identified.

Since the studies of experimental psychology are barely of yesterday, much remains to be done before fully satisfactory results are obtained; but the brilliant thought of Fechner can lead us (above all through his distinction of the direct and associative elements or factors of aesthetic pleasure) to the adequate posing of the gravest [p. 201] question, today barely sketched, of objective beauty. Thus too we shall discover new elements of aesthetic pleasure, because, as Aristotle says very well<sup>1</sup>, "acts specifically different cannot be completed except by pleasures different in kind. The acts of thought differ from the acts of the senses; and the latter are no less different among themselves. The pleasures that complete them must, then, differ. Each pleasure belongs, exclusively, to the act that it completes." What is called aesthetic enjoyment is, as one sees, a most complex synthesis of distinct pleasures; but we hope that the reader of this chapter will readily grant that, after reading it, he has inquired somewhat further on this particular matter than before deciding to undertake it.

<sup>1</sup> "Nicomachean Ethics." Chapter X, 5.

The enormous significance of a psychological theory of art is readily apparent. Nevertheless, Aesthetics is not a chapter of general Psychology. The ideas of Kant, Schiller, Schopenhauer, Bergson, etc., to which we have had recourse in elucidating the problems of beauty, are not purely psychological ideas. *Einfühlung*, like transcendental apperception, is a primary act of the spirit, and not a phenomenon of psychic association, as we set forth in due course<sup>2</sup>. The efforts of the psychologists who try to resolve into psychic phenomena, which they consider [p. 202] simpler, these acts of empathic projection seem to us useless. Moreover, Aesthetics as the Philosophy of Art embraces at least two non-psychological questions: What is the social cause of the work of art? What is the value of humanity's artistic elaboration? Both profound problems are discussed in the immediately following chapter of this book, and in the XVI, where we affirm, as the reader will see, the irreducible world of beauty.

## Capítulo XV. El arte como fenómeno social. Individualismo y colectivismo. El arte como expresión del alma colectiva — Chapter XV. Art as a Social Phenomenon. Individualism and Collectivism. Art as an Expression of the Collective Soul

[p. 205] There is a fundamental opposition between society as a natural phenomenon and society as a community for the elaboration of culture. In society as a natural phenomenon, everything is determined by the play of social forces or factors, concurrent with the aggregate and concomitant with it. Society, so considered, is an evolutionary cosmic fact, synergic, a biological fact of adaptation and a psychic phenomenon of the consciousness of the species, prior to humanity itself. In it there concur the geographic or telluric action of climate, fauna, and flora; the anthropological action of race, heredity, and population; the action, also properly social, of the division of labor and imitation; but, alongside these determinants, which make of society a regular manifestation, however highly complex, of the universal energies, there exists the spontaneous life of the community, which manifests itself in the family, law, the State, etc., and, above all, in religion, language, art, philosophy, and science.

[p. 206] The opposition between society and community for culture is intimately related to the other fundamental antithesis, that of individualism and collectivism. The more individual a social phenomenon is, the more it reveals its character of spontaneity, which makes it break the frame of sociological determinism so as to assimilate it to art; for, precisely, art is, of all social fruits, the freest and most autonomous, or, in other terms, the most individual, without ceasing on that account to obey, in part, the determinism of the other social causes concurrent with genius.

The object of this chapter is to determine what there is of the spontaneous and irreducible, and what there is of the collective, in the work of art.

A purely individualist conception of art is false; but an aesthetics that neglects the individual factor, and claims to deliver to us the secret of humanity's artistic production by means of collective causes and actions, is false too, and with greater reason. Let us avail ourselves of some examples that will clarify the meaning of the thesis we maintain. The symphony derives from the typical form of instrumental music: the sonata. It was created by Ph. Emmanuel Bach, with antecedents, nevertheless, in the Italian classics and Scarlatti; Mozart, Haydn, and Beethoven developed it. In the Ninth Symphony the classical composition of this musical genre was modified by the inclusion of a final piece with [p. 207] choruses; and Wagner, lastly, transformed the dramatic symphony into the symphonic drama.

Here is a genre which, in evolving, reveals to us: first, the continuity of the social action of a school, owing to the multiple imitations of a model; and second, the most individual, irreducible action of the creative genius, who in the course of musical history goes by the names of Bach, Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Wagner. The evolution of the symphony is, then, an individual and collective fact, indissolubly collective and individual. Only that the collective has resulted from converting an individual model into the social law of the artistic taste of a century.

"Ludwig Richter relates, in the 'Memoirs' of his life," says Wölfflin in his "Fundamental Concepts in the History of Art," "that once, finding himself at Tivoli with three young comrades like himself, they set about painting a piece of the landscape, firmly resolved not to depart in the least from nature; in spite of which, although the model was the same for all, and each one gathered with talent what his eyes saw, four wholly different pictures resulted; as different from one another as were the personalities of the four painters."

Nevertheless, Taine is right, in part, when he writes<sup>1</sup>. "Among human works, the work of art seems [p. 208] the most fortuitous; one would think it was born by chance, without rule or reason, given over to the accidental, the unforeseen, and the arbitrary. Indeed, when the artist creates, he does so according to his personal fancy; and if the public approves, it is in accordance with its passing taste. The artist's inventions and the public's sympathies, all of it is spontaneous, free, and as capricious, in appearance, as the wind that blows. Yet, like the wind that blows, everything has its precise conditions and its fixed laws." On this last point we do not agree with the rigid determinism of the celebrated critic. There is a ground of perennial indeterminacy in the evolution of art, which does not exist in the movements of the wind, and it is the personality of the creative artist. Since the work of art results from the projection of the individual soul upon the world, art is always (within the determinism of races, the various social milieus, the schools, and the different historical moments of its evolution) something singular and genuine. It would be impossible that the mass of images, sensations, ideas, emotions, etc., of three contemporary poets, affiliated with the same literary sect and developing the same artistic theme, should coincide in their expression: Alfred de Vigny in "La Maison du Berger," Lamartine in "Le Lac," and Victor Hugo in "La Tristesse d'Olympio," forged three sovereign lyric poems, which have in common only the poet's spiritual posture before nature; but which, in everything else, show themselves [p. 209] as different as works of art always are in what they have of the absolute. Meteorology will become an exact science and the wind a physical phenomenon as intelligible by its law as the fall of bodies is today, for example, and then still it will remain a truth that, above all the collective, sociological reasons that make of art a fruit of civil life, the ground of indeterminacy persists: the creative genius, poetic intuition.

<sup>1</sup> "Philosophie de l'Art." Preface.

"Such individual modes, or styles, are naturally more pronounced when the effort bears upon one and the same object of nature. Botticelli and Lorenzo di Credi are contemporary artists and of the form and as creation, which differs from the closing years of the fourteen-hundreds; but when Botticelli draws a female body, the result is so much his own, so peculiar as an interpretation of form and as creation, that it differs from a female nude by Lorenzo di Credi as unmistakably and fundamentally as an oak from a linden"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> Wölfflin. Opus cit. p. 3 of the Spanish translation.

Difference, individuality, is the essence of things. Human personality, the most individual thing, the most different in the universe, and art, the apotheosis of that supreme difference. Everything would be unique in artistic creation, if artists did not set their originality against the common ground of the [p. 210] ideas and sentiments of the spiritual community to which they belong. If this common ground did not exist, the work of art would be a pure mystical state, hermetic and ineffable. As art develops in history, artistic creations become less and less explicable by civil tradition, and never, before or after, completely reducible to it. The physicist Mach has written that a scientific law is a limitation of possibilities. Nothing more. Race, milieu, moment, schools, artistic traditions, and the progress of technique are likewise mere limitations of the possibilities of creative genius. As in the Italian farce, in which each character may say what he pleases, within the general plot of the dramatic piece, which, in its general outlines, has been defined by the author. Mere limitation of possibilities are the race and the Spanish traditions of the seventeenth century for a Lope, a Calderón, or a Tirso. Their dramas and comedies express the essential aesthetic difference, within the bosom of the community to which they belong.

But if it proves inexact to consider art a pure social phenomenon, to make of it an exclusive expression of the individual soul cannot be admitted either. The reason for this lies in the very general psychology of *Einfühlung*, which we have developed in earlier pages. The soul of the artist, wholly projected upon the object of aesthetic intuition, does not for that reason detach itself from the [p. 211] collective sentiments and the ideas of the community. An artist is a man of his epoch, of his race, and of his historical moment, as Taine would say; consequently, he projects, in his creation, the ideas of his time and the preoccupations of his nation. Moreover, the objects upon which empathic projection is carried out vary with the epochs, and change to such a point that, for example, the conception of a new Iliad due to a contemporary poet seems impossible to us. Strictly speaking, humanity has had two great epic poets, Homer and Camoens (Virgil is only the most glorious moon of the unfailing Greek sun). One sang the Trojan War and the other the marvelous expedition of the Portuguese in the era of the maritime discoveries. The other indisputable epics are the great national songs of France, Germany, and Spain; the songs of heroic deeds, such as the *Chanson de Roland*, the Nibelungs, and the Romancero.

Today humanity could not engender (as it has not engendered them in connection with the War of the Nations) new epic poems like the Iliad or the Lusiads. The reason is that we do not have the epic soul, after the manner of the Greeks, who made of the Apollonian poem not only the basis of their poetry, epic and dramatic, but the foundation of their social culture. Homer is for Plato, as for Alexander, the very essence of Hellas.

Therefore, not only in the subjective and internal sphere do collective preoccupations accompany the artist [p. 212] in the elaboration of the work of art and project themselves into his creation; they also contribute, powerfully, to exciting poetic intuition in the direction that the community to which the artist belongs prefers. They are causes concurrent with the efficient cause: individual poetic intuition.

On the other hand, although without genuine personality there are no works of art, the truly creative genius is exceptional. He is called, in tragedy, Aeschylus; in symphonic music, Haydn or Mozart; in sculpture, Donatello or Michelangelo; in lyric poetry, Shelley or Leopardi, Shakespeare in drama, Cervantes in the novel, Schubert in song. And, between the man of genius, who initiates an artistic revolution, and the public that in the end accepts it, there is a series of *poetae minores* who form the *trait d'union* between the spiritual community and the work of genius. Around Lope and Calderón, for example, there exists a group of great spirits devoted, like them, to dramatic composition; and the Calderonian comedy and drama of cloak and sword are, as Menéndez y Pelayo very well says, "what we would call today comedies of manners of the middle class." "In all of them the dominant passion that serves as motive, at the same time as impulse and nucleus of the plot, is love"<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> "Calderón y su Teatro." Pp. 341 and 342.

Not every invention is an invention in the strict sense, nor is every work of art, however individual it may always be, something absolutely [p. 213] original. False is the hypothesis of Carlyle, who sees in human history. "A labyrinth and chaos; an abatis of trees and brush-wood; a world-wide jungle, at once growing and dying."<sup>1</sup>. False also, because exclusive, is the rival theory, that of Taine. Neither the pure individualism of the English mystic nor the collectivism of the French philosopher exhausts the explanation of the history of art. Genius and its spiritual community concur in artistic elaboration. To point out how far the common contribution reaches, and in what the singularity of the artist consists, is one of the most complex and difficult tasks of criticism.

<sup>1</sup> Oliver Cromwell's letters and speeches.

The exaggeration opposed to that committed by Carlyle, concerning the part that society plays in production, led Tolstoy to affirm that art is purely one of the means of civil communication among men; an activity whose object consists in transmitting, from one individual to another, the best and most elevated sentiments of the human soul.

In this way, the most conspicuous ethicism or moralism is substituted for the *consensus* of the human race, which has always given, as the object of art, in all races and epochs, the expression of beauty. There are great and imperishable works of art whose object is hatred. Shakespeare's Othello, which, were it lacking, would leave the treasure of beauty diminished and battered, is a drama in which, [p. 214] the protagonist is as essential as the exceptional villain who intelligently pursues him. Hatred is beautiful, as love and lust are beautiful, and abnegation and sacrifice sublime. Therefore, in the face of the extremes of artistic collectivism, one may repeat the energetic expression of the great Germanic individualist, who has left us the truest and most beautiful of the interpretations of tragic art in his book "Die Geburt der Tragödie oder Griechenthum und Pessimismus:" "You are beautiful, and therefore I love you, life, even though you be cruel!"

In sum, art is, in a certain sense at least, an aspect of the collective soul, as Wundt says, intimately related to the evolution of language, religion, and customs. In the first artistic works, the condition of the social state and the progressive development of the imagination are reflected; "just as the primitive forms of life allow us to ascertain the influence of the physical medium better than the higher organisms do," which already enjoy a definite individuality; but, as the evolution of art is realized, the store of aesthetic forms, expressions, and elements is so copious that artistic production depends, above all, on individual genius, the synthesizer of those forms and elements, according to the urgency of its own uniqueness, efficient cause of the purely human world that gravitates, as Nietzsche would say, in the ideal region situated "Jenseits von Gut und Böse," beyond Good and Evil.

## Capítulo XVI. El mundo irreductible de la belleza. La estética y la lógica. La estética y la metafísica. La estética y la ética — Chapter XVI. The Irreducible World of Beauty. Aesthetics and Logic. Aesthetics and Metaphysics. Aesthetics and Ethics

[p. 217] Logic is the science of the concept, Aesthetics the science of intuition. To intuit is to know individualities; to proceed by concepts is to rise to genera. The generic is abstract; the individual, concrete; but knowledge results from the synthesis of intuition and logical thought.

The world enters through the channel of sensation and is schematized in the logical categories, becoming ever more distilled in the systems of ideas that the sciences form. This is one of the activities of the human mind. "Every idea tends toward action," as Leibniz said. Logic is the science of the great norms that ideas obey in tending toward action. The mass of consciousness constrains itself in its development, in order to prepare the future action of the organism in time and space. Effective ideas are true; but the world of the spirit is much wider, and can develop in other directions.

Through the ministry of consciousness, we are related to our spiritual life. Through the senses and consciousness, also, to the world external to [p. 218] us. If, instead of attending to the future and possible act, we fall into a state of contemplation (whether it concern a moment or episode of our inner life or of nature), if we persist in such a state, and become the pure subject of knowledge of which Schopenhauer speaks, our faculties play freely, as Kant observed; our ideas combine (without the logical subjection that schematizes and selects them) with our memories, images, and emotions; and all this psychic mass (useless for action, essentially unsuited as a preparatory act for the life of our organism, in its ordinary history of adaptation to a given environment) is projected, as we have explained, with inevitable and unconscious energy, upon the concrete individuals of the inner or outer world that provoke it, and constitutes empathic projection, the vivification of reality by human consciousness, poetic intuition, creative inspiration. This is the other activity of the mind. Its aesthetic activity.

The first law of the artist, of the painter, for example, is to paint; to paint merely for the sake of doing so, for the enjoyment of carrying out an activity that has no end other than itself. Disinterested intuition is the first indivisible moment of artistic creation. It signifies the forgetting and abandonment of all things, in order to attend only to that which forms the very object of intuition. Kant, Schopenhauer, and Bergson saw and studied only this [p. 219] first instant in the elaboration of the work of art; but the artist cannot remain inactive in the ecstasy of purposiveness without purpose; rather, his whole soul diffuses itself (*Einfühlung*), and projects itself upon what incites or amazes him, forming, in this way, a new creature, useless by its origin and essence for the logical and industrial ends of humanity, and which becomes one of the many exceptional inhabitants of that purely human world, the irreducible kingdom of beauty.

Art, of course, never quarrels with moral or philosophical purposes, but its proper essence neither needs nor presupposes them. To relive upon the canvas the singular nature of things, to perceive it in its characteristic individuality, without ever generalizing, without dogmatizing, without serving, confusedly or consciously, hypothetical ideologies, is to be a painter of genius. This alone suffices to endure. Then the soul pours with all its psychic wealth (thoughts, memories, images, sensations, sentiments) upon the object of its emotion, expresses itself fatally, externalizing itself in the work of art. For this reason, *verbi gratia*, the dwarfs and monsters of Velázquez enchant us as insuperably beautiful works, while we rightly disdain the irksome, grotesque academicism of the literary painting that Lessing censured. The fact is that Velázquez knew, by a most genuine intuition, not by deliberate intention, how to make us penetrate into existence [p. 220] itself, in its powerful individuality, in its marvelous subtlety.

On our work table we contemplate, every night, a small work of art, one of the lesser creatures that dwell in the region of beauty. It is a small bronze marabou, motionless, always motionless. It stands on a piece of black marble, carved expressly to hold the ash of the cigarettes that we are wont to consume while composing these pages on aesthetics. The voracious bird inclines, with sad sardonic humor, its absurd and respectable body over the ash that accumulates in the shining concavity of the black marble. How long are the bird's legs! How bald its brow, how enormous its beak! And how its silent wings cover the bronze marabou! Sad and enigmatic as a fakir, it remains standing, impassive, pensive, perched on its pedestal of black marble, full of ash!...

Nowhere else will there be a marabou like this one, which never stirs. Nowhere else will the earth be so black and the water so briny and sad as in the gleam of the black marble, stained with ash, which reflects our vigils while we meditate on aesthetic ideas... Art is always unique, always unreal, purely human, exclusively human; the proper expression of a consciousness!

Novalis has the profound saying: "All true ontology begins in Aesthetics." We believe [p. 221] it exact. Metaphysics, that is, the synthetic thought of reality, differs from pure scientific generality as much as art does. Philosophy starts from a first intuition (Platonic idea, Aristotelian potency and act, monads, Schopenhauerian will, Bergsonian *élan vital*); this intuition, by its nature, is absolutely indiscernible from poetic intuition. Note how there is always an anthropomorphic datum, purely human, in every ontological construction; an *Einfühlung* or empathic projection of our psyche upon the enigma that Kant called the thing in itself; but whereas in the arts the fundamental intuition is not dialecticized, in metaphysics it serves as the basis or principle of those various supreme approximations of the spirit to reality: Platonism, Aristotelianism, monadology, voluntarism, Bergsonism. Cathedrals of thoughts raised upon poetic intuitions!

The Platonic idea, Platonism entire, is a duplication of existence (as Aristotle already thought), which makes of the perfect, of the human ideal, the basis that the diverse individualities of the world imperfectly imitate. The potency and act of the Peripatetics are two psychological facts raised to the category of metaphysical principles. Monadology constitutes the apotheosis of anthropomorphism. From God to the dullest monad there is an immense evolutionary gradation and continuity, which are nothing but the empathic projection [p. 222] of Leibniz's spirit upon the data of experience and history. And what, pray, do the will and the *élan vital* (its near metaphysical relative) signify, other than anthropomorphic principles of universal explanation? Metaphysics and art are identified, therefore, in their principle, and diverge in their development and transcendence.

Two ancient and powerful rivals are Art and Morality<sup>1</sup>. Art is contemplation, and Morality action. The one proceeds by intuiting; the other, by acting. Humanity is contemplative and creative at one and the same time. Only an imperfect analysis devotes us to action without art or to art without action. But, can there be inactive artists? Can there be moralists without art?...

<sup>1</sup> See, in Rudolf Eucken's book "Las Grandes Corrientes del Pensamiento Contemporáneo", the history of the age-old conflict, masterfully traced.

Those who moved and stirred the masses in history, far from despising the aesthetic elements of action, availed themselves of them to prepare and dispose their future activity. A moral thought without the "fair covering" of which the Castilian classic speaks may perhaps insinuate itself into the hermetic soul of a gloomy ascetic; but it will never be transformed into a living and redeeming force. The good is hard to attain; virtue rugged, heroism bloody many times. Human art softens the hardness of the just man; it gentles the energy of the [p. 223] virtuous and calms the wounds of the saint; but it preserves for them their ideal value.

A fakir drowsing or dazed in his ecstasy moves no one, though all admire him; a reluctant, surly cenobite repels rather than invites one to follow him; a holy man who mortifies his flesh and disdains the company of people may be the mirror of his own haughtiness, but he will not make flow from his conduct that magical contact which sanctifies others, because it commits them, with gentleness and elegance, to sanctify and surpass themselves.

To leave a trail of light in history, through one's own sacrifice, it is necessary to unite to the beauty of renunciation the formula of the ideal: "The ocean and my doctrine," said the Oriental sage, "both of them grow gradually deeper and deeper. Both preserve their identity amid all their changes; both cast the corpses upon the shore. Just as the great rivers, when they pour into the sea, lose their names and merge with it, so the men of all castes, repudiating their origin, become brothers and count themselves as sons of Sakyamuni." The Inspired One spoke not only as an ascetic but as a poet; he not only acted, he also defined: "the ocean and my doctrine become, gradually, deeper." He defined for the good and the wicked, and said to the latter, in a phrase of imponderable beauty: "The sea and my teaching cast the corpses upon the shore."

A heroic act is always sacred; but it can [p. 224] be anonymous and barren. A heroic act defined and expressed artistically secures its own vitality in an unfailing germination. The first is a historical fact often not gathered up by history; the second is an unwithering example that becomes eternal and will nourish, from its own essence, the secular faith and hope. And there will always be poets to incorporate into the ideal destinies of humanity the redeeming actions. The most sublime poetry is the song of daring deeds. So Homer and Virgil sang ancient heroism; so Dante extolled Saint Francis in his resounding tercets, and the miracle of the Porziuncola flowered and flowers every day upon the mysterious paintings of the Franciscan artists. The wonder-worker of Assisi is the true inspirer of Tuscan civilization. He, with his discipline of poverty, founded Italian art, poetry, and language; all the glory of a race, the noblest of modern races, sprang from the subtle abnegation of a saint. Why go on speaking in Latin, if the solitary of Mount La Verna was renewing history with a life by whose strength the Scipios and the Gracchi might have learned the joy of martyrdom and the purity of intention?

Therefore artists must know that pure contemplation is sterile and unfruitful. If the world had already finished being made, it would be well to spend one's existence contemplating it. But the work of God is not yet finished. The best reason [p. 225] for acting is that history continues before us. Its perspectives extend indefinitely; its problems interlink, its epochs follow one another, its dogmas change, its truths shift; and the Good and the True are being built up from the contingent of small and great concrete goods, of small and concrete truths. The labor of men is, very probably, a task of selection. We must form two lots, within and outside ourselves, the lot of attributes that must die and the one that it is necessary to preserve, purify, and polish. Culture is the polishing of the intelligence, ever more audacious; art is the polishing of sentiment, ever more universal; the good is the polishing of the entire soul. To work upon one's own body and spirit with this human design is the aim of people of good will. And happiness?... We have not come into the world to be happy, but in order not to cease to be so by being strenuous. When we have formed the perfect equation of happiness and virtue, it will be time to set ourselves to contemplating the human work. The world will be the best work of art.

Our Lord Jesus Christ spoke the mystery of creation: "My Father worketh hitherto." That is to say, the creative effort has not yet ended. Existence is being created; it is being formed; it is being wrought. Let us help the act of God. Let us collaborate with Him. God is act, pure act, as the theologians say, following Aristotle. God never rests; He is always [p. 226] awake, always at work. If He dozed, as we do, the sun would go out and the course of the stars would change. His vigil is the arcanum of universal movement. May ours, too, be an arcanum of movement, an overflowing of activity.

But there is the activity that does nothing, the one that undoes and the one that creates. Let us be thus, always creators, always builders, never destroyers of the works that exalt; and let us be so without easy and clumsy optimism, without enervating or tragic pessimism; with nobility, with severe and profound cordiality; like the drop that at last hollows the stone, like the subterranean current that in the end sees the light, like the night that keeps the secret of the day. Let your will say: "Let us help the act of God!"

Let us recall, in concluding, the initial thought of this book, the superfluous power of Schiller, "the incomprehensibility of abundance," as Gracián says. If the human animal spent itself strictly in being an animal, all the energies it treasures would be employed in the realization of the immediate ends of life. In all strictness, life itself might have continued adapting itself to its physical environment, relatively uniform, in the performance of the two genuine functions or activities that distinguish it: nutrition and reproduction. The "law of hunger and of love," as Schiller said. Even the humblest slug nourishes itself and loves; it grows, in nourishing itself, and reproduces in new slugs that will nourish themselves, in their turn, and grow. But vital energy [p. 227] has been, for many thousands of years, trying out new forms, creating new types, ripening lives more complex and extraordinary.

Man is the most complex and extraordinary life of all. Like the slug, he nourishes himself, and reproduces like it; he gives forth, constantly, from himself, new homogeneous beings, who defend themselves from death with their senses, whom pleasure revives and pain warns and instructs. How much more, nevertheless, there is in man! There is in him the energy devoted to the pressing ends of animal action, but there also exists the force that generates unsuspected and magnificent acts.

This excess of vigor, this superabundance, this capital, this hoard, this reserve, which in the saint and the hero, in the inventor or the artist become "incomprehensible," through the fecundity and luxuriance with which they show themselves in their acts, makes of our stock the possible seat of a moral and aesthetic development capable of disdaining what it has created up to now, in view of the marvels that it foresees engendering for tomorrow. Man is a formidable synthesizer of the energies of life and the indefinite possibilities of existence.
