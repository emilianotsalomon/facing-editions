## Die Phänomenologie des Spiritismus — The Phenomenology of Spiritism

Spiritists and their opponents look at one another with that astonishment which the incomprehensible extorts from us. "How can one deny such palpable facts?" say the former. "How can one believe such hair-raising things?" think the latter. This sharp opposition has arisen through fault on both sides. The adherents have not always known how to give their experiments an unobjectionable form; each individual, moreover, commands only a small fraction of the experiences scattered through hundreds of books and journals, and an overview of the whole field of experience could be gained only with difficulty. What was lacking was a phenomenology of Spiritism in which the factual material would have been assembled and systematically grouped. This, precisely, is the reason why the opponents still believe today that Spiritism can be beaten off the field with a few threadbare phrases of enlightenment. They have no idea, either of the number, or of the weight, of the facts. One might with some semblance of right still deny meteorites, but not pebbles. When spiritistic facts trickle down from the sky, one can put up the skeptical umbrella and think oneself safe; but against a downpour it is no help. The opponents do not know that this downpour has long since arrived.

A phenomenology of Spiritism, whose absence has hitherto been so keenly felt, is now before us. It is the book of the Imperial Russian Councillor of State Alexander Aksakow: "Animism and Spiritism."<sup>∗</sup> The genesis of this book is not without interest. A few years ago Eduard von Hartmann wrote a small tract against Spiritism.<sup>∗∗</sup> He cast, as it were, a careless glance upward, noticed a few drops of rain, and put up the aforementioned umbrella. Upon this Aksakow now lets a downpour crash down as if from opened floodgates, against which there is no longer any protection. Hartmann's tract comprises only 118 pages; Aksakow's answer lies before us in two volumes totaling more than 800 pages. This contrast, remarkable enough in itself, is heightened still further when we read in Hartmann: "Since I myself have never attended a séance, I am also not in a position to form a judgment on the reality of the phenomena in question, etc." (16, 23); whereas in Aksakow we read: "Since I became interested in the spiritistic movement from the year 1855 onward, I have not ceased to study it in all its details — and indeed in all parts of the world and in all literatures. Before that I had accepted the facts on the testimony of others; only in 1870 did I attend my first séance, in an intimate circle formed by myself" (Preface 25). Given the circumstance that on Hartmann's side all experience is lacking, while his opponent commands thirty years of study and twenty years of experimental experience, many an eager Spiritist might be tempted to call out to the former: Si tacuisses, philosophus mansisses! [Had you remained silent, you would have remained a philosopher!]

<sup>∗∗</sup> E. von Hartmann: "Der Spiritismus" (Friedrich, Leipzig 1885).

Indeed it has made a painful impression on many readers to see a philosopher quite frankly admit his complete lack of experience in this field, and yet nonetheless decree how the phenomena, if they were facts, would have to be explained. On an alpine hut, the Berliner amuses everyone when, asked by the dairymaid whether there are such high mountains in his homeland too, he says no, but adds: but if we had any, they would be higher. By contrast, investigations conducted at a philosopher's desk do not appear serious when they can be summed up in the words: "I do not know whether, but if indeed, then —." The matter, however, does not lie so simply. If the gauntlet thrown down had been of mere cotton, Aksakow would certainly not have picked it up. The fact that he answered proves that he found valuable elements in Hartmann's tract. Hartmann does not fall into the error of blunt denial; rather he says: "I hold the testimonies of history and of contemporaries so far available, taken in their context, to be a sufficient certification of the assumption that there exist in the human organism still more forces and dispositions than exact science has hitherto explored and fathomed, and a sufficiently urgent summons to science to enter into the exact investigation of this domain of phenomena. On the other hand, I do indeed consider myself competent to deliver a conditionally valid judgment on the conclusions to be drawn from these phenomena in case of their reality, for this is properly the philosopher's task" (23). Hartmann, however, also reminds the Spiritists of the logical principles to which every method of investigation is bound, and in this respect his tract is called by Aksakow himself a "school for Spiritism."

The conclusions, now, which Hartmann draws from the spiritistic phenomena may be summed up as follows: that these phenomena need not be ascribed to spirits, but can be explained from the mediums themselves, who are abnormal but pathological natures. Here, however, it at once becomes apparent that philosophical capacity alone accomplishes nothing whatever, and that experience is quite indispensable; for Hartmann defines the medium in a manner that can indeed be contrived at the desk, but which does not occur in experience. For, in his opinion, mediums are simultaneously auto-somnambulists and, with respect to the circle-sitters, magnetizers. Under psychic stimulation they produce a nerve-force which, converting itself into vibrations of light and heat, becomes physical force and can even, acting at a distance, produce unusual phenomena; it can counteract the gravitation of objects, produce writing at a distance without contact of the pencil, and through such a system of pull- and pressure-lines this nerve-force, which is able to penetrate matter, can even produce the imprints of the medium's organic forms — for instance hands or feet — on sooted surfaces or in other material. With the help of this nerve-force the medium acts upon the spectators like a powerful magnetizer, puts them into a masked somnambulism, and implants in them its own imaginings as hallucinations, so that they believe they see and touch these appearances, which are not real.

The somnambulistic consciousness of the medium has a hyperaesthetic memory, can read thoughts, and can then project into sealed slates the answer read from the spectator's consciousness to the question posed by that same spectator; it is furthermore clairvoyant without the mediation of the eyes — an exclusion of normal sensory perception which, to be sure, still proves no exclusion of an abnormal perception. But where, as for instance in temporal clairvoyance, a real leaping-over of time and space must be acknowledged, there Hartmann, letting every natural explanation drop, resorts to a metaphysical hypothesis, to the essential root of all individuality in the absolute Spirit. With this hypothesis one is reminded of the inseparable umbilical cord which connects every creature with its all-mother Nature, and one thinks that in this umbilical cord too spiritual saps must circulate, which ordinarily only do not become an object of consciousness. "If all individuals of higher and lower order are rooted in the Absolute, then they also have, through it, a second, backward connection with one another, and it needs only that, through an intense interest of the will, a 'rapport' or telephone-connection be established between two individuals in the Absolute, in order that the unconscious spiritual exchange between them may take place even without sensory mediation" (78–79). This hyperbolic explanation of Hartmann's surpasses everything ever claimed by Spiritists. The latter, moreover, have the advantage of a unified explanation of the factual material, whereas Hartmann tears the same asunder into two heterogeneous halves, one of which he ascribes to the nerve-force of the medium, the other to the absolute Spirit. But even in his definition of the medium Hartmann throws truth and falsehood together pell-mell, and his definition of the state of the spectators will certainly be accepted by no Spiritist of even the most moderate experience.

Hartmann arbitrarily equips the medium with all the faculties he needs to explain the phenomena, and then, of course, he spins all these phenomena with the greatest ease out of this arbitrarily conceived figure. Whatever remainder is nevertheless left over, however, is shifted onto the absolute Spirit; even within the same category of phenomena Hartmann distributes the explanation between a double source, in that, for example, he attributes thought-transference at close range to the communication of etheric vibrations, but at great distance again resorts to the metaphysical telephone-connection (81). Hartmann likewise explains transfigurations and materializations with the greatest ease. What the Spiritists call the controlling spirits of the mediums are for him merely fixed types into which the medium's imagination has entered, and into which it submerges its own personality, so that this personality plays its role with the greatest histrionic virtuosity. Should obstacles stand in the way of the satisfaction of this urge — if, for instance, the medium has been bound in knots and fetters as a safeguard against fraud — then it sheds these and walks about transfigured. But should an actual materialization occur, Hartmann is not embarrassed by this either; for now it is a matter of a hallucination in the somnambulistic consciousness of the medium, which it transmits, by virtue of its magnetic force, onto the somnambulistic consciousness of the spectators. "If the medium, for example, has the hallucination of being no longer itself but rather, say, the spirit John King or Katie King, and of appearing and acting as such, then the hallucination will also be transmitted to the recipient, that the medium stepping forth from the curtain is no longer the medium, but John King or Katie King.

If in another case the medium has the illusion that from its epigastrium a mist develops, and out of the mist a spirit-figure, then the fascinated spectator will have the same hallucination" (95). In vain do the Spiritists object that spirit-figures have been photographed, that photographic plates cannot possibly suffer from hallucinations, that Crookes and others even portrayed medium and phantom on the same plate, whereby both the fraud theory and the hallucination theory are at once eliminated. For arguments so weak Hartmann has only a superior smile: "In the photograph taken by Crookes, on which the medium is visible simultaneously with the phantom, there exists the urgent suspicion that, instead of the alleged phantom, it was the medium, and instead of the supposed medium, the medium's clothing stuffed with a cushion, that was photographed in a half-concealed position" (97). In short, all transfigurations are the transmission of an illusion, all materializations the transmission of a hallucination from the medium to the spectators. Hartmann's attempt to explain the phenomena from the medium must be pronounced a complete failure. One may grant him that in his tract he correctly names the conditions under which experiments may be held unobjectionable, and that he offers the Spiritists correct methodological principles by which they should proceed; but Councillor of State Aksakow has shown that Hartmann himself violates these his own principles, and that the Spiritists have long since fulfilled the conditions he recommends. He overwhelms his opponent with facts proving that everything he demands has already occurred, and so the book "Animismus und Spiritismus," originally undertaken as a mere reply, in the course of its composition grew far beyond such an intent.

It has become a handbook that offers the most worth knowing, gathered from the swollen mass of spiritistic literature. Whoever, then, does not wish to, or cannot, take the trouble of reading through this literature, is at least obliged — if he wishes to be heard at all — to read through this handbook, which offers an actual phenomenology of Spiritism. In the history of Spiritism this book has the significance of an event, and it personally relieves me of a great embarrassment; for I can now give the advice frequently requested of me concerning Spiritism in a manner that does not demand too much of the time and trouble of those who ask — a proof of how much Aksakow's book meets an existing need. Even one very occupied by professional business still has time to read through a couple of volumes in order to form a judgment on this most important question of our century, and unless he should be resolved in advance not to concede Spiritism at any price, he will lay the book down convinced that it is a truth, even should he himself lack all experience in this field. There are people enough who declare that only witnessing it with their own eyes could perhaps make them Spiritists — as though they alone possessed a critical pair of eyes! — these people, if they read through Aksakow's book, will have the experience that one can gain a conviction through reading alone. Our enlightened opponents, who see in every Spiritist a person sunk in wild superstition, devoid of criticism, mostly commit the error of regarding our conviction of the truth of Spiritism merely as a result — as though we had adopted Spiritism wholesale through a simple act of will on the basis of mere needs of the heart.

It is, however, self-evident for anyone who has gone through the educational course of our century that he must, from the outset, have set the greatest prejudice against Spiritism, which contradicts the prevailing views so greatly, so that our conviction is only the result of a long inner process of development, and it was only more or less involuntarily that capitulation before the irresistible force of the facts occurred. In short, to most Spiritists their conviction has been forced upon them, after every critical objection they made to themselves had been overcome by the facts of experience. That is precisely why it makes a ridiculous impression on such Spiritists when an opponent presents the first objection that occurs to him as one overlooked by the believers — objections to which, of course, we too brought our sharpness of mind, but which we had to let drop one after another in the course of our experience. Now such an enlightened opponent comes along and wants to screw us back to our own former standpoint, which we already have behind us, because we have since learned better, but on which he himself has only just arrived. The mere circumstance that such objections are by no means of unheard-of novelty, that they are rather advanced in an entirely identical way in every newspaper, should convince him that they are not the product of his individual acumen, but merely commonplaces that occur at the very first degree of reflection. Aksakow too was by no means among those whose conviction had clarified itself without a lengthy inner struggle; on the contrary, it took long years before he was done with himself. He himself describes this process to us: "The materials which I had gathered through reading and practical experience were inexhaustible; but the solution did not come.

On the contrary, with the years all the weak sides of Spiritism became manifest and grew even greater: the tastelessness of the communications, the poverty of their intellectual content, even where they are not commonplaces, the manifestly mystifying and mendacious character of the greater part of the manifestations, the unreliability of the physical phenomena as soon as it was a matter of subjecting them to positive experiment; the credulity, the blindness, the chauvinism of the Spiritists and Spiritualists; finally the fraud, which broke out together with the dark séances and the materializations, and which I have had to find confirmed not only from the literature, but also through my personal experience in my dealings with the most renowned professional mediums — in sum, a mass of doubts, objections, and confusions of every kind only increased the difficulties of the problem" (Preface 26). Every researcher has had similar experiences and gone through the resulting inner struggles. If one nevertheless does not throw down the gun in disgust, this happens precisely because among the unpleasant experiences there are always mingled others that call for the continuation of the work. Instead of discontinuing the investigation on account of this unpleasant accompaniment, one then tells oneself that this accompaniment belongs to the object of investigation itself, that it often even becomes highly instructive, just as, in all scientific investigations, it is precisely what ought not to be that becomes the very lever for solving the problem. Hartmann's discovery, that all spiritistic phenomena are to be explained from the medium itself, belongs by no means to him individually; it is not the result of an allegedly more sober judgment of the opponents, but rather the property of the Spiritists themselves, though from an earlier period of their development. With this discovery, then, the opponents arrive decades too late. Schindler, for

instance, whose "Magisches Geistesleben" appeared in 1857, stands entirely on this standpoint of explaining all phenomena from the medium. Perty held the same view still in the second edition of his "Mystische Erscheinungen," and only personal experiences brought him to abandon a theory back to which the opponents would now like to screw us again. Likewise the jurist Cox later gave up this standpoint, so that Hartmann wrongly claims him for his side. Such conversions, however, did not come about in such a way that researchers of this kind abandoned mediumism entirely and passed over to actual Spiritism; rather, they recognized that both categories of phenomena hold good, that a large part of the phenomena can in fact be explained from the medium, but another part defies this explanation, so that here a cause outside the medium must be assumed. Should this critical separation of the phenomena be lost again, should the two sources of the phenomena be mixed together again, as Hartmann attempts, then a scientific confusion already overcome would thereby be reintroduced. Care has been taken, however, that this tree of allegedly scientific skepticism shall not grow into the sky, and it is precisely Aksakow's book that has contributed significantly to drawing more sharply the boundary line separating the mediumistic phenomena from the properly spiritistic ones. I am, moreover, entirely in agreement that he did not title his book "Mediumismus und Spiritismus." This juxtaposition could even cause confusion, since the medium is necessary for both categories of phenomena. It would therefore be very desirable if, following Aksakow's precedent, the juxtaposition of "Animism and Spiritism" were retained.

Animism comprises those phenomena of which the medium is the cause; Spiritism, those others of which the medium is the mere condition, but whose cause lies in invisible, or only exceptionally visible, intelligent beings. So "Animism and Spiritism" — this is the watchword of critical sobriety; to blur this contrast again, which succeeds only through a confusion of cause and condition, causa and conditio, is uncritical and by no means rests on greater sobriety. The word Animism also offers the advantage that it eliminates that explanatory hypothesis — it is the most superficial of all — which supposes that all phenomena can be explained from the normal faculties of the medium, whereby the whole of Spiritism would be slaughtered into the physiological psychology of the materialists. Among these normal faculties the opponents notoriously also count fraud, and they make no scruple of attributing it not only to all professional mediums but also to the far greater number of private mediums. Aksakow's Animism understands the soul — anima — not in the sense of the materialists, namely as a mere function of the organism, but as an independent substance, distinct from the body, acting beyond the body's periphery, which is not the product but the producer of the body, and to which, precisely for that reason, pre-existence and post-existence must be ascribed. This soul does not coincide with consciousness, but lies outside our consciousness; it is the original element of our individuality, but not merely a psychic element, rather a force-center which both thinks and organizes. So long as this was not observed, the phenomenon of materialization has mistakenly always been held to be purely spiritistic, which it is not.

An organizing soul is capable of forming visible or invisible likenesses of our organs, and so the partial or even complete doppelgänger can under certain circumstances be an animistic phenomenon, one easily confused with a spiritistic materialization. Nevertheless one does not thereby escape actual materialization; for an organizing soul must outlast death, and in doing so naturally retains its organizing capacity, of which it makes use in materializations that are then no longer animistic but spiritistic — though they betray themselves as such only through the further mark that the intellectual content of the manifestations reveals a non-sensory consciousness. Even in spiritistic materializations a bodily resemblance between phantom and medium is often shown, precisely because the medium is bodily involved to a varying degree; but even then the totality of the marks provides proof that the materialization is not merely animistic. One can explain the son from the father, but even the greatest resemblance between the two does not entitle us to deny the son his independent individuality. On the side of the Spiritists, it is undeniable that much has been sinned, in that they frequently took animistic phenomena for spiritistic ones, and set, as it were, too large an explanatory hat upon many problems; Hartmann, however, in seeking to dissolve Spiritism into Animism, falls into the opposite error — his explanatory hat sits on many problems like a child's bonnet on the head of Bismarck. Incidentally, the Spiritists soon recovered from their initial excesses, and, as Aksakow shows in his historical survey of the anti-spiritistic theories, all the explanatory principles set up by Hartmann had already been advanced at the very beginning of the movement: nerve-force, thought-transference, somnambulism, and hallucination.

The pendulum of explanation, which had pointed to Animism, swung back again, however, as the phenomena of materialization became more numerous and more pronounced. With this the hallucination hypothesis fell, and even the animistic one, i.e. the designation of materialization as mere doppelgänger-play. The hallucination theory was eliminated by photographic proof, for even the most scientific skeptic cannot extend the reproach of hallucinating as far as the photographic plates. Phantoms have been photographed in darkrooms that were invisible to those present — a possibility explained by the fact that photographic plates are more sensitive than the retina, reflecting the fluorescent or ultraviolet rays of the spectrum which are invisible even to the sharpest eye. Furthermore, spirit-figures have been photographed which, though not seen by the experimenters, were seen and described by the medium, and which, in agreement with this description, were developed on the plate. These proofs, however, cannot be regarded as absolute; for it has been experimentally established that such photographs can show the partial or complete doppelgänger of the medium (105–106). One comes closer to absolute proof through the photography of visible forms seen by all present: hands, heads, busts, and full figures. This proof was further supplemented by others still. Aksakow gives a list of the proofs furnished for the reality of materialized hands: 1. Through the simultaneous seeing of several persons whose testimony agrees. 2. Through the simultaneous seeing and feeling of several persons whose testimony agrees, where the sensory impression of these two senses was in accord. 3. Through physical effects produced by such hands, as for instance the movement of objects before the eyes of witnesses. 4.

Through the production of durable, physical effects, namely: a) through writing produced in the presence of several persons; b) through impressions produced by the same hand on soft or blackened substances; c) through certain effects produced on the same hand by those present; d) through molds produced by the same hand from a given material; e) through the photography of such hands.

5. Through the weighing of materialized forms, when they attained the full human figure. If the materialization is limited to hands, the rest of the organism can, at most, be optically absent, and this is shown by the fact that the functions of such hands are intelligent. To pick out from the abundance of material only a single testimony, Crookes says: "There came a luminous hand down from the upper part of the room, and after hovering for a few seconds near me, it took the pencil from my hand and quickly wrote on a sheet of paper, threw down the pencil, and then rose up above our heads, gradually vanishing into darkness."<sup>∗</sup> Among the most compelling proofs are surely the plaster casts of materialized hands. They are obtained in the following manner: one fills a vessel with cold water, a second vessel with warm water, on whose surface a layer of melted paraffin floats. One then requests that the materialized hand dip for a moment into the liquid paraffin and then into the cold water. If this is repeated several times, a paraffin glove of a certain thickness forms around the hand. Just as a human hand cannot slip out of a buttoned leather glove closely fitted around the wrist, so too a materialized hand could not slip out of the paraffin glove unless it had the capacity to dematerialize itself within it. The mold left behind can then be filled with plaster and afterward melted away in boiling water. The plaster form then shows, down to the smallest details, the exact shape of the hand; but to the sculptor such a plaster form, because it shows no seams, is utterly inexplicable.

<sup>∗</sup> Psychische Studien. 1874. p. 159.

The weight of this proof lies especially in the difference that can be established between the medium's hand and the hand made of plaster. It was Professor of Geology Denton who in 1875 first hit upon this proof, and he pushed the conditions so far that finally the mold was obtained in a chest locked with a key (171). These experiments, which Hartmann leaves unrefuted, have been varied in many ways, and, as far as conditions are concerned, such molds have been produced in four different manners: 1. The medium is shut off; the acting figure remains invisible. 2. The medium is before the eyes of the spectators; the acting figure remains invisible. 3. The acting figure stands before the eyes of the spectators, the medium is shut off. 4. The figure and the medium are simultaneously before the eyes of the spectators. Finally, it should be mentioned that cases are also reported in which the materialized phantoms offered the spectators their hands, still covered with the molds, so that the glove could be drawn off; and further, that such plaster casts were also obtained by whose peculiarities the person to whom this hand had belonged in life was recognized. Thus at a séance with the medium Eglinton a mold was obtained of the hand of a small child, showing a slight deformity, by which a lady present recognized the hand of her little girl, who at the age of five had drowned in South Africa (203). These molds form the transition to the materializations of whole figures, for which photographic proof is now mainly relevant. According to the conditions under which this proof was obtained, five classes are to be distinguished: 1. The medium is visible; the figure is invisible and is photographed. 2. The medium is invisible; the figure is visible and is photographed. 3.

Medium and figure are visible; the figure alone is photographed. 4. Medium and figure are visible and both are photographed at the same time. 5. Medium and figure are invisible; the latter is photographed in the darkness. As to the rich abundance of photographic proofs I must refer to Aksakow's book. If one further considers that the phantoms were measured, weighed, tested for circulation of the blood and respiration, that they behave like human beings, that the phantom in Crookes's case conversed with those present for two hours and told of its past life, the reader will admit that the hallucination hypothesis, put forward by people who have never experimented or even been spectators, contains a rather strong demand. I, at least, shall refuse for all eternity to swallow such camels. Crookes, incidentally, is not the only one who pushed the proofs so far. Dr. med. Nitchman too, who produced such double photographs, says: "There was generally no failure in these operations; sensitized and fixing baths were used, and for ease of handling the cleaned plates were prepared in advance. I have often followed every spirit-figure right into the cabinet, and have seen it and the medium together. Indeed I have, as it seems to me, the most nearly scientific certainty possible that each spirit-figure and the mortal figure (of the medium) were separate individuals from one another, since I examined them carefully with a number of instruments as to their breathing, their circulation, their size, their weight, their girth, etc. In every spiritual or bodily respect these spirit-figures were majestically noble and utterly enchanting, although they seemed gradually to rise out of the mist and, on the other hand, to vanish instantaneously and absolutely.

I hold that there must be spiritual existences of some kind somewhere, and that the intelligent beings who were present on these occasions were visible, objective spiritual bodies in some form other than the ordinary material form of our earthly life, yet conscious and thinking like ourselves, capable of speech, of locomotion, etc., as if they still walked in the flesh. Having again and again (in full view of competent judges) walked along with the medium on the one side and the 'materialized spirit' on the other, having shaken hands with the latter on his arrival and departure, and having conversed with him for almost an hour — I no longer have any sympathy with such problematic notions as optical and auditory illusions, unconscious cerebration, psychic force, nerve-aura, and so forth.... Reason, logic, inferences, etc., without practical investigation are only a waste of time and strength" (283–285). Hartmann, who believes he ought to be the one to draw the Spiritists' attention to methodological principles of investigation, ought to have considered that he himself commits a methodological error when he does not let himself be guided by respect for the facts, and when doubt becomes his chief purpose. Skepticism is not among those things that become better the further one drives them; only that doubt is critically sober and scientifically justified which knows exactly at what point to stop. The hallucination theory has its insurmountable limit and loses all meaning as soon as, for the proof of reality of spiritistic facts, instruments, photographic plates, scales with recording apparatus, and so on, are employed. If we

see, for example, that at spiritistic séances the mediums lose the amount of weight that the phantoms gain (299), then against such a fact even the subtlest doubts can accomplish nothing. Even if Hartmann himself did not confess that he has never attended a séance, one would have to recognize it from his attempts at explanation. A medium that is at once an entirely passive being and an active magnetizer; that implants hallucinations in itself and transmits them to the spectators, so that these believe they see phantoms; in short, a medium that sleeps and dreams, and spectators who dream along yet do not sleep; material, permanent changes brought about by mere hallucinatory figures; finally, physical instruments all likewise bewitched and behaving contrary to the laws of physics: such a description may be very cleverly contrived, but no Spiritist has ever attended a séance of this kind. With such theories one violates the very first methodological principle, that every explanatory hypothesis must be fitted to the object it explains. It is immediately clear that this principle is the presupposition of every scientific investigation. An explanation must be stretched until it reaches and encompasses the fact to be explained. Hartmann does the opposite of this. The inconvenient facts, which for him form a wholly insoluble knot, he passes over and rejects; the remaining ones he tinkers with and alters until they submit to his explanation. Instead, then, of fitting the explanation to the facts, he fits the facts to the explanation. In this procedure, however, the brain becomes a Procrustean bed for experience. No one will dispute his methodological principle that one should "seek to manage with natural causes as long as possible, and resort to supernatural ones only under urgent necessity."

But when we see that Hartmann disdains the spiritistic explanation because he believes he has a natural cause lying in the medium, yet nevertheless, in explaining clairvoyance, reaches far afield and grasps at the absolute Spirit, this procedure recalls the word of an honest Tyrolean: If I can have really fresh water, I let the wine stand and drink schnapps instead. Aksakow devotes a lengthy consideration to those intellectual manifestations that go beyond the intellectual level of the medium. When one reads that the novel "Edwin Drood," left unfinished by Charles Dickens, was completed by an uneducated psychographic medium in a manner that, in the view of competent judges, Dickens himself could not have done better, this does not yet prove the latter's authorship, but in any case one can no longer say with Hartmann that the spiritual manifestations never go beyond the capacities of the medium or the spectators. This limit is, rather, very frequently exceeded. A very involuntary, and for that very reason very comical, piece of evidence for this was furnished by the well-known materialist Dr. Ludwig Büchner. In 1860, namely, there appeared in Erlangen a book by Hudson Tuttle, translated from the English by Dr. Aschenbrenner: "History and Laws of the Process of Creation." Büchner, and also other of his materialist colleagues, bestowed great applause on this book and printed excerpts from it; indeed Büchner, when he traveled to America, wished to pay his respects to the author and sought him out in Cleveland. But Hudson Tuttle modestly declined all praise. He is a simple farmer who, without having received any special education, at the age of 18 began writing scientific works as a — psychographic medium.

He describes his conversation with Büchner: "I asked him how it came that he cited my writings, of declared spiritual origin, in order thereby to prove materialism? He explained that he had not known that this was their origin; he had supposed that I was a man who devoted himself entirely to science in his leisure. When he was told that the passages he cited were written, after days of physically strenuous labor, by powers higher than my own, he remarked, very politely, that I evidently possessed a great cranial formation, and that I had in any case heard or read this science somewhere."<sup>∗</sup> So when the opponents assert that writing mediums for the most part produce nothing but nonsense, the Spiritists can now, in proof of the contrary, invoke — Prof. Dr. Ludwig Büchner! The intellectual manifestations thus often show themselves independent of the medium's level of education, and when Hartmann asserts: "Only a medium who has learned to write will involuntarily, or at a distance, produce writings," this is not even correct. Mrs. Jenken's child (Kate Fox) began writing at 5½ months (410); a two-month-old infant gave psychographic answers to questions (405), and a girl belonging to Baron Seymour Kirkup psychographed at the age of nine days (417). Speaking mediums, too, occur already in childhood, and not only nowadays, but as early as the beginning of the past century, as may be read in the remarkable book by Misson.<sup>∗∗</sup> Finally, the inspirational character of speaking mediums is proved by the case related by Judge Edmonds: His daughter, a young girl who spoke only English and a little French, spoke in nine or ten different languages, sometimes for an hour at a stretch and with the greatest fluency (423). Even communications in telegraph code and the deaf-mute alphabet occur (445).

<sup>∗∗</sup> Misson: "Théâtre sacré des Cévennes."

If the manifestations contain a clairvoyance across time and space, one cannot yet, with certainty, infer spiritistic inspiration from this, since clairvoyance is among the faculties of somnambulists, and often takes the form of inspiration through dramatic splitting. This explanation, however, is closed to Hartmann, because he does not acknowledge the transcendental subject, and so he must here make the enormous leap to the omniscience of the absolute Subject. But if we apply this yardstick to the individual cases, it becomes evident how much more natural the spiritistic hypothesis is than Hartmann's metaphysical one. Dr. med. Wolfe reports, for example, concerning the medium Mansfield: "I have seen Mr. Mansfield write two communications at the same instant, one with the right hand, the other with the left, and both in a language of which he had no knowledge. While he was occupied with this, he conversed with me about business matters, or continued the conversation begun before this double writing.... In one case I clearly remember that Mr. Mansfield, while writing with two hands in two languages, said to me: Wolfe, did you know a man in Columbia named Jacobs? — I answered yes, and he continued: He is here and wishes you to know that he departed from his body this morning! — This announcement proved to be true." (460.) A similar report is that of Major-General Drayson. In a séance the medium informed him of the presence of a spirit who had died recently in the East, though not in India; his head had been cut off and his body thrown into a canal. Drayson had heard nothing from this friend for three years, and he then learned that the man had set out from India for China; only some years later was the detail of the manner of death also confirmed (504).

Nor is there any lack of manifestations of spirits who are completely unknown both to the medium and to the other persons present, whose statements, however, are confirmed by inquiries. Aksakow took far greater trouble than Hartmann to apply the animistic explanation to the phenomena and to examine the scope of this explanation. There do indeed exist physical and intellectual phenomena which involve an action at a distance of the organism through a psychic principle; but there also exist other phenomena whose peculiarities compel us to posit the same psychic principle underlying them, but freed from the body — that is, to resort to the spiritistic explanation. Every unbiased researcher will have to undertake this sifting of the material. But when we see that the animistic and the spiritistic manifestations are, in their essence, identical, we cannot escape the conclusion that we, living human beings, are in our innermost being identical with those beings who, after their death, are brought to visible appearance — that we, then, in our animistic functions, exceptionally make use of powers that in death step wholly out of latency and become normal. The animistic manifestations occur not exclusively in mediums, but also in somnambulists. All cases of doppelgänger phenomena also belong here, and here too the proof of reality has been furnished photographically and through molds. These animistic phenomena occur, evidently, not through the mediation of the organism but despite it, and from this it follows that what happens without the use of corporeality can also happen without the possession of corporeality — indeed all the more easily.

When we see that a materialization and a doppelgänger resemble each other in all essential features, and that in both cases the resemblance to a being known to us is present, then, by the very same logic by which we relate the doppelgänger to a living human being, we must relate the materialization to a departed one. Bodily resemblance, however, is not the only mark from which the identity of a phantom with a particular deceased person can be inferred. The proof of identity is strengthened when the manifestation occurs in a language foreign to the medium but known to the deceased, or when a deceased deaf-mute takes such possession of a medium that the latter uses the deaf-mute alphabet (660); when the characteristic style or particular linguistic peculiarities of a deceased person flow into the manifestations; when the handwriting, in a language incomprehensible to the medium, resembles that of a deceased person (669); or when the deceased gives numerous accurate details about his life, though he is known neither to the medium nor to the spectators. The proof of identity is strengthened still further when the medium describes a figure visible only to itself, which is then photographed in agreement with the description, and which belongs to a deceased person whom none of those present knew. The proof of identity becomes all the more compelling the more of the aforementioned marks are combined in a single case. Aksakow offers us a whole collection of the most remarkable reports, from whose rich abundance I will mention only that of Livermore, who held 388 séances with Kate Fox, and who, from the 43rd onward, saw the figure of his deceased wife Estella and received various manifestations of the kind mentioned (748–751).

We may, however, infer neither from the physical nor from the intellectual manifestations a corresponding state of the deceased; for it is a fact well known to Spiritists that the deceased, for the duration of their manifestations, must clothe themselves again in the last states of their earthly life. Lichtenberg once said that he would gladly crawl on his knees from Göttingen to Hamburg if he could be sure of finding there a book that would offer him new and essential metaphysical enlightenment. The scholars of today think otherwise. They, who might under certain circumstances need only go around the next street corner to attend a spiritistic séance and find more metaphysical enlightenment there than is contained in thick compendiums, do not find it worth the trouble even to go — indeed, as I myself have experienced — they refuse when they are invited. Nor will they take even the admittedly time-consuming trouble of orienting themselves at least in the literature. Now Aksakow has made it as convenient as possible for them: he offers them the most worth knowing from this extensive literature, assembled in two modest volumes, an extract from hundreds of books and journals, arranged according to scientific points of view. Will our scholars now at least take advantage of this opportunity? I doubt it. As before, they will deliberately evade every opportunity for instruction, so as not to fall under suspicion of needing instruction at all. As before, they will continue to write treatises of great philological erudition on the pros and cons of immortality, repeating the arguments and counter-arguments long since recognized as inadmissible.

For this, however, they will also not escape the reproach of standing, in their own time, as anachronisms; for today the proof of immortality can be furnished empirically — given the favorable opportunity — within five minutes, for anyone whom prejudice does not blind and who is capable of drawing logical conclusions from an empirical fact. Indeed, such scholars will not escape the still far stricter reproach that they are not concerned with truth. As for the other opponents of the modern movement, who devote scarcely a few minutes of reflection to this difficult subject, but then claim to let the torch of enlightenment shine forth in a "Gartenlaube," in the "Neueste Nachrichten," or a "Neue Freie Presse" and similar organs of the press — of such opponents I would rather say nothing; for the opinion of such people does not matter at all. In what precedes I have given a relatively very brief report on Aksakow's remarkable book, but, in order not to give occasion for the opinion that I blindly accept books by fellow believers, I will not, in conclusion, conceal that I have a few objections to make. I miss, for instance, a chapter on the achievements of "psychometric" mediums; I miss a name and subject index, which would be of great use precisely for books of this kind. Furthermore, though it must be acknowledged that Aksakow has thoroughly refuted his opponent Hartmann, the reliance on Hartmann running through the whole book gives the impression of scaffolding left standing, which one would gladly see cleared away, and without which the book would only gain. Finally, I would have preferred to see Aksakow draw the philosophical conclusions that follow from the spiritistic facts more in general terms, rather than emphasizing their significance against the Hartmannian system.

Hartmann has somewhere said that, granting the truth of Spiritism, he would merely be obliged to insert an intervening chapter into his metaphysical system, and, it seems, Aksakow shares this opinion. To me, by contrast, the scope of the spiritistic facts seems to reach much further; the intervening chapter which Hartmann can no longer refuse to insert will burst the ring of his system. Already Hellenbach has furnished proof that, if we insert Spiritism into the world-formula — which today no longer depends on our discretion — then, first of all, the pessimism which in Hartmann is an absolute one will flow into a transcendental optimism. With this, the point is broken off the judgment that Hartmann passes on the world, and the doctrine of the denial of the will, tied to it, loses its ground. The whole phenomenology of the Unconscious must be reworked; for Hartmann knows only two sources of it: the physiologically Unconscious of the individual, and the metaphysically Unconscious of the absolute Spirit; but with the transcendental subject a third source now opens up, and it is precisely Aksakow who lets this same source gush forth as thick as an arm. The morality that Hartmann has vainly tried to ground now receives, in metaphysical individualism, a foundation; the categorical imperative becomes the voice of the transcendental subject. With this the whole doctrine of religion is likewise reshaped. Even aesthetics cannot escape this fate, since the Unconscious in aesthetic production must now be derived from the source of the transcendental subject.

Finally, through a new grounding of individualism, not only Hartmann's philosophy but philosophy in general is, as it were, lifted down from its isolated pedestal; it will then be far less a philosophy about the world than a philosophy about man and his destiny, and the eminently practical consequences thereof will intervene in our social conditions in a far different, transforming way than could happen were the Hartmannian philosophy, with its absolute and therefore paralyzing pessimism, to pass into the flesh and blood of humanity. That is indeed enough to justify the claim that, through the insertion of the aforementioned intervening chapter, the whole ring of the Hartmannian system is burst asunder. Aksakow has proved himself, in his book, the most thorough connoisseur of Spiritism. But his book would have become still more convincing had he — and many a reader will miss this too — undertaken a definition of the anima unknown to us. This, admittedly, would have required a third volume, but it would have contributed essentially to a better understanding of Animism. The facts from which this definition results are far older than those of Spiritism. They are those already known to the ancient Egyptians and Greeks, but which in the Western world were first explored experimentally by Mesmer and his pupils: the facts of somnambulism. Such a third volume might perhaps have made an understanding between Hartmann and Aksakow possible. It was, however, impossible from the outset, because Hartmann, even in his own knowledge, has no substitute for this third volume. He does not know Mesmerism; its literature does not exist for him.

The philosopher of the Unconscious thus knows, of the Unconscious in man, only that small fraction which reveals itself in dream-life, in madness, and in various morbid states, and which has of late received an increment through hypnotism. In a word: Hartmann knows only the physiologically Unconscious, and behind it, further, the Unconscious as world-substance. That between the two there lies also a transcendental subject, he does not know, and will not know so long as he refuses to study somnambulism. Only thus is it explicable that he still maintains the fundamental error of his system: the restriction of our individuality to its earthly form of appearance. In place of the persistence of individuality there enters for him, then, the absorption of the same into the world-substance. But if death signifies the annihilation of the individual, then there can indeed no longer be spirits of the deceased; and so Hartmann is further compelled to force the spiritistic facts into the Unconscious of the medium, and moreover into that mere fraction of the Unconscious which, as merely physiological, adheres to the organism. The inexplicable remainder, however, he shifts onto the world-substance. If this dualism is a faulty one, the same cannot be said of that dualism which Aksakow has set up: Animism and Spiritism. This latter is, rather, unavoidable on logical grounds alone: for man cannot suddenly become, through death, what he never was. He can become, through death, a conscious spirit only in the one case where he was already, during his lifetime, unconsciously a spirit. Death can be a dematerialization of the soul only if birth is the materialization of a soul.

The faculties and powers of spirits must, then, lie latent in our own Unconscious, and if they emerge into appearance in us in abnormal states, analogies must show themselves between the animistic phenomena and the properly spiritistic ones. All the more difficult, then, is it to draw the dividing line between the two. That it must nevertheless be drawn, and that phenomena exist which cannot be fitted into Animism — to have shown this is precisely Aksakow's merit. With this he has set his opponent a very clear task. Hartmann was obliged to draw back, into his own explanatory principle, those phenomena which Aksakow had shown to lie beyond the dividing line and to exceed the Hartmannian principle of explanation. With this the dispute, which forms an event in the history of Spiritism, entered its second phase. Hartmann has, in a second tract,<sup>∗</sup> attempted to solve this task set for him. Since, however, he had in the meantime by no means remedied his previously conceded lack of experience — presumably for want of opportunity — he could, in the second tract too, oppose the facts only with apriorism once again, and already in the introduction he betrays what we are to expect from him. For he keeps a back door open for all cases and claims in advance, for himself, the right to eliminate at will those facts particularly inconvenient to him, in the words: "— that the series of facts must at least appear noteworthy enough to be honored with a serious, though conditional, critique; for no one will take the trouble to attach theoretical discussions to reports that are quite unbelievable" (3). In other words: plausibility is to be the criterion of truth! This principle is as convenient as it is unscientific. With what arbitrariness Hartmann applies it I will now show by a series of typical examples: 1.

Major-General Drayson reports on a young lady who stayed in his house in 1858 and with whom, since she was a medium, he held a séance every evening. In one of these she declared she saw a spirit who claimed to have been an astronomer. Drayson then first asked whether he now knew more than during his lifetime, and, this question being answered affirmatively, he demanded an explanation of the retrograde motion of the moons of Uranus. Drayson now received such an explanation, by which this apparent contradiction with the Kant-Laplace theory was resolved; the retrograde motion was explained from the axial position of Uranus, and this appeared to Drayson so clear and simple that he worked out the problem geometrically, had it printed in 1859 in the "Royal Artillery Institution," and in 1862 repeated the hypothesis in a special tract: "Common Sights in the Heavens." This hypothesis stood in opposition to all astronomical textbooks published up to that time and was at that time simply rejected by the academic scholars. To this report, given at great length in Aksakow (402–405), Hartmann devotes five lines of refutation, saying that this alleged pronouncement on the direction of revolution of the moons of Uranus had probably, unless the medium herself dabbled in astronomy, been picked up in a conversation with some amateur astronomer" (31). But at that time no professional scholar, let alone amateur, had yet hit upon this explanation; nor is it merely an alleged one, but has since been acknowledged by the professional scholars. Now, however, there is the further fact that the same Major-General Drayson, through the mediation of the same lady, communicated in 1859 with the same spirit, and, on asking whether he could tell him of another appearance in our solar system unknown to astronomers, received the answer: Mars has two moons.

Since these moons were in fact discovered 18 years later, on this point at least the disclosure cannot be called a merely "alleged" one. How, then, does Hartmann extricate himself from this embarrassment? Very simply: he passes over this point entirely in silence. 2. Judge Edmonds, one of the highest officials in America, relates<sup>∗</sup> that his own daughter Laura became a speaking medium and spoke in about ten foreign languages. While she was proficient only in English and partly in French, she spoke in trance Polish with

He mentions only the two Indian dialects, which are explicable because Edmonds knew them; of all the other languages Hartmann says nothing, and knows only how to say that the claim that Laura was proficient only in English has not been proven (32). In other words: Hartmann makes Edmonds out to be a fool and Laura a swindler who was, however, at the same time a linguistic genius. But in order to make it seem that he has done his critical duty, Hartmann later returns once more to this case and says: "On the levity with which the reporters blindly assume the mediums' unfamiliarity with foreign languages, or believe them on their bare word, I have already remarked above" (53). — Well, indeed, we have seen how, and on which side, the levity is to be found. 3. The Governor of Wisconsin, Nathaniel Tallmadge, received in a spiritistic séance the instruction to form a circle from his own family and to seat his youngest child, Emily, a girl of thirteen, at the piano. Tallmadge notes that when he came to Wisconsin the land was still so new that any opportunity for music instruction was entirely lacking, so that Emily knew not a single note nor could play the piano. When Tallmadge now formed the circle, Emily took paper and pencil, drew lines, and wrote in notes with all the musical marks. She then threw away the pencil and worked the table as though it were a piano.

Reminded thereby of the instruction she had received, Tallmadge led her to the piano, and now Emily, with bold bearing and with the confidence of an accomplished pianist, played first Beethoven's great waltz, then various well-known songs, and finally an entirely new melody, which she accompanied with improvised text and singing (Aksakow 446). How, then, does Hartmann extricate himself in this case? Again very simply. He merely raises the question of whether Tallmadge, in the preceding years, had not let his daughter out of his sight for even a single hour" (33). So in this case too the father is to be a fool, the daughter a swindler. I will mention only briefly a theoretical explanation that Hartmann attaches to this case, which again shows his lack of experience. He says that musical mediumship, if it occurs at all, could only rest on a spirit giving the medium the suggestion of the piece of music, which, however, would be of no use, because even if he wanted to add to the suggestion of the sound the suggestion of the motor impulses of execution according to their inner sensory character, this would not release, in the medium, the corresponding groups of coordinated movements" (34). But the theory of the Spiritists is that a spirit has two means of setting a medium's organs in motion: either suggestion, or direct possession. Hartmann, however, takes no account of this alternative, drawn from experience. 4. Mr. Livermore held 388 séances with Kate Fox within six years.

From the 43rd onward he came into constant contact with the deceased Estella, and he grounds his proof of identity on various circumstances: that, while he held the medium's hands, the entire figure of Estella was visible even in light; that her figure or bust formed itself out of a globe-round light before the eyes of the spectators and remained visible for a half to a full half-hour; that the image of the figure was reflected in a mirror, so that no hallucination was present; that the phantom communicated with him, either through rapping sounds or through the medium's hand — in the latter case in mirror-writing — messages in French, which Estella spoke fluently, though it was unknown to the medium; that at times cards, which he had provided himself with, were taken away from him and visibly put back, which he then found covered with French messages whose content, style, and expression pointed to Estella; that the handwriting of these messages was entirely identical with Estella's, but bore no resemblance to that of the medium; that Estella's figure was invisibly photographed and the picture recognized by friends.<sup>∗</sup> — How, then, does Hartmann extricate himself in this case? With the following six lines: "Could it really have been so difficult for the medium, who enjoyed the widower's full confidence, to obtain letters or notes of the deceased for inspection, and to memorize a few sentences translated into French by a third party for the purpose of mediumistic writing-down?" (54). 5. When spirit photographs are spoken of, whose resemblance to deceased persons is acknowledged, Hartmann, who from Berlin is much better able to judge this, remarks: "This resemblance will for the most part probably not exceed that of a cloud to a camel" (58). 6. Among the best proofs for the reality of the phantoms the Spiritists count the molds, and to this proof of reality Aksakow devotes the greatest thoroughness (165–219).

What, then, does Hartmann say to this? He first discredits the molds with the remark (110) that all the reports are of American origin. But, as one can see in Aksakow, the very opposite is the case. He then again knows only how to say that the experimenters are fools and the mediums swindlers; for that is what it amounts to when he writes: "Either the medium dips its own hands or feet into the paraffin, or it dips artificial hands and feet, differing from the shape of its own, which it has brought along for the purpose, or an accomplice dips his own limbs, or an accomplice dips artificial limbs" (110). "Since, however, Mr. Aksakow is under the error that the establishment of authentic mediumistic casts would irrefutably prove the materiality of the phantoms, I will nonetheless discuss what, in my opinion, could at most be inferred from such authentic casts. It would be this: the establishment of secondary force-centers on the surface of the paraffin layer to be directly molded, and their grouping, by gradual shifting against one another, in such a way that the paraffin takes on the form of a hand-surface imagined by the somnambulistic consciousness of the medium" (112). The reader will now, indeed, think that such explanations bear a fair resemblance to bad jokes; but they do have the advantage that, if they were correct, the "Philosophy of the Unconscious" would hold its ground even against the molds. Reason enough to put them forward. 7. The Spiritists lay particular weight on the reports of Crookes, who for four years experimented with a young girl in his laboratory, employing all the aids and precautions offered by science. These reports are found in the early volumes of "Psych.

Stud."; but I must confine myself here to mentioning briefly that Crookes succeeded in photographing medium and phantom simultaneously on one plate, and that the phantom once conversed for two hours outside the cabinet with those present, while Crookes repeatedly satisfied himself that during this time the medium lay asleep in the cabinet. What, then, does Hartmann say of these séances, which he already carefully skirts around in his first tract? Nothing more than that Crookes was "completely duped" by his medium, and that his enthusiasm had run away with his critical sobriety" (114, 122). That Hartmann has never seen a phantom does not prevent him from saying that such a thing is only "a soulless puppet that dances according to the imaginings and volitional impulses of the somnambulistic medium, but is by no means the bearer of a consciousness of its own" (87). The claim that phantoms under all circumstances stem from the medium is maintained in the words: "The medium can also dream various types as appearing one after another, or even simultaneously; it can dream complete or incomplete figures, and can dream a gradual emerging and sinking of the figures; it can also dream that it itself, drowsy with sleep, steps out of the cabinet together with the other dream-figure, and can, for its own part, realize this dream through dream-actions. Or it can dream that the other dream-figures develop out of its navel through a kind of umbilical cord, or grow forth out of its breast" (102).

All these dream-images of the medium are then supposed to be transmitted as hallucinations to the spectators, who — although they are awake — dream along with everything!! And such an explanation Hartmann dares to call a scientific one, and to set it against the unscientific explanation of the Spiritists! But explanations that the brain torments itself to produce are not, for that reason, any better than others that occur to it of their own accord. Aksakow reports (529) a case in which a deceased person, unknown to all present, made himself known, gave his name and day of death, and among other things added that he had been the father of eleven children. One wrote to his former place of residence, likewise unknown to those present, and received an answer in which all the statements were confirmed, only it spoke of seven children instead of eleven. To this Hartmann attaches (46) the disparaging remark: "Often enough, indeed, the data are inexact — for instance seven children instead of eleven; but one passes over this as something unimportant, in order to be all the more vividly astonished at what is correct." — But it is only Hartmann who is to be blamed, not Aksakow, who (531, note) adds that a further inquiry showed the number of eleven children given by the deceased to be correct. In another case, about which Aksakow, since it concerns two ladies of his own family, must obviously be better informed than Hartmann, the former states expressly that no affectionate rapport existed between the medium and the deceased. Hartmann, however, who in his first tract on Spiritism had put forward the claim that thought-transference at a distance is possible only where an affectionate rapport exists, speaks — in order to be able to throw the case into this pigeonhole — of the "deeply shaken medium," of an "infatuated friend," and of the medium's interest in the nihilist movement. But all this is pure invention and, according to Aksakow's express remark, was not the case at all.

In his first tract Hartmann says that the medium draws its knowledge from the consciousness of those present. To refute this, Aksakow reports cases of communications whose content could be known neither to the medium nor to those present. At once Hartmann then heightens the mediums' capability, sends their somnambulistic consciousness traveling, and now lets the mediums draw their knowledge from the consciousness of some living person anywhere on earth (41), and for the cases where even that is not sufficient he provides in advance by the explanation that then the telephone-connection to the world-substance comes into play. With such purely arbitrary explanatory hypotheses one can, of course, outdo one's opponent in every case. As the reader has seen, moreover, in the cases I have singled out, Hartmann mostly spares himself the trouble of explaining at all. To all reports he opposes only negations, to all testimonies moral suspicions. With this he indirectly confesses his own inability really to refute Spiritism. In this respect I could spare myself further engagement with his tract. It might, however, interest the reader if I show him, using Hartmann as an example, why Spiritism — as I have already often maintained — is not understood so long as we study it in isolation. Hartmann's harsh rejection is due, in fact, to the circumstance that he leaves the complementary study of somnambulism entirely aside. It is true that he too speaks of a somnambulistic consciousness; but by this he designates phenomena within the physiological sphere, by no means the consciousness of the transcendental subject. Such a subject he rather denies, and therefore he must tear the unified transcendental psychology into two pieces, of which he assigns the one to physiology, the other to the world-substance.

Somnambulism comprises that class of phenomena in which there shows itself, in living man, a usually latent kernel of being that is independent of the organism — indeed, for whose manifestations of activity the organism and its adhering sensory consciousness are actual hindrances. Hence we see it as almost a general rule that mystical activity and passivity presuppose the suppression of sensory consciousness. So in magnetic somnambulism and in the trance of mediums. Whoever now encounters such activities of our latent being, in which it reveals itself as the bearer of an individual consciousness peculiar to itself, must acknowledge the human soul, whether he wills it or not, whether he be pantheist or materialist. He differs from the rest of the believers only in that he places this soul in the Unconscious — because it remains hidden from us in the normal state — but does not thereby make it itself into something unconscious. And whoever, further, encounters in somnambulism auto-diagnosis, prognosis, the remedy-instinct, and the capacity to influence one's own organic functions through auto-suggestion or through accepted suggestion from others, will go on to identify this human soul with the organizing principle of our body. It is therefore not to be wondered at that all magnetizers of great experience in somnambulism recognized the empirical confirmation of the existence of an immortal soul and had to abandon their earlier materialism. So, for instance, Georget, who in his "Physiologie du système nerveux" was still an out-and-out materialist, but scarcely had the book appeared when he became acquainted with somnambulism and found in it the proof of the soul.

But he was able to confirm this his conviction only afterward, in a passage of his testament, to which he asked for the widest possible circulation.<sup>∗</sup> — The same conviction we find in Puységur, Deleuze, and a hundred others; indeed already Giordano Bruno — precisely because magic was to him no foreign domain — calls, in his "Spaccio," the deniers of the soul simply asses.

<sup>∗</sup> Macario: "Du sommeil," 148.

If, now, one has gained through the pursuit of these studies the concept of a thinking and organizing soul — that is, has discovered the spirit in living man himself — then only a small step remains to actual Spiritism; for the matter then concerns only the question of whether intercourse is possible between the discarnate and the incarnate spirits, and whether the former can attain to visible representation. Whoever comes from the study of somnambulism must answer this question in the affirmative; for materialization presupposes only an organizing soul, and this we find precisely in somnambulism. Once one believes in spirits and places one's own soul in the class of these beings — a belief to which Kant too confesses<sup>∗</sup> — one can least of all, in the age of the theory of evolution, maintain an eternal partition between the sensory world and the realm of spirits. The development by which nature is governed can only lead to the point where its separated pieces come into connection, that is, that the separated worlds ripen toward one another. This anyone who knows somnambulism and the theory of development must maintain a priori. The stubbornness with which Hartmann rejects Spiritism is thus sufficiently explained by the fact that the whole literature on somnambulism is unknown to him. One will search in vain, in any of his writings, for a reference to this now already century-old literature. Now Hartmann has, admittedly, come forward precisely with a "Philosophy of the Unconscious"; but the Unconscious in man is for him only physiologically a more or less homogeneous mass without psychological articulation. Sensory consciousness he regards as bound to the upper layers of the brain, but somnambulistic consciousness as bound to the subcortical nerve-centers.

<sup>∗</sup> Kant: "Träume eines Geistersehers."

He even asserts that somnambulistic consciousness is "sunk far more deeply into sensuality than waking consciousness, incapable of self-mastery, more irrational, more fantastical, more erratic. In short, it possesses every characteristic indicating that it is far more closely bound up with the vegetative and animal life-process of the organism than waking consciousness" (68). Had Hartmann studied magnetic somnambulism, he would know that somnambulistic consciousness is not only not "far more closely" bound up with the organism, but is, on the contrary, quite free of it. He would then realize that, for example, clairvoyance in space and time has to do with the organism only indirectly, and only insofar as the visionary images pass into the brain-consciousness, not actively produced by it, but passively received by it and thereby made sensory. He would then no longer be able to refuse to take the step from physiological psychology to transcendental psychology, and only then would he be a true philosopher of the Unconscious. Today he is one only to a very limited extent, because his definition of the Unconscious in man is quite inadequate, and because he simply does not know the most important class of phenomena — that of magnetic somnambulism. One need only read his definition of the Unconscious in man to recognize that he has never seen a somnambulist, still less a medium. He says: "The moods, whims, sudden leaps of thought, interruptions, quirks, willful caprices and capers of the somnambulistic consciousness, which has taken the bit of the waking intellect between its teeth, are just as incalculable as those of a hysteric, and its stubbornness in pursuing an idea once seized upon is no less.

This does not exclude the fact that, over certain stretches of its progression of thought, somnambulistic consciousness too exhibits a clever understanding and skillful combinations, exactly in the same sense in which one can often observe this in the insane, if one enters into their presuppositions, or as it also goes on, quite reasonably, in stretches of dreaming, until nonsense again breaks in between" (15). This definition fits the physiological Unconscious, in which the brain is source and stage of the imaginings, but not the transcendental Unconscious, in which the brain is only the passively receiving stage of the imaginings. One can grant Hartmann only this: that precisely because of this identity of the stage, a mixture of the two series of phenomena often occurs, whereby the transcendental is physiologically contaminated. This is shown by the normal dream and by madness, but no longer by true somnambulism, in which the bodily sphere has been brought to silence. Precisely in the insane, however, there is shown — something our psychiatrists, to be sure, do not know, but which alone suffices to refute Hartmann — that their consciousness is intact as soon as they are placed into magnetic somnambulism. In the Unconscious, then, as Hartmann has defined it above, no soul can indeed be discovered. But no one has ever wanted to look for it there either. It can, however, be found in the more deeply lying Unconscious that Mesmer and his pupils discovered, and whose phenomena can be interpreted only in the sense of a metaphysical individualism. These facts have been known for a hundred years already — leaving antiquity quite aside — and therefore a "Philosophy of the Unconscious" that takes no account of them is an anachronism, because it does not maintain continuity in science. Hartmann's philosophy could indeed already have been refuted by Mesmer.

The reader can now imagine what results when Hartmann applies the Unconscious, as he understands it, as a yardstick to the spiritistic phenomena. One half of them must then be explained crookedly, the other simply denied. And that is indeed what has happened. That Unconscious which shows itself prominently in magnetic somnambulism — namely the transcendental subject — would be far more equal to the task of explaining Spiritism from the medium than the physiologically Unconscious, which shows itself in dream, madness, and hysteria. And yet, although I thus have a more comprehensive explanatory principle ahead of Hartmann, I nevertheless hold it to be insufficient, and am, as the reader knows, compelled to shift a considerable part of the spiritistic phenomena onto an objective cause outside the medium, namely onto another order of beings. So too Aksakow. He too explains part of the phenomena from the medium, and calls these animistic. But the anima is for him far more than Hartmann's physiologically Unconscious; for him it is an individual soul with its own consciousness and with organizing powers. And yet he finds this anima insufficient to explain Spiritism. Aksakow, then, measures an object with a meter-stick and says the stick is too short; whereupon Hartmann takes a decimeter-stick and declares that this is long enough. It is as if one man said: 2×2 does not even make 5; the other, however: 2×2 makes even 6. Characteristic of Hartmann's manner of fencing is also his examination of the spiritistic problem of immortality, particularly with reference to pessimism. Now the view of the Spiritists is that the soul underlying the animistic phenomena outlasts death. The Spiritists further acknowledge earthly pessimism — that is, since this superlative is not applicable for want of an object of comparison — they acknowledge earthly evil.

Earthly pessimism, however, flows for the Spiritists into a transcendental optimism, because, first, the doctrine of immortality satisfies the strongest instinct of man, the will to live; and because, second, the state beyond, although somnambulism gives us of it only a faint conception, appears as a relatively blessed one. That is why the magnetic somnambulists — whom Hartmann does not know — have always praised their state as a happy one and have not feared death, but rather the awakening, that is, the return to sensory consciousness. That is also why the Christian mystics have interpreted the ecstatic states as a "rapture into heaven." That is finally why the Indian mystic sees in ecstasy even a merging with Brahma, that is, a becoming God. For Hartmann, now, there exists no personal immortality. He does not concede that it exists; but he assumes it in order to be able to continue his academic investigation and to ask what would follow if our Unconscious were immortal. In doing so, however, he immediately again substitutes his own changeling for the transcendental subject, and now, of course, it is not difficult for him to let immortality flow into pessimism. He constructs a downright dreadful state beyond, roughly equivalent to a permanently declared hysteria and madness. Thus he seeks to create the appearance that the Spiritists themselves pour water onto his pessimistic mill. This procedure of Hartmann's — of foisting sheer nonsense onto his opponents' pen — runs through all his polemical writings. Here, however, the confusion of concepts lies quite openly exposed, by which he manages to throw the pessimistic cowl over Spiritism. But the Unconscious in Aksakow is something quite different from the Unconscious in Hartmann. In Aksakow the soul is something un-known by the sensory human being; in Hartmann — in that he concedes it only hypothetically — it is something unconscious in itself.

Hartmann thus wishes to create the appearance of proceeding from the same premise as the Spiritists: the persistence of the Unconscious. But then he presents us as people who lack only logical sobriety in order to arrive at such a beyond — one which indeed follows from his Unconscious, but not from ours. The sober reader, however, will not let himself be dazzled by Hartmann's tract; as often as he comes upon the words "the Unconscious" and "the somnambulistic consciousness," he will tell himself that Hartmann means by them something other than the Spiritists do. I have said above, with regard to immortality, that man, already in this life, is unconsciously a spirit; but I have not said that he is an unconscious spirit. This, however, would have been my view in Hartmann's sense. He says: "If the spirit, as an individual, were to persist after death, it could only persist as absolutely unconscious; for if it loses the highest consciousness, that of the cerebral cortex, it will all the more certainly lose the lower consciousnesses, down to the cellular and atomic consciousness of its body.... But that which is allegedly supposed to be proved by them (the spiritistic experiences) — the persistence of memory-capable organic remnants without spirit and vital force — would furnish one of the most dreadful and repulsive chapters of pessimism, were one not entitled to dismiss it as a phantastical superstitious fabrication" (69, 72). We Spiritists, however, protest against the paternity of such a fabrication; the child is Hartmann's child, not ours. Likewise the alleged moral non-accountability of the spirits (74) holds only if the Unconscious meant by Hartmann were later to become a spirit; for, as is well known, the magnetic somnambulists — of whom Hartmann knows nothing — show not only an intellectual but also a moral enhancement.

Finally Hartmann says: "From this point of view spiritistic practice appears as a bodily, spiritual, and moral danger, as a presumptuous game with dangerous and uncanny powers, without sense or purpose. Church and police would therefore have every reason to curb such reprehensible mischief with all their power, were they not right in preferring to leave this morbid excrescence of an overheated fantasy to the social power of natural healing and to medicine" (76). This ogling toward the police, however, amounts only to a confession of one's own inability to deal philosophically with Spiritism, and all that is now still lacking is the proposal of a press law forbidding philosophers in Germany to attack the dogmas issued by the philosophical papacy in Berlin. Under these circumstances it is surely not too harsh a judgment when I say that Hartmann's two anti-spiritistic tracts constitute a contribution to eristic, but none to philosophy. The spiritistic phenomena are, after all, facts. The opponents fight against these facts with mere arguments. But this is the act only of an inverted Don Quixote; for if it is superfluous to level a heavy lance against mere windmills, it is the height of folly to want to run one's head against a wall, and to declare realities impossible. However slowly people's habits of thought may change, it is nevertheless only a question of time before Spiritism finds its recognition. Its facts, however, now prove the existence of an individual soul and its persistence, and this view must necessarily influence, in the most favorable sense, our whole conception of the world and of life, and hence also our conduct of life.

Aksakow, at the close of his preface, has posed a question to himself: "At the ebb of my life I sometimes ask myself: have I really done well to devote so much time, labor, and means to the study and dissemination of the phenomena of this field? Have I not taken a wrong path? Have I not chased after an illusion? Have I not lost an existence, without anything seeming to justify or repay my effort?" But to this question of Aksakow's no other answer can be given than the one he has given himself: "For the use of an earthly life there can be no more exalted purpose than to attempt to prove the transcendental nature of the human being, who is called to a far more exalted destiny than phenomenal existence!"
