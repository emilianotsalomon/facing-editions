### Preliminary Remarks.

Friends who have read the following pages in manuscript have advised against their publication, because they fear that such expositions, precisely when they are to some degree understood, could mislead readers into the opinion that, having *comprehended* the matter itself, they had also *apprehended* it. Such a conceit could prove ruinous; let everyone, therefore, hereby be earnestly warned against it.

For the rest, this concern presupposes nothing less than a complete misunderstanding of the philosophy that makes itself heard in this work, nothing less than the most perverse misuse of this philosophy, a misuse diametrically opposed to its very own essence. This philosophy teaches, precisely in the identity of being and knowing, that the knowing of the Concept is an actual knowing only insofar as it coincides with the being [p. IV] and life in the object of the Concept. Only in knowing is being transfigured into its truth, namely into consciousness—not into self-consciousness, which on the contrary is overcome and effaced in knowing—but into God-consciousness. Thus being, according to its truth, is life in God, knowing is God-consciousness, and hence being and knowing are identical.

We must add, further, that in general comprehending and apprehending are preceded, accompanied, and followed by being comprehended and apprehended. For how could man know and comprehend the truth, if it did not know him and comprehend him within itself; how could he apprehend it, if it had not first grasped and apprehended him? —

Whoever, nevertheless, seeks to speculate about the truth without living in the truth with the disciple of love, whoever moves among concepts without experiencing the renewing word of life in his heart, lacks, despite all outward comprehending, along with life in Christianity also actual knowing, which concerns not a part of the spirit but the spirit itself, indeed is this spirit itself.

Herein, therefore, lies the ground which, against that abstracted from the manifold experiences of our days [p. V] concern justifies the publication of these leaves, written down in rapid succession; for what, misunderstood and misused, works ruinously and fatally, that, understood and used, can only work salutarily and quickeningly.

Admittedly, however, the content with which we are here concerned is in itself difficult, and to that extent exposed to particular misunderstandings, so that the claim to exactness in execution is doubled. The task here before us becomes still more precarious because it had at first to confine itself to the results and purposes, as well as to the differences and boundary-marks within one and the same sphere, but on the other hand could not remain standing at these boundaries, and rather dared not shrink from entering into the content itself and occupying itself with it. To satisfy both sides of this task at once, only an attempt, a beginning, has been made here, without the concern about misuse being increased by such imperfection.

What this beginning is not itself, that it can become precisely by stepping forth publicly as it is, in its first shape. If it is no finished work, it can yet be effective; [p. VI] and what it does not effect in the author himself, that it can effect in others and better ones. What is at work has in general no more misunderstanding to fear than what is finished. A finished work will be exposed to misunderstanding precisely the more, the more it anticipates the effect it is to expect, the less it leaves to this free effect itself. Incidentally, an experience of eighteen hundred years teaches that precisely the simplest, most open, most living truths are met by the grossest misunderstandings of the world.

Under such circumstances this slight writing too will, to be sure, be unable to escape the most various misunderstandings, and will become an offence to many. Those prejudices which do not find the simple and complete Gospel again in this philosophy, or hold it to be endangered, are sufficiently met in the writing itself; to the opposite ones, however, which think to find the Gospel here completely again, and yet do not know it from its source, do not accept it wholly, do not go back to the source, this warning is dedicated, apart from the writing itself.

The author must warn once more, earnestly warn, that no one prepare for himself, out of abstract concepts, the sword whereby he [p. VII] will slay himself, if he thereby cheats himself of the Gospel, after he believes he has comprehended it.

The misery of negativity can be borne only by one from whom the positive truth beneath it does not slip away, and the dangerous but unavoidable path of an all-dissolving, all-decomposing and all-crushing *dialectic* is endured only by—the *speculative* spirit, which is given to the subject when it holds fast in prayer and in faith, and in all its distresses seeks refuge in Scripture, as the Word of God.

The more deeply and earnestly abstraction, dissecting and dismembering on all sides and in all distinctions, penetrates to the last points, indeed to the very blood, and threatens to annihilate us or the object or both, the more visibly and vividly shall we, after such toils and experiences, find again in the Word of God the undivided wholeness and bodily fullness of the truth, which gives all in One, and which, when it once more, sharper than any two-edged sword, severs and shatters and crushes, also binds up again, and heals, and raises up.

### Introduction.

It belongs indisputably to the essence, and hence to the unfailing effect, of living Christian faith that we endeavour to transpose ourselves into the men *with and beside* us, as into our brothers, as into members of the same humanity, to enter into their manner of representation and thought, to understand their views and such insights as they may have, as well as their dispositions, and thereby to come into community, into actual contact and reciprocal action with them. More precisely, what matters is that we seek to transpose ourselves into others, not as into others, but as into the same flesh and blood, with love. Indeed, this belongs to the following of our Head, who did not disdain to transpose Himself into us; only that what in Him was humiliation serves to our furtherance. Even in Him, after all, this self-emptying in the Incarnation is humiliation only insofar as we look to our unworthiness, whereas in relation to God’s eternal counsel it is grounded in the necessity of the divine essence, and belongs to the loftiness of this essence!

Precisely for this reason it is to be regarded as a dangerous aberration when dear Christians deliberately withdraw from those who are, allegedly or in fact, outside, or when they turn away too hastily and scornfully from such as are being led by other ways to the one narrow and strait way. This aberration shows itself in particular also in relation to the age, insofar as Christians are tempted to ignore their age, or, more precisely, the [p. 2] scientific strivings of the same, to keep them far from themselves, which moreover amounts, more or less, to an unchristian putting on of airs. Still more questionable is it if we perhaps judge and persecute the latest results of human researches according to their outer side, without having arrived at insight into them. But we should already be judging if we wished to decry all human researches summarily as merely human handiwork. —

The task of *accommodating oneself to the age* cannot, however, especially in our age, namely in relation to the philosophy of the same and its present culminating point, easily be wholly declined without sin by those Christians whom their professional circumstances have called to science. At least, what may perhaps be permitted to one or another according to his individuality can never hold as a rule, to release from this participation.

The philosophy of our time calls itself, indeed, a *Christian* one; it wishes to count not as a furtherance or perfection of Christianity, but as its fruit and work; it calls itself, as the common property of the human race, the highest product of Christianity; it expresses its respect for the revealed Word of God, as the given absolute truth, so unconditionally, and inveighs so tirelessly against all distortion and emptying-out of the real import of Holy Scripture, and against its loose volatilization into pure—self-made spirit and bare human understanding, that it compels special attention even from that Christian who has enough for himself in his simple, living faith, and becomes certain in the representational element of absolute truth. At least it would be very daring, indeed not to be justified, if we, without taking closer knowledge of them, wished to regard these scientific strivings, whose earnestness and depth are not to be mistaken, with the suspicion that in the end the truth [p. 3] of revealed religion in this philosophy becomes, *by means of* the Concept, another than that given *immediately* in representation.

Nevertheless it is not to be denied that precisely on this point sin has been committed, within and without the walls of Zion, of which, alas, the phenomena of the day furnish manifold proofs in sad misunderstandings and dissonances. Indeed, the Bible-believing Christians seem to agree with their opponents, who call themselves the intelligent rationalists, in nothing except the accusations against speculative philosophy. Were it not superfluous and odious, this almost verbal agreement could easily be demonstrated from individual examples in our literature. So much is also undeniable, that rationalism remains true and consistent to itself when, as the subjective, abstract-sensuous wisdom of the understanding, it opposes speculative philosophy, as the objective thought, since its standpoint, from which it cannot separate without annihilating itself, at once distorts the speculative results and divests them of their validity. If now the so-called supranaturalism agrees with this, it is in this respect itself rationalistic. But as certainly as it is essentially different from rationalism, so certainly is it also different from it in all respects, consequently also in its relation to speculative philosophy. It is therefore not to the system of Christian theology, but to the aberration of individual Christian theologians, that it is to be ascribed if individual doctrines of speculative philosophy are subjected by them to the abstract-sensuous understanding, quite as the truths of revealed religion are by rationalism, and are thereby violated and perverted in their own proper essence. How inconsistently the Christian theologians proceed in this is plain, for they thereby fall back into a sphere which they, as unreal and mendacious, recognize as little as do the philosophers, and according to which neither theology nor [p. 4] philosophy can be judged; they judge speculative philosophy by a standard from which they themselves withdraw and must withdraw. If it is consistent for the rationalist to judge speculative philosophy also rationalistically, since he looks at everything with the same eyes, then it is inconsistent for the evangelical Christian to proceed just as rationalistically with philosophy, when he does not look at everything that is with the same eyes by means of which he becomes certain of his faith. —

In these reflections there lies at the same time, for those Christian theologians who have occupied themselves with the philosophy of their time and have come to an understanding of it, a call to express themselves conscientiously and without reserve about it, and about the relation of this philosophy to unadulterated Christian theology, for the instruction of others. Only this would have to be done in generally intelligible language, and not in the terminology of the school, as indeed it is to be regarded as a proof of real appropriation and living penetration if we have learned to translate a foreign work of thought into our own language.

This call we believe we confirm and strengthen by ourselves making an imperfect, fragmentary beginning, for which we need, besides the respect and the gratitude to which this philosophy of itself obliges us, above all love.

*Respect* is demanded by this philosophy already by the renunciation alone of all subjective notions and opinions, the surrender to the objective thought and its self-movement, and the strict labour in its service; for it must plough its field in the sweat of its face down to the depth, where the subjective thought is wont to fly gladly and genially over the surface. *Gratitude* is vowed to it by him for whom it has secured the victory over manifold doubts of the understanding, furnished weapons against the sham wisdom of the world, and procured the thought and [p. 5] word to secret feelings, indeed has paved the way back to simple faith. But we need *love* in order truly to transpose ourselves into the other soul, so as to seek in it the final result of its strivings and, where possible, to find the disposition and insight that they bring forth in the other soul.

With scholars one often comes nearest the goal if one seeks them out not in finished writings but in living life, for example in conversation and correspondence, or in individual avowals. So, too, it might be nearest and simplest to approach this philosophy in that way.

*“If I cannot comprehend what is present in Christianity as the absolute truth through philosophy in the pure form of knowing, so that the Idea itself is this form, then I want to know nothing more of any philosophy.”*

So writes a disciple of this philosophy to his teacher<sup>∗)</sup>. We wish to consider these simple, honest words more closely according to the sense of this philosophy in general, and in particular according to the context from which they are taken, by translating them word for word, so that nothing of their content may be lost.

Hitherto I had believed in the Word of God, as the truth itself, “for the quite simple reason that the spirit of the human race in this respect”—namely with regard to the objective reality of the Word on which faith rests—“does not let itself be deceived.” For what “the community,” the Church, as the body of Christ, in which He expresses Himself, unanimously

believes, and has always unanimously believed, that cannot deceive; by virtue of its objective validity it must maintain the mastery over all individual and isolated doubting and opining. It attests its validity by its unanimous objectivity, whereas everything human and subjective splinters within itself, and in each individual is something different.

But in that I believed because the Church believed, I was accustomed to intuit the truth by means of the representational element, i.e. as a represented truth, as separate and different from me, hence as transcendent, i.e. as an Other than I, as outside of me, the intuiting one, so that intuiting and the intuited fall apart, and the view never attains to insight, devotion never to thought.

After I had come to consciousness of this, namely of this difference of the truth from the knowing of it, this consciousness, and in its course science, i.e. the dialectic of it, took the representational element from me, and made the truth of it disputable, indeed brought me into discord with myself. For if the truth, as represented—and I had it only as such—is an Other than myself, then I am, as I just am—so I concluded without delay—also an Other than it, hence untrue, for the truth is outside of me, and I am not in the truth. Because it is estranged from me, I am estranged from myself. For the truth is the universal, whole, One, beside and outside of which nothing is actual: if I now am actual, or at least was originally actual, then I too must belong to the truth, as the sole and only actuality, or at least have originally belonged to it. If now, as I find myself at present, this truth is nevertheless estranged from me, then I, this I, am estranged from myself, from my original essence. If I am conscious of the truth as outside of me, then I am properly myself [p. 7] not conscious of it, and consequently not conscious of myself, and thus fallen out with and within myself. In that I seek the truth, I seek myself; and I seek it in that I want to know it; in that I demand to know it, I seek precisely nothing other than to sublate the strangeness and otherness of the truth, and so the strangeness of myself or of my original essence; for if the truth were not presupposedly my original essence, nothing would be more futile and impossible than to sublate the strangeness of an absolutely strange thing. All knowing points to an *original* unity of knowing with the truth, for knowing consists precisely in this sublation of the opposition, of the strangeness, which, of course, in turn cannot become possible through me, as the now untrue, but precisely always only through the objective truth in its further movement without my doing, if it is possible at all.

Or am I perhaps myself, as I am, this I, the truth itself? Well then, what I represent to myself in faith, as estranged from me, is also estranged from the truth. But how does it come about, then, that I am urged and driven to seek the truth? For what I have, I need not seek.

So it came about that I endeavoured “to sublate the highest disunion and highest despair brought about in me” by the *dialectic* of consciousness, “and so in the element of knowing,” i.e. in the *speculative* element, “to win reconciliation.” For, I said to myself, if I can merely represent the truth to myself, but not comprehend it, i.e. grasp it merely as represented, transcendent, but not as immanent, not as identical with knowing and comprehending itself, or, more clearly, if I can know the truth merely as outside of me, not as in me, can know it only so that my knowing is not the universal knowing, and this not the truth itself, if I cannot become so certain of it through the Concept that I myself am comprehended in it, [p. 8] and only through it become certain of myself, or, in other words, if an adequate cognition of the truth, i.e. being in the truth itself, is so restricted for me that I know it merely as object—then it can only take faith from me, but not replace it; but if this is so, then I want to know nothing more of all knowing, and renounce all thinking and knowing; for if it must once and for all remain in representation, and cannot attain to the immanent Concept, then it is less than the living *representation* which I already had in faith, and had entire, without stunting and splintering, whereby the finite understanding wants to spoil the full possession for me. —

So far goes our translation of the above confession, and indeed human beings awakened to consciousness by thought also fall apart into those who renounce science, and those who do not wish to become untrue to philosophy even in its dark, dialectical stations, lamentingly proclaiming the death of faith.

But in order to remain true to philosophy, the latter must either fall out with themselves, with their faith, or find again in science, i.e. in the form of knowing, actually that which they formerly had in faith, i.e. in the form of representation, as absolute truth, yet so that science does not so much replace faith as have it within itself. This is the stage of *absolute knowing*. Those who stand upon it we call the *Immanent Ones*.

Those, however, who do not get so far must for the time being renounce philosophy altogether, as such, namely as something higher than faith is. But among these a further important difference emerges. For since they take leave of this robber only at the moment in which science has taken objective faith from them, it seems as [p. 9] if the renunciation came too late, since the harm has already been done and cannot be undone. Indeed, many by such renunciation get no further than to non-knowing, in which objective, given faith also goes under as an idol, as crude materialism, and from which, as a wretched substitute, a subjective, purely spiritual, ideal, self-made faith arises, which, if it remains true to itself, volatilizes into the universal Idea. In the result, therefore, non-knowing, or the philosophy of non-knowing, which has indeed itself called itself un-philosophy, coincides actually with the philosophy of nothing, which develops in Idealism and Pantheism, except that that un-philosophy, because of a feeling striving against its consistency, will not admit to itself the loss of reality it has suffered. This, then, is the stage of *non-knowing*. Those who move upon it we call the *Inner Ones*, or *Inward Ones* [Innerlichen, Inwendigen], since they do not, consistently, distinguish themselves, in ground (the subjective Idea) and in result (the nothing), from the Idealists, whom the non-knowing call by this name<sup>∗)</sup>.

But this renunciation of philosophy, which gets only as far as non-knowing, without the faith destroyed by the dismissed science being restored, is, looked at more closely, only a half renunciation. As the philosophy of nothing is only half, unfinished knowing, so the philosophy of non-knowing, if objective faith remains destroyed, is only half non-knowing. For that unhappy element of science which destroys faith, and for that very reason has drawn dismissal upon science itself, has actually not been dismissed, for otherwise objective faith would not be regarded as destroyed by it, could not remain destroyed. complete, i.e. consistent renunciation it belongs rather that that single unhappy element too is not recognized, whereby its influence on objective faith is of itself removed; and this complete, unrestricted renunciation is also what was indicated in the above alternative as a possible way out. Indeed, it is not to be seen why we should not also dismiss precisely that element of science on account of which we dismiss the whole of science with all its still unknown elements, since it is precisely it that occasions the dismissal. But after, through the consistency of this renunciation, its *one-sided*, negative influence on the objective faith that stands above it has been withdrawn for ever from thought, or rather from the isolated elements of thought, and with it the highest authority which it wished to usurp, and faith itself, as the fidelity of unconditional trust in revealed truth, is secured, it cannot fail that nevertheless reason is employed in the service of faith and under the discipline of the Word, as the truth, in order to bring the given representation more and more to life and to understanding. In this altered relation the thought, torn into individual elements, hence subjective, and therefore dismissed, is taken up again as objective thought, and so there arises the stage of *faith and knowing*, which for the time being therefore ranks knowing second, because it seems to come afterwards, and can serve faith but not command it. This stage, which separates faith and knowing, and for the time being subordinates the latter to the former, but in this subordination employs thought for an ever deeper insight into faith, so that thought can only benefit, but not harm, faith—this stage of the *cognition of faith* is designated, from the stage of absolute knowing, which has the truth in the form of truth, as the true that is given in *representation*, and *interwoven with thoughts*, [p. 11] but not permeated by thought, because it has not come so far with thought as with faith, and knows the faith bestowed upon it to be independent of its understanding, although it hopes to become what absolute knowing claims already to be. Those who stand on this middle stage are not ashamed if we for the time being call them, with Jacobi, the *Outward Ones* [die Auswendigen], until perhaps between the Outward One and the Immanent One, between faith and knowing, a complete reconciliation and interpenetration comes about.

This faith and knowing of the Outward Ones, or of the Realists, stands therefore for the time being midway between non-knowing and absolute knowing. We also leave it for now untouched, since for the time being we wish only to come to know more closely the two sides which enclose it. For if it is our desire to come to know the philosophy of our time, which announces itself as absolute knowing, more closely and thoroughly in its latest results, that is, in its relations to Christianity, then it is expedient first to grasp its opposite, namely absolute non-knowing, which the philosophy of the most recent past has shown itself to be, *and still shows* itself to be—for the past has not ceased to be in the present—as ingenuously as possible.

In order now to take up and grasp both sides of the human spirit and of the truth itself, one after the other, as unfalsified and uncoloured as possible, we might abstract and extract the universal *spirit* of them, or attempt a complete, systematic extract of the whole material. The latter would exceed the bounds of a beginning, of an attempt, which is what is aimed at here, or even our powers; the former would call forth an articulated series of interconnected and mutually following results and judgements; but both would afford a total survey requisite [p. 12] for criticism. Instead of this we take a third way, even if it tears apart and scatters that continuous series of abstracted and summed-up final judgements, in that for this once we leave the precision of the school, much as we honour it, and follow the stream of life instead of guiding it. It is therefore a matter of nothing other than the attempt, on both sides, to reach freshly into full life, and where we grasp it, to hold it fast, to bring it to the spot and call it to account, in order to experience, in the singular that meets us, the interest of the whole.

### I.

### Non-Knowing.

What man sees, he also believes he knows; and when he later becomes convinced that seeing is not yet knowing, the former nevertheless affords him some substitute for the latter, and representation can take the place of the Concept, which is why in this sphere he does not particularly trouble himself about the latter. Hence non-knowing does not, at least, first come to light in the sphere of visible things, or generally in the sphere of representation. But what man does not see, that he also does not know; he wants to know it, however, precisely because he does not see it, and when he nevertheless does not succeed, only then does it come about that he becomes distinctly conscious of non-knowing; and now a substitute is found for this as well, for here it is immediately replaced by the inner sense, as there by the outer. In the place of the Concept steps the universal Idea, and what the eye accomplishes there is mediated here by a nameless feeling, which itself seems to be immediate.

Now that man concerns himself less with what he sees than with what he does not see, and thus looks away from himself, he does not ask further what he himself is; but because he cannot regard himself as the origin of himself, there arises at once, in place of the first question, the last of all questions, the question:

#### What is God?

Non-knowing answers this question by itself; indeed, it first arises in consciousness through this question, and proves itself precisely in it in its whole *ingenuousness*.

[p. 14]

God is; that is the first. *God is God*; that is the second and last. He alone is equal to Himself, and apart from Him nothing is equal to Him.

Herewith the truth is *immediately* certain, and, as immediate, withdrawn from thought and its realm, from knowing, yet more certain than all mediated knowing. This is the innermost declaration of conscience, faith, subjective faith.

From this all the rest follows. God is—all that we cannot know. He is separated and different from what He Himself is not,—*toto coelo*—extramundane, transcendent, and yet also in and with us; He subsists in essence and is actual, as the One who is, and yet is no individual, no singular one.

He is no singular one, and yet Person, indeed personality Himself; Person, and yet absolutely infinite, boundless, everywhere and nowhere, spirit without body.

That this sentence by sentence sublates itself and contradicts itself does not escape non-knowing. But nothing further follows from it than that God is incomprehensible, unutterable, invisible. The same already lies in the supreme principle according to its immediacy, in the principle that God is *only equal to Himself*.

According to His essence He is unknown to us, but not according to His existence. As transcendent and extramundane He cannot be known; as intramundane He can be only felt in His effects.

He finds himself in an eternal splendour, / Us he has brought into darkness, / And for you only day and night will do.

With this Holy Scripture, too, seems to agree, for the Apostle Paul teaches that God dwells in an unapproachable light, to which no one can come; but the same Apostle also teaches that God is manifested in the flesh, because He can come to us when we cannot come to Him; the same Apostle

*On Divine Things*, p. 36. [p. 15] proclaimed the unknown God as the revealed. Whereas non-knowing presupposes for cognition an inaccessibility on both sides, but for feeling a communion which on neither side first needs an approach, since it is interrupted by nothing.

Herewith the personality of God is rescued at the expense of the understanding, and certainly not bought too dear, if it costs merely my understanding. For who has not experienced in himself, in the course of his life and thinking, this revolt of the understanding against God’s independent and self-conscious existence? And who would not rather give up everything, in order only to save and preserve God’s personality and, in it, himself as conditioned by it?

“The more perfectly, quietly and purely you collect yourself in your inner being, the more distinctly will you perceive: *He is!*—who made the eye: *He sees!* who planted the ear: *He hears!* who prepared this heart: *He loves!* who bore this spirit out of Himself: *He wills and knows and is!*”

*He is*, because *He is*, exalted above every proof and above every concept. He *knows*, indeed He is the knowing of Himself, as He is being, but He cannot be known. So far reaches the warrant of the inner sense, but no further; for *how* God sees, hears, loves and wills and knows and is *what* He is, you cannot think without drawing Him down from His height, His universality.

We see already here that this God, however great He may be, is born from man himself, only that to the latter nothing belongs but finitude, to the former nothing but infinitude. Woe to us if He also *loves* as *this heart* which He has prepared, and *wills* as *this will*!

And yet the truth in this immediacy, too, is not to be mistaken. A Christian says the same thing.

But instead of this non-knowing leading precisely to the necessity and actuality of the revelation of the God hidden in Himself, it confines itself to the natural revelation given in conscience, however much this contradicts the naturalness of thought.

Thus far the contradictions which the understanding heaps together, because its competence is denied, are of no influence on the immediately given representation of the *person* of God. But we go further, to learn whether non-knowing also remains faithful to itself henceforth.

He who gave the wind its weight, who set the water its certain measure, who made for the rain a limit, and for lightning and thunder the way, and assigned to every nature its bounds,—is He not Himself the measure?—No, we hear the answer, appealing to Job 28:21–27: He is the measure only insofar as He is the author of the measure in others, but He Himself is immeasurable, He *devised* the measure, and shape, law and finite being, but not in order to be it Himself. For Himself He sets no limits, but for everything else, for He has ordered all things by measure, number and weight, but He Himself does not belong to this All, the Creator not to creation.

He did not ask, like Pilate, but earnestly and unceasingly: What is truth? and so he may also here have heard the answer that lies in the question, the answer which Pilate saw and heard, and yet did not see and hear. The truth is the man who is, who really is there! *Est vir qui adest.* [It is the man who is present.]

But this man is after all also a human being, like every human being. On this the Roman governor, a heathen, is indeed in complete agreement with him who is baptized into this man, [p. 30] the Christian philosopher: both are also agreed that they find no guilt in this man.

If nevertheless this man is just a human being, like every human being, then he cannot be the truth; for since man, as he just is, does not know the truth, he cannot be it either.

But for that very reason the question arises more closely:

#### What is man?

The question: What is God? is identical with the question: What is truth? Otherwise either truth or God would be an empty concept devoid of content. But both questions stand again in such an interaction with the second question: *What is man?* that with the one the other too would be answered, and the point from which we start can be regarded as indifferent; for actually we ask with both questions nothing other than: *What is God in relation to man? what is man in relation to God?*

From the connection of the theological question with the anthropological there develops at this stage of consciousness the question: *How does man attain to God?* Why this question comes to stand precisely so, and what influence this position has upon the answer or its non-appearance, that can be judged only later.

Perhaps we now arrive, through self-cognition, at the cognition of God. But how are we to arrive at self-cognition? If our cognition of God depends on this, then our self-cognition too will become dependent on our cognition of God, *so long as we have nothing but ourselves*. If, then, God according to his innermost essence does not come to consciousness, this non-knowing [p. 31] will also cling to self-cognition, even though here as there it comes to individual assertions and assurances.

God is infinite, man finite;—a more decided opposition there cannot be.—God is the truth, man—a part of the truth, for he is the creature of God, God the Father, man the child.

This creature is now, curiously, on the one hand not only unlike its Creator, but also, as finite, as non-knowing, wholly opposed to the infinite, all-knowing Father, from whom it nevertheless descends; on the other hand, however, man too, as a creature of God, is *nobly created*; that is the first; *nobly born*, that is immediately the second, for what should lie in the middle between the creation and the actual existence of the single creature?

*Is it really so*, is there in this inference no fallacy, is no middle term really missing; then from it everything else follows to the question: What is man?

He is all that he can be. God in the small! like God! and for that very reason able to divine God in the great, but not to cognize Him, because he is not God Himself. Only God cognizes Himself.

The God in the small cannot cognize the God in the great, although the latter is so inborn in the former that from the small God the great one proceeds, and in the former becomes man; it cannot, because the appearance is not being itself, cannot, because the copy is separated from the archetype *equally originally* through the finitude which belongs to the former, in opposition to the infinitude which belongs to the latter.

From likeness to God follows the communion of man with God, from the finitude of man the separation between God and man; the former proves itself in the indestructible certainty of faith, by means of feeling, the latter in the incapacity [p. 32] and nullity of all knowing, which resists the essence of God, as the finite resists the infinite. But this non-knowing is at the same time immediate knowing,

“We know of God and his will, because we—*born* of God, *created* after his image, are his kind and race.”

From this it follows that God is *immediately* present to us through his image in our innermost self.

For that reason every external revelation, too, is impossible. God reveals himself either in us, or not at all, and indeed not in such a way that God comes into us from outside, for he is inborn in us.

“As little as a false god can *exist* outside the human soul for himself, so little can the true one *appear* outside it. As man feels and forms himself, so he represents to himself, only more mightily, the Godhead.”

Only in passing do we point out how here the truth unmistakably shines through in the error, but the error predominates, in which, in place of God, an idol is substituted, the golden calf made by human hands.

Because at this standpoint of non-knowing and formlessness man lets no image of God be given to him, he makes it himself. According to the Bible man shall not make himself an image of God, but rather accept God in the form in which he reveals himself. Here this commandment is stood on its head.

Incidentally it may be remarked here that Jacobi, in agreement with the aesthetic tendency of our time and its manner of education, calls that soul *beautiful* which holds itself beautiful and loves itself, whereas elsewhere a confidant of Jacobi’s designates as the beautiful soul that soul which does not hold itself beautiful and finds no pleasure in itself, and which therefore can in no case maintain the predicate attributed to it without contradiction; for if it is what it holds itself to be, then it is not beautiful; but if it is beautiful and yet denies, misjudges and thereby disgraces this beauty, it becomes untrue, and thereby forfeits the beauty, which is identical with truth.

But we go further. Let us only weigh Woldemar’s constant doctrine. “Just, virtuous, noble, excellent be what the just, virtuous, noble, excellent man, in accordance with his character, practises, performs and produces; *no other ground* would these concepts have; the nobler mind generates them *out of itself*, and recognizes no higher law than its better impulse, its purer and loftier taste;—Or: As the artistic genius, through the impression of his works, gives to art its models and its law, so does the moral genius give them to freedom.”

[p. 36]

Hence what pleases him is right to him, “hence his contempt for public opinion, and for that Delphic oracular saying: Act according to the laws of your city; hence his stupid defiance, hence his arrogance, which brought him to a fall.”

“You trust Woldemar’s *beautiful soul*. Precisely that which you call so, I distrust in the highest degree; it seduces him, weakens him, drives him about on a boundless sea, has made him an enthusiast!—ah, a hapless, incurable fantast.”

II, 162 ff. 166 ff.

So, too, in the further course Woldemar’s moral genius really proved itself. For he foundered on his subjectivity, he foundered on man’s self-deification. But through this he also came to his senses. “He searched through every fold of his being, and soon discovered, with contrite shame, that even at the place where he had held himself wholly pure he could no longer hold himself pure. He shuddered before the abyss—at which he still stood: *before the depths of his heart*.”

So it comes to the confession: “Whoever relies on his heart is a fool!” And Henriette adds—how she knows it we are not told, but it cannot well derive from the dismissed heart—: *Trust in love! it takes everything, but it gives everything!*

II, 281 ff.

Thereby the self-confession concludes in Christian maxims, if, that is, what is really meant is the love that takes everything we ascribe to ourselves, and in return gives everything from above, out of grace.

Everywhere here Christianity comes to mind only in passing; morality maintains the dominion.

“You can easily imagine,” so Stolberg writes to Jacobi, “what I miss in your Woldemar, so rich in splendid passages: [p. 37] the warm, enlivening breath of Christianity. Why does the society of the intelligent, loving and excellent men, who think so deeply, who feel so purely, loftily and finely, confine itself to Aristotelian philosophy? Dear brother, if I put myself in Woldemar’s place, and my friends had wanted to attune me again to pure harmony through those beautiful passages from Aristotle, I should have applied to them the words of Horace:

*Hiscene versiculis speras tibi posse dolores*

*Atque aestus, curasque graves de pectore pelli.* [Do you hope that with these little verses the pains and passions and heavy cares can be driven from your breast?]

“*Wonder-working* religion is able to act upon passion too, be it to ennoble it, be it to extirpate it; but reason, and moreover reason in the mouth of others,

*What can she more than tell us, we are fools?*”

As Woldemar, so too thinks Allwill. “What is more reliable than the heart of the nobly born?”

p. 189.

“He could place no trust in a supreme force of will outside the human heart.”

pp. XIII, XIV.

And yet this very heart, in the further course, has more than one thing to object against itself.

“Boxes on the ear occur to me at once when I see people with *exalted* dispositions approach, who do not even have *upright* dispositions. And I am no better satisfied with them when they accompany even their *beautiful* dispositions with so-called beautiful actions, for to anyone who has a soft heart and some fire in the blood it is easier to do such things than to leave them undone. But avoiding *evil*—that requires *other powers*; there the *whole man* must pull himself together, exert himself, often to the point of [p. 38] annihilation, and in the end find that he had too little in the powers of his whole humanity. Once more! It is easy, very easy, to do all manner of good; and to do great things is always a pleasure; but to remain without sin, without misdeed—that is—oh how hard! But also, how far exalted above everything! What is the most wondrous tumbler against the unshakable one in the struggle?”

p. 205.

So near to the truth, and yet we remain outside! To avoid evil requires *other* powers, but which? The whole man must pull himself together, but where is this *whole man*?

And our moralist comes still nearer to the simple truth when he brands all self-righteousness as “base boasting.”

“Base boasting that your heart”—in its inner development left to itself—“beats ever freer and freer. It cannot beat freely so long as it has secrets of wickedness and shame to conceal; so long as it must shrink back before the gaze of the Blameless,—must suffocate in its blood before the breath of the Pure,—so that only your brow may remain white, when he designates things of darkness by their names, and you feel he is speaking of your deeds.”

p. 220 ff.

“We are a pitiable race,” says Biederthal, “a limping race,” he repeats in the face of Woldemar’s beautiful soul.

II, 208. 217.

But thus far, and no further. Now enough has also been done, and the beautiful soul holds out no longer against the merciless reproaches, “which you make against human nature, as if everything good were alien to it and only tormented it.”

[p. 39]

“Dear friend! Man cannot cast himself away so without having first cast away the whole of nature together with its Author. For both, God and nature, *insofar as they are anything for man*, must indeed be in man,—must be his own concept, his own feeling. Whence do you take the representations of a truth and wisdom, an existence and capacity, compared with which human truth and wisdom, human capacity and existence seem so contemptible to you?”

II, p. 212.

Here God becomes all at once again the subjective feeling of the same heart that has wickedness and shame to conceal, and all evil becomes—less good, human weakness. Here it would have been time to consider more closely the two neighbours and inhabitants of the human heart, *God and nature*, to distinguish them more determinately, and to distinguish again the nature that has proceeded from God from that which has come to be out of it. But it does not come so far; and so it is not hard, then, to get over evil itself.

“What man has sinned will now soon be atoned for.”

Repentance is the satisfaction, penance is the reconciliation itself, and thus man, without further ado, pronounces himself quits and free, after a few tears of repentance.

“It would be dreadful, on account of sin, to become mistrustful of all—*human*—virtue, and to want to guard oneself against its *peculiar* power, the freedom of the soul, as against an evil spirit.”

II, p. 239.

Thus self-righteousness is let in again by the back door. But still more closely and deeply is self-palliation [p. 40] grounded, when we infer from *being* to the goodness of being. How could evil *exist* in God’s order of the world? absolute evil? It is this question which at this stage of spirit opens a new path to convince oneself of man’s innate goodness, which ever anew repeats itself in the most various expressions and guises. To be sure, the preliminary question should first have been examined, whether being, actual being, in this sense really pertains to man at all. But there thinking breaks off at the very moment when, through the completion of the dialectic, it could perceive the positive element. Instead of this, non-knowing, which after all knows nothing, presupposes man’s actual being as immediately certain, as indeed the essence of this non-knowing consists generally in this, that it does not arrive at complete non-knowing, but always fends off the nothing, as the result of the dialectic.

A second question would be, to what extent the inference from original goodness to innate goodness might be logically correct, and whether the heart is nobly born for the reason that it is nobly created. But non-knowing does not enter into that. To have been created good in Adam it gladly concedes; but now it denies father and mother, it denies its descent, its lowly birth in sins, and boasts of being nobly born.

Thus Clara, too, blinds herself with complacency.

“That is it, that the Author of the world could create only after his image, and had to give to every being as much *truth* as he imparted *life* to it.”

It is well to note that the beautiful souls of that time not only believe and accept nothing of Bible and catechism, but in fact know nothing of them.

“We seem to be a breath, often only the shadow of a breath, or, as an old poet expressed himself, [p. 41] a shadow’s dream. But a being that were nothing but shadow, a being that were sheer dream, is a non-entity. *We are*, we live, and it is impossible that there should be a kind of life and existence that were not a kind of life and existence of the highest Being itself.”

But precisely at this critical point the conversation about evil, and about its incompatibility with the concept of being, was “interrupted by the message: *supper is served*,” and not taken up again.—How instructive is this interruption!

Allwill, pp. 132, 133.

Now all at once evil is transposed into the nature of the finite being, as the natural consequence of finitude; it is not really evil, but only less good. Here the philosophy of non-knowing indeed remains wholly consistent. If evil is still to be spoken of at all, then the finite is the evil, and this finite shows itself in cognition as non-knowing, in the will as sensuality. Both are just a lack, lack of divine knowing, which does not exclude human knowing, lack of divine goodness, which does not exclude human goodness. Both are a lack that lies in finitude itself. The copy is indeed not the archetype.

p. 245.

“That philosophical curiosity which is troubled and amazed about the existence and origin of the imperfect, the nugatory and the evil, should it not be permitted to be regarded as an *obscure* consciousness of the divine likeness in our reason?

“*No one is good but the One God.* Instead, then, of asking: Whence come the imperfect, the nugatory and the evil? we ought rather to reverse the question, and to marvel that *finite* [p. 42] creatures are capable of asking after truth, of commanding the good to themselves, and of laying claim to happiness?”

p. 132.

With one stroke of the pen we have come so far that we, forgetting our origin, need no longer wonder about evil, no longer be disturbed about it, but rather, mindful of our finitude, must wonder at the good in man and rejoice over it.

But here man betrays himself again, not merely this or that beautiful soul—here it comes closer to light where we are really lacking, and why even the cognition of sin alone becomes so hard for us. That the full cognition of sin becomes so hard for us lies precisely in sin itself, because we love ourselves too much, because self-cognition pains us, it hurts the heart, which wants to be beautiful. It torments also our natural faith in God, which believes it justifies God when it defends his creature, for why did he create creatures whose fall and misery he foresaw? Now it comes also to this, that we let evil, finite, imperfect, sensuous run together in motley fashion through and into one another. Still more turbid does non-knowing become, because, without further inquiry, by virtue of the immediacy of knowing, it presupposes abstract finitude and sensuality not only as actual, but also as created in us and original. It comes also to this, that we derive the good which we actually find in our heart also from the heart, but ascribe evil, at least the inclination to it, not to our freedom, however much we otherwise think of it, but to our finitude, our sensuality. As the fundamental prejudice insinuates itself and settles in, as though evil and good dwelt side by side in man and both came from man, at once all cognition [p. 43] is cut off and stunted, and the work of spirit crippled. Indeed we even find, in our very sin, good dispositions and our noble birth, because we do not consider that all evil is a dissembled, disfigured good, and hence imitates its semblance, only that it is inverted. And now we add the last thing, in order by no means to come out of the comfortable darkness about ourselves, by simply rejecting and reversing the question whence evil comes, and thereby rolling the guilt onto God, which we make good again by making evil somewhat better. And yet for those who have come to see that they know nothing out of themselves, nothing further was needed than that they let themselves be instructed by the Word of God. For if non-knowing were the *simplicity of heart and poverty of spirit* that is pronounced blessed in the Sermon on the Mount, if it really felt hunger and thirst after righteousness, then a single, earnest, clear glance into the third chapter of Genesis would suffice to come to an understanding about itself and the world.

But non-knowing indeed does not come to understanding, and so our friend Jacobi too stays closer to his Woldemar and Allwill, and to all the beautiful souls who feed on such excellences as on themselves, than he himself knew and wished, as he stood closer to the idealism and nihilism that he combated than he supposed. Sin is for him nothing but sensuality, sensuality in itself nothing but a necessary limit for this life, which itself must be held within limits, lest with titanic arrogance it touch the supersensible realm in the field of cognition and degenerate into the materialism of the Christian Church, and in the domain of morality lest it fall into individual vices, individual sins—as if sensuality and sin were eradicated by their remaining in ambush and not coming to open outbreak.

[p. 44]

Generally, however, abstraction plies its wretched mischief with this *sensuality*, which is itself the abstraction, in that it stops at this abstraction. Sensuality is the first and last thing that natural, that is, abstract man holds up at once, like a shield, when sin is put before him. In fact our friend is so thoroughly abstract that he now scolds sensuality, now praises it, because he cannot distinguish human and true, abstract and concrete sensuality; and Clara certainly speaks truly from his soul when she writes to Sylli,

Allwill, p. 112 ff.

that for her life beyond she gives up her whole sensuality, and on this side contends for her whole sensuality, “that one may leave it in honour.”

“I will by no means take hands and feet with me out of the world, and even decline wings, in case they should be offered me in their place.”

We would wish that she not be taken at her word. But here, for the earth, on this side, she is willing to have neither hands, nor feet, nor eyes torn out.

Whereby she, to be sure—but what does it harm him who has the truth in himself?—comes into conflict, regarding the *beyond*, regarding true sensuality, with the wondrous prophecy of the prophet Ezekiel, ch. 37, and regarding *this side*, regarding abstract sensuality, with the teaching of Christ (Matt. 5:29; Mark 9:45 ff.). And who would want to be so sensuous, so materialistic, as to stay with the letter?

What this innocent non-knowing, with its subjective faith, to which it ever again seeks to cling fast, may hold concerning continuance after death, is thereby already pronounced. For this world it will not let its flesh be taken from it, its abstract sensuality be touched,—because God has given it [p. 45] —but for that world it declines even the wings of the angels, into which perhaps the body of the soul might be transfigured, and become identical with it in concrete sensuality.

Hereby it accommodates itself wholly to the common representations of unchristian popular philosophy, in that the Christian revelation of the resurrection and transfiguration of the flesh is set aside as oriental, human mysticism, and is volatilized and emptied into an abstract immortality of the soul. A spiritless Gnosticism thinks to spiritualize the truth when it takes from it marrow and bone, Manichaean thoughtlessness tries to make it comprehensible when it deliberately withdraws from the soul the organism of the body, and with it the last point of support of the Concept, so that nothing at all may remain to be thought; and non-knowing is consistent when it joins itself to these representations, for so much is certain, that nothing can be known and nothing thought of a soul that has divested itself of its body, as the organ of its expression and the condition of its personality.—

But from all these abstractions a feeling nevertheless rescues, a dark feeling of concrete sensuality, which presses for reality with a compulsion that overpowers the negativity of the understanding. Thus, amid all the *individual* inconsistencies, the *universal* consistency preserves itself intact, which holds fast to the fundamental idea of *God’s* being and consciousness, vitality and personality, and of *man’s* immediate communion with God. This communion, as immediate, is without difficulty; it needs no mediation, because God, as the archetype, ideal, proceeds without more ado from the breast of man, as the copy. Yet this subjectivity of God, whereby he reveals himself to us, is not of such a kind that it could help us to a cognition of God; rather this archetype, *abstracted* from the copy, withdraws itself from the Concept for this very reason, that it rests on the [p. 46] abstract Concept of infinity and universality, and thereby forms the opposition to the abstract finitude and singularity of the copy.

Nevertheless God is—so subjective faith lets itself be heard, in accordance with the inner declaration of feeling and conscience, as of what is most certain—the absolute reality and objectivity of the Idea, outside us and above us, hence also above our concepts, which comprehend nothing, and let nothing count as real that is not finite.—

Here, too, it is to be seen how this non-knowing imagines that it knows and comprehends what is finite, from which it can again be inferred what it understands by knowing and comprehending.—

If our faith, so this faith itself confesses, on the indestructible basis of feeling and conscience, were not more than our understanding, we should have to deny, with this understanding of ours, personality to God, and with Him to ourselves.

Our *non-knowing* of God consists accordingly in the immediacy of our *knowing* of God, of which we can give ourselves no account, and to which we cling the more firmly the more mediation through the Concept opposes itself to it.

This knowing is certain as immediate, but as immediate also indeterminate; as indeterminate it presses all the more for reality, because it misses it; as indeterminate it is at the same time non-knowing, from which we set out.

That knowing, as immediate, is also indeterminate follows of itself, for both predicates are identical with one another. How would determinacy, determination, grasping be conceivable without mediation? And how would determination and mediation be admissible with respect to God, the Infinite, whom every determination, every mediation, every thought would render finite in an anthropopathic manner, who would render himself finite if he determined himself?

Hegel’s Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences, 2nd ed., pp. 73, 58.

[p. 47]

If, then, this non-knowing announces itself elsewhere as an absolute knowing springing *immediately* from human nature, and calls this knowing the most certain, by which precisely subjective faith is understood,

On Divine Things, p. 152.

Schelling’s Memorial, p. 108.

this apparent contradiction explains itself from the predicate that is attached to knowing, for as immediate knowing it is indeterminate; as immediate, indeterminate knowing, however, it is also identical with non-knowing, since all knowing presupposes mediation, determination, thought. The knowing that is conceded here relates to God’s existence, which I must *acknowledge* like any existent thing, even if I cannot cognize it; the knowing that is denied, to God’s incomprehensibility, which thought must not touch, for God calls out to it: “Do not come near; take off your shoes from your feet; for the place on which you stand is a holy land.”

This Word of God forbids man to approach with his own understanding. But in consequence he does not approach at all, and if afterwards he nevertheless does so, he comes up again in his own shoes, and burns—the shoes and the feet.

[p. 48]

### II.

### Absolute Knowing.

All activity of spirit proves itself as spirit precisely by this, that it has as its striving and its task to take up into itself the tranquil being opposed to it, and thereby to sublate the dualism that separates it from being, so as to be not at, but in the object. Both, striving and task, belong not to a particular faculty or part of spirit, which would be as it were independent of the remaining parts, but precisely to the entire activity of spirit. Hence it is also not to be laid to the charge of human spirit in and for itself, that is, of the spirit that reveals itself to man, but precisely of man himself in his abstract naturalness, of the fragmentation of spirit into individual directions and the arbitrary operation with isolated selfish powers, if the striving is in no way satisfied, the task in no way solved, whereby it finally comes to this, that being and knowing separate entirely, and the former, as the indestructible, is posited precisely in this, that it does not know and is not known.

From this alone many phenomena in philosophy are already explained, which therefore appears to consist of knowing and non-knowing, of non-being and being.

Even in Schelling’s speculative knowing, which had declared war on all non-knowing, a non-knowing has still remained behind, namely the non-knowing of the Absolute as the objective and unconscious, the non-knowing of the ground in God, as an insoluble remainder. On this non-knowing rests the symbolic knowing with which this philosophy ends in religion, or seemed to end.

Schelling’s Memorial, p. 192.

Schelling’s Philosophical Writings, I, 432.

Hinrichs’s Religion in Its Inner Relation to Science, 143, 145.

[p. 49]

In symbolic knowing the revelation, which is to unveil and sublate the mystery, is itself a mystery, whereby it turns into its opposite, in that absolute truth conceals and veils itself as itself behind the appearance, as its revelation. Revelation is just as much dissemblance. Herewith at the same time the limit of this philosophy, as *philosophy of nature*, is found, and the transition to the philosophy of *spirit* furnished. No less is herein also the meaning of the mystery and of mysticism indicated, at least in general. The mystery is on the one hand the disclosed, which not only discloses itself in itself, but also discloses itself to others, and initiates them, μυεῖν—; but on the other hand the closed, which not only closes itself in itself, but also closes the mouth of the epopt, and blinds the eyes, the invisible, the unutterable—μύειν—.

Now, however, the unutterable is to be sublated, in order to arrive at absolute knowing, wherein already lies the presupposition that nature in itself, or being, precisely because it cannot be known, or insofar as it cannot be known, is to be regarded as a shape untrue over against the self-knowing spirit.

What is now at stake is nothing less than to complete the identity of knowing with the known, of revelation with the revealed, at the expense of fixed being, to complete it so that knowing is the truth itself, and this cannot be thought outside the knowing of it.

Being is untrue and unactual, because it is unconscious; true and actual is only spirit, whereby finite and infinite depart of themselves from actuality. The difference of spirit and nature is therefore not to be grasped merely quantitatively, as if spirit were only a More as against nature, but rather [p. 50] qualitatively, in that spirit, as against the nature opposed to it, is the only true and actual.

Provisionally it seems that from this a correspondence as well as a difference falls out between non-knowing and absolute knowing. Both agree, namely, in this, that they ascribe to being an uncognizability; but they differ in this, that non-knowing ascribes actuality to this being, whereas absolute knowing denies to mere being, along with cognizability, actuality too. For non-knowing, being and non-knowing, for absolute knowing, non-being and non-knowing, are identical. If, further, non-knowing predicates being not merely as actual, but also as true, hence as rational, although inaccessible to reason, as conscious of itself, although not known, then this does not indeed lie in its logical consistency, but it is none the less peculiar to it, until it works itself wholly free from faith.

We heard earlier that non-knowing has much to say of an elevation above nature, but it could not itself rise above nature, and despite all appeal to the supernatural could not rescue itself out of naturalness. The elevation above nature would have transformed non-knowing into knowing, but it lies in the nature of non-knowing that it does not know what it means “to elevate oneself above nature.” Even the philosophy of nature does not, as such, yet raise itself wholly above nature; this is reserved for the philosophy of spirit.

Herewith the transition from non-knowing to knowing is at least indicated; and if it is now no longer so much our concern to understand the points of contact and the differences which result, step by step, between absolute knowing and non-knowing, of which the former, the points of contact, hold fast the truth in error, the latter, the differences, the prevailing error, if it rather matters to us that we get to know more closely the results of absolute [p. 51] knowing and thereby of itself its relation to Christianity, then it is advisable, at the very least, to let pass before us individual utterances of this philosophy, if possible the most captious and dangerous, or the most decried. That here too, where a whole, a closed system lies before us, just as before, where only fragments of an agitated soul met us, we hold in the first place to *singularities*, to notable passages, to the word and the letter, cannot be held against us, for the whole must be found in the singular, if only we otherwise succeed in becoming conscious of the connection and learning to understand the letter. This method, however, also has the good point that it holds us to determinate concepts, and with determinateness promises the greatest possible intelligibility.

Here the question renews itself in the first place:

#### What is God?

to which we would only too gladly receive a ready, determinate answer. For so long as we do not know God, we know nothing at all, for what is there outside God and without God? So long, however, as God confronts the subject only as object, hence only in nature, he cannot be cognized, for it lies in the *nature of the objective* that it stands opposed to knowing, to the subject, and impenetrably resists.

*“According to this insight, which must justify itself through the exposition of the system itself, everything therefore depends on grasping and expressing the true not as substance, but just as much as subject;”*

Phenomenology of Spirit, p. XX. [p. 52] *“and this subject is just as much the substance.”*

p. 759.

In this principle there seems to lie something twofold, namely first the harmless point, that substance is not selfless, the self not without substance, without objectivity, and secondly the captious point, that the self of the known is identical with the self of the knower. The first is clear and unambiguous, and not merely in agreement with Scripture, but drawn first of all from Scripture. The second is questionable and requires an explanation, which has already been indicated. For actual cognizing it is required that the object cease to be an other than the intuiting, but also remain just as much object, the former because every difference between the intuiting and its object alters the truth, the latter because every cognition necessarily presupposes an object, since even self-cognition requires nothing less than that the self detach itself from itself in order to become object to itself.

Herewith the difference between outer and inner, objective and subjective, is just as much recognized as sublated; and if, according to Jacobi, outwardly and objectively revelation is not thinkable, but rather the truth becomes man in us ourselves,

On Divine Things, p. 55.

so, according to the above principle, conversely the truth does indeed come from without, as substance, to us, yet so that it must also be in us and be taken up inwardly, in order to become identical with knowing. If, then, there the subject everywhere has sat for the depiction of the object, the human spirit for the portrait of the world-spirit, the untrue one for the image of truth, the idol for the image of God, so here it is reversed, where substance is the first and the subject approaches only with self-emptying, to listen, and not to join in speaking.

[p. 53]

“That the *true* is *actual* only as system, or that substance is essentially subject, is expressed in the representation that pronounces the Absolute as *Spirit*,—the most sublime Concept, and the one that belongs to the more recent time and its religion. *The spiritual* alone is the *actual*; it is the essence, or that which has being *in itself*,—that which relates itself, or the determinate, *other-being* and *being-for-self*—and in this determinateness, or its being-outside-itself, abiding in itself; or it is *in and for itself*.”

Phenomenology, XXVIII ff.

Here the identity of substance with the subject is more closely designated as actuality, as being-in-and-for-itself, so that this being-in-and-for-itself proves itself first as in itself, as simple substance, then as divided substance, insofar as it splinters into object and subject, but finally as itself, namely as being-in-and-for-itself, whereby the immanence of things in the subject, as their truth, is pronounced. Truth becomes actual only as system, that is, it becomes actual only as unity, as immanent unity, for system is the whole that supports itself, and does not know itself outside its content. Thus “the true is the whole. The whole, however, is only the essence completing itself through its development.”

Phenom. XXIII.

“*Truth* is not only in itself completely equal to *certainty*, but also has the *shape* of the certainty of itself, or it is in its existence, that is to say, for the knowing spirit in the *form* of the knowing of itself. Truth is the *content*, which in religion is still unequal to its certainty. This equality, however, consists in the content’s receiving the shape of the *self*.”

p. 753.

[p. 54]

So long as God, as the absolute truth, appears to the subject, as to representation, still as an Other than itself, as an object, hence more or less as an idol, that is, as a God separated from the subject, so long is God not yet cognized as He Himself, for the Concept of God is still outside God, the latter, as the represented, on the far side, the former on this. Only to Christianity is the sublation of this partition-wall, whereby God becomes God, to be thanked, which Christian science has to express as the identity of the content with the form, that is, of the truth with knowing.

In the religion of Spirit, as the *revelation*, the absolute Spirit, as the content, or object of truth, has become manifest. In the science of this religion, as the *self-manifestation* of the absolute Spirit, this content has become identical with the form of knowing, so that this manifestation is the absolute proof of the absolute Spirit by itself as the absolute truth.

#### Hinrichs, *Religion in Its Inner Relation to Science*, p. 263.

The truth lies revealed in the written Word of God, as the content or material; but this content receives the form of truth only with the feast of Pentecost, which each individual has to experience.

“The object of religion is indeed for itself the infinite object, which is to comprise everything within itself; but its representations do not remain true to themselves, in that the world, too, remains self-subsistent for it outside the Infinite, and what it gives out as the highest truth is at the same time to be unfathomable, a mystery, and uncognizable, a given, and *to remain only in the form of a given and external thing for the distinguishing consciousness*. In it the true is in feeling, intuition, in presentiment, in representation, in devotion in general, *also interwoven with thoughts*, but the truth is [p. 55] not in the *form of truth*.—Philosophy, on the other hand, can be regarded as the science of *freedom*; for since in it the foreignness of the objects, and with it the finiteness of consciousness, vanishes, *in it alone* contingency, natural necessity, and the relation to an externality in general, and hence dependence, longing and fear, fall away; only in philosophy is reason throughout *by itself*.—For the same reason reason in this science also has not the one-sidedness of a subjective rationality, as if it were the property of a peculiar talent or the gift of a special divine fortune—or misfortune—but since it is nothing but reason in the consciousness of itself, it is by its nature capable of being universal science. Nor is it *idealism*, in which the content of knowing has only the determination of a product posited by the I, a subjective product enclosed within self-consciousness; because reason is conscious of itself as of being, the subjectivity of the I, which knows itself as a particular over against the objects, and its determinations as within it and distinct from what is other, found outside and above it, is sublated and sunk into rational universality.”

#### *Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences*, p. 7 ff., 1st ed.

In all these statements the same is repeated, though in different stages and relations, namely, that substance is just as much subject, but the subject just as much substance, and that thereby reason proves itself as absolute reality and science in the immanence of the Concept.

Religion and philosophy are distinguished as transcendence and immanence, hence as dependence and freedom, except that immanence and freedom do not exclude that transcendence and dependence. Here [p. 56] it comes, moreover, merely to this, that the difference between transcendence and immanence be grasped and recognized, and that the latter also be conceded to man; for whether really all religion, or only the beginning of childlike trust in the *given* Word of God, falls into the sphere here outlined, whether living, experienced faith, Pentecostal faith, which develops out of the obedience of ecclesiastical faith, may be charged with transcendence, is a dispute about words, for it is recognized here too, but designated with another name, as philosophy.

Now, however, all at once the most various voices rise up together and through one another, for it and against it.

Some cry out loudly: There is the prophecy fulfilled all at once: “Everything is a ghost! At this time the bitter sweat of earnestness will be dried from every brow; wiped away from every eye the tear of longing; there will be nothing but laughter among men.” O how much nearer to true faith in God lies Jacobi’s struggling and striving than this dissolution of all things into nothing. *Phenomenology* is the *doctrine of ghosts*; ghost is the non-being out of which it strives, ghost is the being and actuality of Spirit to which it brings it. Of ghosts, to be sure, the philosopher need have no fear, they are idols without content; but religion works out blessedness with fear and trembling, because it is referred to the reality of God.—

Others add: Is that not true self-deification, since God becomes actual only as subject and the truth is not without itself, as if it is identical with my knowing and is resolved purely in my knowing without any remainder?

How shallow is this wisdom, cries another, which in a single paltry formula does not so much explain as dissolve the wonder of being, the supreme wonder of divine being, in that under great [p. 57] pomp from the plain Concept of appropriation, as the requirement of all living cognition, abolishes the eternal difference between subject and object, and then plays with it again, like the cat with the mouse, so that it both is and is not. The wisdom is so shallow and airy that it fills the stomach and yet cannot still the hunger, the real hunger.

Others reflect, and seek in Holy Scripture the standard by which this philosophy is to be judged, and now they ask whether this is not in agreement with that. For if it is promised us that God himself will make his dwelling in us, if the body of man is called a temple of the Spirit of God, then nothing else is thereby said than that God glorifies himself in man, in the subject. Is, then, the individual God for that reason, because universal being reveals itself to him as universal knowing, not as a strange rigid God, but as Spirit?

Still others come forward and ask what, then, is actually meant by this? Are not these universal pronouncements capable of any interpretation one pleases? And why the Pythian language, unless something is to be hidden behind it that one does not wish to confess honestly? Or does God really remain subsisting for himself? No, “the God of this philosophy is the universe,” and not different from the universe.

Others add: Are we, then, willing and permitted to pick out that interpretation which identifies this system with the crudest pantheism? Or have we not learned precisely from this system that dialectic, indeed, at every stage of Spirit dissolves and volatilizes reality, whereas speculation in the negation always holds fast once more to the positive element?

But we must go on step by step, in order to hear the system about itself, and to learn to understand it out of itself.

We set out from the substantiality of the absolute essence, which sets the *singular* self into [p. 58] accidentality, indeed swallows up in its abyss. The Absolute is only equal to itself. But the dialectic drives us, through the most various relations between both, to the dreadful frivolity of the comedy, which pronounces the proposition: “*The self is the absolute essence*,” whereby the starting-point is inverted, in that “the essence, which was previously substance and in which the self was accidentality, has sunk down to a predicate, so that now Spirit in this self-consciousness, to which nothing in the form of essence stands opposed, has lost its consciousness,” in that it is its own God; the subject is everything. On the other hand, however, in the opposite side too the subject is not overcome, or overcome only with the loss of substance and of all essentiality, and thus with the consciousness of the loss also of the knowing of itself, of substance and of the self.

Precisely in this, however, speculation recognizes in both moments their one-sidedness and untruth, and in the absolute essence itself “the union and interpenetration of *both natures*, in which both”—not as predicates with an inequality of value opposed to the essence, but—“are with equal value just as essentially moments, and thus Spirit, which is just as much *consciousness* of itself, as of its objective substance, as it is simple *self-consciousness* remaining in itself.”

Only now do we learn how we come to the cognition of God, who at first confronted us only as substance, *hence unknown*, and what was meant when the subjectivity of substance and the substantiality of the subject were demanded. God becomes essentially *God-man*, and in the God-man, only in Him, do we cognize God; in the God-man is the fullness of the Godhead—not hidden, but manifest. Thereby God is cognized and transfigured *as Spirit*.

“*Spirit is this: not to be meaning, not the inner, but to* [p. 59] be the actual. The simple eternal essence would therefore be Spirit only in the empty word, if it remained with the representation and the expression of the eternal essence.”

As essence it is a mere abstraction; but it is in fact just as much in itself, substance, as for itself, subject, and the identity of subject and object. As such it is concrete, actual, and indeed actual by becoming a singular, and in this singularity the whole fullness of substantiality passes over.

#### *Phenomenology*, pp. 718, 719.

But as this singular it also dies again.

“The death of the Mediator is not merely the death of his natural soul, or of his particular being-for-self; it is not only the already dead shell, drawn off from the essence, that dies, but also the abstraction of the divine essence.”

p. 737.

Thus the Spirit of Christ becomes the universal self-consciousness, in which He has promised to be with us daily.

But do we hear in this anything other than what has already often been said? Not God in general, not God as world-being is the highest, but *this God* in actuality! But neither is this Christ the highest, but he is so only as a sublated This, in that he casts away his actuality, and thus in fact presents himself as a universal. In that He thereby dies according to his subjective and objective side, according to soul and body, he is in this self-emptying and sublation of Himself actually sublated, that is, preserved, for Spirit remains, as absolute, that is, in the universal there always remains substance and accident. And what, then, is this Christ otherwise than the God become flesh in every individual; and what is the universal and common Spirit otherwise than humanity, which proceeds [p. 60] from the Father and the Son, and first becomes actual in this humanity itself?——

Yet let us first hear how the system of absolute knowing may answer for itself to these complaints.

According to the philosophy of nature that immediately preceded this philosophy, Christ is the summit of the appearance of God in the finite, but only symbolically.

#### Schelling’s *Lectures on the Method of Academic Study*, p. 185.

As such an appearance He is not God Himself, but the symbol of God, in that He only indicates Him, hence properly not symbolic, but allegorical.

#### Hinrichs, *Religion*, etc., pp. 144, 145.

“But that the highest essence is seen, heard, felt as an existent self-consciousness, this is in fact the completion of its Concept, and through this completion the essence is as immediately there as it is essence.”

#### *Phenomenology*, p. 712.

Hereby Christ is cognized as God himself, as *this* man who is God Himself, and beside Him no Other.

#### Hinrichs, *Aesthetic Lectures on Goethe’s Faust*, pp. 81, 85.

“He is not merely a singular self-consciousness, *like every other man also*, but as the same, the universal spiritual consciousness of the world, or its Spirit, which in its unity with the divine Spirit is the revelation of itself as all truth, *is the universal Spirit of humanity itself in its unity with the divine spiritual essence*. For the true Concept of this revelation [p. 61] is the knowing of truth, and therefore the pure knowing and the truth itself.”

#### Ibid.

The Spirit of humanity as such, that is, as not fallen, or as raised up again from the Fall, is surely not one peculiar to it, but precisely God’s Spirit; and God’s Spirit is not only the universal Spirit of humanity, but the universal Spirit of humanity in its unity with the divine essence.

But we cannot conceal from ourselves that, nevertheless, many shake their heads, for they fear that in spite of all these assurances and turns of phrase their Christ, who called himself the way and the truth and the life, will be borne to the grave for a second time, as This Christ. We answer them: He calls Himself, indeed, the vine, and us the branches. Is He, then, not now, as this vine, the universal Spirit, who lives in his branches until the end of the days? We repeat once more that we mean this Christ, the actual Christ, through whom God revealed himself in the Old Testament, and became man in the New. It is no accommodation, but the truth itself, when by the *sublated* This we understand the Crucified, the Dead, the Risen, and him who ascended into heaven. But they do not trust us, all the same, and we would not trust ourselves, if we believed that thereby the personality of God, our Saviour, and his self-consciousness outside his branches were endangered.

Therefore we now gather together the statements of absolute knowing, in order to translate them into our language, even if the reproach should strike us that we thereby descend into the manner of representation, and thereby the truth is precisely represented, hence altered.

We believe we are speaking in the sense of that philosophy when we preface that the *represented* [p. 62] truth is not altered by the elevation to the immanent Concept, for if, as represented [*vorgestellte*], it is at the same time predicated as dissembled [*verstellte*], the predicate does not refer to a change of its content or of its reality, but to a change of its position [*Stellung*], that is, of its relation to me, in that this relation becomes from a transcendent one an immanent one, and the content from a represented one an understood one. The truth was, as represented, not an other than itself, but an other than me, and as such it was an other than itself, and not itself, only insofar as it, as the universal, could not have the Other, namely intuiting, outside itself.

Immanence, however, does not consist in this, that the singular subject, as such, gets the condition of itself into its power and has the life in itself, but in this, that knowing is grasped as universal knowing, as universal subject, and thus as identical with universal being, the universal object, and the singular subject becomes conscious of its communion with this living universality and of itself in it. If, therefore, Scripture teaches that man cannot attain to God and to the cognition of God from himself, from his subjectivity separated from God, it proves itself as that knowing which says nothing other than that man can attain to God, as the universal truth, only through God, as the universal knowing.

If knowing, as the universal subject, is not identical with the truth, as the universal object, and the former does not become equal to the latter, then knowing is something other than the known, in that the form does not become equal to its content, and the Concept has its object outside itself, instead of the true Concept having to have its object in itself and itself in its object, having to become immanent, if it is to be a true Concept, that is, a Concept identical with the truth.

[p. 63]

Hereby, however, it is not said that the *singular* subject, nor that subjective knowing, can be equal to God, and must be equal to God, in order to cognize God, but rather that it can never be God, yet, in order to be in God and to cognize God, must give up its subjectivity, which separates it from God, in order to arrive at the universal knowing, which is God.

God Himself is not merely eternal being, but also the knowing of Himself, and indeed, as being in Himself, the knowing of Himself in Himself, God’s self-consciousness, and as being in the Other, the knowing of Himself outside Himself, God’s consciousness in the world, in the singular beings, as creatures of God, whereby this being-outside-itself is then again sublated, that is, just as much dissolved, in that the singular beings are in God, are in God according to their being and knowing, as preserved, in that they are not God himself, but rather only God is He Himself in Himself. If God actually *is* in and with his creatures, which Scripture teaches, then the knowing of God is also in them,—because he only is in that he knows himself,—and this knowing of God in man is precisely universal reason, which is not my reason, nor yet a common or universal faculty, but being itself, the identity of being and knowing.

All knowing of itself can be regarded as being in itself, all knowing of the Other as being in the Other, as living transposition into the Other of the Other, that is, into the Other of the subject, which is thus the Other of the Other.

#### Hegel’s *Encyclopaedia*, 1st ed., §. 188.

#### Hegel’s *Subjective Logic*, p. 388.

God knows Himself only in that He is in Himself. God knows the world, humanity, only insofar as he is in it, or, if it has not remained in Him, transposes Himself, for His part, into it. Man knows God only [p. 64] insofar as he is in Him, or, if he has fallen away, is transposed into Him again. But man can be in God only through God, and, when he has once ceased to be so, can be transposed into God only through God, and indeed only insofar as God has first transposed himself into him and himself become man and revealed himself to him. Only in this revelation, only in Jesus Christ, does man cognize God, and he has no name in which he is to worship God but the name of the Son of Man.

Just as God now transposes himself through the living thought, which is thus the deed, into humanity, in order to know it, in that He is it, and transposes himself not merely into humanity in general, but becomes flesh as a *singular man in a determinate time and in a determinate place*, in order to know this fate of man, to be individuated, in that he bears it, so man too is posited, or transposed, into God only through God, in order to know God, but not in that he is God himself, but in that he is in God.

Only through God can you be in God, and only in God cognize God, yet so that He has first known you, and as man has revealed Himself to man. Only through God can you also come again to Him; every other thought, of being or becoming something from oneself, belongs already to sin.

But here an objection confronts us of itself, which we may not evade, because by means of its dazzling surface it lays claim to thoroughness. The being of God was to be identical with the knowing of God. That was the starting-point that we had to explain, and the explanation remained true to it also *in relation to God*. But *in relation to man* there enters all at once, so runs the accusation, in place of plain explanation, quite softly and imperceptibly, a very essential alteration in the words, for now the knowing of God or God-knowing is no longer set equal to the being of God, but to the being [p. 65] in God. This, however, is no interpretation, but a reinterpretation of the foreign doctrine, whereby this doctrine falls into discord with itself, because, if consistently adhered to, it would prove too much, namely nothing less than the divinity of man.

This objection seems to be consistent, in that it presses for consistency and tries to demonstrate inconsistency; it must be examined more closely, so that it may learn to understand itself.

The being of God and the knowing of God are One, that is, God *is* actual only insofar as He knows Himself: with His consciousness His existence arises and vanishes. *That was the first*, the relation of being and knowing to God, as the absolute object, which is itself absolute subject; and with this Scripture agrees.

As the knowing of God in Himself is One with the being of God in Himself, so also knowing and being of God in me are One, that is, God is actual in me only insofar as he *actually* comes to consciousness in me, only insofar as the Spirit of God searches and knows me, and thereby awakens me myself to consciousness. The being and knowing of God in me contains, therefore, not merely the cognition which God has of me, but also the cognition which I have of Him, and which can be more or less clouded by the I, the more or less it steps out of the identity with God’s cognition of me. *That was the second*, the relation of being and knowing to man,—substance is just as much subject,—for which again the promise speaks.

Insofar as God, the *actual*, objective God, is in me and comes to consciousness in me, the universal in the particular, insofar am I also in Him, the particular in the universal, because God by this inward turning does not cease to be the objective God, the universal Spirit, does not become I. To that extent the [p. 66] being in God conditions the cognition of God, because I cognize him only insofar as I am in Him. Were He merely in me, He would not be cognized according to His substantiality and thereby according to His own most proper essence, for His essence is also this, to be outside me and above me.

Since He is now also outside me and above me, He would, at least in this His essential relation, be wholly removed from my cognition, if I were not in Him. Consequently I arrive at an objective cognition of God only when I am just as much in Him as He is in me, is in my consciousness. *That was the consequence of the second*, whereby the Concept proves itself immanent,—substance is and remains just as much substance,—with which the teaching of Scripture on communion with God through reconciliation in Christ agrees.

Here, however, is precisely where our explanation is accused of an infidelity, namely, of a deviation from the Concept that was to be explained, for this Concept presupposes not merely immanence, but identity. This identity lies immediately in the Concept as set up, whereby substance is made the predicate of the subject. Thus the identity of being and knowing leads immediately to self-deification, which is indeed avoided by that turn, but avoided at the expense of consistency. Philosophy remains true to itself when it sets the subject as the predicate of substance, or rather wholly equal to it, that is, in that it cognizes God himself in Christ, but it does not remain true to itself if it now does not also, conversely, want to make substance equal to the subject.

Consistency brings with it, that is the second and positive side of the objection, that I myself become God, in that I know God, because the being and knowing of God is identical, that is, so inseparably belongs together that the one cannot be thought without the other. *To know God is to be God.* If God, in that [p. 67] he knows man, is himself man, then also man, in that he knows God, is God himself; that is the inevitable consequence of absolute knowing, which it may not conceal from itself. And in this consists the accusation against the consistency of the foreign doctrine, and against the inconsistency of our explanation.

We could answer, as far as the system is concerned, with this,—it is also already contained in the above,—that substance is *just as much* subject, but also *not more* subject than substance, or, in other words of the same system, that identity does not exclude difference. But to this it would be answered us that this realistic idealism is precisely true pantheism, namely the loose doctrine that God,—the Absolute,—comes to existence and to consciousness in man,—the particular,—but also, held fast as this particular, that is, in this determination (*determinatio, negatio*), is just as much different from the Absolute.

More specifically, we could reply that in the accusation another Concept of being and knowing has been put in its place, namely the abstract Concept, rigid in itself, whereas in the contested presentation the real Concept is taken as the ground, which proves itself real and living in that being, like knowing, each of them, is not merely identity with itself, but overcomes the rigidity of it, in that it just as much has the difference in itself. Or, to approach the common understanding still more closely, we could point out that being and knowing, when it is posited as predicate, that is, in its identity with the subject, is different according to the difference of the subject itself, hence is one thing if God, another if I, this I, is the subject. But we should then still remain in an abstract formalism of the system, which has rather to show itself as system by stepping out of itself and cognizing itself in the Other, whereby [p. 68] it overcomes the last abstraction, and shows itself as self-emptying, as love, in that it does not remain shut up in itself, but precisely to that moment which opposes it, for its part does not oppose itself, but transposes itself into it.

We must therefore go more closely into the above objection of the sensuously abstract understanding, and indeed in such a way that we approach it with love, because it does not wish to approach us. We must consequently, first, give it an account of the consistency of our explanation, in that it is asked whether the consistency of the system is preserved in it; but we must also, second, call it itself to account, whether it for its part has proceeded consistently and faithfully in the accusation of self-deification.

First of all it must in any case be maintained that in our equation no term be altered without the other, and if one suffers an alteration, the same befalls the other also. In order to approach the accuser, let us translate our Concept and its further movement into mathematical equations:

Knowing = Being

Knowing of God = Being of God

Knowing of God in himself = Being of God in himself

Knowing of God in me = Being of God in me

Knowing of myself in God = Being of myself in God.

Concerning the third and fourth equations it is to be remarked that here already in advance not only the difference of being, but also the difference of knowing in relation to God and to man comes to light. As it is not the same whether and how God is in himself and in me, so it is also not the same whether and how he comes to consciousness in himself and in me.

In the last equation the subject of being and knowing alters, so that with its subject the whole judgment becomes imperfect, insufficient, and [p. 69] thereby demands a third term or element, which, if it is equal to one term of the equation, must be equal to the other also. For in the last judgment it is stated that I am in God only insofar as I know myself in Him, and know myself in Him only insofar as I am in Him, so that the one term is dependent on the other and both exist only together. Now, however, the truth that I know myself in God is again dependent on the presupposition that I know God himself, and nothing is more certain than that I cannot cognize myself, still less know myself in God, if I do not know God himself. To know oneself in God and God-knowing are thus so connected with one another, and set equal to one another, that the one is absorbed in the other, whereby the identity of both propositions is stated. For God I know only insofar as I know myself in God, and in God I know myself only insofar as I know God. But if both are identical, then knowing God and being in God are also identical, because being in God and knowing oneself in God are identical. Who is able to separate these three terms: being in God, knowing oneself in God, knowing God, from their inseparable connection? Hereby it is then proved that, in relation to man, knowing God does not presuppose being God, but being in God.

Our procedure seems hereby justified; it remains for us to examine the procedure of the sensuous understanding, which it observes in that it infers self-deification from the principle of identity. In thinking as ponderous, that is, sluggish, as it is frivolous, it passes summarily from the judgment:

Knowing of God = Being of God

to the conclusion:

therefore knowing God [*Gott wissen*] = being God [*Gott seyn*]

and from there to the final result:

If I assert that I know God, I must assert that I am God himself.

[p. 70]

Here the understanding is now to be convicted, according to its own logic, that in the very midst of the undertaking to set others right it goes astray step by step and comes to a fall.

In the first conclusion the first term has been altered, and the second term has remained unaltered, whereby that gross incongruity is occasioned, which nevertheless is ascribed not to its own fault but to the opponent. The alteration consists in this, that in the first term God suddenly enters the accusative, whereas in the second he remains in the nominative. We should have nothing to remark against the alteration of the first term if the consequent kept pace with it, that is, if the conclusion were drawn:

Knowing God [*Gott wissen*] = having God [*Gott haben*],

whose two sides are as equal to each other as not knowing God and seeking God.

Hereby the absolute object of knowing and being would also in the second term of the equation, following the lead of the first term, be put in the accusative. Now, to be sure, a further alteration has entered, which in the first term at least does not so visibly come forward, in that *being* has been changed into *having*, whereas knowing has not altered. Yet this transformation has taken place without our doing; having has substituted itself for being,—I speak in human fashion,—because being rejects every accusative from itself. Having is also a being, but a being with the accusative, a being that is not itself what it has, hence no absolute being, no being-in-itself that has the life in itself, but a conditioned being, being in the Other, in the absolute object, which conditions it. The Concept: *having God* excludes of itself *being God*, and includes *being in God* together with *the being of God in us*. He who has God is not God himself, but he must, in order nevertheless to be, be conditioned, be in Him, for if you were separated from Him, He would not be *your* God. The property [p. 71] that we ascribe to ourselves in Him, we *have* not merely by His being in us, but, in order that He may remain He himself, first by our being in Him. *Having God* is therefore *being in God*, whereby then being again enters into the term of the equation, and hereby our transformation, challenged in the above objection as surreptitious, is also justified from the same side which convicts the objection of its own inconsistency. Strictly speaking, then, man is not at all, but he has merely being<sup>∗)</sup>; he also does not know, but he has merely knowing. *Strictly speaking*, non-knowing probably meant to say the same.

But we go further, for it turns out that the transition also from that first conclusion: *knowing God = being God*, to the final result: *I = God*, is surreptitious. For in *God-knowing* it was still undetermined who could know Him, whether it might not be God Himself, but now I come in, this I, by which I *mean* myself, although I *say* the universal.

∗) The difference between the being and cognizing that belong to God, and that which belongs to man, is expressed in this, that God is and cognizes in Himself, but man is and cognizes not in himself, but in God, or, that God is and cognizes, whereas man neither is nor cognizes, but only has being and cognition, and indeed from being and cognizing, that is, from God. If, accordingly, in our equation the one term: “*to cognize God*” is more closely determined and rectified as: “*to have the cognition of God*,” that is, to have from Him, and through Him, and in Him the cognition which He is from Himself, through Himself, and in Himself, then the other term also determines itself accordingly of itself, as: “*to have the being of God*,” that is, to have from Him, through Him, and in Him the being which He *is* from Himself, through Himself, and in Himself. But a different being is *being in itself*, a different *being in Him*; a different thing is *to be* being, a different thing *to have* being. In this way, in the further development of the equation and in its application to man, with cognizing *being* also falls away—for cognizing is also being—whereas *having* passes over to both sides.

[p. 72]

In fact the premise: *I know God*, as it stands there and is *meant*, is as skewed as the conclusion: *Therefore I am God*, is absurd. The premise is, namely, a ὕστερον πρότερον, in that I, as subject, am, if not presupposed to God, yet to the predicate “knowing God,” since I must first know God before I can know myself, and must first know myself before I can be, in that I can become conscious of myself only in God, indeed God must first of all know me, before I can know God and myself in God. Nevertheless I stand there already complete, before it comes to God. More specifically, however, the premise has the inappropriate and awkward feature that I stand in the nominative over against God, and He in the accusative, as if I, as such, simply, could know God, or asserted that I know God, which is wholly foreign to absolute knowing, and first comes to speech in the sphere of the understanding through a complete inversion of the Concepts. It is rather perfectly in accordance with absolute knowing that I, as this I, whom the sensuous understanding means, cannot know God, hence know God only as a sublated This, that is, *negatively* through self-emptying, *positively* through God, or, in other words, know God only insofar as I am in God, thus not this I for myself. That I can know only that in which I am, and that, in order to be in an Other, in an object, the object, as Other, and I, as the Other, must be sublated, was indeed stated in advance at the very beginning.

But what deceives and blinds most is this, that in the domain of the understanding the speculative Ideas imperceptibly receive a quite different sense. All the more, however, is it to be expected that speculation, as it steps out of its aloofness, will grow strong precisely in daily intercourse and struggle with *this* understanding, and through practice, through experience itself, will gain in determinateness of concepts.

In what has gone before, however, the objection, as though knowing God dissolved the objectivity of God, or presupposed this dissolution, has at the same time been completely refuted, at least for those who do not merely read but also think along with us, and develop what has been said further by their own activity. If, to be sure, our dear readers are unwilling to put themselves in our place, it avails us nothing that we put ourselves in theirs. But we wish to accompany them also a few steps in this further development.

When we say that God is not the *Unutterable* but the *uttered* God, the Word Itself, and in the Word He Himself—or, more precisely, when we assert that God can be cognized—then thereby his personality, his being in and for himself, is rather already uttered, indeed presupposed. What is not actual, not actual in itself, what is not in and for itself, cannot be cognized either. What merely is in itself, existence without consciousness, is non-actual, irrational, uncognizable. Only the actual comes to cognition. In denying to yourself the cognition of God, indeed the possibility of this cognition, you have already denied to him his being in and for himself. In that the cognition of God, as you maintain, sublates the objective being of God, the mere being, it has sublated only that which God is not, what in general is not [p. 75] is, in that cognition first finds itself transposed, through this sublation, from non-actuality into actuality.

Far from the absolute object of being and knowing being altered in its self-subsistent essence by the fact that it is known, it rather remains precisely thereby itself, object, substance. For it is not merely, as the universal *knowing* in *me*, thus content of all knowing, but also, as *known*, *object*, as object, self-consciousness *in Himself*, consequently—to speak in human fashion—*outside me, above me*, hence at first represented, but then so represented that I am in Him, that is, known. As knowing, God is in me,—but with that His being-for-Himself seems endangered—; so He is, as represented, outside me, but, because He nevertheless comes to representation for me, known, that is, so represented that I am in Him,—with that His objectivity is preserved.—

That I am in God, this excludes so little the opposite, that God is in me, that rather this first becomes possible through that, just as a beloved being first is in me, lives and works in my heart, by my loving it, that is, by my transposing myself into it; I must live in it, so that it may live in me, and its life, thinking, willing may become mine.—

That I know God outside me, as object, that I know Him in me, as subject, falls to theoretical and to practical cognition respectively; neither is without the other.

As a mere being, not actual for itself, God cannot actually be cognized. This proves itself in *the revelation of God in nature*. The revelation in *nature* conceals God, in that he is not himself the appearance, but hides behind his revelation, and thereby utters in the revelation the highest irony. In the *world* God appears only as an essence that outside this and that [p. 76] appearance is not. But God is not the essence dependent on this and that appearance, so that essence and its appearance would be two different things, and with the appearance the essence also would vanish, and consequently could not be cognized; rather He is in Himself the appearance of His essence, He is in His Word He Himself, that is, He is actual; and this is the *revelation in His Word*. Thus God becomes *manifest* to me in the Word, provided only that I am *open*; in the Word I know the actuality of God, provided only that I myself am actual, or, since two actualities, as absolute, are not conceivable, provided that I know myself within this actuality. The only question, then, is to what extent I must not know myself outside this truth and actuality, and consequently myself as untrue and non-actual, hence also as not knowing—because separated from God as knowing—; the question is whether I am really in God. That is: the question is:

#### What is Man?

The connection between theology and anthropology has already been indicated above. One question drives on to the other, one answer is conditioned by the other, for each explains itself reciprocally from the other, and neither completely without the other.

For this reason, too, the question how the cognition of God is possible, and what God is, has been answered only formally, but not according to its content. For up to now we have learned nothing except that, and how, God comes to consciousness for us, that and how in Him, as in the Spirit that is in and for itself, all beings are, from which it follows that these beings, as His creatures, cognize Him. Nevertheless we find man, like all visible beings, without this cognition of God; indeed man comes in part to the conscious conviction of the incomprehensibility and uncognizability of God.

[p. 77]

Already above, therefore, the questions of theology and anthropology were joined together, and resolved into *one question*, into the question: *How does man arrive at God.* Why the question in that station had to receive precisely this position, is here to be seen in full light: the ground lies in the fact that non-knowing by its nature proceeded from the subject. From this it is at the same time explained how the same question must shape itself here, for since absolute knowing proceeds from the Absolute, hence from the substance, as the objective Word of God, there can also be no talk of the subject as the first. Here, then, the question is not how man arrives at God, but *how God arrives at man.*

So much is clear even now, that the first question had to draw the disaster of its consequences after it already through its position alone, for man out of himself really cannot arrive at God, and of a *How*, hence of a means, there could in any case be no talk in that region of immediacy, whence it dissolves in itself.

As earlier the bold assertion of non-knowing, that man makes God finite in order to cognize God, received its rectification only through a complete change of its position, so now the question how man arrives at God has been set right, only by being turned around, so that it acquires its true sense. And as non-knowing in this anthropological question has already cut off and stunted the answer for itself by its position, so also in the supreme principle of its theology, that God is and remains equal only to Himself, there was already contained the πρῶτον ψεῦδος, which had to have all its results contrary to Scripture as its consequence. Incidentally it is to be remarked that the same could be demonstrated also in other assertions of non-knowing, which may be found out without further guidance.

[p. 78]

We, however, keep to our question as it has now developed for us, only that we first simplify it, in order to approach it gradually more closely, by asking once more:

#### What is Man?

Man as he now is, and this and that man as he is for himself, we find to be other than the *actual* man, than man in and for himself.

Man in and for himself, according to his real Concept, that is, according to his actuality, is subjectively and objectively in God, for actuality consists precisely in the identity of being-for-self and being-in-itself.

Man in the world, as he appears to us, also forms a concept, but an abstract concept of humanity; this man we find precisely in the world, that is, only objectively in God, but not subjectively in God, whereby his abstraction is as much uttered as—though only for us—sublated.

When we say: *Man is good*, we ordinarily do not mean what we say. If we mean what we say, we understand by it either only the objective immanence which we have just mentioned, or we mean unconsciously the actual man, in that we confuse the actual man with this and that man, and most readily with ourselves. When we say: *Man is evil*, we understand by it this and that man, and all men, as they are, apart from their actuality, and we are prompted to this in many ways by ourselves, only that we must not in the process forget the actual man, man as he is created.

Herewith we now find in man the same contradiction that meets us everywhere *in the world*, the contradiction that he can regard the evil, the abstract, as something peculiar and innate to him, to his present *state*, and yet this very evil, and hence his [p. 79] present state itself, as something alien to him, namely to his actuality, to his original essence, while on the other hand he can and must regard the good, the concrete, the divine, just as well as something that has become alien to him, the fallen man, and yet as something peculiar to him, namely to his origin, and created in him.

To that extent man in his present, that is, abstract or fallen state, in consequence of that contradiction, is Nothing, for he ascribes the evil as well as the good to an alien being, which in different relations is his Other, the good to his origin, which he himself is not, the evil to his separation from his original relation, which accordingly likewise is not his proper essence.

Hence many phenomena in one’s own heart, as in the world, and all opposed opinions are explained. But we cannot enter into this here.

In short, as we find man, we find him caught in abstraction from the absolute truth, namely as estranged from this truth, and thereby from his actuality.

Is this abstraction from actuality, then, the fall from God, with which the history of the human race begins and proceeds?

Or is it no voluntary guilt, but necessity in the world-order, which may perhaps be demonstrated as an element of development, as a beginning *a priori*, and thereby would have to be regarded as the basis of all appearance, all reality, all good?

In these two questions we gather together those doubts which first force themselves upon us, and indeed as accusations, and which in our day are only too often repeated. To answer them we must enter into them, because they, being abstract, will not approach us.

[p. 80]

The first question I understand, and therefore I can answer it in the affirmative. That abstraction is the Concept of the Fall given to representation, and nothing other than this itself, only raised from representation to the Concept, to insight into the essence. The abstraction is the absolute discreteness of the hard heart, which is for itself, and rejects continuity with the universal, but in making itself the essence in its rigid isolation, proves itself, as the inequality of its being-within-itself with the universal, to be evil, hence to be the null.

More difficult, however, is the other question, for it rests on such a confusion of heterogeneous representations that the accusation seems not to fit the accused at all. It also seems to have hidden itself only behind other words, for had it stepped forth more openly, the familiar charge of pantheism would once more have been renewed. But in order to answer it, we must first attend to *its* representation of necessity.

Necessary is the evil as what has happened. What has happened cannot be made unhappened. Every deed has stepped out of its indeterminacy. The first was free to us, in the second we are bondsmen. The evil is necessary because, as having happened, it cannot be otherwise. Where philosophy enters, sin is already there, and now it is indeed necessary, sin, with all its consequences, and in particular the struggle of man, which philosophy pursues from stage to stage, and presents dialectically as a path of development. It is necessary because, as having happened, it cannot be otherwise.

But it could surely have been otherwise? There we suddenly stand at the limits of our wit, where the understanding snaps, namely at the limits of being, or rather already in non-being, where nothing more can be thought. But if you think to rescue your freedom by this alone, that it could have been otherwise, [p. 81] then we grant you that it could as certainly have been otherwise as it is *in itself* actually otherwise.

But it *ought* also to have been otherwise, and this philosophy posits no ought? Admittedly there ought to be no ought, you ought not to ought, but to be. In the Concept there is indeed no ought, but being in and for itself; herewith, however, it is already uttered that the evil, being-for-self, *ought* not to be. Actually it ought as certainly to have been otherwise,—so that there could have been no talk of any ought, of any law,—as it is to become otherwise through the sublation of the abstraction, as we shall soon see.

And how can the evil be regarded in this philosophy as the condition of reality, as the basis of life and becoming, when precisely according to it the evil has existence only in so far as it is not evil in itself, and is objectively in being, in the good, in God? If precisely according to it the evil for itself is the null, the non-actual, the abstract, how could it also again ground and condition actuality and reality? Only the first, crudest view of the understanding can make being and becoming conditional on the evil and the imperfect.

When I was still a boy, I had to hear dozens of times that God had placed us in this imperfect world, as in a school of probation, so that we ourselves might become something, and, in the struggle with sensuality, learn to raise ourselves more and more from the imperfect to the perfect. At that time I certainly did not understand what was said thereby, namely that according to this God had made everything bad, so that we ourselves should make it better, that is, so that we should only learn to feel and to try ourselves, hence become sinners. But the strange arrangement struck me nonetheless, and the question was always at hand, why God did not from the outset arrange his world according to his good pleasure, and thereby spare himself vexation and us an effort, which would have cost him nothing. In the above accusation [p. 82] is actually laid to the charge of speculative philosophy nothing other than that shallow doctrine of the Enlightenment of the understanding, which at present lies in its death-throes, but admittedly struggles against its death all the more powerfully and convulsively.

But let us go further, in order to answer the remaining accusations that are entwined with one another.

It is simply plucked out of the air when castles in the air of *a priori* construction are laid to the charge of speculative philosophy.

Nor does this philosophy find the evil *a priori*; it does not invent it, but finds it in experience and through experience, yet it finds it also in itself, hence *a priori*, as outside itself, hence *a posteriori*, just as it in general proceeds from the given, *a posteriori*, and raises this to the Concept, and thereby proceeds *a priori*. And where, moreover, has the prejudice concerning the murky difference between *a priori* and *a posteriori*, synthetic and analytic cognition been more thoroughly exposed than in this philosophy?

But finally there still remains the reproach of pantheism, according to which in this philosophy *in the end* or *at bottom* everything is One, evil and good. We shall come back to this later; here we are brief, out of impatience at the repeated reproach, by granting it outright. *In the end*, it is to be hoped, everything is to be balanced out, good and evil, as One. And *at bottom* good and evil are also One, namely in so far as the evil has a ground. Now *for itself*, however, it has no ground, and in this being-for-self its essence consists, and in so far it is also not One with the good. But the essence in which it shows itself, and without which it is not, has its ground in God, and also objectively, that is, unconscious to itself, conscious to us, has received its ground in God

But we are not yet at the end of these doubts, for now the same attack returns, as if nothing had happened to it, from another side, and in another shape, but with renewed force. It is the nature of doubt that it ever clothes itself in new forms, wherefore reason in the struggle with it must ever repeat itself, because for its part it is always the same. This new form of doubt asserts that in general all philosophy, all wisdom of the understanding, however profound and *consistent* it may be, must necessarily endanger either God’s absolute reality and unity, or the freedom of individual beings. If God is *absolute*,—to which the understanding on the one hand compels us—then, by an inescapable compulsion of the *consistent* understanding, every creature is conditioned by Him, consequently not free, and freedom is only illusion, every apparently free action is grounded in the iron necessity of the Absolute. But if the creature is free,—and to this the understanding compels us on the other hand—then God is not absolute, not All in One; the free beings are as many self-subsistent gods beside Him.

This attack appears to be weak. For to the disjunctive prolepsis “Either—Or” there is opposed, according to the old mode of procedure of logic, the dilemma in “Neither—Nor”; neither is man’s freedom endangered by God’s absoluteness, nor God’s absolute reality by the freedom of the singular.

To arrive at insight into this we need only ask what is to be understood by the Absolute on the one hand, and by freedom on the other, for with this the whole attack seems capable of being beaten down, whether we start from the Absolute or from freedom. At least it follows at once from the disjunction of both judgments itself, that to the real [p. 84] and speculative Concept of absolute necessity and freedom, and of their identity, an abstract, lifeless, sensuous, machine-like concept, which separates the connected members and opposes them to one another, has been substituted without our noticing it; a transformation which must befall us every time the speculative truth is grasped in the sensuous manner of the understanding and is defiled by the sin that clings to us and perverts all concepts.

Freedom, according to its true Concept and essence, is the absolute will; as absolute will it is determinate in itself. God is free in that He is not hindered from being His own essence. Herewith freedom separates itself from arbitrariness, which is not determinate, but wills to be this and that, and through this indeterminateness hinders the essence in which it shows itself from being its vocation. Arbitrariness is the opposite of freedom, the bondage of sin. In God there is no arbitrariness. God is free because He is the power to be Himself. Man was, is, or becomes free, in so far as he was not, is not, or will not be hindered from being in God, that is, in freedom, and from being his original vocation. In this sense it is true when it has been said: “I see well that you would like to be free, that is, arbitrary; for my part I do not desire to be free.”

But precisely through this refutation the above attack gains new strength, because only now does its proper meaning develop. For granted that absolute being, thought as life, excludes freedom so little that it is rather this itself, granted that being in the Absolute does not disturb the freedom of the singular but alone grounds it, still so much the more certain, and demonstrated in the refutation itself, is it that the Concept of the Absolute and of absolute freedom excludes all arbitrariness, and with arbitrariness all evil, as impossible. If God is absolute, then everything is [p. 85] in this absolute necessity and freedom, everything conditioned in God, hence good, or rather there is no longer any talk of good and evil; arbitrariness and sin are only illusion. But if there is a difference between good and evil, then God is not the absolute essence that conditions all, grasps all in Himself and sustains all in himself. From this dilemma no philosophy, no wisdom of reason can save itself; it denies either God, or sin as the fruit of arbitrariness. Thus, more precisely determined, runs the above reproach.

If reason is accused of being unable to comprehend the evil, and of having to deny it according to its consistency, the accusation seems all the more telling, as the evil is the irrational, hence falls outside reason, consequently also outside actuality, which, as the creation of reason, cannot be irrational. Reason must therefore deny the evil, even if in the individual subject it itself succumbs to the reality of this evil. Yes, God is the eternal negation of the evil itself, and thus identical with reason. According to reason, sin and arbitrariness in the Absolute are indeed only an empty illusion,—we must grant the accusation literally—and for the simple reason that all sin rests on defection, on aberration and illusion, hence on nothing. Who then has ever denied that sin is sheer illusion? In that we thus, following the guidance of the reproach, carry out further the consistent result of reason, it coincides unnoticed with the revelation from which it seems to have been taken. With this, however, the reproach is also annihilated, because we grant it that according to reason in its consistency, but just so according to faith, all arbitrariness and sin rest on illusion, which is also confirmed by the fact that this arbitrariness imagines itself free and self-subsistent, and yet consists in the most disgraceful bondage. The man who is originally free in the absolute [p. 86] essence, has through the Fall from the Absolute become a bondsman, a bondsman of God, whom he must nevertheless serve, and a bondsman of his Self, to which he is abandoned.

One thing, however, is to be added, namely this, that with the objective actuality of sin and arbitrariness, which evaporates as empty illusion, the subjective being and essence of the evil is not denied. Rather the evil consists precisely in this being-for-self, thereby in the thoroughgoing contradiction with objective actuality, that is, in despair.

Were the evil abstract absence of the good, privation, abstract negation, then in no respect would actuality belong to it, for only the good, God, is actual; what could still be besides?—And yet the evil also is. If we now wished to ascribe to the evil, besides the absence of the good, the presence of something else,—because feeling teaches us that the evil, the negative, asserts and proves positive actuality, then we come into the unthinkable conflict of an actuality split into two actualities, which is no less Manichaean for denying the original dualism. So here too we do not reach the goal, for only when the truth of that feeling is not merely felt but cognized does there grow for us the truth of the Concept of the evil, that is, its compatibility with actuality. The evil is not absent good, but objectively present, yet perverted, opposed, distorted good. To the evil, therefore, there belongs also an actuality, but an actuality stood on its head. Because, therefore, actuality does not belong to it in and for itself, for actuality is being in and for itself, there belongs to it only half actuality, namely an actuality in itself, merely in itself, that is, a being and working which it does not know and will, hence subjective non-actuality, and secondly an actuality for itself, [p. 87] merely for itself, that is, being-for-self, hence objective non-actuality.

What the evil is in itself, that does not concern it itself, as being-for-self, and hence it is restricted to its subjective being and essence.

This subjective, hence half actuality of the evil is not to be understood, however, as though it remained shut up without manifestation in the disposition of a single subject torn from God. Rather it proves and manifests itself precisely thereby as subjective actuality, that it carries along with itself and infects all subjects bound together by blood into One family, into One whole, indeed draws the whole nature bound up with it along with itself into its Fall. As this actuality it is the evil world, the fallen creation, hence in its subjectivity objective, external, yet so that thereby the world-order of God is not *in itself* disturbed, hence in itself no effect of the evil is possible. Yes, it is to be added that God’s creation has fallen with man not only so far as it was bound up with him, but has also fallen in such a manner that this bond itself is no longer the original one, but hangs together only as in splinters. Actually it is our guilt that in the wide world every creature sighs and languishes for redemption from its enchantment. So it speaks in a thousand accusations to the Lord of creation from every mute stone, from every flower, and from the whole wondrous animal world!

Yet for our purpose it is only so much that is to be held fast, that to the evil, to be sure, *in a certain measure* and relatively, actuality, but, as to the irrational, not the full, actual actuality belongs. If the Self were merely sublated, freed from itself, it would be no more; if redemption is to benefit it, it must itself remain, and know itself reconciled with the good. *Phenomenology*, p. 621. [p. 92] to itself and through the abstraction from communion becomes evil. But they are also again both equally good, in so far as each steps out of its singularity, brings this to sacrifice, and in the opposite knows itself. The good is good when it acknowledges the evil as good, that is, lets the fixed difference go, and knows the evil as a moment in the whole, and thus cognizes it as itself. The evil is at once good when it cognizes itself, according to its rigid, self-made discreteness, as evil, and thereby relinquishes itself, as the singular, but in the sublation of itself, as this singular, finds itself again as universal essence. Without this sublation of that difference which inhibits continuity, however, it is not only the subjectivity of the “beautiful soul,” which raises itself as “the moral genius above the determinate law and every content of duty, and knows the inner voice of its immediate knowing as divine voice,” as “the self sharpened to the last abstraction, consuming itself in spiritual consumption,” that is to be called evil; but also the universal side opposed to this singularity, which calls that subjectivity evil, and in the objective, unchangeable law of universal duty opposes itself to subjective genius, has the same stated character of the evil, in that it has no more right to appeal to the law than the particular consciousness has to appeal to its own.

Both moments have already come before our eyes earlier in *Allwill* and *Woldemar* and their company; they now come nearer to consciousness as the subjective disposition of the beautiful soul, and as the abstract law of universal duty; but with this they also seem to sink into the abyss of the indifferent Absolute. For actually both become reconciled and good only by renouncing, being evil for themselves, this their rigid abstraction, and as sublated [p. 93] Thises, “in fact present themselves as universal,” and thus cognize themselves in the Other.

But if we look more closely, the result of this dialectic, which for itself comes to nothing, is *in fact* nothing worse than that good and evil form no abiding, fixed opposition, and that, in so far as they prove themselves an opposition, the good, as mere counter-pole of the evil, is itself evil, from which it then follows of itself that the actual, concrete good, as reconciliation, stands above the opposition, or—because this expression again contains for the understanding an abstraction—consists in the sublation of the opposition, and thus manifests itself as actual, as universal.

But against this result, too, a notable voice rises up, for according to it individuality as such seems to be evil, and only from the sublation of singularity does the good, as indifferent, seem to proceed, and thus also all determinateness to evaporate into empty universality, and to have to take refuge in it, in order to be redeemed from the evil. According to this, then, nothing singular, as this singular, would be good, but it first becomes good as a sublated This? Is this anything other than what pantheism has always taught, more or less concealed?

To this we answer that the singular is not evil *in itself*, but becomes so only by separating itself as singular, tearing itself loose from the continuity of the universal and wanting to count as the essence for itself, whereas the singular that knows itself as a moment in the whole, and in the sublation of itself knows itself one with the Other of itself, is good, because it remains in continuity with the Other.

The singular would then, after all, be good merely by knowing itself in communion with the evil, and as

To this we must answer and confess: Yes! The evil is no less a moment than the good opposed to it; it is not evil in itself, but becomes so only through the self-will of separation. Whoever, therefore, regards the evil as an other of himself, himself falls into this separation, in that he denies his community with it, as with a moment of fluid universality. “Keep yourselves down to the lowly,” and let everyone esteem himself less than his neighbor. Jesus himself ate with the tax collectors and sinners.

We have said that the singular is not evil in itself, as it has been said elsewhere that it is not the finite that is evil, but the finite puffed up into self-being. But we ought to have gone further still; we must now confess outright that in itself nothing singular, nothing finite, and also nothing infinite exists at all. In actuality nothing is finite, nothing infinite, for both are abstractions, which have come into the world through sin, which tears everything asunder. Hence it also comes about that the infinite, like the finite, cannot be known, but on the other hand cannot itself know either: it comes about because to both abstractions no actual being belongs, wherein the connection between being and knowing, existence and consciousness, actuality and reason manifests itself once again.

We can now also concede that the finite is indeed evil—what we previously wished to deny—for it is only through sin that it is this abstract, isolated finitude, and with this non-knowing has come into the world.

And, not to pass over in silence the last point: *God*—in His wrath and His love—is *good* precisely because he is not abstract. In that He punishes, [p. 95] and in that He forgives, he knows the fallen creature as *His* creature.

But once again the sensuous understanding, in apparent alliance with the supernatural Word of God, throws down the gauntlet before philosophy, for now it certainly believes it can convict it of having proceeded not from objective revelation, but, in arbitrary self-exaltation, from itself.

The essence of the good, so it says, consists in moderation and abstinence; the immoderate is the evil. But the essence of moderation and abstinence consists in this, that the singular contents itself with being it, and lets other singulars be beside it, without tearing them into itself. Indeed, God Himself proves this abstinence; God sets Himself limits, He negates, on the one hand, the limitless, He negates, on the other, being His creature. But what does philosophy do? According to it the very essence of the good consists in an expansion scattered in all directions, in a limitless confusion and diffusion, whereby nothing remains standing beside another, everything flows over into everything else, and the singular wants to be all, to have all.

We spare the opponent the further exposition of his accusation; for we must without more ado take up the gauntlet, in order, where possible, to convict our adversary that he has good reason to insist on determinate concepts, because he himself does not arrive at them.

If moderation really consists only in this, that the singular lets it suffice to be itself, then the good consists in not keeping even this singular self for itself. If abstinence is posited only in this, that the individual tolerates other individuals beside itself, then it is merely external, for the good consists precisely in this, that the individual does not leave the individuals standing outside and beside itself [p. 96] and does not remain standing beside them, but places itself in community with them, and gives up the aloofness of closed-off individuality in order to be in the other. *Immoderate* would be the individual that wants to be all itself, to have all itself; and yet the understanding, that is, the sensuous understanding, transfers this representation to the individual that wants to be nothing for itself, to have nothing for itself. It forgets that moderation, abstinence, contentment express in themselves merely a negative determination, that they contain a commandment only in relation to being-for-self, that the negative determination does not exclude the positive content, which consists in this, that the singular that wants to be nothing for itself, to have nothing for itself, shall nevertheless gain all. The singular does not want to be all, but that this be, be in and for itself; it does not want to have all, but to be had by all, and itself not to be. But the measure in God, the negation in God, which the understanding otherwise does not acknowledge, and now suddenly presupposes—this negation, precisely as the eternal, free deed of God, is the condition not only of his personality in general and of its hypostases, but also of his particular Incarnation. And as the necessity of the divine essence does not sublate its freedom, so little is the measure in God a hindrance for God to enter with his whole essentiality into the singularity of the creature, that on the contrary this first becomes possible through that measure, and precisely herein the abstinence of God proves itself as self-humiliation. So much for the refutation of the last attack!—

To be sure, all these concepts in this abstraction are at first only formulas, dry and unfruitful; but only do not turn away so rigidly and coldly and strangely, do not be, for your part, so proud and so aloofly closed off against speculation, which you accuse of pride and coldness, [p. 97] and take to heart only the one thing, that this very abstraction itself is the evil. Rather transpose yourself in a living way into the concepts of philosophy, and let them penetrate from the understanding your whole, undivided spiritual being; only first suffer them, and first take them into your *disposition*, and you will experience their life and their truth, that is, their agreement with the Word of God, of which they are the translation, in your own heart.

The more you make present to yourself and render intuitable the fluid continuity of singulars with one another in the universal—since none retains anything for itself and wants to count for itself—the more clearly will you come to know, in yourself and in the fairest soul, the hard rind of the rigid heart which withdraws from that continuity. The more repugnant this or that in the other becomes to you, because it is encountered in an other, as in one supposedly alien, the more will you need the determinate Concept, in order to become conscious of your community with the other, with the alien and repugnant. What you tread underfoot like dust stands on a level with you.

We have come here to a point where philosophy becomes edifying, the same strict philosophy of the Concept which “must guard against wanting to be edifying.”

For as it has to overcome *devotion*, insofar as this thinks only *of* God, and—trembles, because it does not think *in* God, so by virtue of its discipline and determinateness it also declares war on that edification which dissolves into indeterminate feelings, and “looks down contemptuously on determinateness, the Horos, and intentionally keeps itself at a distance from the Concept and necessity, as from reflection which dwells only in the finite.” But as it does not on that account cease really to *edify* man, so there rests precisely upon

*Phenomenology*, p. XI. [p. 98] the speculative ground which we have just demonstrated all true asceticism, which releases the closed-off heart from being-for-self, and opens it to the universal, and brings it as an offering.

From here there also opens a deep, weighty, momentous view into the heredity of sin, which is as *innate* in human nature as the good was *created* in it—a view into the depths of the human heart, which will not acknowledge this community in sin, and precisely by repudiating this heredity of innate sin shows itself to be the evil heart, the rigid being-for-self, whereby it denies human community, indeed its own heart’s blood, and tears itself away from the universal bond and separates itself, in that the child renounces its father and its father’s guilt, the child’s child its first forefather and his Fall, man humanity and its burden of sin, and will know nothing of this as of something alien, instead of sharing the burden of what all in one member have to answer for, as the whole body suffers when one organ is sick.—

The good, on the other hand, is the universal; as universal it also has the evil in itself and under itself, but not as what it is for itself, not as an abstract moment, as which it is only evil, but as sublated, as which it is, namely in itself, good.

From this the doctrine of reconciliation, after it has been given and *revealed* in the *religion of Spirit*, seems to *manifest* itself in the *philosophy of Spirit*, to prove itself in its actuality and necessity. This *necessity* of reconciliation does no detriment to the *free grace* of God, or at least only in the domain of the sensuous understanding, where a representation foreign to it is substituted for the concept of necessity. Here, however, this necessity of reconciliation, as following from the divine essence of the universal [p. 99] follows, is nothing other than the freedom of God himself, according to which he is not hindered from being his own essence, and is not hindered even by the falling away of the creature, because he is free. Whoever wished to connect any arbitrariness with the concept of free grace would quite properly be transferring to God the representation that comes from sin, thereby carrying sin over into his faith as well, and sinning anew against God’s freedom.

That the necessity of reconciliation has really been manifested in the foregoing, however, follows at once from the concept of the latter, which in its ground and essence rests on the continuity of the universal, and consequently not only knows all being-for-self as objectively annulled, as in itself not for itself, but must also sublate it subjectively, because otherwise it would still remain for itself on its own account, and so would not be completely, not *actually* annulled.

And if it had previously emerged that redemption, as redemption from abstraction, cannot be effected by the abstract self, which is precisely what is to be redeemed, but only by the divine essence, from which it is separated, so there has now developed one moment more, namely that redemption would not be possible even through the divine essence, insofar as it is thought as separated from the human essence and would accordingly itself be abstract, and hence that redemption becomes possible only through the divine essence proving itself to be the universal, and as such not abstracting from the human essence, but in the fullness of time becoming it itself, and indeed factually and personally, in all relations, in life and in death becoming it itself. Since God now becomes like man in all respects, He also bears the sin of men, only that He Himself remains without sin, as man in himself is without sin, that is, in God. He bears the sin of the world, for he is not merely man as he was before the Fall, but is placed into the whole distress of the [p. 100] fallen creature; he is himself without sin, that is, not without alien sin, but without his own, for he is indeed wholly the fallen creature itself, but not as it is for itself, for he has come precisely to sublate this being-for-self. Yet he bears this alien sin as his own, for he is himself man, he also bears the consequences of sin, our sickness and our pains. As in Adam all men forsook God, so Christ, in that he shares in this humanity and takes upon himself alone the judgment of God upon it, is forsaken by God.—This Incarnation of God, however, would not be actual, not personal and factual, and consequently also not capable of sublating abstraction, if the divine essence still retained something for itself, if it hid itself, as in nature, so also in its Word, behind its appearance, without being this itself; it would be only a half transposition into humanity, and no transposition at all into the individual man, if the fullness of the Godhead did not empty itself exclusively and without remainder in this Incarnation.

Only in that the divine essence in deed thus empties and humiliates itself is it cognized as God Himself, as the living God, because it thereby declares itself as concrete universal, whereby it is confirmed that the singular has indeed separated itself from the universal, but not the latter from the former. But if this is so, the sum of Christian faith is contained therein, raised from representation to the Concept, yet preserved in the latter unchanged.

Yet here too we hear from more than one side an objection of the sensuous understanding, to which we must give answer. If God is good only as universal, if he becomes good only through his singularization, through his particular Incarnation at the beginning of the Christian era, then before that, in the whole span of time since the Fall of the first man, indeed since the Fall of the angel, he was not good, was less good, for even if he [p. 101] has not let revelations and condescensions fail in this span of time, yet he became man Himself only in the fullness of time, only in that determinate time was personally and essentially placed into the whole distress of humanity, and consequently became good only thereby, but also only so long as he remained in this distress, that is, only in the days of the flesh.

To this the answer is that to this distress there belonged also the singular moment of time, to which, as to this one and no other, he submitted in the fullness of time. In and for itself, however, before God and in God all time is eternal, all future and past is present, and the eternal counsel of self-emptying and Incarnation is the eternal deed of the same, which belongs to the one Person of the undivided triune Godhead.

Or, to remain with time as it appears to us: is man himself forever in this flesh, and does he therefore cease to be man when he ceases to be in the flesh? As man, then, is in the flesh only for a time, and remains man even after the days of the flesh, so God too, in order to become like us in all things, came into the flesh in the fullness of time, but also remained man after the Resurrection and Ascension, and as the Son of Man he will come again to judge the living and the dead. As all men are in God, because outside God there is nothing, so God too, from whom, and through whom, and in whom all things are, is in every individual man, and thereby God, the triune God, is himself man, now and in eternity. How could man put on Christ, represent and glorify Christ in himself, if Christ did not put him on, and represent and glorify himself in him?—

We stand hereby on a height which gives us light enough to illuminate and transfigure every single point of the system. In that we use it to take at least a comprehensive general retrospect [p. 102] there are three stations which we have to distinguish. *First*, the absolute essence is for consciousness the alien object, condemning singularity, to which, as the unchangeable substance, it sees itself opposed in its singularity. The *second time*, however, the absolute essence is itself a shape of singularity, as human consciousness itself is, so that into *this* singularity the whole mode of existence passes over. *Lastly*, however, the consciousness of man himself finds itself, as this singular, *in* the Unchangeable, in God, and in that, by means of the second moment, it has the joy of finding itself *therein*, it becomes conscious of its singularity as reconciled with the universal.

But already we see the understanding smile compassionately again at the clever deduction which believes it has explained subjectively, in a few short, dry and meagre formulas, the objectively revealed mystery of the holy Trinity. It is the same understanding which, convicted of its nullity by the Word of God, falls silent in astonishment before its objective content, but against the appropriation developing in philosophy on the ground of objective revelation suddenly comes to speech again as the same natural understanding, like and unlike the pious priest Zechariah; like, because the priest, like the understanding in general, fell silent before the miracle of God, but then in the unfolding of the miracle came to speech again through a new miracle: unlike, because he was reborn to speech not as the same natural man, but as the new man out of God, not in order to testify against the supernatural unfolding, but, full of the Holy Spirit and released from naturalness, to preach the Word of God, which appears supernatural to fallen nature, but thereby proves itself to be actual, to be the true nature.

What does this understanding now say in its opposition to philosophy, when, under the shelter of the orthodoxy which it has preserved for itself, it lets itself be heard more closely on the doctrine that vexes it? There are chiefly three objections which we shall first hear, and with which we shall stay, for those we do not hear we cannot consider so long as they do not become known to us.

The doctrine of human wisdom, so we hear, which announces itself as immanent development, and yet proves itself to be only a subjective dissemblance of the pure Word, endangers first the revealed Trinity of God, in particular the personality and independence of the Father and the Son, for under the name of absolute substance and of the subject emptied out into singularity both appearances perish *as these* appearances, in order to make room for the Spirit that is in and for itself; they are preserved in the universal Spirit only as sublated, their truth lies in their sublation, against the Spirit they evaporate as appearances untrue for themselves. Nothing remains but pure Spirit. That is the *first* thing which that understanding opposes with which alone we have to do, the understanding which still holds fast to faith, but keeps it only beside itself, and itself only beside it, without arriving at the unification and interpenetration of both.

Again, however, this universal Spirit is not the person of the Holy Spirit, which the Word of God represents to us, but a concept—the sublimated concept of the infinite substance—which fears being drawn down into common representation and distorted, if we were to think of a determinate person, of the personality of this Spirit. That is the *second* confession to which the subjective understanding, which has objective faith beside itself, feels driven by a holy awe, which we honor and share, [p. 104] out of fear of arbitrary alteration and displacement of the revealed Word of God.

And if, finally, the agreement of knowing with biblical faith were to be demonstrated even against these accusations, already raised earlier but not yet thoroughly disposed of, if the determinate representation were really to be preserved in the Concept, what use then are those hollow formulas to us, which make cold but not warm, puff up the understanding but do not enlighten it, can neither touch nor bend the will, can make the rigid heart more rigid still but not break it, and cannot heal the broken one? Or can you kneel down and adore before the beings that haunt these formulas? and if you adore, do you not kneel before your own idols? That is the *third* thing which the human spirit raises in warning reply—the spirit to which the truth has not yet opened itself in the form of truth—and which it must raise, because in the abstractions, whose necessity and result it does not know, it misses the fullness of life which it has experienced in the Word of God and come to know in its heart.

It is now time that we answer the accusations in turn.

*To the first.* The confusion of immanence with subjective inwardness has already been disposed of; we need only refer back to it: the former proceeds from the substance and knows itself in it, while the latter proceeds from its own subject and does not get out. But are those formulas supposed to endanger the Trinity? It is true that substance and subject, the substance of the absolute essence, and the subject, into whose singularity the absolute essence passes over, are *for themselves* untrue appearances, because they are completed only in *Spirit*. But are Father and Son therefore less actual and personal because they are one with the Spirit? Are they not rather actual and personal only in that they are neither merely in themselves nor merely for [p. 105] themselves, but in and for themselves? This being-in-and-for-itself is, however, expressed in the Trinity, and thereby representation is reconciled with the Concept. Or does not the mystery of the Trinity consist precisely in this, that no person is without the other, and hence each for itself without the other would not be? Is it not revealed in Scripture that in the fullness of time the *true* worshippers worship the *Father*, not merely as this Father, John 4:23, but in the *truth*, which is Christ, 14:6, and in the *Spirit*, who is another than the Father and the Son, John 14:16, and yet the same, as the Spirit of truth, 14:17, hence as the *triune God*? Would not every personality of God without the others be untrue, and is there any other true God than the triune? And if, despite all reference to the mysterious linguistic meaning, you take offence at this, that Christ reveals his actuality and Godhead only as a sublated This, is he therefore less than this, or less, as sublated this, the truth? Would He not in truth be unactual as this child, as this man, had he not also died, risen, and ascended into heaven? 1 Cor. 15:17. Do you not confess with the Apostle that Christ is yesterday and today and the same in eternity?

*To the second.* If, accordingly, the personality of the Father and the Son is not denied, but rather in this philosophy appropriated to the understanding through the Concept, and derived from the essence of the Trinity itself, then the personality of the Spirit is hereby saved, only that in each person the fullness of the Godhead has its being and is, undivided and unseparated. By the unity of God no numerical unity, by the Trinity of the hypostases no fragmentation and separation is to be understood. Such representations are themselves untrue, at home only in a fallen world. And does not our philosophy teach literally that “the *simple* eternal essence would be Spirit only [p. 106] in the empty word, if it remained at the representation and expression of the eternal essence”? And yet it must let itself be charged with a pantheistic unitarianism? If you now really have faith, we think we are not preaching to deaf ears? But he who does not have it perhaps finds on these thorny ways the way to the fullness of life.

*To the third.* If these formulas, which, according to their outward appearance, often repeated, seldom understood, more often dragged down to a pantheistic view of the understanding, remain standing over against you only externally, and seem cold and hollow, if they do not open themselves to you and will not disclose their content, is it your fault or theirs? Take good care that you do not, as an opponent of philosophy, fall into the same fault to which many of its disciples succumb, when formalism becomes their downfall. Beware also of taking for fruits of philosophy what the misunderstanding of it is to blame for. Is spiritual pride a fruit of faith, because the misuse of bestowed grace so easily has it as a consequence? But in order that the despised formulas may come closer to you, read them once more, and translate them into your own language. Have you not yourself experienced—I speak to you, to whom faith has been given—that God without the Son and the Holy Spirit is really only substance, and that just as much the Son as this Son passes by and goes under, mute and alien to you, that he, without the Father who precedes him—I speak humanly—without the Spirit who follows him—I speak humanly—is precisely only a single self-consciousness? Have you not experienced that only in the Holy Spirit is the certainty given of the community, restored through Christ, of man with God and with his fellows? The substance is, as mere object, outside you; the subject of this substance, too, is for you only object, for it is indeed a self-consciousness which, like yourself, appears in the shape of singularity, but yet again [p. 107] outside you and separated from you, like every other singular subject of humanity, which as singular is also isolated. Your singularity, then, is still not reconciled with the alien singularity in which the absolute essence is hidden and enclosed, until God as the Spirit that is in and for itself reveals himself to you in his holy and undivided Trinity. If God were what he is only in himself, objectively, or only for himself, subjectively, he would be man too only in himself, and so not Himself, or only for himself, and so not for us, and thereby the community of God with man, and with this community the revelation of God, would be excluded. But if you further complain about the dry bread of philosophy, beware lest the enjoyment of the eternally fresh fullness of life that wells forth from the Word of God become for you a fleshly enjoyment, turn into an enjoyment that shuns the strict labor of thinking and doing, and rejects every furtherance of cognition, because the way leads through thorns and abstractions, and demands renunciation, self-denial. Self-emptying is not everyone’s affair; but whoever receives it with faith, to him we leave the further development of these hints, which were meant to serve for the correction of those objections.

We could in general break off here, since we have sought only the key to the comprehension of philosophy, and have not to pursue it through the whole system step by step and on all sides. But there is still something on our heart that cannot be suppressed. On one and another question that lies close to our heart we have received in this philosophy at least a general reply and answer; only one has remained wholly untouched. How, then, does it stand in this circle of thoughts, so we ask lastly with anxiety, with the continued existence of humanity after death?

[p. 108]

It has often been reproached to the newest philosophy that it takes no account of the common, and for that very reason not groundless, needs of men, and has no answer ready for them, at least no direct answer. Scarcely and seldom do the words concerning God and the Trinity of God, concerning freedom and morality, meet us—and of immortality there is no talk at all.

It is not to be forgotten that all these concepts in this form belong chiefly to the doctrine of faith and of morals; yet the supreme principles of the same must nonetheless lie in philosophy, even if their elaboration had to be left to the particular sciences. Indeed, we have also attempted to demonstrate these principles, though not to elaborate them, at least in general, in relation to God and the Son of God and the Spirit of God, in relation to freedom and morality. It remains still for us to seek to understand this philosophy also in relation to continuance after death, for we would mock our science itself, and sin against our cognition, if we believed we could dismiss such needs, which are also ours, insofar as we do not abstract, and the reproaches formed from them, with a lofty, scornful answer. And so there are repeated here the same objections and accusations which we have already disposed of in other respects.

“The idea of a *personal* immortality,” so it sounds *unanimously* from the *most diverse* sides, “is wholly foreign to this philosophy, and it asserts and must assert that man, his spirit as his body, returns into the bosom of the planet, out of which he, like the plant, grew forth, when he has run through the series of changes conditioned by the measure of his life-force.”

Indeed this philosophy has the air as if it regarded, if not the spirit, yet body and soul as abstractions [p. 109] which death dissolves, and it therefore matters to hear it on this point too, at least in individual, detached sentences.

“The abstract essence is estranged from itself, it has natural existence and selfish actuality; this its otherness, or its sensuous presence”—that is, this its first estrangement in natural existence—“is taken back by the second becoming-other”—that is, by the second estrangement in death—“and posited as sublated,” that is, as annihilated and preserved, “as universal; thereby the essence in it”—that is, in the concretely universal presence—“has become itself; the immediate existence of actuality has ceased to be alien and external to it, in that it is as sublated universal; this death is therefore its arising as *Spirit*.”

“Not only is death the death of the natural side or of particular being-for-self, there dies not only the already dead husk stripped from the essence, but also the abstraction of the divine essence.”

These utterances at first confirm the prejudice of the sensuous understanding, that this very universal, which as sublated is to be Spirit, is the very naive expression of a purely pantheistic way of thinking; they therefore require explanation.

The life of man in his natural, estranged existence, that is, in his Fall, is the first abstraction, the first negation, to which man has fallen victim here below through sin; death is the second abstraction, the second negation; as the completion of abstraction, as negation of the negation, it is however also the sublation of the same, and hence the arising of the previously natural, external existence as Spirit. In death there dies not only the abstract body, which is regarded as a part of the

p. 737. [p. 110] whole machine, but also the abstract soul, which counts as the other part, and out of this death of the *abstract*, divided man there arises concrete man, actual man, as Spirit, as person, that is, in the identity of body and soul.

The abstraction according to which man consists of two parts is a consequence of sin; it has as its consequence the second abstraction, death, as the wages of sin, and thereby man dies off from his abstraction toward the resurrection as *Spirit*, which “the death of the Mediator” has earned for him.

The Fall of man is the first death; upon this follows the second death, in which the first death dies.

In that this philosophy now explains physical death according to its significance, it also constantly urges and presses toward the death of abstraction, which we are to die daily even before bodily death, and finally to expect in bodily death itself.

For if we do not die with Christ, we shall not rise with Him either.

Hereby the objection that is just now meeting us is dispatched in advance, the objection as if, according to this theory of death, death mediated redemption in every individual without exception and unconditionally.

If death, so runs the objection, in consequence of the death of Jesus effects the abstraction from the first abstraction, and thereby negates the negation itself, then faith is superfluous, on which nevertheless Scripture and the Church make justification, and thereby actual redemption, dependent. Death strikes the righteous and the unrighteous, the believing and the unbelieving, the good and the evil; consequently it must also make them all alike, that is, all righteous and blessed.

To this the answer is that precisely according to the innermost principle of this philosophy not death in

p. 761. [p. 111] itself, not death objectively, and this as little as death for itself, the merely subjective death, the self-will of self-denial, but only actual death, that is, the death which occurs subjectively and objectively at once, in consequence of the death of Jesus on the cross, through whose communication it can have the power of redemption. Death in itself is the non-existent death, is not death, is dead death, the death of a head of cabbage.

Actual death is the Christian, that is, the death into which the subject enters just as much as death enters into it. That the subject goes into death is the work of faith, which kills the I; that death goes into this subject, the work of the objective truth of that faith; but for itself alone death in itself is nothing, nothing but death precisely, the death of a selfless head of cabbage, as everything in itself is nothing. Neither does the death of Jesus redeem you objectively, nor the objective death of this your subject, but only the actual death of Jesus, and through this your actual death; but the former becomes actual only in justifying faith, the latter in the daily death of the I and of one’s own will which that faith works.

So we find here again too the deep doctrine of Scripture, according to which the unhappy duplicity of the human essence, fallen apart into *body* and *soul*, can be reconciled again only through the *Spirit*. In Scripture the soul counts like the body (σωμα, ψυχη) in this fragmentation moving toward death, and without the Spirit for mere flesh; and flesh and blood (σαρξ και αίμα) shall not inherit the kingdom of God, for they must die away as abstractions. It is sown a natural body, a body with the soul, a soulish body (σωμα ψυχικον), and there shall rise a spiritual body (σωμα πνευματικον). But the spiritual body is not the first, but the natural, afterward the spiritual.

*Phenomenology*, p. 541.

[p. 112]

The body of the soul is different from the soul, but the body of the Spirit is not different from the Spirit, as from itself, it is not a part of the whole, but the whole itself in its unity, in its expression. The Spirit is neither soul nor body, but spiritual body.

With this these provisional remarks conclude, which can be of influence only as a timid beginning, as an awkward attempt, and as a summons to further explanations, in that they are meant not to close the files but to open them, so as on the one hand to develop ever more clearly and sharply the relation of this philosophy to Christianity under the discipline of the Word of God, on the other hand, however, to free philosophy itself ever more from its abstraction, and to bring it, as common property, nearer to common comprehension. For this, however, more detailed demonstrations are needed than these aphoristic remarks. If these cannot eliminate all doubts and misunderstandings, they nevertheless point, in the resolution of *a few* doubts, to the source from which *all* misunderstandings flow, as a guideline for our procedure. Or even if they were not suited to remove a single misunderstanding entirely, or if they should even, through awkwardness in the presentation, arouse new misunderstandings, they could still serve to stimulate us to *conscientious obedience in learning*, which so easily remains standing at the outer side and at one-sided results, and substitutes another sense for these—to *love in understanding*—for without love, without transposition into the other, as little as without the understanding is any comprehension possible—and above all to *Christian caution in judgment before understanding*. At the same time, however, in these explanations is contained the confession how we have understood the newest philosophy [p. 113] and have sought to appropriate it. If we find ourselves, through subjective apprehension, in the whole or at least in the particular, in an essential delusion, well then perhaps several find themselves in the same delusion, who would be helped out of the dream together with us by an open refutation and thorough instruction, for if this instruction took place in love, that is, with the wish to approach the weaker, and in order to transpose oneself into him as into oneself, then it is to be expected that we too can accept and understand the instruction.

For let this also be confessed, that we can often scarcely guard ourselves against the suspicion that we might have drawn alien teaching, against its ultimate sense and will, into our circle, and worked it up for our representations, in order to find between it and immediate faith the agreement. On the other hand we are convinced that a system of philosophy, as having arisen objectively and independent of the individual subject, can, according to its consequence, differ essentially from the view and disposition of one or another of its teachers or pupils, that is, that the system can really say something other than the individual adherent means; hence from the fact that occasionally the formalism of this doctrine conflicts with our apprehension, not too much may be concluded.

Could we now also be convinced that we really stood within this philosophy, that we had at least arrived at a comprehension of it in general, had penetrated from representation to the Concept, and hence might also maintain a judgment about the form in which it has expressed itself up to now, we would give it, or rather *its beginning*, to consider whether *in its progress* it would not gain in light and determinateness, if it attached itself more decisively to the Word of God, out of which it has developed, and, more determinately, [p. 114] that is, more *nameably*, proceeded from *sin*, which has manifested itself to it as *abstraction*, without whose presupposition no understanding of the world, without whose recognition no self-cognition, without whose sublation no cognition of God is possible. For what a night lies over all relations where this cognition is lacking, and how all at once everything inexplicable is explained, every contradiction resolved, every shadow intelligible, when we learn that all the imperfections beneath the moon are not God’s but our work, the work of free creatures, who as God’s creatures had to be free, were free in Him, and became bondmen because they forsook Him?

It is not strange to us that in a system it may be regarded as a matter of indifference from which point we proceed, for it proves itself a system precisely thereby, as an organic whole, and in its living immanence, that in every point it is itself and has its principle in every point. But precisely for that reason every point in this respect is also to be brought forward, and held fast, and called by name.

If we insist on the name, lay weight on the name, then it is first from this philosophy that we have learned to recognize the power of language and of its determinations, the essentiality of the determinate name prescribed by language, and the precarious state of affairs concerning the nameless.

This says at the same time that science must not hold representation in low esteem, but by means of the Concept must find again the abstract, transcendent representation as living, immanent, transfigured. Not thought is the highest—so we would wish to call out, not to this philosophy, but to its formalism, and to turn its own words upon it—*not thought is the highest, but representation, the shape*, only that it is to be recognized as immanent, and not as a transient but as an essential appearance, identical with the essence [p. 115] of the essence. Knowing is not merely knowing, but just as much being, the Concept just as much representation. That is what this philosophy itself teaches, and we owe it especially enlightenment about this; but its formalism seems itself not to act accordingly, and easily an abstract concept is again played into its hands in place of the real Concept. If knowing is just as much in the truth, as absolute reality, as the latter is in it, then it follows of itself that it does not presuppose a particular faculty, but the entire, undivided activity of spirit; it is the being of spirit, which includes and cherishes the Concept as well as representation, and faith as itself. Of this the formalism of this doctrine itself does not always seem to think; for, that we may conceal nothing, more than once in the domain of this pure knowing it has become so incorporeal and spectral and so uncanny to us that we have yearned quite earnestly for persons and shapes, and then have sought and found refuge nowhere else than in the Word of God, indeed have often been refreshed to the very marrow and bone by a single saying of the Bible, as by the power of God.—So sensuous do we feel ourselves, that for the sake of comprehending we do not wish to dispense with grasping with the hands.—

Knowing is preceded by a representation, *intuition*, which, as unconscious, is less than the former together with the latter; but should not a *beholding* follow it as well, which is more than all conceptual knowing? *Immanent beholding* has already been designated by language as *insight*.

What we here believe,—and know, that we shall there *behold*. If we here cognize God, and live, move, and have our being in the knowing of God, we shall *see* Him there as He is, and face to face. Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall behold God.

If these representations and hopes are childish, then we hold to the disciple, who cannot enough [p. 116] extol and praise and repeat that he has seen with his eyes, touched with his hands, heard with his ears the Word of Life that was from the beginning and became flesh.

Blessed are those who do not see, and yet believe. John 20:29. But blessed also are the eyes that are to see in glory what the disciples saw in lowliness, and for that reason were pronounced blessed. Matt. 13:16.

### III.

### The Cognition of Faith

or

### Faith and Knowing.

The *truth* of non-knowing reduces itself, according to the negative moment of its content, to natural non-knowing, to the non-knowing of man out of himself; only that it does not itself recognize this limitation. For therein lies precisely the *error* of that non-knowing, that, proceeding from the subject and perishing in it, it nevertheless pronounces non-knowing universally and absolutely, and thus infers from subjective incapacity an absolute impossibility.

According to the positive result the *truth* of this absolute non-knowing lies, veiled and dissembled, in the presentiment of the necessity of another, supernatural source of life and cognition, which opens itself in faith; only that it does not itself reach this, does not accept it, precisely because it proceeds from the subject, and does not come out of it, whence in its naive ingenuousness and innocent ignorance it invents, as the result of an *immediate* communion of man with God, a faith which,

*Phenomenology*, pp. 708, 712. [p. 117] rejecting all external, objective revelation, enthrones the deity born from the innermost sanctuary of humanity, holds divine service with itself, and dances around its golden calf at will and pleasure. This subjective faith is the positive *error* of all non-knowing.

Thus subjective non-knowing is widened into the absolute, and the absolute, substantial faith is stunted into the subjective and volatilized.

To this non-knowing stands opposed speculative, absolute knowing, which, in renouncing the subjectivity of the philosophizing subject, without fear of losing it, experiences the death of the same. According to its point of departure it therefore rests in the faith—given at first as prejudice, and after many struggles and defeats transfigured at last into the Concept—in the eternal truth of the substance, as well as in the trust in the restored communion of man with God, with the insight that this restoration consists in *mediation*, and requires that mediation which is revealed in the Word of God. Therefore speculative philosophy finds in the Word of God its content, which it does not make for itself, and therein its truth, as absolute truth.

How, accordingly, that non-knowing with its subjective faith relates to the Christian faith in revelation needs no repetition. It has the semblance of what the latter really gives, the semblance of humility, or what is the same, natural humility, only not actual, evangelical humility. But even in so far it can approach Christian faith, and prepare minds for it, as it maintains and sharpens the hunger and thirst after truth, whereas philosophy, half understood, easily satisfies. At least subjective faith, because it still wants to believe, and seeks God, does not let us fall asleep so easily as the *ἐποχή* and *ἀταραξία* of a philosophy that has stuck halfway.

[p. 118]

Closer, on the other hand, to the faith in revelation seems to stand the philosophy that has penetrated to the absolute Concept, indeed it seems, as to content, to coincide with revelation, and consequently also—if we do not infer too quickly—with the *faith* in revelation.

We have been able to observe proud philosophy in the catechization which faith conducts with it, as Margaret does with the scholastic freethinker whom she loves. If on the way of its development it could not stand its ground, and to a thousand questions had not one answer, it was nevertheless at the goal of its course united with faith, and bound together in one confession of faith. We have also seen how, conversely, philosophy takes faith in hand for instruction and calls it to account, but tries itself against it in vain, how it suffers one defeat after another, until it, the mistress, in the struggle with faith grows up into its pupil, and thereby grows to be a match for faith, in that it experiences its truth, and is reconciled with it.

Nevertheless a difference seems to remain, which both parties, though in different ways, are wont to acknowledge. Absolute knowing distinguishes itself from faith in that it predicates the latter as objective, and thereby means to surpass and transfigure it in form, without the content changing. Faith, on the contrary, fears from philosophy an arbitrary distortion of the content, and, with the same words and results, a different, indeed opposed sense; it does not trust its forms and ways, because it finds in them human crutches and props, which cannot prop up the divine truth but only spoil it, and are all the more dispensable as the Word of God carries and props itself. Knowing seems to think faith within itself, indeed beneath itself, faith to think knowing outside itself; it cannot, set over against philosophy, suppress the apprehension concerning certain secret [p. 119] articles of the same, and how could it promise itself, from the restless mobility of a dialectic that turns everything upon its head, the fidelity and obedience with which it itself stays by the speech of its Master, which guides it into all truth?

But not enough that faith distinguishes itself from knowing, as from an Other, it seems to distinguish itself also within itself, for in that it determines itself more closely, and thereby cognizes itself as *cognition of faith*, it falls apart within itself into *faith* and *knowing*, and finds within itself the element which it fears and flees. As knowing found faith within itself, so now faith too finds knowing within itself, for *faith* is *cognition of faith*.

Thus knowing seems to confront faith not merely outside of faith but also within it. If in this case knowing moves within faith itself, from which nevertheless it also distinguishes itself again, so in the former case faith seems to lie in knowing, because without community no comparison, without connection between the two no difference between the two would be conceivable. In so far as knowing shows itself within faith, it seems only a moment of life, but faith the whole of life; but in so far as faith emerges within knowing, faith in turn seems only a member in the realm of knowing, and the latter to assert the dominion.

This different position of faith and knowing betrays a difference in the views which faith has formed of itself, and knowing of faith, knowing of itself, and faith of knowing. From this difference we can for the time being abstract, for if hitherto what mattered to us was the relation of determinate philosophies to Christian faith, we can now be concerned only with the difference between faith and knowing in general, [p. 120] more closely with the relation between *true* faith and *true* knowing. Whereby the difference of one-sided views must settle itself; for there can be only One faith that is the true one, only One knowing that is the true one; the true faith, however, can have no other representation of knowing than the true knowing has of itself, and conversely, yet so that those different representations, too, of this and that are taken up into the truth of the Concept, and come to understanding.

In seeking the difference, we already presuppose it. Between faith and knowing there is as surely a difference as *language* has designated it clearly enough. But wherein this difference *properly* consists, about that much strife has arisen in recent times especially, and the difference between the two can at the least not be greater than the difference about this difference among men. If truth is to be the object, more closely the content, of both modes of cognition, then the difference too cannot be greater than the identity of the two. If unity cannot repel difference, then difference too cannot deny unity. For what kind of faith would that be that did not know what it believed, or what kind of knowing that had its particular conviction and its universal faith only as distinct things *alongside* one another, or what would one think of it if my religion wished to renounce my philosophy, and the latter the former?

Thus at the very entrance of our inquiry the strange spectacle shows itself that precisely the separation and tearing asunder of both concepts, in that we hold them fast, sublates each concept in its self-subsistence, and shatters the conception of the One truth in the most unpleasing and bewildering fashion. The more you divide, the sharper you separate, the less you will succeed; the result is always—Nothing here, Nothing there. The *division* can only lead us to [p. 121] abstract concepts, which have no reality because they are abstract, and so after the separation is accomplished nothing remains over that could be realized. The *holding fast* of the difference therefore affords the magical spectacle of an enchantment, for as life is stopped in its course and arrested, it is at once paralyzed, and no longer the life that we seek. In this holding fast consists truly the whole essence or non-essence of magic.

Hereby dialectic, in that it volatilizes the difference and presses for unity, casts a doubtful prognostic upon our whole inquiry. But this can at least not turn us away and frighten us off at once at the entrance; for unity we have in any case acknowledged and presupposed, otherwise we could not ask after the difference. This difference we must, precisely on account of the connection we have already recognized, pursue more closely, to see whether and how far both modes can be separated from one another, what each is for itself and without the other.

The difference could now consist *either* in the content, so that to faith and knowing a different object, a different material fell, *or* in the form, in so far as both, with the same content, might belong to a different form, perhaps a more perfect and a less perfect, *or* in both at once, in that the one also determines the other, *or* finally in this, that one of the two were only content, the other only form, hence the one the content of the other, the other the form of that.

In the latter case the difference would be found at once, but also sublated again in its self-subsistence, because it would then reduce itself to the universal difference between form and content, which themselves are not to be separated from one another, and mutually presuppose each other. If you rob the content of its shape, its expression, the form of its content, so [p. 122] nothing remains here, and nothing there; but where nothing is, there is also no difference. We do indeed find in faith the content of knowing, in knowing the form of faith, but also conversely in faith the particular form of knowing, in knowing the content which faith accepts. We thus find this and that, but a lasting difference we do not find.

Still more easily does the difference fall apart which was presupposed as possible in the first Either-Or, for with the same form the content cannot be different, with the same content the form too must be the same, because form and content mutually determine each other, and stand in living reciprocal action to one another. Consequently the difference can at least not lie merely in the content, and just as little merely in the form.

Hence nothing remains but the difference in form and content at once. But in order to discover this, we can always proceed either from the one or from the other, since it might in the first instance emerge only in one respect, only that, when once a difference results in one respect, be it with regard to the form or the content, we shall pursue it further, in order to cognize it also in the other respect.

If we look first to the *form*, the question arises whether faith and knowing are perhaps to be regarded as different stages of development, which follow upon and after one another, or as different colours of unequal value, which subsist alongside one another, in that by means of cloudiness they indeed represent the light itself, as their truth, but also, in so far, more or less dissemble and conceal, as we indeed truly see the truth, but not uncoloured, nor find the whole light of truth in its fullness, but only single rays, of which each requires the others for completion, yet one contains more light than the other. The later stage is higher than the [p. 123] earlier, and red shows more light than blue. But because this higher stage, whether it fall to knowing or to faith, does not leave the lower behind, but presupposes it, and takes it along with it, indeed is itself, in that it develops out of it, as the plant out of the root, this difference is as quickly sublated as found, since no member is without the other, none subsists for itself, none either can be regarded as the lower or the higher, because each is the other. The leaves are not merely the negation of the root, but at the same time its self-affirmation, hence the root itself.

Both modes of cognition are therefore neither after one another nor alongside one another, but with and in one another, the one already contained in the other, and so contained that of a subordination there can be no talk, neither in time nor in rank. Without the root the tree withers, without the tree the root rots; in the tree the root too is, in the root the tree already is. But the comparison also limps, it is not sufficient, because in the world of the senses what in spirit cannot be separated falls apart in space and time; and with this the last difference in form seems to dissolve. The more or less, the higher or lower, is therefore no difference *between* faith and knowing, but a difference *in* faith, as *in knowing*, hence growth in this and that is given. Your faith grows with your knowing, as your knowing with your faith, as the root with the tree, the tree with the root.

Were we to understand by faith the mustard seed from which the tree of the cognition of faith grows forth, it is yet not to be overlooked that the mustard seed in itself can be only the beginning of faith, but faith itself in its fullness is represented only by the developed mustard seed. The development of faith can be nothing other than what it itself is and contains; but if you wish to call this development cognition of faith, [p. 124] it is for that reason neither more nor less than faith.

But if quantitatively no difference can be maintained, but rather vanishes at once when we think to banish it, a qualitative difference might perhaps result, which would be less fragile; if in the form no firm difference will emerge, the question arises whether such a difference may not perhaps be descried in the object, in the content. Now this much indeed seems certain, that truth, as universal being, is only One, for truth cannot be different from itself. Were this truth now the content of faith and of knowing, nothing could be more certain than that the two could not be different as to content. If therefore the difference is to lie in the content, they cannot in any case both have truth in common with one another; rather only one member of this duality must be assigned truth, hence actuality, the other on the contrary untruth, hence unactuality, as its object. Now however for itself, that is, apart from God, the world is the untrue and unactual, or the impure and lower, which would accordingly have to be either the content of faith or of knowing.

Indeed, sometimes to knowing, as *worldly wisdom*, the world, and only the world, hence untruth, and to faith, as the *cognition of God*, absolute truth, sometimes conversely to knowing, as the higher form of cognition, the pure and highest, or the suprasensible, to faith on the contrary, as the lower mode of conception, only relative, veiled or sensible truth has been assigned exclusively as content. But precisely on this exclusivity the whole difference founders, for it leads to contradictions which fall apart in themselves. Relative truth could contain truth only in so far as the relation to the truth, after which it is named, is preserved; in so far as it [p. 125] is excluded from this relation, and a determinate form of cognition is restricted exclusively to relative truth, it ceases to be truth. This relative truth and actuality belongs to the world, to the world in its relation to God; but if it is thought without this relation, it is unactual and untrue. The exclusive cognition of the world for itself and without God would therefore be nothing other than cognizing the untrue without the light of truth, whereby the contradiction reaches its summit, for what is untrue can no more be known than believed. Thus the world too for itself—and to the world belongs man too—can be the object neither of faith nor of knowing. In order to cognize the world, it must be conceived in its relation to God; for without God it is not, and can therefore also not be cognized without cognition of God. Exclusive worldly wisdom is therefore a self-refuting contradiction.

As worldly wisdom is thus conditioned by the cognition of God, so too is the latter not to be detached from the former; you cannot cognize the one and ignore the other. To cognize the world can mean nothing other than to cognize the truth of the world, the truth in that which for itself is untrue, and this truth is God.

There is therefore no faith without knowing, no knowing without faith, for only he who cognizes God cognizes the world too, and he who cognizes the world cognizes God too; he who does not cognize the world in its sin and untruth does not cognize it in its truth either, does not cognize God. Only the truth uncovers untruth for us. He who does not cognize in sensible appearance the supernatural ground, does not cognize the appearance either. He who in the supernatural essence of God does not also cognize the nature and person of God, does not cognize the supernaturalness of God either. Where, then, does the difference *remain*? Where is cognition of world and self to be found, without [p. 126] faith? where faith without cognition of world and self?

If there is nevertheless a worldly wisdom that is separated from faith, then it moves in Nothing and around Nothing and is itself Nothing; to know of Nothing is non-knowing. But we do not ask after a difference between faith and non-knowing, but between faith and knowing. We seek and do not find; or we find differences indeed, but they do not hold, they are not lasting, in that each time they sublate themselves again.

But even if faith and knowing have One content, One object, in so far as both consist in the cognition of God and of the Kingdom of God, yet the content of knowing seems to extend further, in that it also embraces external nature and finite spirit, “which does not fall within the circuit of religion.” This is indeed again a specific difference, only that again it does not *remain* what it is, for it vanishes with the cognition of nature and of finite spirit itself, as soon as you separate these from the cognition of faith.

It shows itself here again that all knowing without faith is non-knowing. Well may there be current, concerning this and that, concerning nature and spirit, body and soul, right and state, items of information and views enough, without their springing from faith or being One with faith; but for that very reason they do not arrive at cognition, not at insight. If Lalande searched the whole heaven and found God nowhere, then, for all the heavenly bodies, he did not see heaven itself, and amid all astronomical information had to do without any astronomical cognition.

Let the heavens but turn bright, / A thousand you count, and beyond them yet.

[p. 127]

On the other side there may well also be a faith that does not penetrate into all the relations of life, not into all provinces of the particular sciences, but this again lies not in faith itself, but in the limit which it finds in the individuals. For in the nature of faith it lies rather to be a leaven, which leavens through and renews even the remotest parts of the whole. When faith sunders itself from any branch of the cognition of the world, there the right power of faith is lacking in the believers. This does not however exclude that to every individual his particular sphere of action is assigned. Rather it is for every individual necessary and salutary to cognize his limits and hold them sacred; only this limit lies not in faith in general, but in the single man.

In general, therefore, the content can ground no firm difference between faith and knowing, for what is, whatever is there, falls to faith as to knowing, without firm limits being set up between the two, without any object being reserved exclusively for faith or for knowing.

If now neither in the form nor in the content a firm, dividing difference, a determinate limit separating both domains, emerges, we must now go back once more from the content, which showed us at least fleeting differences, to the form, without abstracting from the content as we have found it. Before, in the form, because we had found as yet no content, we distinguished merely degree and measure, without being able thereby to distinguish faith and knowing. In relation to the content the question next was *what* we believe, *what* we know, and if we now go back to the form with its content, because the latter alone also did not lead to an essential difference, the question arises *how* [p. 128] we believe and know, *how* we come to this and that, and remain in it. This *How* can however be no other than the *What*, because we now no longer abstract from the content, as being. It is therefore either divine, heavenly, supernatural, hence immediate in relation to our own work in the matter, or human, worldly, natural, hence self-made, self-mediated. But even according to this, knowing and faith cannot be distinguished, for of this we have already convinced ourselves, that all worldly, natural knowing, as distinguished from divine, supernatural faith, is void. Void knowing, however, is nothing other than non-knowing. If, then, there is a knowing, if we do not wish to give up knowing for the sake of non-knowing, then it must, like faith, be divine and supernatural; as supernatural, philosophy and faith must have the Word of God as their sole foundation, and the annihilation of fallen nature, redemption from nature, as their purpose. Both are supernatural, in so far as they raise man above fallen nature, which cannot be effected by nature itself; but both are also natural in so far as they are to have as their consequence the restoration of actual nature. By this naturalness, then, they do not distinguish themselves from one another, but both together from that contentless suprasensibility on which rationalism wears itself out until it is raised above itself. It is precisely this suprasensibility, therefore, in which rationalism betrays its naturalness; this suprasensibility is in its essence like bad, fallen sensuousness, for both labour at abstraction. In so far only rationalism is suprasensible, philosophy and faith on the contrary are given back to true sensuousness, whereby we cognize both in their agreement, but not in their difference.

Again a vain attempt to find a difference! We could hold fast neither a formal nor a [p. 129] substantive, neither a quantitative nor a qualitative difference on the path we had entered, in that it dissolved itself again and again in the categories of relation and modality.

But the fault lies perhaps in the path we have entered upon, in that we ourselves first sought it. It is now time to follow the paths which others have gone; nothing remains to us but to look about us, how others have expressed themselves on this, and turned and twisted, in order to fix a difference. We must see whether these differences, too, volatilize when we fix them more sharply. To that end we hold to ready-made distinctions, as we find them, as in books and conversations they are wont to meet us pell-mell, without engaging in a classification of them, with which we might easily do them violence. We are thereby referred once more, as before, to the *singular*, as it meets us, and on occasion repeats *the Same* in different manners and expressions, in order thereby to enter into a conversation with it.

1.

*Does the difference we seek perhaps consist in thoughts?*

Knowing would then be the work of thinking, and this thinking itself. But what then remains, and in this respect, for faith? No one will, after all, call thoughtless the faith which he has himself experienced. If it gives us thoughts which are not our thoughts, we experience the same in actual thinking, which conflicts with our natural thoughts, and consists in actual thinking, not in an isolated thinking. In and with faith actual thinking is also given, and aroused to ever further development. The actuality, that is, the vitality and activity of faith, consists precisely in its signs and fruits, which from it itself

[p. 130]

follow, and with it, as the utterance with the inward, hence cohere inseparably. These fruits, however, again consist not merely in that outward activity which, after the *hands*, we preeminently call *handling* [*handeln*], but in every actual activity, as it manifests itself in disposition or in will and in thought.

It might be objected to us that we found ourselves in a self-deception, in that “the consciousness, which is indeed as it were the universal receptacle, as for our thoughts so for our feelings and acts of will, in short the unity of our being and knowing, were confounded with the thought, or the Concept, which is only a determinate activity of our I living in consciousness.” The difference between consciousness and the Concept is thus to consist in this, that consciousness bears the I, the thought on the contrary is borne by the I. “My thoughts I can produce, my consciousness not.” But of the thoughts which the isolated I itself produces there is as little question in philosophy as in faith, for both have to do only with the thoughts which the I receives. If however the difference is to lie in this, that the thought is a determinate activity, the same is to be said of consciousness, which cannot consist in an indeterminate activity. Consciousness becomes actual only by being determinate. The Concept is consciousness in its highest activity, Spirit itself, not a fragmented, torn-off, wilful product of the understanding.

If, then, philosophy is to consist in thinking, and to have its peculiar essence therein, nothing is fairer than that we leave it also entirely to it, that we do not restrict it to the degenerate varieties of thinking. But if we concede to it actual thinking, it has thereby also actual being, life, and doing, which it identifies with faith.

[p. 131]

2.

*But the true, objective faith does not, after all, begin with thinking*; it does not at least spring from thought, as knowing does, for it comes over us, and enlightens us, beyond all our thinking and understanding. Hereby faith is the beginning and foundation of all thinking; but the latter and knowing, as the result of obedient thinking, is first the fruit of faith.

It is indeed true that one’s own self-thinking must cease to make room for faith, but the same is also the condition of all actual thinking and knowing, which, like faith, comes over us without our knowing how it happens to us. We do not know it at the beginning, and of the beginning there was just now the question. In the further course, however, faith and knowing are only in consciousness, in thought. Faith and knowing come from above, as the Thought which moves, bears, purifies, determines and transfigures the human thought.

In fact, in the difference which would hold faith fast as the beginning of knowing, as beginning knowing, and knowing as the end of faith, as completed faith, the identity of both is most decidedly expressed, for faith is knowing in its beginning, and knowing in its completion is again faith in its completion. But were faith something other than knowing, and as this other the beginning of knowing, then knowing itself is without beginning, hence it comes like faith over us, and enlightens us beyond all our thinking and understanding.

3.

Or are we to repeat the same in other expressions and turns, in order to grasp the difference from another side? *Faith*, so many say, *comes without our cooperation*, knowing

[p. 132]

*presupposes self-activity*, hence the former is immediate, the latter mediated by ourselves, faith is of supernatural, knowing of natural origin, the former does not come out of us, but into us, but the latter generates itself out of itself, and does not deny its void origin, it is as corrupt and darkened as the subject that begets it. But all these differences volatilize at once as we rectify and determine the indeterminate expressions in which they move.

Out of itself knowing does indeed generate itself,—therein consists its immanent actuality,—as faith develops out of itself, and thereby becomes certainty. But precisely for that reason knowing generates itself out of the single subject as little as faith does. Knowing would rather not generate itself out of itself if it produced itself independently out of the subject, or could even merely reproduce. Knowing would no longer be what it is, truth, that is, no longer the living, universal, divine being, if it were the birth of the non-universal. Science may indeed often enough have gone astray into such aberrations in single subjects, and thereby have become sin to sinful man, but these aberrations are not science itself. If it is reproached to science that it “wants to generate its entire content itself,” then this is either, as we have shown, no reproach, for whence is truth to come otherwise than from truth? or the reproach rests on a confusion of what comes out of itself with what comes out of the isolated subject. In the latter case it means actually the opposite of what it says, namely that knowing comes not out of itself, but from elsewhere, namely out of the subject, hence is and remains untrue, like the latter. But this reproach cannot be made to philosophy itself, but only to the single, erring philosopher. The actual [p. 133] philosopher regards knowing not as *his* knowing, his knowing not as *his* work and property, nor as a fruit of subjective faculties of spirit. For, so philosophy itself teaches,—for if the faculty of spirit of the creature is still the immediate power of God in the creature, then it is not subjective, but it is God himself immediately who generates knowing in the creature, in that He does not merely give the faculty once for all and then withdraw, and leave the subject to itself, but he who gives the faculty also himself works the accomplishing. In this case God is not only the A[lpha], but also the O[mega]. But if that faculty in the creature has become subjective, it is no longer the faculty it originally was, no longer the power of God in the subject, but—the power of the subject, hence a contradiction which falls apart in itself, for what is outside God is outside power. In that the power which comes from God is separated from God and left to the subject alone, it has already turned over into its opposite, into sheer incapacity. Now God has indeed been the A[lpha]—but not the O[mega].

“What I know,” so our opponents say, “that I need not believe; I believe only what I do not know; faith first enters where my knowing ceases.” The only question is what is understood by knowing in this statement of the first immediate faith. If we illuminate this objection more closely, determine and survey it, it can want to say nothing other than what is self-evident, even if it means something other than it says. What I can procure for myself, so runs the translation of those indeterminate phrases, that I need not have given to me; what I have out of and in myself, that I need not have bestowed on me: only when my own property is exhausted and does not suffice for my necessary subsistence do I consent to alms. The objection thus means my knowing, my own work of [p. 134] knowing, which consists in non-knowing and begins and ends with it, but not the actual knowing. If, then, knowing is human knowing, the difference is certainly clear: it is the difference between knowing and non-knowing; if, on the other hand, knowing is the gift of God through his Spirit, and the latter’s revelation and communication, then it is One with faith. Out of His mouth come cognition and understanding. Prov. 2:6.

If it is objected to us that philosophy too, to be sure, must resign itself to acknowledging the gifts of God, but then presumes to deal freely with them, and to trade at usury, on its own account, with the pound once given and thereby left to itself, it must reject this accusation, which touches only rationalism, altogether, since it itself first teaches and proves that precisely such a separation of the gift from the Giver does not proceed from him, but would be self-willed self-emancipation, hence a denial of the Giver, a misuse of the gift, which is factually acknowledged as a gift only by this, that it is not torn away from the Giver, but is received with thanksgiving as an uninterruptedly and steadily flowing fountain of grace.

What difference, then, remains between faith and knowing, since both have to expect not merely their capacity but also its realization only from God and out of God? Here and there the beginning is the same, namely that man, convicted of his subjectivity, hence of his own incapacity, trusts nothing to himself, everything to the truth—nothing to himself, because he seeks, hence does not have,—everything to the truth, because otherwise he would not seek, unless he hoped to find, because he would have to abandon himself to non-knowing, were he not animated by a certain confidence of that which he hopes for, and a not-doubting of that which he does not yet see.

[p. 135]

From this it follows at the same time that non-knowing, as the non-knowing of man out of himself, is as proper to actual knowing as it is to faith, and again, that natural, selfish, self-willed thinking is just as alien and opposed to that knowing as to faith.

Such trust we have through Christ toward God, not that we build upon ourselves through ourselves, as at Babel, but that through Christ we trust in God; not that we are competent of ourselves to think anything as from ourselves, but that we are competent to think and to comprehend (λογίσασθαι) something, that is, from God and out of God, and through Christ, who is the Logos, who teaches us logic, and has humbled himself that we may learn to know and comprehend him.

4.

But already the difference, hardly dismissed, returns in other words. Faith teaches *praying*, philosophy *working*. To believe is to pray, to think is to work. But is prayer without work, work without prayer, then actual and effective? And is this work, then, our selfish activity, and prayer idle because it *receives* the activity? Or is prayer only the devotion that thinks, to be sure, on God, but not at the same time in God?

Everywhere it is confirmed that praying and working are themselves only inseparable members of one whole, like faith and good works. Who dares to tear the whole apart?

5.

Philosophy wishes to show us the way *how man may arrive at God*, but faith reveals to us the way *how God comes to man*.

If the reproach of subjectivity is hereby to be made against philosophy, it is in the above [p. 136] already dismissed. But if this reproach is set aside, then no difference remains either, for it cannot fail that man comes to God in that God comes to man, only that throughout the former is conditioned by the latter.

The way back to God is mediated by God himself, in that He comes to man; to this extent all cognition of God is possible only through mediation. He mediates this way immediately through himself; to this extent the way is immediate, knowing as well as faith.

6.

If, nevertheless, an *immediacy* is ascribed to faith which is not supposed to belong to knowing, then it must be asked more closely what is to be understood by this immediacy. Purely immediate God himself is not, for he is mediated through himself. If, now, faith is immediate, it is so only insofar as it is mediated from God and through God. In this sense it is immediate in its beginning, immediate in its progress, for he who has begun the work will also complete it. The same holds also of knowing, as we have seen.

But this immediacy does not exclude mediation. This shows itself in the beginning as in the further movement of faith; this further movement is itself nothing other than mediation, working-up, *steady* development, whether it take place in a sequence articulated according to the rules of inference and art, or in a connection outwardly less visible; all working-up presupposes articulation, even if not every single member steps forth determinately.

Immediate, then, are faith and knowing, as given, preserved, furthered by God Himself, consequently insofar as both do not find beginning and progress in a third; in themselves both are mediated, because member develops itself upon member, and the content in its parts hangs together with one another, without the subject [p. 137] adding anything, for in One everything is given to it; but both are mediating insofar as they presuppose a relation of man to God, which itself is no longer immediate, but requires mediation through God himself.

Since both first come to be, they proceed from the premise that the original immediate communion with God has been interrupted. It is proper only to non-knowing, only to subjective faith, to dream of an uninterrupted communion with God, and to scorn the mediation without which the restoration of the communion is impossible.

7.

But *immediacy* has yet other sides, which seem to belong exclusively to faith, for it needs no human capacity in order to work through it, it much rather destroys and shatters all human power in order to impart its own power, *whereas knowing comes to us through the cloudy medium of human reason*, and hence is itself clouded.

It is just as true that faith does not need human reason in order to gain entry as that it works upon it and through it. How could it be perceived without reason? Or if you wish to call the medium through which faith communicates itself *heart*, is this heart, then, before faith less corrupted and perverted than reason, which is supposed to be the peculiar organ of knowing? Is this heart not itself reason, in that it perceives what is given and revealed to it? To be sure, reason as a fallen, isolated, human capacity is impure; but what it perceives is pure, and by its content purifies the impure vessel, the impure form, which perceives, namely reason itself. In that reason perceives the truth, it is, into the universal reason, as the absolute reality,

[p. 138]

transposed. Without this rebirth no faith, no knowing thrives.

8.

In this respect, too, faith seems to be immediate, *that it delivers all at once, wholly and essentially, the truth which comes to knowing piecemeal, and is set before it as an infinite task*, so that it indeed receives light at all times, but is also to receive ever more.

But that property of faith belongs to knowing also, just as this property of knowing accrues to faith also.

If faith has life, then on its part, too, life consists in becoming and growth. Therefore the Word of God is compared to a water which becomes ever deeper and deeper the further we advance in faith, and suffices for the child as for the giant, and offers itself wholly to the one as to the other, but is never exhausted. It is true that faith, as the unconditioned trust, wholly independent of subjective judgment, in the truth of the Word of God, delivers the entire content of revelation all at once; we do not first wait now for understanding in detail, we accept in faith what is disclosed and what is not yet disclosed with equal trust. But it is also just as true, first, that this faith in general already presupposes the appropriation of that trust through experience,—all appropriation, however, is understanding,—secondly, that it strives to appropriate the given to the subject more and more, as well in general as in particulars and piecemeal, and that it ascribes this to its own activity, and to no act of knowing different from it.

On the other hand, knowing too is disclosed all at once in general, given entire in its principle; but thereby, to be sure, the ever-continuing penetration of the whole, like the expansion in detail

[p. 139]

is not excluded. Absolute knowing is knowing of the Absolute, hence whole, but for that reason not closed off, not finished once for all.

If Augustine says: *Credo ut intelligam* [I believe in order to understand], this, more closely developed, is nothing other than: *Credo ut credam, intelligo ut intelligam* [I believe in order to believe, I understand in order to understand]. If it has been said: *Ex fide in cognitionem ascenditur* [From faith one ascends to cognition], this is not truer than the simpler: *Ex fide in fidem, ex cognitione in cognitionem ascenditur* [From faith one ascends to faith, from cognition to cognition]. What is to develop out of faith can be nothing other than faith, and if knowing develops out of faith, faith out of knowing, then the identity of both is already expressed.

9.

*Here we believe, there we shall behold.* Hereby the difference between faith and beholding is expressed, but not the difference between faith and knowing, for the latter too, in this respect, steps to the side of faith. The immediate revelation of God, on which faith and knowing rest, is given in the *Word*; but the highest immediacy is promised in *beholding*, in that we are to *see* Him as He is, and face to face. Our knowing is fragmentary, and our prophesying is fragmentary, and our believing is imperfect. But when the perfect comes, the fragmentary will cease.—Blessed are those who do not see, and yet believe. John 20:29. Blessed are those who cannot grasp with their natural understanding the wondrous mysteries of God, and yet attain to insight into them and cognition of them.—Blessed, however, are also the eyes which, what they see sensibly, also see and recognize spiritually. Luke 10:23. John 14:9.

10.

*But does faith prove itself in another activity of spirit than knowing?* That proceeds from the heart, hence from the will, this

[p. 140]

from the understanding. By their fruits you shall know them; the fruits point back to the sources.

Indeed there are such degenerate forms of knowing as touch only individual parts of the spirit, without filling it itself through and through, but in the essence of the Concept there lies nothing of this. And it would not be difficult to point out such degenerate forms of faith too in experience.

Our time, however, is charged in particular, as the error peculiar to it “in the domain of cognition,” with nothing less than “the tearing loose of the intellectual power from its natural connection with our feeling and acting being, and the rendering independent of the thinking power into a usurped freedom and dominion in the domain of all being and life.” And we must sharpen this reproach, hence turn it back upon itself. It itself falls under the reproach it makes to its time, when it posits a domain of cognition beside the domain of all being and life. But it falls also, secondly, itself into error by calling the postulated connection natural, whereas it has been torn apart precisely through *naturalness*. If the connection was original, it is not therefore natural; natural is rather the disunion of the powers of spirit in natural man.

So, too, it has been charged against philosophy in general that it wants merely “to come to God by the intellectual way,” whereas “the way proceeds from the heart, and goes on along the highway of faith?”

But what kind of philosophy is that which does not lay claim to all activity of spirit at the same time, and pass over into the disposition, and what kind of faith is that which does not enlighten the understanding, and does not renew the whole man? If philosophy is really spirit, then it must also proceed out of the whole spirit, pervade the whole spirit, and what was dead [p. 141] make alive, what was torn and dismembered, join together. How, then, can it be laid to the charge of science if “our living, inner self is torn out of its joints through dismemberment, or made into a sum in arithmetic?”

There may well be a light in this world of sin which shines but does not warm, a light by which we freeze, and a fire which warms but does not shine; but is that little night-lamp with which Faust thinks to redeem himself and Gretchen the actual knowing, and this gloomy, lightless fire the true faith? Is the actual understanding, then, according to its true essence, the understanding left to itself, the Concept, according to its actuality, the dead, abstract Concept? If knowing does not warm and improve, then neither does it truly enlighten; if faith does not enlighten, then it has not yet warmed the cold heart either, not yet broken the evil will. Truly, nothing is more foreign to faith, but also nothing so unscientific, as such dismemberment. A philosophy without heart, and a faith without understanding, are themselves mere abstractions from the true life and being of knowing and of faith.

But whoever is left cold by philosophy, or whomsoever actual faith does not enlighten, let him look well to where the fault lies. It lies in him, not in knowing, not in faith. The former finds himself still outside philosophy, the latter outside faith.

11.

If knowing and faith are distinguished as theory and practice, as being and life, as looking on and acting, as word and deed, then this is the same difference which we have just dismissed; it is a difference in which that opposition, which wished to divide the two ways as working and praying, simply turns around. Theory stands still before the objects, practice appropriates them to the subject, and transforms

[p. 142]

them into sap and blood. But does not faith also go upon *objects*, since it consists essentially in the cognition of the objective God and of his hypostases and his great deeds? And does not knowing, on the other hand, lead us to the *cognition* of objects, hence to practical appropriation? In this respect too knowing is the more precise determination of faith, faith the more precise determination of knowing; we could say that the one is the property of the other, if only by property something is not understood that merely hangs on the thing, and plays alongside it, without being the thing itself. The whole relation of man to God and world is purely *theoretical*, for he has nothing to make himself, but to see what is made, but also purely *practical*, insofar as, though not he himself, yet God lives and works in him.—If the world cries out time and again: Act! Act! and cries itself hoarse over it, it betrays thereby not only its selfhood, its delight in making for itself, as if the universe depended on it, and its unbelief in the activity of God, as if this had to wait for our activity, but also its blind ignorance of the relation between God and man, between theory and practice.

If eternal *life* consists in this, that we *cognize* God and Jesus, as the truth, John 17:3, if hence life consists in cognizing, then cognizing, as theory, cannot be opposed to life, to the life of faith, as practice; rather, cognizing is like faith, according to Scripture, John 3:16; 17:3, in this, that both are life and work life. All life of the creature consists in the cognition of God; doing and creating He has reserved to Himself, but he does and works in the creature, as in his instrument. Only in sin is there the creature’s own activity, self-activity; where there is no sin, the life of the creature consists in this, that we look on at what God does and [p. 143] works, and does, what he does and works in us. Only in this respect, and only in this respect, does it make sense when man is expected to help increase the sum of the good, and to further the kingdom of God.

12.

The difference may be found, however, perhaps if we once more invert the relation of faith and knowing.

*Faith has the positive, real, substantial, objective, knowing on the other hand the negative, ideal, formal, subjective, as its theme*; that teaches the existence of the essence, this the essence of existence, faith realizes the Idea, philosophy idealizes reality, faith rests on facts, philosophy has to do with their sublation, in order to arrive at thought, or in other words, faith is the content, the substance itself, philosophy the form!

Nothing but expressions for the same difference! And so speak not only those who are touched neither by faith nor by knowing, but also others, who one-sidedly put to the account of the one what just as well must be reckoned to the other.

The faith in the absolute *reality* of God is at the same time a *cognition* of God according to his essence, and hereby the idealization is accomplished. In that philosophy strives to sublate the facts, that is, to explain them according to their essence, to transfigure intuition into insight, it evidently also rests on facts, which it does not alter but takes up into itself, whereby it keeps pace with faith, which also on its part has to think the given after the fact, to appropriate it, and to possess it as something thought, and hereby to prove itself as living faith. In this [p. 144] respect thinking has been designated by language more precisely as *after-thinking* [Nachdenken], which faith, if it wishes to subsist for itself, can also not take from itself, and cannot leave to knowing, as an Other of itself.

In after-thinking it is already contained that thinking, like faith, proceeds from the given, from being, and follows after it, and that being is to be regarded as prejudice until it is thought in faith and knowing, and thereby becomes identical with faith and knowing as *consciousness*, whence it follows that both, faith and knowing, as identical with a third, being, are also identical with one another among themselves.—First being is given as prejudice in representation, then in judgment it becomes unequal to itself and at odds with itself, until in the Concept it becomes equal to itself, the form equal to the content.

That the given, transcendent object comes to life in us, and insofar becomes subjective, immanent, without ceasing to be itself, objective, both modes of cognition therefore have in common. They both become actual only as system, that is, only as an all-embracing unity, as a self-supporting whole, which takes its opposite, as what is outside it, into itself, and teaches us to know the relations of the objects among one another.

Faith is not “crude realism,” knowing not “pure idealism.” But that the content is not without its form, the form not without content, rather the content is its form, and the form cannot be separated from its content, has already been said above.

Philosophy acknowledges the objective as being, which is transfigured in consciousness; it thereby leads it back to its ground, which is spirit, and acknowledges in the ground nothing but spirit. Herein, however, faith agrees completely with it. Or is this spirit, then, the subject of the singularized [p. 145] I? Is not rather this subject, as such, for itself, itself only objective? and, as objective, itself sublated?

And yet philosophy, as science, must have thrown at it, by the faith which agrees with it literally in the result, in the confession, “a confusion of the condition (subject) with the object, the transference of something merely subjective into the realm of the objective,” and must let itself be accused of a self-deception which it itself first brought to consciousness under the name of a “hypostasis.” To be sure, the actual hypostasis, the truth of it, is only “in the highest Spirit,” as the unity of being and knowing, of the objective and the subjective; but does not this highest Spirit, as the Spirit of God, impart his Spirit to all those who believe in him and ask him for it? Does philosophy, then, seek “the absolute Spirit in the human spirit,” since with faith it rests upon the communication of the Holy Spirit?

It is charged against it that it knows how to speak much of universal reason, and of the sole validity of the objective Spirit, but thereby only smuggles in subjective reason by another way. But why is it, then, charged against it alone, when it comes to a fall in the individual? Or has it not also befallen faith that, darkened by sin, it has become sin, and thereby the spirit of the subject, the own will of the individual, has substituted itself for the Spirit of God, the Holy Spirit, and by a secret back door has smuggled itself in again? Is there not also a spiritual pride opposed to vain pride of reason? If this is to be put to the account not of faith but of unbelief, then that is also to be ascribed not to philosophy but to its fall, to unphilosophy.

It is true, indeed, that the self-externalization taken by itself is still very problematic, for it is a question what [p. 146] takes the place of the externalized self. Empty the spirit of man never is; either God fills it, or the evil spirit. If God fills it, then it comes to faith and to cognition. By these fruits it can recognize whether God has really filled it: they work peace, but not the peace which the world gives.

13.

Or if we look back once more, in this respect too, in respect of the given, objective reality of being, from the movements, strivings and results of both activities of spirit, as though they were two different ones, to their *beginning*, then here too the difference at once confronts us, that philosophy, far from acknowledging anything outside itself, presupposes nothing, begins with nothing, as though it had first to create being, whereas faith according to its own peculiar essence consists in the presupposition, in the trust in the reality already given.

And yet the Nothing also precedes faith, which we first escape through it. On the other hand, all philosophy too rests on presupposition, for if it presupposed nothing it could have nothing to seek, it merely looks on how the given reproduces itself for it. And if, nevertheless, it pretends to presuppose Nothing, then this Nothing is itself a presupposition, nothing else is thereby designated than the beginning, out of which everything comes to be, not the Nothing, out of which nothing comes to be.

14.

But we must go more closely into the beginning of both activities of spirit, as different ones; here is the place where we must put philosophy, as knowing, to a more specific examination, test it more determinately, and ask it on its conscience, because it slips away from us only too easily through general statements.

[p. 147]

It supposes that it acknowledges the object, attaches itself to the given, indeed is the given itself; it wants to take the truth only from substance, and thereby to set itself on a par with faith; it asserts the objectivity and substantiality of truth, which is, to be sure, subjectivized, appropriated in knowing, yet in such a way that the single subject adds nothing to it, and takes nothing from it. Therefore we want to take it at its word, and call it to account, in order to learn how matters actually stand with it.

Where, then, is a philosophy that would proceed from *Holy Scripture*, and acknowledge the Bible as its sole and supreme principle? Here the true actuality and objectivity is given in the Word of God. Is this, then, in writing and to the letter, as it is given and preserved, the principle and foundation, the sole source and presupposition of philosophy? It may well have borrowed one thing and another from it,—and what is true in it is indeed only filched from it,—but does it acknowledge the Bible as the giver, or does it not think to have found the same independently, by another way, namely out of itself?

Out of itself? On that we have already come to an understanding. Out of itself knowing indeed has everything that it has, insofar as it grasps itself as immanent, as being in the truth. But the meaning of this question was only whether *human* reason does not imagine that it has the truth, which it first stole from the Bible, out of itself?

How could we deny this? Of that we make manifold experiences daily: we see, indeed, how precisely those who commend *their* reason as a *gift* of God nevertheless use this gift not as gift, not as a present, but as an absolute, independent property. Only that this rationalism of natural reason has nothing to do with speculative philosophy, since it much rather, precisely on that account, [p. 148] despises and scorns all actual philosophy, like objective Bible-faith.

Therefore we ask more closely after the proper relation of speculative philosophy, as the proper science, to the *Bible*. To faith the Bible is the source and rule of all revelation. Is it this now also to philosophy, which likewise wants to ground itself on objective truth, but always lets itself be heard about it only in general, and for precisely that reason was accused of hypostatization?

So much lies outwardly in plain view, that it expressly does not proceed from the Bible, but from the subject. At most it comes, after long detours, on its own ways at last to the Bible, instead of proceeding from it, as if it had first to furnish a foundation for Scripture, which is supposed to be its ground, and had to prepare a base and support for the truth from somewhere else. For if the science of reason agrees with the Bible in its results, if it also coincides with it at the end of its career not merely according to the words but in deed and truth, it nevertheless seems to have come to the goal by another way, and not to have derived its result from the Bible, so that two revelations seem to meet and thereby to confirm one another, in that faith, on the ground of Scripture, philosophy on the other hand, on the ground of a universal objective reason, which distinguishes itself from this given Bible, as *this* one, by universality, go their particular way, and only at last in the confession reach the same goal.

Herewith the whole difference around which we move comes down to the difference between holy Scripture and universal, objective reason. By universal reason, however, we understand nothing other than the Spirit of God, who, according to Scripture, is communicated to us in faith and unto faith. Insofar, now, as all Scripture is inspired by the Spirit of God, [p. 149] insofar the difference is also again sublated. But insofar as it nevertheless maintains itself and makes itself felt, faith is touched by it no less than knowing. For no one understands Holy Scripture except through the Holy Spirit. It is He who opens the understanding of the Bible, which he himself dictated, to each individual. So long as we do not let ourselves be moved and enlightened by the Spirit of its Author, so long do we also not understand the Bible. Not the Bible, therefore, but the Spirit of its Author, in that he becomes the universal, common Spirit, is the beginning and the living principle of all faith.

If it is now said that philosophy does not proceed from the Bible, but is driven to it only at last, then there is further the difference not to be overlooked between the philosophy which *becomes*, in that it *seeks*, and the philosophy which *is*. In becoming, and seeking, it does, to be sure, not yet proceed from that which it seeks; while it still seeks, it seeks the truth which it apprehends in the Bible, and in it itself. So, too, faith, while it becomes, proceeds not from the Bible, but toward the Bible, in which it grasps the truth, and thereby itself. It is a prejudice to seek the principle, and with it the Concept, of philosophy in its point of departure, in its beginning, since both are rather to be found, as One, first in its completion.

But this too science has in common with faith, that both must ever again begin anew, and, when they are there, ever again also become, if they have recognized Scripture as the Word of God, and could now proceed from it, must ever again also go toward it. Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!

The Bible is essentially history, it rests on facts, and consists of facts; it treats of the sin of the human race and of the great deeds of God in relation to it. We [p. 150] begin with this, that we *marvel at* them and *look at* them, until, enlightened by the Spirit of God, we *see into* them more and more in faith and knowing, and learn to laud and praise.

15.

The heart is restless and remains restless, wherever it may seek rest, until it finds rest in You, O Lord! Therefore faith, once it has found, holds fast unwaveringly to the *personality of the living God*, and rests not in its own, but in the self-consciousness of the absolute Spirit; for what could lead it astray in it itself? But reason, which does not give itself up to it, but is left to itself, presses on constantly to *negation*,—it cannot do otherwise,—and does not rest until the highest personality also sublates itself in it; indeed it would have to charge God himself with abstraction, according to its principles left to themselves, if it did not want to negate Him in His being-for-self. Only by divesting Him of his being-for-self, as the last, most obstinate abstraction, and with it of all personality, does it think it can acquit Him of the evil which consists precisely in this abstraction.

Of this we must grant one thing and another, only not the difference between faith and knowing. It is certainly not to be disputed that according to a consistent philosophy, unless it will turn aside halfway, God keeps nothing for himself, and only in the externalization of himself is He Himself, namely God, good. But is, then, in God the externalization of Himself a loss of Himself? or the communication of His Spirit and essence a cessation of being in Himself? Or does, conversely, the creature thereby become God Himself, that the divine being is communicated to it? And does not the difference consist precisely in this, that it is *first communicated, given*, to it? What God

[p. 151]

has from Himself, that has the creature not from itself, but from Him?

Here, for faith as for knowing, eyes of the Spirit are needed! Where they are not yet given to us, there we must remain standing in obedience, and adore. How could you, indeed, transfer to this domain the representations of an abstract world of sense? What you reproach philosophy with, that faith also teaches you, that you accept daily obediently in faith. For what does He keep for Himself, who has given up and sacrificed His only-begotten Son, and in Him Himself, for us? *God is love*, and love consists in this, not to be for itself and in itself, but to be in the Other, as in itself.

Often enough the sublation of being-for-self is charged against philosophy as a self-willed outrage peculiar to it, whereby it endangers personality. But the more we are deemed worthy of entering into the depths of the revelation of God, the more will it be confirmed that even immediately in Scripture this being-for-self sublates itself, and in the being in and for itself comes to reconciliation.

Being-for-self, being-in-itself, being-from-itself can certainly belong only to God, whereas in man, in that he wants to seize it for himself, it becomes sin; indeed sin consists in this, that we want to be like God, and want to be ourselves that essence of God, to be for itself, in itself, and from itself. This is *certainly the first*, but precisely only the first, not the last.

For hereby we fall at once into the contradiction, first, that what is good in God, hence absolutely good, is nevertheless not good in man, hence cannot be absolutely good, and secondly, that what at the beginning brings about the Fall, indeed is the Fall itself, Gen. 3:5, is on the other hand again set before us as the goal, commanded in the Law, Lev. 11:44–45; 19:1; Matt. 5:48; Mark 12:30–31, and promised in the Gospel, 1 John 3:2.

[p. 152]

But this contradiction, at which we need, to be sure, not stumble if the Bible led us only up to here but no further, at which we also in fact do not stumble so long as revelation, for a trial in obedience, actually lets it stand, this contradiction resolves itself in Scripture, in revelation itself, in that that being-for-self of God, the self-sufficiency of God, sublates itself, in that God himself abolishes his self-sufficiency, and thus reveals himself to us.

Truly God’s essence reveals itself as the sublation of his being-for-self, and not only in relation to the world, from which he does not withhold himself, as we have already said, for, we say it once more, what, then, does he keep for himself who gives himself up? John 3:16. Phil. 2:6–8, but also in relation to his own essence, through the hypostases, whereby he is Himself in the other Person, and thereby manifests himself as the Spirit existing in and for itself.

In these hypostases, revealed to us by God’s overflowing grace, and revealing themselves ever more each day, it is revealed that in God too love requires an Other than itself, and that it can love this Other only as person, for both belong to the essence of love. We speak even with a flower, as with a person, when we love it, and call it thou, whereby we personify it, in that we love it. In these distinct Persons of God, who sublate the self-sufficient being-for-self, it is also revealed that *the sublation of this being-for-self endangers personality so little that this is rather first thereby raised to itself and multiplied*. Now God too loves not himself, not abstractly himself, but he loves the other Persons of his essence as himself, as Persons of the Godhead, just as we are to love our neighbor as ourselves, as beings who we ourselves are, as persons of humanity. Now there opens itself [p. 153] us also in the Persons of the holy Trinity a reciprocal giving and taking, a receiving and giving back again, being in itself and being in the Other. The Father has *given* to the Son, not creaturely life, but the life in Himself. What belongs to the Father belongs also to the Son, and what belongs to the Son belongs also to the Father. The Son does not do his own will, but the will of the Father. And the Holy Spirit proceeds from both, He is in both, and both are in Him, and no less is He, as the third Person, in Himself; and again each Person in the other, each God out of God. But the Triunity does not sublate the threefoldness. The Father has everything from himself, the Son has everything from the Father, but as in Himself, the Spirit proceeds from the Father and from the Son, only that no Person remains for itself, but is ever again in the other, back into the other, and out of the other proceeds again.

Hereby, then, being-for-self is sublated and transfigured in being-in-and-for-itself, which is the *second*; a result in which again Scripture and philosophy, faith and knowing agree, if both are not the same, which they at least are in this respect.

But also in other respects the reproach of pantheism, which is supposed to separate knowing from faith, is disposed of.

Not to philosophy, but to the natural understanding is the universe the revealed God himself, *Deus explicitus*, the world-force the hidden God, *Deus implicitus*. Philosophy and faith, on the other hand, lead, on the ground of revelation, to the *Person* of God, who is beyond the world and within the world at once, and to the Persons of God, from whom, and through whom, and in whom is everything that is. Or is God less personal because he is All in All, or is he less All in All because he is personal?

[p. 154]

Whoever accuses science of pantheism, and by the deified All understands the world, nature, and creature, himself falls into the pantheism of the sensuous understanding, in that he regards world and All as one, whereas God and All are one, in that he ascribes to the world a reality that belongs to God alone, and distinguishes God and All, as if God were not All in All. Science, on the contrary, confesses, in agreement with faith, that outside God there is nothing.

16.

“Philosophy places its highest pride and lays its greatest weight on proof, that is, on the demonstration of the necessity of its content. To show the necessity of its content is its first concern. How else could God be comprehended?”

“But only the God of this philosophy lets himself be comprehended, the God of revelation not. Of Him it is written: Lord, unsearchable are your ways! The fact of the Incarnation of the divine Son stands in divine revelation as an act of *love*; but where *necessity* enters, love ceases.”

Yet this objection has already been disposed of; this difference between faith and knowing has already dissolved itself in the consideration of a determinate philosophy. It rests on a sad confusion of external compulsion with inner necessity. Because God according to his essence is love, love also urges and impels Him; He can be nothing other than He Himself. Herewith he proves and reveals himself as absolute freedom, in that he is his innermost essence, love, moved by himself, constrained by no other, hence at the same time as absolute necessity, which is identical with essentiality. What else there might be to say about conceptuality and about the demonstrability [p. 155] of philosophy will at once be developed more closely.

17.

*Faith believes revelation as the absolute truth even where it does not comprehend, where it is not identical with knowing,* in that it subjects and subordinates every single subject to the given, as to what is given by God. Knowing, on the other hand, acknowledges nothing that it cannot comprehend, or rather, that does not appropriate itself to the individual subject, for through such acknowledging it would cease to be cognizing?

Such disobedience may well exist in the field of cognition, but true science never makes revealed truth dependent on the measure of cognition that is now given to the individual. There is a subjective, undisciplined thinking which would raise abstract incapacity to the highest authority; but is this philosophy? Is rationalism reason? Science consists, to be sure, in comprehending, as faith does in experiencing; but for that reason it cannot occur to the individual to comprehend or to live through everything singular himself, and to make its existence depend on this, or to reject as irrational what is repugnant to natural reason. *Apud Te, Domine, rerum omnium irrationabilium sempiternae vivunt rationes.* [With You, Lord, live the eternal reasons of all irrational things.] Does mathematics, then, reject the phenomena which it designates as irrational because they elude its formulas? Faith, too, asserts its rationality, the rationality and actuality of all its doctrines, without thereby being able to make this or that doctrine dependent on the weak, perverted reason of the natural man. Something can be rational without my understanding understanding it, indeed without my reason perceiving it; one or the other can also elude the faith of the individual without its therefore

[p. 156]

being any less revealed. In general, however, the reproach of subjectivity that is usually made against thinking has already been rebutted above; there really is a subjective thinking, we have already conceded that, as there is a subjective, self-made faith, but as the latter cannot be foisted upon objective faith, so the former, too, cannot be regarded as in accord with true thinking.

18.

The difference, too, between *living-through* and *comprehending*, *experiencing* and *cognizing* cannot be carried out. The heart can live through and experience nothing, the understanding can comprehend and cognize nothing, but only the spirit in its totality. Such dismemberment is once and for all rejected. As reason belongs to knowing, as its content, so understanding belongs to faith, as its form.

19.

*But philosophy brings to the word, to the Concept, what faith receives but cannot give out of itself?*

As if faith did not receive precisely the Word. What it receives does not consist in indeterminate, dissolving feelings; it has marrow and bone, and pierces marrow and bone. Those who received the spirit of faith also received the spirit of tongues, as the Spirit of God.

But faith does acknowledge, in the most decided expressions, the inexpressibility and incomprehensibility of God. Rom. 11:33–36. 1 Cor. 13:12. 1 John 3:2. Ps. 139:6. 92:6–7. Isa. 55:8–9. Philosophy, on the other hand, believes it does not have God if it does not comprehend him. Yet Scripture also teaches that God is known to *His own*, John 10:14. Acts 17:23. Conversely, philosophy, too, holds to what it does not comprehend, in order to learn to comprehend it.

[p. 157]

Yet God certainly is and remains incomprehensible, first, in so far as the human understanding left to itself cannot comprehend him—man does not cognize God from himself, but from God, in that God reveals himself—secondly, in so far as the essence of God cannot be dissolved into abstract formulas—but does the circle withdraw from the concept because it resists quadrature?—and thirdly, in so far as the absolute Concept, absolute cognition, according to the very essence of the Absolute has no end, and is consequently unfathomable and inexhaustible, in so far as absolute comprehensibility, regarded finitely, is infinite, hence in a finite manner incomprehensible.

Precisely in this incomprehensibility and unsearchableness of God for the *human* understanding the Apostle demonstrates the depth of the riches of the *divine* wisdom and cognition *revealed to us*, which, like the unfathomable and inexhaustible sea of Ezekiel, the more it discloses itself to us, the deeper we enter, proves itself ever higher and ever deeper, ever richer and ever simpler. For who has cognized the mind of the Lord from himself, or thought it out? Or who has given God anything beforehand? How poor is man without God, how exceedingly rich through God! Or is the Concept less rich that is exceedingly rich and inexhaustible? So the very word of the Apostle, which seems unconditionally to confirm the unknowability of God, teaches the unfathomably deep riches of the cognition of God through revelation, by which it is conditioned. And this is confirmed by the fact that the supernatural light of this revelation is set over against the darkness of natural non-knowing. Rom. 11:33–36.

Indeed it is therefore faith which, given from above, makes the incomprehensible comprehensible and searches out the unsearchable, without being held by a finite limit. “What no eye has seen, and no ear has heard, and into no man’s heart has [p. 158] entered, what God has prepared for those who love him; but to us God has revealed it through *His* Spirit. *For the Spirit searches all things, even the depths of the Godhead.*” What is in God, no one knows, except the Spirit of God. But we have not received the spirit of the world, but the Spirit from God, that we may know what is given to us by God. 1 Cor. 2:9 ff. Therefore it is given to Christians to know the mystery of the kingdom of God; but to others it is not given, except in parables and representations, for mere representations are images and parables. Matt. 13:11. Luke 8:10. The unutterable words which Paul heard in his rapture must have been uttered, because he heard them; he says only that no man, as such, can utter them.

But the difference between the objective Concept, in which philosophy moves and which compels to cognition, and the inner experience to which faith appeals, without being able to communicate it, still seems not to have been resolved. Jacobi testifies from his own, manifold experience that for the Bible-believers in dispute with philosophers there remains finally nothing left but to withdraw personally into the strong fortress of individual feeling or of individual experience, and to let those who are outside stand outside<sup>∗)</sup>. Here, then, faith lets the other stand outside. Elsewhere it has been said: “When a man, regarding something, appeals not to the nature and concept of the thing, or at least to grounds, the *universality of the understanding*, but to his feeling, then there is nothing else to be done than to let him stand, because he thereby refuses himself the *community* of rationality, and shuts himself up in his isolated subjectivity,

∗) Jacobi’s *Correspondence.* II, 458.

[p. 159]

particularity.”<sup>∗)</sup> Here, on the contrary, it seems to be philosophy that lets feeling, and hence also faith, which appeals only to its particular feeling, stand outside, and thereby thinks to repay like with like.

It is true: no one can give another his faith. Each must be taught immediately by God himself. When Christians are called upon: “Give us of your oil,” they would gladly do so, but they cannot; with bleeding hearts they must let everyone who calls upon them stand outside, and point to him who wills that all be helped. Herein is shown, on the one hand, the limit of human communication, on the other, the limit of human receptivity.

Nor is it to be denied what Schiller says:

When the gentlemen know nothing substantial more to reply,

They quickly shove it off into one’s conscience.<sup>∗∗)</sup>

In the end, indeed, nothing more remains; but the conscience to which the appeal is made is not a particular one, but the one common to all men, and the spirit that awakens the conscience, enlightens reason, and transposes it into the universal, is not the gentlemen’s own spirit. Hereby one is referred not to a subjective, inaccessible something, but to an objective that must become subjective in order to become accessible and intelligible to me.

Nicodemus was a master in Israel, and yet the Teacher of all men could not make himself intelligible to him; it was impossible to force divine truth upon the sensuous understanding.

∗) Hegel’s *Encyclopaedia*, p. 243 in the first, pp. 417, 418 in the second edition.

∗∗) *Evangelical Church Gazette.* 1828, No. 61, p. 483.

[p. 160]

It is no different with philosophy either. Or does it not also have its point, which cannot be learned, cannot be externally forced upon one and extorted, or, more correctly, which cannot be transferred from one *man* to another? And is this not precisely the vital point? We can grasp a whole system with the understanding, learn and teach philosophy, and still the standpoint is lacking on which what has been learned obtains light and truth. The philosopher, too, celebrates his Pentecost. Without rebirth no one comes out of the sphere of the natural understanding into the speculative heights of the living Concept. The celebrated bridge that transposes us all at once out of the psychological horizon into the philosophical circle of light, everyone must cross, and whoever is not conscious of this difference may well not have found the bridge itself, and may still be wandering up and down on this side bank of the dividing stream. —

But truth, after all, consists according to its innermost essence in its necessity; it has its compulsion in itself. For that reason we think that it would also have to let itself be compelled and forced upon us, so that we could not resist. Or at least it would have to let itself be thoroughly demonstrated that we could not evade it. This thorough, compelling, objective and universally valid demonstration, however, we miss with regard to the chief content of faith. All proofs, all means of defense, all weapons that the understanding uses, receive their striking and conquering force only from the faith that they are to prove; according to all experience they have probative force only for those to whom faith has already been given.

Behold! Man can indeed resist truth, almighty truth, after he has fallen from truth, but only so long until his eyes are opened to his guilt, to his wretched condition. For when he first perceives his sin, when he feels not only his sin, but also the wrath of God [p. 161] when he not only confesses—to which, admittedly, he readily assents—“I am a sinner,” but also experiences and confesses: “I am a damned sinner,” then the arms of eternal love already open, and now he can no longer resist the truth, now he must accept it, in that it takes him up. Now no compulsion is needed any more, now he receives grace upon grace. —

Yet what, then, do we really understand by that thorough demonstration that we miss? Do we not seek it in our own interior, instead of in the interior of the matter? Do we not seek the centre and centre of gravity in the subject instead of in the truth? in our own fallen reason, instead of in prayer for the Spirit of God? And do we not thereby fall into the very error that we reproach the Bible-believers with? into the error of subjectivity, which lacks objective compulsion? Or is it not the self-made that we call thorough, when we put it together in our own thoughts, and which nevertheless, when it is made, effects nothing and proves nothing, because it is nothing?

Precisely because truth has its compulsion, its compelling dominion, in itself, for that very reason it cannot lie in the proof, as something different from truth itself. Precisely because it is spirit, it can be cognized only by the whole undivided spirit, for that very reason it is inaccessible to the isolated understanding and its proofs. Precisely because it belongs to universal reason, it cannot come to the isolated, fallen understanding of man. If faith, then, cannot penetrate into one part of the dismembered essence of man, why should it not turn to the other, and appeal from the understanding to conscience, from proof to inner experience?

As faith does not obtain its support from proof, but proof first obtains life and strength from faith, so too is all speculative knowing given in [p. 162] itself, and cannot be positively compelled by proof of the understanding. Philosophy, too, must learn that its opponents have ears to hear and do not hear, and eyes to see and do not see.

If the understanding is to prove the truth by itself, from what is it to take the proof, other than from the truth, which itself first has to be proved? This necessary circle philosophy acknowledges just as faith does.

To be sure, there is no lack of attempts by the understanding to leap over itself. More than once it has undertaken to measure and test itself by itself; it has made it hard for itself to investigate thoroughly its capacity to cognize the truth, and in this self-illumination has again and again had to presuppose the very capacity that was first to be investigated. It may also be only too easy for something of these critical endeavours to creep into actual philosophy, for man is ever anew tempted to fall back into the self-being at which he fell. Nevertheless, the opposite way is proper to speculative philosophy, as to faith, namely not to make subjective reason its own judge, not to subject it to its own critique, not to the critique of this abstract, pure reason, but to universal reason, and to let the understanding be tested, governed, and led into all truth by the Spirit of God.

The appeal to the determinate, inner experience of life, which is charged against faith, is also not to be confused with the subjectivity of feeling, which refuses community, for that reference from external proof to actual living and having rather presupposes the community which feeling rejects, in that it believes and expects that what has come to one individual of humanity is barred to none, because humanity [p. 163] is one, and hence is suited and called to the community of its individuals.

This very community, in which according to philosophy, too, rationality consists, is so essential to faith that it creates it everywhere it enters; it gathers those who were scattered, it destroys the Babylonian confusion of tongues, as the first Pentecost proves, it founds, through the Holy Spirit, a communion of saints, as we already see in the Catechism in the Church’s explanation of the third article. Faith does not rest on feelings that withdraw into themselves and leave the neighbor outside, and thus cut off all community, but, like knowing, according to its innermost essence on the *universality* of reason, whereby actual community arises; it is like knowing in this too, that it does not stop at the mere universality of the *understanding*.

If nevertheless the world complains of having to remain outside, because for its part it withdraws from the community of faith, this is not the fault of faith; it is not faith that lets us stand outside, but we ourselves who let it stand. Experience proves that it goes no differently with philosophy.

Those who will not step forward, who are outside and want only to look on curiously, without giving themselves to it, nevertheless demand sign and proof, and no sign shall be given them but the sign of the prophet Jonah. The Jews demand a sign, and the Greeks ask for human wisdom and human power of proof, 1 Cor. 1:22, but they do not receive what they demand; for he who said: “Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away,” has also said: “The wicked and adulterous generation seeks a sign, and no sign shall be given it, except the sign of the prophet Jonah.” Thereby all who ask for signs and proofs are referred once and for all to one [p. 164] sign, to which they are to hold, and which sooner or later discloses itself to all who earnestly ask for it, for the kingdom of heaven suffers violence from those who seize it.

20.

*As symbolics stands to dogmatics, the Church to the school, so stand faith and knowing.* The Church is infallible, for it remains literally with Scripture, without entering upon a coherent development of the revealed truths, it takes its doctrines as they are given, without inferring further from them, without developing more from them than is given, beside the revelation, which it accepts with gratitude, it leaves the mystery in the revelation unimpaired, and without laying hands on it. But the school seeks to order the given truths in a human fashion, to subject them to a unity, to demonstrate the connection, and to work up the raw material, to develop it, to join it by intermediate links, indeed—to multiply it; therefore it can easily fall into error and deviate from simple faith.

Examples will illustrate. The Church stays with the individual Bible verses in which the Trinity of God is revealed, without permitting itself any addition; but dogmatics presses on to system, not without danger, now of confusing the Persons, now of endangering the unity of God, as soon as it goes one step further than it has light from God. It is not the Church, but the school, that has come to grief over the doctrine of the Holy Spirit, because it wants to move in its own words. — In Jesus God himself has made satisfaction, in Him the Godhead itself has suffered, as in every man humanity suffers, as with one member the whole body, with my body my soul feels pain. But now science needs only to take one more step on its own account, and it

[p. 165]

falls into Patripassianism. The Church cannot be so affected. — The Church teaches us, according to the revelation of Scripture, to cognize God the Father as the Creator of heaven and earth, *from* whom all is made that is made, and the Son as the Word that was in the beginning, *through* which all things were made, and without it nothing was made that was made. To this it faithfully holds, and watches strictly over every particle. But how easily it happens in the school that both are mixed together, and thereby clouded, and cognition constrained? and how often has it happened? — Concerning the last things, in particular concerning the aeons of damnation, whose worm does not die, whose fire is not quenched, there rests in Scripture more than one veil, which the Church does not dare to lift, but the school with its youths at Sais wants to have more light than is revealed.

And yet this difference too vanishes when we bring it closer to true faith and true knowing. Faith too demands life and coherence, faith too lives and weaves in constant and progressive development, just as, conversely, the science of faith is as different from self-willed sham wisdom as faith from superstition. As to the Church, so to the school belongs the stewardship over the mysteries of the kingdom of God. The steward does not keep house in his own name but in the name of the householder; but neither does he bury his pound, but puts it to usury, as a pious and faithful servant who is set over much when he has been faithful over little. Who, then, measures out the sum of the revelations deposited in the Bible? Who knows, then, how much is revealed in Scripture? —

Moreover, what we have illustrated in individual articles of faith of the Church is also shown in Christian ethics in the ten commandments. The understanding by itself, the understanding left to itself, also lets [p. 166] not a single commandment of duty stand unmoved; it knows how to turn and twist all commandments at pleasure; it knows how to use them, but also to alter them. You are lost if you do not recognize in every commandment the immediate, absolute, unalterable Word of God, and therein perceive the measured command of your God. The understanding by itself will concede to you only a relative usefulness of the commandments, and where you come into temptation and enticement to transgress them, will demonstrate the permissibility, for it thinks itself lord over them.

But this understanding by itself is the sham wisdom of the world, which is not to be confused with the knowing that is in and for itself. This understanding would reveal itself still more clearly and more fearfully in its negative, all-destroying monstrosity, would separate itself still more determinately from knowing, if it were not held in bounds and set right, now by the external legal order, which involuntarily impresses it, now by a feeling which we call natural, but which we really have to regard as the effect of antecedent, supernatural grace. Thus it happens that the understanding more often succumbs than it outbids feeling, right, and faith, which, to be sure, does also occur.

Still more clearly the understanding shows itself, in the face of the doctrine of law and morals of the *New* Testament, in its disobedience, as do faith and science in their obedience. When the renunciation [Entäußerung] of all property, Matt. 19:21, and of all offending members, Matt. 5:29–30; 18:8–9, is required for perfection, when every resistance in defense of our body and property, Matt. 5:39–41, every care and trouble for the nourishment of the body, Matt. 6:24–34. 1 Cor. 7:32, every oath, Matt. 5:33–37, is forbidden, when divorce is placed on a par with adultery, Matt. 5:32. Heb. 13:4, marriage among the divorced is declared simply adultery, Matt. 19:9, when not marrying is set above marrying, [p. 167] and all marital community is ascribed to the hardness of the human heart, and yet, according to Scripture, is permitted by God to avert worse temptation, indeed is blessed for this world order and sanctified as a shadow-image of the earlier and the future archetype, Matt. 19:4–12. 22:2, 29–30. 9:15. 1 Cor. 7. Gen. 2:18, then not a letter is lost from all these revelations, sense and letter must remain standing. But for that very reason science will alter a jot of it as little as faith; on the other hand, faith can renounce an understanding of it as little as science can. “Not everyone grasps this saying. Let him grasp it who can,” only that the arriving at understanding not be left to the understanding left to itself, but to faith and to knowing. Both press and drive us to let ourselves be transposed into the Spirit of God, from whom the Word speaks, and into the kingdom of God, in which every letter is valid untranslated, both warn that nothing be lost of the fullness of the import, and nothing lightened of the gravity, both teach that all collisions of duties are only apparent, and arise only in this world of sin, but cannot touch the kingdom of God, both recall the distance of the state, ordained for the world of sinners, from the *original and eternal* order of God in the kingdom of God, so that we are here as though we were there, and have as though we had not, whereby we keep the higher law before our eyes while we are obedient to the lower.

21.

*All faith is miracle-faith*, faith rests on miracles. This is so true that the truth of faith consists precisely in these miracles. For whoever has recognized his natural corruption knows that he can be saved only through a miracle.

[p. 168]

But do we not experience the same in the ways of philosophy? Whoever has recognized his natural blindness, whoever no longer knows how to counsel or help himself, he too experiences that only through a miracle is he called out of the darkness to the wondrous light.

*Who* else could redeem us from sin and darkness but the wonderful God? *What* else but the deed of God, which fulfills the promise and seals the Word? How else but by this deed of God being also done and suffered by man, by the bearer of all humanity,—since it is to be imputed to all humanity,—*how* else but so that it goes to the innermost heart- and life-blood, and penetrates through and through?

22.

Faith points us by means of feeling to the *dependence* of the creature on God, but science makes us *free* by means of thought.

This difference is so indeterminate that we need only determine it more closely to see it vanish. The question is only *from what* science makes us free. Does thought make us free from that on which faith makes us dependent?

If thought makes us free not from God but from the opposite, from the world, then it does the same as faith effects, in that it makes us dependent on God, thereby brings us into communion with God. Dependence on God is freedom from the world, freedom in God, for God is freedom.

In feeling ourselves we feel ourselves dependent, and in our creatureliness dependent on the world. This natural feeling precedes faith, as it does knowing; it sighs for redemption. But in feeling ourselves dependent in *faith*, we feel ourselves dependent *on God*, dependent on the free essence of God, hence free in God, outside God in bondage. *Servire Deo libertas.* [To serve God is freedom.] In that we

[p. 169]

come to an understanding about our dependence, we experience that it is grounded in God; but dependence on God, according to God’s innermost essence, is freedom in God, being in God, just as being outside God is nothing other than being outside freedom.

Only in God is freedom. In no other way can any philosophy make free. According to Scripture, however, faith in the Word of God, abiding in His speech, effects the cognition of the truth, and the truth effects freedom. If you *believe*, you will *know* the truth, and the truth will make you *free*. Through obedience in faith man becomes free in God.

23.

Whereas earlier the heart, or the will, was to be claimed exclusively for faith, now feeling is assigned to it as its organ, whereas philosophy attempts to raise us not only above natural feeling but also above the feeling awakened to self-consciousness. Self-consciousness is the last potency of natural feeling; with the former we begin, with the latter we end, without finding peace in either. Therefore all thinking aims at the extinction of feeling and of self-consciousness, because at the equalization of self-consciousness with consciousness. And toward what does faith press and drive, when it becomes our justification? Toward what else but the restoration of the condition before the Fall, hence the restoration of God-consciousness, which after the Fall was followed by abstract self-consciousness, as the pain and torment of life?—I live, yet now not I, but Christ lives in me!

24.

If we look further into the essence of both modes of cognition, it is easily seen,—what in other

[p. 170]

words has already been said several times,—that *faith in the externalization of the object to the subject, but knowing in the externalization of the subject to the object*, finds its origin, its arising and subsisting, and in so far faith proves itself passive, knowing self-active. But it is also easy to comprehend that the one cannot be thought without the other, indeed cannot even come to appearance in time as a sequence, as a one-after-the-other; for as true as it is in itself that God first loved us, because he is love itself, so undeniable it is also that for us the love of God, the externalization of himself to us, does not come to be before the moment which awakens love toward Him, the externalization of ourselves to Him.

The opposition, too, between suffering and doing vanishes if we want to hold it fast. We are accustomed, to be sure, to regard thinking, if not as our work, yet as pure activity. In this respect we even speak of freedom in thinking, as if we could think what we wanted, and did not have to think. Actually, however, thinking consists just as much in suffering, in letting happen and watching how the thought develops, and in the strict discipline in which the objective thought moves, whereby all arbitrary, self-pleasing, self-complacent argumentation, which we ourselves are wont to produce, is of itself excluded.

Were we to hold fast the above difference, which was found in the mode of externalization, then faith would consist in the love of God to men, knowing in the love of men to God. But is not the former also the basis of all knowing, and the latter also the work of all faith?

25.

Another difference results, in that knowing raises itself to absolute knowing, and thereby *the*

[p. 171]

*form coincides with the content of knowing*, knowing with the truth.

But is faith, then, less absolute? or is faith something other than truth itself? For what can be understood by the identity of form with its content other than the immanence of cognition in the truth? so that knowing is thought not as a means or capacity but as the truth itself, and the object not outside knowing. In order to know, I must not remain standing outside, but step forward, in order to be in the truth. But it is just so with faith, for only the unbelievers are outside. We believe in God, not in an idol; already in this the immanence of faith is indicated.

26.

Of like import is the difference which would restrict faith to the *external representation*, and claim the *real Concept* exclusively for knowing, for immanence, and with it speculative insight, is as essential to faith as to knowing, just as, conversely, philosophy, though it has to overcome the external, abstractly sensuous representation, can never outgrow actual, immanent representation, as the basis of all reality, if it is not to sink back from the actual Concept into the abstract concept.

But we must also acknowledge the faith which remains with external representation and trusts it, without having attained to insight. This childlike faith is the beginning of true, living faith, the beginning which also remains in the progress and goes along with it; it consists in the obedience which bows beneath the mighty Word of God, which trusts Scripture and the Church, which its Lord does not forsake, more than itself, and which to universal reason, as it reveals itself in time and as the work from [p. 172] God has maintained itself through all time, subordinates its own understanding.<sup>∗)</sup> Only hold fast to this faith, and remain true to the Bible and the Church; so the Spirit of God will work more and more in you, he will lead you into all truth, and transfigure external representation into actual, living representation. All science goes the same way; without obedience and trust, without discipline and fidelity it cannot thrive.

Rather is every Christian a regenerate philosopher, every philosopher a simple-minded Christian.

And if it is nevertheless true that philosophy is not everyone’s affair, as experience indeed teaches, then it is no different with faith, as the Apostle testifies and experience confirms.

28.

But finally there comes still a difference to speech, which until now has always remained in ambush, has always announced itself only indirectly, and again and again concealed itself and entangled itself in other differences, until at last it steps forth itself and alone. This difference concerns *sin*, and the *pain of sin*, not merely sin in general, but the *damnableness* of sin, from the cognition of which living faith in redemption breaks forth. This faith consists in this, that it always experiences the pain of sin and the grace that overcomes it at once, and in the exultant jubilation over the glad tidings never forgets from what it had fallen, and how deeply it had fallen, and how it must still wrestle daily with the flesh. The grace of God in Christ is therefore recognized and tasted only in the measure in which the weight of the sin that condemns to death and perdition has been borne. “To whom little is forgiven, the same loves little.” And whoever has not felt himself lost and condemned for himself does not know the rebirth out of grace either. Jesus seeks what is lost, He is only for the sick, for the healthy have no need of the physician, nor yet *the sick* who are their own physician, for to them applies the terrible saying: Physician, help yourself!

In the light of faith the eyes of the spirit recognize everywhere *in nature* the Fall and the curse that weighs upon it, and the groaning creature, *in the subject* sin and guilt, which crush and condemn the heart; and if our heart thus [p. 175] condemns us, how much more must God condemn us according to his holiness? What faith sees in nature and in man that is glorious and good, that it knows how to interpret from the token of the covenant, the rainbow.

In this way faith attains to the understanding of the world, which otherwise remains eternally closed, for all supposed knowing and comprehending.

How, then, does even the most earnest, the deepest speculation stand in this regard? Even if it does not ignore sin, even if it does not regard evil as mere imperfection—where remains the pain of sin? Even if in the *negative* essence, in the ever *negating* spirit of evil, it cannot deny the *positive* enmity against God—how easily it is nonetheless lifted over it! not as over a guilt, but as over an evil. Or else it holds sin to be impotent, because it will recognize its positive resistance as little as that which it resists. When faith *overcomes* sin, it is *dispatched* by speculation summarily, without much ado. Because to philosophy the holiness and blamelessness of God appear only as *light*, sin is for it only *darkness*, mist and shadow, with which it can indeed sooner be done than with its own guilt. Hence the perversity of the heart is for it less an abomination than an object of mockery, more particular than universal, the hardness of the human heart not so much hard and obdurate as spiritual limitation and incapacity. In unbelief it recognizes only its shallowness, and in the materialistic view of the world, which it develops now theoretically, now practically, the naive expression of its spiritlessness, which it otherwise seeks to conceal in supersensualities. In short, in sin it does not recognize what is actual in it and rebels against God; hence it also does not strive for redemption from the superior power of evil,—but sees in sin only emptiness and nullity,—hence it also seeks only [p. 176] redemption from this unreality. Or where, then, has the cry for help for redemption become loud in philosophy, for redemption from the compelling power of evil, which exceeds the sinner’s own powers?——

In reply to this accusation it is above all not to be denied that philosophy does indeed often founder on these rocks, which are here held up to it one by one, if it does not perhaps hold up to itself its own temptations. Hence it may also come about that in it the condition of the poor sinner on the part of the subject does not emerge sufficiently. But it does not lie in its essence if the subject that gives itself to it does not let itself be chastened by it, if it does not bend and stoop, or if it even lays its own pride to its charge.—All things are *yours*; but if you do not use them as you ought, then it is also *your* guilt.—

If we enter more closely into the above difference, it is in fact striking that even those who find speculative philosophy hard, who indeed cannot sufficiently complain over these difficulties of abstracting from abstractions, and who, it may be, find it convenient to withdraw from this hardness, nevertheless join in the charge, as though philosophy made things too easy for itself with sin. If it made the redemption of the unhappy self-consciousness so easy, it could not be so hard. Really, then, it is precisely the sinful flesh that makes it so hard for us to abstract from this and to accept redemption. Indeed, it is impossible until the Spirit of God enlightens and redeems us.

It does happen that the Spirit of God, as the universal reason, has moved and worked upon man for quite some time, has already gained influence over him, and yet cannot convict him of his sinfulness in its whole, monstrous magnitude. But the time also comes when he chastises us, and convicts us, and instructs us concerning sin, concerning righteousness, [p. 177] and concerning judgment; and the judgment consists in this, that the prince of this world is judged, and in grace sin is *overcome*. This is very often the course of our experiences, which we undergo in faith and in knowing. At the same time, and above all, we must call the accusers’ attention once more to the essence and the Concept of philosophy, as the continuous, uninterruptedly coherent, and uninterruptible movement of objective thought—which we only too easily overlook in it when we sit in judgment upon it, however much it forces itself anew upon us. So long as objective thought, and in it the Spirit of truth, which is not our spirit, holds sway as the element of all cognition through all stages of human development, and works upon us, and makes itself heard in chastising and enlightening—and in this alone consists philosophy, which is subsequently presented again as it has been received—so long as the Spirit speaks and develops itself in us ourselves, so long the subject necessarily recedes, which is being heard and worked upon; it must be silent and has nothing to say. “*Speak, Lord, for your servant hears!*” While the subject is silent, and listens to the further speech of the objective Spirit, it can well *experience* its pain of sin, but not *confess* it.

Only by overlooking this moment do we arrive at that difference which resolves itself at once when, in speculation as in the cognition of faith—even though in other respects the two were different—we learn to distinguish what is given to us, what we hear, what works upon us, from the effect that what is given makes upon us. Faith and cognition work as surely as they are actually heard. Hence they need not at first express their effect, because it follows of itself. Hence the Word of God presses not for works, but first of all for faith, from which they follow of themselves.

[p. 178]

Hence philosophy does not at first interrupt itself with ascetic and parenetic practical application, because these do not fail to appear in the subject if the former is truly understood, and would be of no avail if it is not understood. To be sure, speculation too can prove edifying, as the Epistle of James follows upon the Epistle to the Romans. And where it does not make itself heard in this way, do you know, too, what it quietly works upon the subject, and what this subject, after strict labour under the discipline of the Spirit, who bids it be silent, may confess and make confession of before God upon its knees?

Moreover, if in individual branches of philosophy sin is set forth more as darkness than as wickedness, the fault lies in the fragmentation of the different sides of the human spirit, according to which sin too manifests itself, in the intellectual respect, as darkness and blindness.

And if, finally, the science of thought finds in sin at first only nullity and emptiness, it must nevertheless go further, and it lies in its very own essence to ask after the ground and essence of this non-essence. And what other answer can come to it than that which is revealed in Scripture?

“In that science, through its longing for *truth*, recognizes the need of *love*, it also confesses its initial poverty,” poverty in truth and love, “its ignorance” and its lovelessness, or rather this state of affairs with the subject. “It confesses to proceed from nullity, and so it must also openly acknowledge the *ground* of this nullity of man, amid so much open and hidden wealth, which he does not understand and in his ignorance does not know how to use; it must admit his fall, and must regard God’s condescension to nothingness in order to raise man up again as a self-emptying for man’s sake.

[p. 179]

Man’s *thought* too is affected by the Fall; its originally unhindered activity and evidence is hindered, it has fallen into the captivity of representations and feelings, into the power of the so-called associations of ideas; it too needed redemption.”

Holy Scripture, to be sure, has proscribed and condemned “the wisdom of this world,” 1 Cor. 1:20–21; 3:19, and “heathen philosophy,” as the “vain deviation according to the teaching of men and the ordinances of the world,” Col. 2:8; but to the wisdom of this world, which the world gives, there is opposed the cognition of the world and its sin, which God gives, and to the philosophy that is empty and vain, and turns away from God, and robs us while it bestows gifts upon us, because it loves human wisdom and world-ordinances, there is opposed that philosophy which loves the wisdom of God, and at the feet of Jesus listens quietly to the foolish preaching of the Gospel, without demanding signs with the Jews, without asking with the Greeks after human wisdom and human proofs. 1 Cor. 1:22–23.

29.

We have already several times thought to have come to the end of the perpetual process of distinguishing, but as often as we suppose we are finished with it, so often new differences arise again, or at least new relations, in which differences already dismissed renew themselves and return with intensified force. In this way, however, we can never be finished, unless we renounce this endless casuistry and refer back once and for all to the principles in which its dismissal and settlement lie. Only *one* difference must we still hear, because it is only too often repeated and urged against philosophy. It concerns the *ought*, and will also settle itself in the concept of the ought.

[p. 180]

Faith teaches us that we are not what we *ought* to be, but through Him in whom we believe are *to* become what we are not. With this, being, our being, is condemned, and to the ought is given the prize that is held out to us as the jewel for which we strive. Conversely, however, speculative philosophy holds to the *being* that is there, and finds, as the science of reason, in actuality and presence the reason that faith regards as otherworldly, as future; with this it releases itself from the burdensome *ought*, and presses only for cognition of the actual, as of the rational, which is not so impotent as merely to ought and not to be; indeed, it scorns the ought, as the demand of a being that was already there before the demand, and did not first have to wait for it.

But is the being that faith rejects, then, one and the same with the being that science finds, and the ought that faith holds out one and the same with the ought that science subordinates? It requires in fact only a serious glance to recognize that in this supposed difference as well, as in all differences, the accord of the cognition of faith with the cognition of reason, supposedly different from it, only the more proves itself.

The being that faith condemns as our present state is precisely our human, sinful being, the being-for-self, and with it the unactuality of our being, non-being. The ought that it holds out to us is the admonition to the being originally bestowed upon us, which we owe, because through our guilt we have forfeited the good entrusted to us, the demand to enter again into this being, which is lacking to us, but not to itself, but not the demand first to make this being. The being, on the other hand, which reason raises above the ought, and intuits as independent of it, is precisely the actual [p. 181] being from which we have fallen, without its being for that reason any less actual in and for itself. And the ought, which is so contemptuously rejected and mocked, is the fancy that being took heed of it in order to be, as though being, because it is lacking to us, were not there at all, and were waiting precisely for us, until we make it. Reason acknowledges the ought only insofar as this acknowledges being as already actually existing, and refers man, who is not in it, to this as to the present actuality.

If this clarification concerning the different meaning of the same words is not yet sufficient to settle the above difference, we hold ourselves more closely to the content of the Christian faith itself, which seems to accuse philosophy on account of that difference.

The Christian faith is nothing other than faith in Christ, that is, faith in Him who first for us, and thereupon also in us, fulfills the law, transfigures the ought into willing, willing into deed, and thus into being, and consequently sublates it. If in this the faith in the Gospel proves itself the end of the law, it cannot escape offence and misunderstanding from the world. And yet it should make itself guilty, toward the science of reason, of this very misunderstanding under which it must suffer in the world, if the latter directs its whole essence, all its meditating and perceiving, away from the *ought-to-be*, and toward the actual being that is held out to us, in order to invite us to accept it as a gift of grace, and thereby to discharge the claims that we owed, but could not fulfil?

Indeed language has one word for guilt and ought—ὀφείλειν, *debere*—and Scripture teaches us, in the light of revelation, the connection between sin and law, between guilt and indebtedness—ὀφείλημα, *debitum*. We are not what we [p. 182] ought to be, but what we are not, that we *ought* to be, that we are *bound* to be, only because we are *guilty* of not actually being, because through our guilt we have incurred the loss of the being entrusted to us. So Scripture teaches, and so we believe. And if now, accordingly, the relations of being and ought develop themselves in the Concept, if reason, as existing, gives the honor to being as actually existing, and on the other hand refers the ought back to its position, how should we be able to mistake what is thereby said, how should we not recognize again in the Concept what we already had and comprehended in representation? And does not experience daily teach that this ought, which should remind us daily of our guilt and indebtedness, conversely turns into the most insufferable arrogance, in that man, to whom the law is given in order to obey, makes himself the lawgiver, or, instead of accepting what is prepared for him, imagines that he must first himself make the being that he is to receive again.

With this, then, this difference too, which wears itself out on the ought, is sufficiently settled. For the rest, all these differences in their one-sided conception are as little to be laid to the charge of the cognition of faith as of the cognition of reason, for we have come to know the unity of both; but they fall equally to the charge of philosophy, as it must let itself be deformed in individual subjects, if not as a whole, yet in particular respects, and to that of faith, when in individual subjects, if not as a whole, yet in certain directions, it is infected and clouded by sin. And in what sinner is reason, in what sinner faith, wholly pure, and always pure?

But enough of separating and uniting! From the identity winding itself everywhere through the difference it is explained at the same time how it comes about that to us so often in the simple confession of faith [p. 183] of a living Christian the deepest result of speculative philosophy is met with, and in the most remote philosophemes the children’s doctrine of the catechism. Hence it also comes about that everything that runs counter to the Christian faith and contradicts biblical revelation is also rejected by philosophy, and that where revelation is silent, philosophy too is at an end.

As identity has everywhere asserted itself, so too the true state of affairs concerning the difference has everywhere come to light. This difference bears the common lot of all those oppositions which are members of one organic whole, in which the identity of being and non-being reveals itself, in that they are just as much as they are not. For this very reason it has been impossible to find such a difference as could hold faith and knowing apart as two different and yet independent modes of cognition and of life, for we could not, individual aberrations aside, think living faith without thinking and knowing, with which it is essentially connected, nor thinking and knowing without faith, as the sole foundation and reality of all knowing, nor faith without activity, thinking without suffering, nor content without form, not without the form essential to it, nor form without content, not without the content identical with the form.

When it has been said: “Philosophy cannot believe; it would annihilate itself through faith,” or “it is just as certain that philosophy does not believe as that faith does not philosophize,”—does not think, we have seen how these and similar pronouncements settle themselves and turn about, for we found that all philosophy has the object, and with it faith, as its foundation, and without faith would annihilate itself, would evaporate in unactual Ideas, and that conversely faith [p. 184] recognizes thought as its essence, that both, in order to be, mutually quicken, govern, and enlighten one another. All philosophy *believes*, as surely as it seeks and finds ground, faith *thinks*, as surely as it subsists in the spiritual life.

*Philosophia, obiter libata, a Deo abducit, penitus hausta, reducit ad eundem* [Philosophy, sipped lightly, leads away from God; drunk deeply, leads back to Him]. What holds for superficial, half philosophy, for non-philosophy, does not touch philosophy itself.

But why so many words? Who denies, then, that *true* philosophy leads to God, and so far goes one way with faith, has one ground? When we take the field against philosophy, we *mean* not the true philosophy, but the false, which is the ruling philosophy, the philosophy of the day.

Nevertheless you contend against philosophy as such. Why, then, do you not say that you contend against sham philosophy, against non-philosophy? Something lies behind that. All actual philosophy is true philosophy. Why, then, do you call that philosophy which is non-philosophy? Why do you call untrue philosophy philosophy? Why else than because actual philosophy, the philosophy of the Concept, seems to you untrue?

It is in fact already to be feared from this, what will presently be confirmed in detail, that you persecute precisely the true philosophy, as the actual. And this we all indeed do only too easily, now because its thoughts are not our thoughts, now because its thoughts are turned and twisted by the understanding into other thoughts.

“True philosophy,” so many opponents of philosophy confess, “true philosophy is inseparable from religion, indeed it is, rightly understood, itself religion, which, when it fills our life, leads to the cognition of God, but—only by the practical way.” Why this But? Is, then, this practical [p. 185] way not essential to philosophy, and belonging to its very own Concept? Is philosophy even conceivable without this practical way?

But how they mean it soon becomes clear to us, for the same opponents at once declare themselves again against thought, against the real Concept, in which precisely the practical way comes to light. “Thinking,” they say, “is neither the element of religion nor that of virtue, but the element of religion is a pure heart,” as if this heart were not also the element of thinking. “Not to the pure Concept,” they say, “but only to the pure heart is God accessible.” Thus not to the spirit itself, not to the whole human essence reborn out of the Spirit, but only to a fraction of man, made in thought by abstraction? “Not the understanding,” they say further, “not thinking, nor yet the heart, is what leads to truth in the highest sense, but the sacrifice of the heart.” To be sure, it is not merely the sacrifice of one’s own heart, but also the sacrifice of one’s own understanding, out of which spirit is reborn as the identity of heart and understanding, and on which all faith rests, but on which all philosophy too has rested from of old. Faith and philosophy thrive toward actuality the more, the more decided, complete, and faithful the sacrifice is; the less is sacrificed, the weaker and more incomplete are faith and knowing. If in no man philosophy has always been wholly pure and faithful, so too here below no faith remains without strife and temptation.

But our opponents, beloved in the Lord, do the utmost when they presume and say: “As little as God can be laid hold of with hands, so little can he be apprehended with the Concept.” This simile is to beat philosophy from the field at one stroke. Philosophy answers just as briefly with [p. 186] the testimony of the disciple whom the Lord loves: As surely as God—the Word that was from the beginning, the Word of life—has let himself be beheld with our eyes and touched with our hands, so surely He does not withdraw himself from the Concept either, whereby the Spirit, as being in Him, cognizes Him. As surely as God imparts himself in the holy sacraments, according to his promise, through visible and palpable elements, and lets himself be enjoyed in these elements, so surely is God Spirit, who in the flesh imparts himself actually and essentially to the flesh, as to the body of the spirit, and hence to the spirit, to the Concept—not to the abstract Concept.—

Where such misunderstandings assert themselves, and seek to take a place even among the brethren in Christ, there many and lengthy words are indeed not superfluous in order to defend the identity of speculative philosophy of religion with the Christian cognition of faith. Indeed, it is precisely now time to draw attention to this in general and in particular, because the light, loose, cold times are just now passing, which sought to dispose of the whole heavy and rich import of Christian dogmatics and symbolics, which runs through all Christian centuries, in the manner of Erasmus, as scholastic subtleties, as dry formulas and useless brooding, without being touched in the least by the inwardness and fidelity of the diligence, by the unmistakable earnestness of the contemplation, by the love and depth in the inquiry, by the holy awe and the strict obedience in the stiff formulas and standing predicates. But whoever has taken even single glimpses into this rich world of spirit will soon stand still in wonder, and look on with joy at how the Lord builds his Church through all the centuries, and how at all times the disciples of Jesus dwell at each word in the Word of God, follow up every hint, unfold in every truth its inexhaustible wealth on all sides and in all relations, in every image [p. 187] besides its general meaning also pursue its actuality, develop the interpretation according to every sense that lets the word itself stand unfalsified, and bring together all the treasures of the Bible, in order to carry out the system down to the smallest parts, down to the finest veins—all in order more and more to appropriate the rich Word, as the power of God, to live ever more deeply into it, and with all the senses to imbibe it ever more firmly into all the pores.<sup>∗)</sup> Or are we really still now to think that such intercourse with God, because it first sets thought in motion, should leave the heart cold, and the *hands* with which we *act* lie slack and quiet in the lap? Of all the words of Holy Scripture none is superfluous, for all Scripture, inspired by God, is profitable for teaching, for reproof, for improvement, for discipline in righteousness.——

So much for peace between faith and knowing, which, as knowing, can dispense with the closer designation “true knowing,” because all knowing is true, as all faith is, for superstition is not faith.

Nevertheless the difference between faith and knowing cannot be denied, identity does not exclude difference, and in the error that we have demonstrated in the differences there lies truth too; but this difference is only not to be grasped as if we could stop at its abstractions,

[p. 188]

just as if one subject could live merely in faith, another merely in knowing,—but rather in such a way that in each mode the other indeed finds itself and distinguishes itself, but also at the same time knows itself as inseparably bound. Hence we need only hold all the individual differences quite rigidly and sharply apart from one another, and set them against each other, in order to see them vanish in one relation after another.

Thinking and faith are therefore to be regarded as parts of one living whole, which are not self-subsistent for themselves, so that as separated they cannot maintain themselves in actuality, and, nevertheless separated, turn into distorted images of the holiest. “Only by mutually permeating each other can the essence make itself known.” Both distinguish themselves as consciousness and existence, the same being belongs to both, but being is not actual unless it is consciousness. Properly, then, we deny less the difference than the separation.

Hence faith too is not to be separated from philosophy without annihilating it, and if in most recent times the dispute has even been renewed whether a *Christian* philosophy is *possible*, which is indeed strange enough, since it has been *actual* since the oldest times,—but sham philosophy does not trouble itself about actuality,—then on the contrary it is to be asserted that no philosophy other than Christian is possible, as indeed none is actual. In the so-called heathen philosophy of antiquity, as well as in the not so-called heathen philosophy of later times and of our own, only so much is true as is Christian.

To separate philosophy from Christianity is therefore in fact nothing other than to divorce it from its content and purpose, namely from truth. Such a separation is a divorce: divorce presupposes adultery, a complete falling out with the truth, shipwreck of faith and of knowing.

[p. 189]

The Apostle Paul too seems to acknowledge as well as to sublate the much-discussed difference when he says: *“I know in whom I believe!”*

Happy are we if we can say it after him with a good conscience, for it is a precious thing that the heart be made firm; and it becomes firm and certain only when it *knows* in whom it *believes*.

### Afterword.

In the second section of the foregoing work it was only *by way of example*, in the case of a few particularly captious doctrines of speculative philosophy, that their being understood, and, in the case of a few especially frequent prejudices and misunderstandings about it, their refutation, were brought closer to representation. For that very reason no completeness of content could be aimed at, but only hints toward further understanding. Meanwhile one example has remained unmentioned which would have been especially suited to those purposes, since even within the school of this philosophy it receives nourishment for misunderstandings through its formalism, whereby it appears to enter into opposition with Holy Scripture and the Church.

The formalism of this philosophy, namely, designates—not without danger of mixing and confusing abstractions and concrete representations with one another—the original state of man in and after creation as *being-in-itself*, the state through the Fall and after the Fall, on the other hand, as *being-for-self*, as consciousness of the separation from God, of the discord between subject and object, as cognition of good and evil, hence as the dualism of being-in-itself and being-for-self, but finally the state through redemption as the return to the original unity, with the difference that this is raised and transfigured from the indifferent being-in-itself to *being-in-and-for-itself*.

[p. 191]

From these formulas, raw and dead as they are at first, there results, however, with sensuous apprehension and handling, only too easily, not only outside the school but partly within it as well, the same unscriptural view of the Fall and of the state that preceded it, which we find expressed in the critical philosophy, namely in Kant<sup>∗)</sup> and Schiller<sup>∗∗)</sup>, and with which we believed ourselves at least philosophically done, though it has kept its currency in the domain of unbelief. Indeed, this current view seems, in the way in which the Master of the speculative philosophy of our time has still recently expressed himself on it [in the *Yearbooks for Scientific Criticism*. June 1828. Nos. 105, 106. p. 841.], to have obtained a confirmation very welcome to rationalism.

If God, as the good, as that which is, on the one hand, and man, as the nothing, as evil, on the other hand, are merely *presupposed*, then “there is lacking here,” in the Master’s own words, “the moment of *creation* in general, and of man in particular according to God’s likeness, and from there on of the *transition* from this merely original, merely *in-itself* unity of human nature with the divine, which has not yet entered into existence, to what is expressed as the *semblance* or the nothing. The semblance determines itself to the more concrete, which is consciousness and freedom, and the *difficulty* is that this semblance contains not only the source of evil, the eating, *falling away* from the likeness, from the tree of *cognition*

of good and evil, but also the principle of the return to the likeness; so that God himself is introduced saying: Behold, Adam has become *like one of Us*, and knows what is good and evil (Gen. 3:22)—the passage which constitutes the *other* side to the *first* meaning of cognizing, and which is usually far too little considered in its depth, indeed is customarily scarcely even noticed.”<sup>∗)</sup>

What are we to say to this? and how are we to bring the abstract Concept nearer to representation?

So much is certain first of all, that man, as he is now, as he is after the Fall, not as he was created but as he is born, is at first only *in itself*, that is, lies in a deep, dull sleep of sin, without cognizing good and evil, without distinguishing subject and object, without bringing to consciousness the separation from God and the discord of his essence. As he awakens to consciousness, he is *for himself*, and now substance and subject step apart, and over to different sides. This self-consciousness develops gradually, and indeed first as consciousness of difference in general, in which the sleep of sin as such continues even after the awakening, and thereafter as consciousness of the cause of this discord, cognition of sin and pain of sin. Self-consciousness becomes consciousness of guilt, and with this the state of unblessedness, upon which finally follows redemption out of this discord, and the consciousness of it, whereby being, split into in-itself and for-itself, is transfigured into *being-in-and-for-itself*. This is the course of the human essence after the Fall, and in accordance with divine revelation.

[p. 193]

It is further in accordance with Scripture that the original state of innocence and divine likeness is torn apart by sin, but through redemption is not merely restored but exalted and glorified more than before. Heb. 2:16, 11; 2 Cor. 3:18; Luke 15:7. Insofar the Fall, against the intention of the devil, but in accordance with his specious promises, has become in God’s hands the means of glorifying God in *man* still more. What the serpent promises in order to deceive, Gen. 3:5, that God confirms, Gen. 3:22, 7, in order to deprive the deception of its force, and to corroborate the truth that lies in the deception against its knowledge and will. Whereby it wants to corrupt man, thereby is he, contrary to merit and worthiness, glorified.

And what is there to object to this, if the representations of the two states, the original and the last, perfected state, are designated in the Concept as being-in-itself and being-in-and-for-itself? whereby they are distinguished not only from the state of sin torn apart in being-for-self, but also from one another as the lower and the higher. Only that this being-in-itself, in which man was created, is also to be distinguished from that being-in-itself in which he is born. The difference consists in this, that there the separation from God is not yet, here is not known, there innocence, here ignorance. The original being-in-itself is childhood, but not the childhood that we now see before our eyes, although traces of that childhood are still left over in this one, only disfigured. Mark 10:14. Thus this unstained being-in-itself is divine likeness, which does not exclude the transformation into the *same* image from one radiance to another.

It is no less in accordance with Scripture that man is nothing, nothing before the Fall, nothing after the [p. 194] Fall. But this nothing and that nothing are not the same nothing. There he is nothing, because what he is is not *he*, but God in him, here he is nothing,—because he is no longer in God, and through being-for-self has stepped out of what is. And what is there to object to this, if this annihilation of the original nothing proves itself to be self-affirmation, since we cannot deny that sin consists in self-emancipation, and has had unhappy self-consciousness as its consequence. Through the annihilation of the original nothing man has indeed attained self-subsistence, only that this, for itself, is vain semblance and nothing, hence it again requires another nothing, in order to sublate the sinful nothing and to preserve it in being-in-and-for-itself. It is a harsh expression when it is said that in the Fall the principle of redemption is already given, but nothing else can be understood by this than that from it the seed is to proceed that puts an end to sin and crushes the serpent’s head.

Now it is also explained that Adam first entered into existence through sin, insofar as only thereby did he attain separate existence, whereas before it was not he, but God in him.

If, accordingly, sin is regarded on the one hand as Fall, on the other as a moment of development, it is not to be overlooked that that side belongs to man, whose guilt therefore is not to be excused or justified, but this side, on the other hand, pertains to God, whom sin must serve, according to Scripture, as a means for development. Scripture also teaches that God foresaw the Fall, and prepared redemption from eternity. Eph. 3:11; 1:4.

Error arises only when this consideration in any way becomes a temptation to justify man in his sin, and [p. 195] to lighten the heavy weight of inborn and personal sin. This error is not to be laid to the charge of speculation, which consists rather in this, that it discovers the truth in the unscriptural error which extols the Fall of mankind as a giant stride of the same toward perfection, and which cannot be refuted in any other way than by acknowledging the truth hidden beneath it and vindicating it for the highest Being, because it is his deed and not mankind’s, but redounds to the latter’s good.
