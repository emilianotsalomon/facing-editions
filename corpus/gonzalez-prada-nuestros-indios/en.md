Manuel González Prada

## NUESTROS INDIOS — OUR INDIANS

### I — I

The most prominent sociologists regard Sociology as a science still in the making and clamour for the advent of its Newton, its Lavoisier or its Lyell; and yet in no books do dogmatic or arbitrary assertions swarm so thickly as in the works produced by the heirs and epigones of Comte. Sociology may be called not only the art of giving new names to old things but the science of contradictory assertions. If one great sociologist states a proposition, we may be sure that another sociologist, no less great, argues for the diametrically opposite one. Just as some pedagogues bring to mind the tutors of Scribe, so many sociologists make one think of Molière's physicians: Le Bon and Tarde are not far removed from Diafoirus and Purgon.

Let us cite race as one of the points on which authors diverge most. While some see in it the principal factor of social dynamics and sum up history as a struggle of races, others reduce the radius of ethnic action so far that they repeat with Durkheim: "We know of no social phenomenon that stands in unquestionable dependence on race." Novicow, although he judges Durkheim's opinion exaggerated, does not hesitate to affirm that "race, like species, is to a certain extent a subjective category of our mind, without external reality"; and he exclaims in a generous burst of humanity: "All those alleged incapacities of the yellow and black peoples are the chimeras of sick minds. Whoever dares to say to a race: thus far shalt thou come and no further, is a blind man and a fool."

What a convenient invention Ethnology is in the hands of certain men! Once the division of Humanity into superior and inferior races is admitted, once the superiority of the whites is recognised, and with it their right to monopolise the government of the Planet, nothing is more natural than the suppression of the Black in Africa, of the redskin in the United States, of the Tagalog in the Philippines, of the Indian in Peru. Since the supreme law of life is fulfilled in the selection or elimination of the weak and the unadaptable, the violent eliminators or suppressors merely hasten the slow and lazy work of nature: they exchange the tortoise's crawl for the horse's gallop. Many do not write it, but they let it be read between the lines, as Pearson does when he speaks of "the solidarity of the civilised men of the European race in the face of Nature and human barbarism." Where one reads "human barbarism," translate "man without a white skin."

But it is no longer only the suppression of Blacks and yellow peoples that is decreed: within the white race itself, nations are being classified into peoples destined to grow great and live and peoples condemned to degenerate and die. Ever since Demolins published his book A quoi tient la supériorité des Anglo-Saxons, the fashion of extolling the Anglo-Saxons and disparaging the Latins has grown fiercer. (Although some Latins can be called such only as Atahualpa could be called a Galician or Moctezuma a Provençal.) In Europe and America we are witnessing the flowering of many Cassandras who spend their lives prophesying the burning and disappearance of the new Troy. Some pessimists, believing themselves the Deucalions of the coming flood and even Nietzsche's supermen, judge the disappearance of their own race as though it concerned prehistoric beings or the Moon. An axiom has not been formulated but is followed: the crimes and vices of the English or North Americans are things inherent in the human species and do not betray the decadence of a people; whereas the crimes and vices of the French or Italians are anomalies and point to the degeneration of a race. Fortunately Oscar Wilde and General MacDonald were not born in Paris, nor did Emperor Wilhelm's Round Table hold its sessions in Rome.

It seems needless to say that we do not take seriously dilettanti like Paul Bourget or fumistes like Maurice Barrès when they hurl thunderbolts at cosmopolitanism and bewail the decadence of the "noble French race" because the daughter of a syphilitic count and a consumptive marchioness lets herself be seduced by a healthy, vigorous young fellow without quarterings of nobility. As for Monsieur Gustave Le Bon, we must admire him for his vast learning and his great moral elevation, although he represents the exaggeration of Spencer, as Max Nordau does that of Lombroso and Haeckel that of Darwin. He deserves to be called the Bossuet of Sociology, not to say its Torquemada or its Herod. Were he not worthy of consideration for his observations on black light, we would say that he is to Sociology what Doctor Sangrado is to Medicine.

Le Bon warns us that "he by no means takes the term race in the anthropological sense, because pure races have long since all but disappeared, except among savage peoples," and, so that we may have a safe road to walk on, he decrees: "Among civilised peoples there are only historical races, that is, races created entirely by the events of history." According to Lebonian dogmatism, the Spanish American nations already constitute one of these races, but a race so singular that it has passed headlong from childhood to decrepitude, covering in less than a century the trajectory that other peoples travelled in three, four, five and even six thousand years. "The 22 Latin republics of America," he says in his Psychologie du Socialisme, "although situated in the richest regions of the Globe, are incapable of turning their immense resources to account. . . The final destiny of this half of America is to return to primitive barbarism, unless the United States renders it the immense service of conquering it. . . To bring the richest regions of the Globe down to the level of the Black republics of Santo Domingo and Haiti: that is what the Latin race has achieved with half of America in less than a century."

One might object to Le Bon that he mistakes a child's skin rash for the senile gangrene of a nonagenarian, a youth's hebephrenia for an old man's homicidal madness. Since when do revolutions herald decrepitude and death? None of the Spanish American nations today displays the political and social misery that reigned in feudal Europe; yet the feudal age is considered a stage of evolution, while the era of Spanish American revolutions is regarded as an irremediable and definitive condition. We might also object by setting Le Bon the optimist against Le Bon the pessimist, as one might set Saint Augustine the bishop against Saint Augustine the pagan. "It is possible," Le Bon asserts, "that after a series of profound calamities, of upheavals almost never seen in History," the Latin peoples, taught by experience. . . "will attempt the hard enterprise of acquiring the qualities they lack in order thenceforth to succeed in life. . . Apostles can do much because they manage to transform opinion, and opinion is queen today. . . History is so full of the unforeseen, the world is on the way to undergoing such profound modifications, that it is impossible today to foresee the destiny of empires." If the fate of nations cannot be foreseen, how does he announce the death of the Spanish American republics? What the Latin empires can accomplish in Europe, may not nations of the same origin attempt in the New World? Or are there two sociological laws, one for the "Latins" of America and another for the "Latins" of Europe? Perhaps; but fortunately Le Bon's assertions are like nails: one drives out the other.

It is plain, then, that if Auguste Comte meant to make Sociology an eminently positive science, some of his heirs are turning it into a heap of ramblings without scientific foundation.

### II — II

In The Race Struggle, Ludwig Gumplowicz says: "Every powerful essential ethnic element seeks to make every weak element found within its radius of power, or that enters it, serve its ends." First the Conquistadors, then their descendants, formed in the countries of America an ethnic element powerful enough to subjugate and exploit the natives. Even if Las Casas's assertions are branded exaggerated, it cannot be denied that, thanks to the avaricious cruelty of the exploiters, the weak element is close to extinction in some American countries. The ants that domesticate aphids in order to milk them do not imitate the improvidence of the "white man; they do not destroy their productive animal."

To Gumplowicz's formula we should add a law that greatly influences our way of being: when an individual rises above the level of his social class, he usually becomes its worst enemy. During the enslavement of the Black, there were no overseers more ferocious than Blacks themselves; today there are perhaps no oppressors of the native so harsh as natives themselves, once Hispanicised and invested with some authority.

The true tyrant of the masses, the one who uses some Indians to fleece and oppress the others, is the "encastado," the man of mixed caste, a word that covers the cholo or mestizo of the sierra as well as the mulatto and zambo of the coast. In Peru we see an ethnic superposition: leaving aside the Europeans and the very small number of national whites or creoles, the population divides into two fractions very unequal in number, the "encastados" or dominators and the natives or dominated. One to two hundred thousand individuals have placed themselves on top of three million.

There exists an offensive and defensive alliance, an exchange of services, between the dominators of the capital and those of the provinces: if the gamonal of the sierra serves as political agent to the grandee of Lima, the grandee of Lima defends the gamonal of the sierra when he barbarously abuses the Indian. Few social groups have committed so many iniquities or appear with features so black as the Spaniards and encastados in Peru. Revolutions, squandering and bankruptcies seem as nothing beside the glacial greed of the encastados to wring the juice out of human flesh. The pain and death of their fellow men has mattered very little to them when that pain and that death have yielded a few soles of profit. They decimated the Indian with the repartimientos and the mitas; they imported the Black to make him groan under the overseers' whip; they devoured the Chinese, giving him a handful of rice for ten and even fifteen hours of labour; they took the Kanaka from his islands to let him die of homesickness in the barracks of the haciendas; today they seek to bring in the Japanese (when immigration is spoken of in Peru, it is not a matter of obtaining free men who will till the soil on their own account and after some years become small proprietors: what is wanted is pariahs who will alienate their freedom and supply the maximum of work for the minimum of wages.) The Black seems to be dwindling, the Chinese is disappearing, the Kanaka has left no trace, the Japanese shows no sign of lending himself to servitude; but the Indian remains, for three to four hundred years of cruelties have not managed to exterminate him: the "wretch" stubbornly insists on living!

The Viceroys of Peru never ceased to condemn the outrages, nor did they spare efforts to secure "the preservation, good treatment and relief of the Indians"; the Kings of Spain, yielding to "the compassion of their noble and Catholic souls," conceived humanitarian measures or seconded those begun by the Viceroys. Good intentions abounded in the Royal Decrees. We do not know whether the Laws of the Indies form a pyramid as high as Chimborazo; but we know that the evil went on just the same, although there were sometimes exemplary punishments. Nor could it have been otherwise: the exploitation of the vanquished was officially ordered, and humanity and justice were asked of those who carried out the exploitation; iniquities were to be committed humanely and injustices consummated equitably. To root out the abuses it would have been necessary to abolish the repartimientos and the mitas — in a word, to change the whole colonial regime. Without the toil of the American Indian, the coffers of the Spanish treasury would have been emptied. The riches sent from the colonies to the Metropolis were nothing but blood and tears turned into gold.

The Republic follows the traditions of the Viceroyalty. Presidents in their messages plead for the redemption of the oppressed and call themselves "protectors of the indigenous race"; congresses draw up laws that leave the "Declaration of the Rights of Man" behind; ministers of the Interior issue decrees, send notes to the prefects and appoint commissions of inquiry, all "with the noble purpose of securing the guarantees of the disinherited class"; but messages, laws, decrees, notes and commissions come down to hypocritical jeremiads, words without echo, well-thumbed files. The authorities who issue peremptory orders from Lima to the departments know they will not be obeyed; the prefects who receive the warnings from the Capital also know that no harm will come to them for not carrying them out. What the Marquis of Mancera said in his "Memoria" in 1648 must be repeated today, reading "governors and landowners" in place of corregidores and caciques: "These poor Indians have for enemies the greed of their Corregidores, of their Priests and of their Caciques, all bent on growing rich from their sweat; a Viceroy's zeal and authority would be needed for each one; trusting in the distance, obedience is evaded, and there is neither strength nor perseverance to lodge the complaint a second time." ("Memorias de los Virreyes del Perú, Marqués de Mancera y Conde de Salvatierra," published by José Toribio Polo. Lima, 1889.) "Evading obedience" counts for much in the mouth of a viceroy; but the declaration that escaped the defenders of the natives of Chucuito counts for more (La Raza indígena del Perú en los albores del siglo XX, page VI, second pamphlet. Lima, 1903).

There is no lack of Indianophiles who in their individual or collective initiatives behave as the Governments do in their official action. The associations formed to liberate the unredeemed race have been nothing more than political contraband sheltered under a philanthropic flag. In defending the Indian, compassion has been exploited, just as patriotism is trafficked in today by invoking Tacna and Arica. For the redeemers to act in good faith, they would have to undergo a moral transformation overnight, repent on measuring the horror of their iniquities, form the inviolable resolve to obey justice, wish to turn from tigers into men. Is that within the bounds of possibility?

Meanwhile, and as a general rule, the "dominators" approach the Indian to deceive him, oppress him or corrupt him. And we must recall that it is not only the national "encastado" who acts with inhumanity or bad faith: when Europeans become wool buyers, miners or landowners, they show themselves good extortioners and magnificent torturers, rivalling the old encomenderos and the present-day hacendados. The white-skinned animal, wherever he may be born, suffers from the gold sickness: in the end he yields to the instinct of rapacity.

### III — III

Under the Republic, does the Indian suffer less than under Spanish rule? If there are no longer corregimientos or encomiendas, forced labour and conscription remain. What we make him suffer is enough to bring down upon us the execration of all humane people. We keep him in ignorance and servitude, we debase him in the barracks, we brutalise him with alcohol, we hurl him into the civil wars to be torn to pieces, and from time to time we organise manhunts and massacres like those of Amantaní, Ilave and Huanta. (A truthful and well-informed person provides us with the following facts: "Massacre of Amantaní.—Barely had Piérola's first dictatorship begun when the Indians of Amantaní, an island in Titicaca, lynched a gamonal who had committed the imprudence of forcing them to perform military drill. The response was the dispatch from Puno of two warships, which ferociously bombarded the island from 6 in the morning to 6 in the evening. The slaughter was horrible, and to this day the number of Indians who perished that day, without distinction of age or sex, is unknown. One sees only skeletons, still whitening, wedged waist-deep in the clefts of the rocks, in the attitude of seeking refuge." Ilave and Huanta were carried out under Piérola's second administration.)

It is not written but it is observed, the axiom that the Indian has no rights, only obligations. Where he is concerned, a personal complaint is taken for insubordination, a collective demand for an attempted uprising. The Spanish royalists killed the Indian when he tried to shake off the yoke of the conquerors; we, the national republicans, exterminate him when he protests against onerous taxes or tires of bearing in silence the iniquities of some satrap.

Our form of government comes down to one great lie, for a state in which two or three million individuals live outside the law does not deserve to be called a democratic republic. If on the coast one glimpses a glimmer of guarantees under a mockery of a republic, in the interior one touches the violation of every right under a true feudal regime. There no Codes govern and no courts of justice hold sway, because landowners and "gamonales" settle every question, arrogating to themselves the roles of judges and executors of the sentences. The political authorities, far from supporting the weak and the poor, nearly always help the rich and strong. There are regions where justices of the peace and governors belong to the servants of the hacienda. What governor, what subprefect, what prefect would dare to stand face to face against a landowner?

A hacienda is formed by the accumulation of small plots seized from their lawful owners; a master exercises over his peons the authority of a Norman baron. Not only does he influence the appointment of governors, mayors and justices of the peace, but he makes marriages, designates heirs, divides inheritances, and, so that the sons may pay off their father's debts, subjects them to a servitude that often lasts their whole lives. He imposes tremendous punishments, such as the "corma," flogging, the field stocks and death; and ludicrous ones, such as shaving the head and cold-water enemas. Whoever respects neither lives nor property would work a miracle if he showed any regard for the honour of women: every Indian woman, single or married, may serve as the target of the "master's" brutal desires. An abduction, a rape, a seduction mean little when it is held that Indian women are to be possessed by main force. And in spite of everything, the Indian does not speak to the master without kneeling and kissing his hand. Let it not be said that the territorial lords act thus out of ignorance or lack of culture: the sons of some landowners go to Europe as children, are educated in France or England and return to Peru with every appearance of civilised people; but no sooner do they shut themselves up on their haciendas than they lose the European varnish and act with more inhumanity and violence than their fathers: with the hat, the poncho and the "roncadoras" spurs, the wild beast reappears. In sum: the haciendas constitute kingdoms in the heart of the Republic; the landowners play the part of autocrats in the midst of democracy.

### IV — IV

To gloss over the Government's neglect and the inhumanity of the despoilers, some pessimists à la Le Bon brand the Indian's forehead with a stigma of infamy: they accuse him of being refractory to civilisation. Anyone would imagine that in all our towns there stand splendid schools, bustling with eminent, well-paid teachers, and that the classrooms remain empty because the children, obeying their parents' orders, do not come to be educated. One would also imagine that the natives fail to follow the edifying examples of the ruling classes, or crucify without the slightest scruple every preacher of lofty and generous ideas. The Indian received what he was given: fanaticism and cane liquor.

Let us see: what is meant by civilisation? Above industry and art, above erudition and science, morality shines like a point of light at the apex of a great pyramid. Not theological morality, founded on a posthumous sanction, but human morality, which seeks no sanction and would not seek one far from the Earth. The summum of morality, for individuals as for societies, consists in having transformed the struggle of man against man into mutual agreement for life. Where there is no justice, mercy or benevolence, there is no civilisation; where the struggle for life is proclaimed as social law, barbarism reigns. What is it worth to acquire the learning of an Aristotle while keeping the heart of a tiger? What does it matter to possess the artistic gift of a Michelangelo while bearing the soul of a pig? Better than passing through the world shedding the light of art or science is to go distilling the honey of goodness. Highly civilised societies would deserve the name if in them doing good had passed from obligation to custom, if the kind act had become an instinctive impulse. Have the rulers of Peru attained that degree of moral development? Have they the right to regard the Indian as a being incapable of civilisation?

The political and social organisation of the ancient empire is admired today by European reformers and revolutionaries. True, Atahualpa did not know the Lord's Prayer, nor did Chalcuchímac ponder the mystery of the Trinity; but the cult of the Sun was perhaps less absurd than the Catholic Religion, and the great Priest of Pachacamac perhaps did not outdo Father Valverde in ferocity. If the subject of Huayna Cápac accepted civilisation, we find no reason why the Indian of the Republic should reject it, unless the whole race had suffered an irremediable physiological decline. Morally speaking, the native of the Republic shows himself inferior to the native whom the conquerors found; but moral depression caused by political servitude is not the same as an absolute impossibility of becoming civilised owing to organic constitution. In any case, on whom would the blame fall?

The facts belie the pessimists. Whenever the Indian is taught in schools, or educated by simple contact with civilised people, he acquires the same degree of morality and culture as the descendant of the Spaniard. At every turn we rub shoulders with yellow-skinned men who dress, eat and think like the "mellifluous gentlemen of Lima." We see Indians in the Chambers, municipalities, the judiciary, universities and athenaeums, where they show themselves neither more venal nor more ignorant than those of other races. It is impossible to apportion responsibilities in the totum revolutum of national politics so as to say what harm was done by the mestizos, the mulattoes and the whites. There is such a promiscuity of bloods and colours, each individual represents so many lawful or unlawful mixtures, that before a great many Peruvians we would be at a loss to determine the dose of black and yellow their organisms contain: no one deserves the title of pure white, though he have blue eyes and fair hair. We need only recall that the ruler of the broadest vision belonged to the indigenous race: his name was Santa Cruz. So did a hundred others, some brave to the point of heroism, like Cahuide, some faithful to the point of martyrdom, like Olaya.

Novicow is right to assert that "the alleged incapacities of the yellow and black peoples are the chimeras of sick minds." Indeed, there is no generous deed that cannot be performed by some Black or some yellow man, just as there is no infamous act that cannot be committed by some white. During the invasion of China in 1900, the yellow men of Japan gave lessons in humanity to the whites of Russia and Germany. We do not recall whether the Blacks of Africa ever gave such lessons to the Boers of the Transvaal or the English of the Cape; but we do know that the Anglo-Saxon Kitchener shows himself as ferocious in the Sudan as Behanzin in Dahomey. If instead of comparing one white-skinned multitude with other dark-skinned multitudes we compare one individual with another, we shall see that in the midst of white civilisation there abound men who are Kaffirs and redskins within. As flowers of the race, or representative men, let us name the King of England and the Emperor of Germany: do Edward VII and Wilhelm II deserve to be compared with the Indian Benito Juárez and the Black Booker Washington? Those who before occupying a throne lived in the tavern, the gaming house and the brothel, those who from the summit of an empire order slaughter sparing neither children, old men nor women, bear whiteness on their skin but hide blackness in their souls.

Does the abasement of the indigenous race depend on ignorance alone? Certainly, the national ignorance seems a fable when one considers that in many villages of the interior there is not a single man able to read or write; that during the War of the Pacific the natives viewed the struggle between the two nations as a civil quarrel between General Chile and General Peru; that not long ago the emissaries of Chucuito went to Tacna imagining they would find the President of the Republic there.

Some pedagogues (rivalling the sellers of panaceas) imagine that once a man knows the tributaries of the Amazon and the mean temperature of Berlin, he has gone halfway towards solving every social question. If by some superhuman phenomenon the nation's illiterates were to wake tomorrow not only knowing how to read and write but holding university diplomas, the problem of the Indian would not be solved: the proletariat of the ignorant would be succeeded by that of bachelors and doctors. Doctors without patients, lawyers without clients, engineers without works, writers without a public, artists without customers, teachers without pupils abound in the most civilised nations, forming the countless army of brains with light and stomachs without bread. Where the haciendas of the coast total four or five thousand fanegadas, where the estates of the sierra measure thirty and even fifty leagues, the nation must divide into lords and serfs.

If education often turns the impulsive brute into a reasonable and magnanimous being, instruction teaches him and lights up the path he must follow so as not to go astray at the crossroads of life. But glimpsing a path is not the same as following it to the end: one needs firmness of will and vigour in one's feet. One also needs a spirit of pride and rebellion, not of submission and deference like the soldier's and the monk's. Instruction can keep a man in baseness and servitude: the eunuchs and grammarians of Byzantium were instructed. To occupy on Earth the place that is his due instead of accepting the one assigned to him; to ask for and take his mouthful; to claim his roof and his patch of ground — this is the right of every rational being.

Nothing changes a man's psychology more quickly or more radically than property: on shaking off the slavery of the belly, he grows a hundred spans taller. Merely by acquiring something, the individual climbs a few rungs on the social ladder, because classes come down to groups ranked by the amount of their wealth. Unlike the hot-air balloon, he rises highest who weighs most. To whoever says "the school," let the answer be: "the school and bread."

The question of the Indian is economic and social rather than pedagogical. How is it to be solved? Not long ago a German conceived the idea of restoring the Empire of the Incas: he learned Quechua, made his way among the Indian communities of Cuzco, began to win followers, and might perhaps have attempted an uprising had death not overtaken him on his return from a journey through Europe. But is such a restoration possible today? In attempting it, in trying to carry it out, one would obtain nothing but a shrunken imitation of a past greatness.

The condition of the native can improve in two ways: either the hearts of the oppressors are moved to the point of recognising the rights of the oppressed, or the spirit of the oppressed acquires manliness enough to teach the oppressors a lesson. If the Indian spent on rifles and cartridges all the money he squanders on alcohol and fiestas, if in a corner of his hut or a hole in a rock he hid a weapon, his condition would change: he would make his property and his life respected. He would answer violence with violence, teaching a lesson to the master who snatches his wool, the soldier who conscripts him in the government's name, the montonero guerrilla who steals his cattle and beasts of burden.

Let the Indian be preached not humility and resignation but pride and rebellion. What has he gained by three or four hundred years of conformity and patience? The fewer authorities he endures, the greater the harms he escapes. There is one revealing fact: greater well-being reigns in the districts farthest from the great haciendas; more order and tranquillity are enjoyed in the villages least frequented by the authorities.

In sum: the Indian will redeem himself through his own effort, not through the humanisation of his oppressors. Every white man is, more or less, a Pizarro, a Valverde or an Areche. (1904; later included in Horas de Lucha, Lima, 1908)
