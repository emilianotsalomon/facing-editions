## Días Alcióneos — Halcyon Days

To Antonio Caso and Alfonso Reyes

In the middle of winter, after the monotonous reign of fog, the halcyon days have arrived. A luminous peace pours over the valley of ancient lacustrine Ilion, and in the classic Wood, pride of the *rusticatio mexicana*, the strife of the seasons merges into a harmony of Venetian splendor. Beside the spare and leafless winter ash, the cedar colors its foliage with autumnal red; and in contrast with the unextinguished dark greenness of the pines, the yellowed carpet of dead leaves extends.

More than a peaceful concert of seasons, one might say it is the victory of autumn; it subdues them, fuses them, triumphs in the broad purplish tonality that envelops the landscapes. Free of summer reverberations, the solar light unifies the unsullied azure and fills the ground with the gold of vintage. Violet imposes its dominion over the arcades.

When the vespertine sky pales with the setting sun, a rosy tint begins to ascend from the west. The strange tint, of miraculous softness and tenderness, grows by moments, invades all the west, and finally fades in the advancing shadows. In the wood, the grave arboreal mass, where rounded crowns are outlined, suggests the vision of a pantheist painter; the terrible majesty of the pine grove evokes the spirit of Turner.

A new messenger of peace, in the western horizon the arc of the crescent moon is illuminated, and with it the mystical star invoked by Wolfram. The vast serenity of the starry night descends, imperatorial, over the calm of the valley.

Fleeting splendor of the halcyon days! Do you not surprise, poet, a jocund rhythm in the great palpitation of the fecund mother? Do you not notice, philosopher, a sudden revelation of supreme harmony? The magic of the atmosphere awakens the longing to erect over the aerial sidereal country the most free, the Aristophanic olympus of birds. For Alcyone nests there, the legendary bird, the sorrowful wife of Ceyx, to whom the gods granted the gift of diffusing such benefits in the middle of the misty season.

Dissolved tomorrow, the fleeting prestige, grey will reign again. And then, instead of the clamorous hymns of the Aristophanic birds, the grave words of the old academic dialogue come to mind. Socrates speaks: "The immortals being so powerful, we who are mortal and insignificant in every way, who cannot encompass the great nor scarcely the small, and who waver most of the time even over those very things that happen around us, are not competent to speak with certainty of halcyons or nightingales. This celebrated legend of your mournful hymns, O bird that modulates laments!, I will relate to my children just as our fathers transmitted it to us, and I will celebrate many times the piety and tenderness of your conjugal love, telling them moreover of the high honor you obtained from the gods..."

Mexico, January 1908.

## Á Leonor M. Feltz en Santo Domingo — To Leonor M. Feltz in Santo Domingo

How long the time has flowed, friend and compatriot, since, moving away from our land, I abandoned the familiar gathering and the readings of your house! The exclusively intellectual life I had led before has been succeeded by long and varied experience of peoples and countries, of ideas and things; distance and years seem to have imposed pauses on our correspondence; and perhaps you think that the memory of old days has already clouded over in me...

And yet, these pages should bear witness to the contrary. It will not escape you, if you read them attentively, how your influence — which you already believed distant, which you perhaps never judged to be much — persists in them.

I know that at first you will declare surprise. You will say that in your gatherings we read and talked as companions and no mastery or discipleship was apparent; that behind me lay the heritage of my intellectual household; that my stay in the North taught me what you could not; that even lands similar to ours will have taught me something...

I do not tell you that you are the only influence I acknowledge. But the others have been, when personal, familiar; when foreign, only of atmosphere.

That you were not a teacher in the readings of your salon? That many times you did not choose them, since my brother and I looked for the books? Our very freedom of action gave more efficacy to your influence. Max and I had barely left adolescence, and you, with ten or twelve more years, with your perspicacity and your knowledge and your refinement, already walked surely through the regions of thought and art. Your love of intellectual substance, your gift of psychology, your taste for good style — could they not but orient our inclinations?

Of myself I know you guided me in the way of modern literature. What a multitude of books we went through during the year in which I attended your house, and above all, what a river of commentary flowed then! Your taste, without forgetting the respect due to the classics, to Shakespeare (whom we then reread almost in its entirety), to the Spanish masters, guided us in traversing the Castilian poetry of both worlds, the Spanish theater from the origins of Romanticism, the French novel, the work of Tolstoy, that of D'Annunzio, the dramas of Hauptmann and Sudermann, recent Scandinavian literature, and in particular the theater of Ibsen, whose passionate cult was the soul of your gatherings.

I tell you that that was a decisive epoch for me. My themes are already different; then pragmatism was not spoken of (hardly had it emerged), nor Bergson, nor Bernard Shaw, nor the criticism of Mauclair, nor the new Spanish literature. But your influence has continued presiding over my hours of study.

And here is its fruit. Ah! My life too is different. The enthusiastic adolescence, exclusive in the cult of the intellectual, sometimes taciturn for internal reasons, never external, disappeared to give way to laborious youth, toiling to overcome ambient pressures, the iron circles that limit the anxious aspiration for boundless space. Before I had all the hours for study; today I can save only a few for it — the tranquil hours, the serene and clear days, the halcyon days.

And this labor of my hours of study, of my halcyon days, goes today to remind you of an entire year of intellectual activity that you directed and whose influence endures; it goes toward you, to the distant and sad homeland, sad like all its sons, solitary like them in the intimacy of its sorrows and its misunderstood longings.

Mexico, October 1909.

## El Positivismo de Comte — Comte's Positivism

To give lectures on positivism may seem in Europe an attempt of little current interest or of only historical interest. Not so in our America: among us, positivism is still a living thing. In Mexico, the philosophy of Comte, fused with theories of Spencer and ideas of Mill, is the official philosophy, since it reigns in education from the reform directed by Gabino Barreda, and is invoked as the ideological base of the prevailing political tendencies. Although the positivists have not managed to implant here, as in Brazil, the ecclesiastical rites of the religion that Comte added to his philosophical conception, Mexican Comtism has its periodical organ (the *Revista positiva*), in whose support is employed a tenacity similar to that shown in favor of the same cause by the celebrated Juan Enrique Lagarrigue in Chile. *Sotto voce*, part of the youth already follows other paths; but the criticism of positivist ideas (not conservative criticism, not Catholic, but advanced — that which is inspired by the contemporary intellectual movement) has barely begun with the memorable discourse of D. Justo Sierra in honor of Barreda (1908).

An easy lecture on John Stuart Mill, on the occasion of his centenary (1906); two serious and brilliant works on Nietzsche (1907) and Max Stirner (1908): these were the credentials for the exercise of philosophical criticism that the lecturer, Antonio Caso, could show. Those works demonstrated that the lecturer is one of the most capable men here to undertake, with philosophical criteria and extensive documentation, the historical-critical study of positivism, formulating an impartial judgment obtainable neither from positivist sectarians nor from their frank enemies the Catholics. From Caso one could expect a free and varied study, enriched with the opinions of recent criticism; truly, many expected it.

And here it is that the three lectures on Comte and his precursors barely respond to what was expected. Neither in the historical part, nor in the expository, nor in the critical has the lecturer introduced the desired elements of novelty: he has contented himself, in general, with the exposition, the tracing of origins, and the laudatory judgments that the partisans of positivism have long presented to us. Caso has not abstained from criticism, but from frank censure: he has exercised the critical function only halfway.

If it were a matter of popularization, all would be well; but who can believe that positivism needs to be popularized in Mexico more than it already has been? On this point it is apt to recall Mill's words in his *Auguste Comte and Positivism* (1865): "While a writer has few readers and no influence... the only thing that need be taken account of is what we can learn from him... But if the important place that Comte has already won among European thinkers, and the growing influence of his principal work, make it easier and more encouraging today to carry to minds the solid parts of his philosophy, they also indicate that it is no longer inopportune to discuss his errors. The errors into which he has fallen, whatever they may be, may already exert harmful influence, and to expose them freely will no longer be prejudicial."

The subsequent criticism has not been sparing in putting Mill's counsel into practice; on the contrary it has gone to extremes. With both excesses dispelled, the French thinker already belongs to the history of philosophy; not dominating it as a supreme summit, but integrating it, as a rocky peak, the mountain range whose nucleus is the Kantian critique. "These last years, affirms the eminent Boutroux, have seen the glory of Comte reflourish. It has sufficed, for this philosopher to occupy his definitive place among the masters of humanity, that the stereotyped judgments of his panegyrists and his detractors be disdained, and that he be read."

Nobody speaks of a return to Comte as one speaks of a return to Kant. The readers of the voluminous *Course*, among the new European generations, are probably no more than those of (I cite as an example) Fichte's *Doctrine of Science*; notoriously they are far fewer than those of Hegel and Schopenhauer: while these two names are constantly mentioned in philosophical journals and books, that of Comte is cited from time to time. Primitive positivism is already buried. Of it remain two or three initial impulses, integrated into the philosophical tradition of the 19th century; the text is falling into oblivion.

Contemporary independent criticism sees in Comte a thinker guided by social rather than philosophical tendencies: his ideal was the perfect organization of society, and to lay the foundation of his political conceptions he constructed his philosophical system. "His originality," writes Lévy-Bruhl, "lies in asking science and philosophy for the principles of social reorganization, the true aim of his efforts." Giovanni Papini calls him a *mathematical Messiah*.

The *Course of Positive Philosophy* (1830–1842) appears between the essay on *Positive Politics* (1822–1824) and the vast *System* of the same name in four volumes (1851–1854). His *Religion of Humanity* was born dead, and very few have been its faithful. The *Positivist Catechism*, George Henry Lewes opined, did him more harm than all his enemies' attacks could.

Judging the *Course* in the philosophical terrain, criticism points out foundational deficiencies. The negligent or arbitrary use of the terms *metaphysics, philosophy*, and *science* leads Comte to believe himself free of the first, by setting aside the explanation of causes and essences, and capable of constituting the second with purely scientific notions. "In defining metaphysics," observes Louis Liard, "he seems to have in mind above all scholasticism. But the destruction of scholastic entities was accomplished, not by science, but by metaphysics itself."

"Since he takes metaphysics," asserts Edward Caird, "in the sense of philosophy in the scholastic mode, it could not be said that for him true metaphysics is a contemptible form of thought.... Hostility to metaphysics — if by metaphysics is understood the explanation of facts of experience by means of entities or causes unverifiable by experience — characterizes all of modern philosophy, idealist or sensualist. It manifests in Descartes as in Bacon, in Kant and Hegel as in Locke and Hume."

But what is characteristic of the metaphysician is the tendency to the unification of human conceptions, to the "totalization of experience" (Kant), to "thinking things together" (Hegel); and Comte, obsessed at once by the desire for unity and by the sense of the plurality of things, places himself in an ambiguous situation. "His opinions on the gravest problems," says De Roberty, "contradict each other so often as to disconcert criticism... He is monist and pluralist (or dualist) at once. He does not impose on himself the effort to reconcile his agnosticism with his monism."

It could not be said that criticism has reached agreement on Comte's attitude toward the problem of unity. He postulated the superposition of natural phenomena, according to his classification of the sciences — a concatenation in which some have wished to see an evolutionary principle — which can be understood in this critique of Bergson (*Creative Evolution*): "Post-Kantian philosophy accepts from mechanism the idea of one science, the same for all kinds of reality... It marks in nature the same articulations that mechanism saw; from mechanism it keeps all the design: only the colors have changed."

Comte postulated, at the same time, the independence, irreducibility, and discontinuity of the phenomena studied by each science. However, more than once he tried hypotheses of universality. The unity decisively advocated by Comte is that which should be obtained by the supremacy of the sociological viewpoint: he predicts "the spontaneous advent of true unity in the system of positive philosophy." But the truth is that Comte fails to discover the unifying principle that will derive from sociology, nor how the superior will explain the inferior.

Comte's tendencies toward systematic monism are only momentary aspects of his thought: during almost all the *Course* he proceeds with agnostic caution: hence, as George Trumbull Ladd perspicaciously observes, results "a certain subtle irony in the fact that the word positivism has come to represent on so large a scale negative conclusions, precisely in the spheres of philosophy, morality, and religion."

Carrying this attitude to its logical consequences, Comte could have given the formula of William James's pragmatic pluralism. Émile Corra opines that Comte was a pragmatist *avant la lettre*. But his purpose of confining himself to scientific ideas took him away from strictly philosophical discussion; his pragmatism is not critical, and his agnosticism has vague limits.

"He has not felt," expresses Höffding, "all the sharpness of the problem of knowledge... He recognizes that one can arrive only at a result that approximates reality, but his practical viewpoint prevents him from discussing to what extent our knowledge can be called an exact reflection of reality. It suffices him that the knowledge we possess can practically serve to orient us."

His very disdain for psychological introspection gives his system the aspect of exclusive objectivism. "He suppresses," thinks Fouillée, "the psychological viewpoint... In reality, consciousness occupies no place whatsoever in his system."

His norms are always pragmatic. "We must," he writes in lecture LIX of the *Course*, "seek to know only the laws of phenomena susceptible of exerting some influence over humanity." The anxiety for order leads him to legislate against investigations he judges rash. Relying on the immutability of the laws of nature, he erects the dogmatism of science "that tolerates the freedom of faith," says Windelband, "as little as theology tolerated it in the Middle Ages."

In sum, Comte fails to justify, with precise analysis, either his concept of the relativity of knowledge, or his faith in science and his hopes of philosophical unity: he posits them *a priori*, and throughout his work tends to support them with incidental reasons. Positivism is, in the end, "dogmatism without critique" (Liard).

Nietzsche manages to discover the base of idealist metaphysics on which the positivist creed rests: "Positivists are the last idealists of knowledge... Their will to truth at any cost, their faith in the absolute, unconditional value of truth and science, are only an infinitely refined, subtle form of the ascetic and Christian spirit. Our faith in science is always founded on a metaphysical belief; we too, the thinkers of today, the atheists, the anti-metaphysicians — we too draw this animating faith from the conflagration kindled by a belief a thousand years old, by that Christian faith which was also Plato's, and which teaches that God is truth, and truth divine."

But if the metaphysics implied in Comte's work is ambiguous and weak, his philosophy of the sciences, on the other hand, is one of the most powerful efforts of the 19th century. Comte's enterprise was to demonstrate that in the scientific order, experimental notions had already been reached, thanks to which a vast body of doctrine could be formed capable of satisfying the intellectual needs of the disoriented majority.

The law of three states, though not originally his (for Turgot had formulated it), served to illuminate not a few questions of the intellectual evolution of humanity. The classification of the sciences served as a point of departure for the constitution of the contemporary encyclopedia. Comte brought to philosophy no essentially new notion, but placed at its disposal, in better order than before, the ensemble of the sciences; his role had soon to cease being active and to become historical.

But his services are not limited to that: a brilliant popularizer, he gave the first vigorous impulse to the movement that, in carrying to the majority the philosophical agitation in the form of simple speculations, democratized reason and proclaimed that, according to Ladd's phrase, "in philosophy nobody remains excluded."

Mexico, 1909.

## El Positivismo Independiente — Independent Positivism

If Antonio Caso's three lectures on Comte and his precursors meant little, for their lack of novelty and criticism, the four subsequent ones, devoted to independent positivism, largely made up for the initial deficiency. In his first dissertations, the lecturer presented Comte's philosophy as a dogmatic monument difficult to touch; now, the edifice appeared sinking slowly, like the colonial buildings of Mexico City, and perhaps close to disappearing from the face of the earth.

The nucleus of his last lectures was this affirmation: the definitive formula of the positivist criterion is the experientialism of John Stuart Mill; the critical idealism according to which the subjectivity of knowledge cannot be overcome, nor can the reality of the external world be derived from experience, but only the order it presents to us. Mill is the one who studies with true critical determination the problem of knowledge; and for this reason, his positivism is the only one that survives, fruitful and exemplary.

As can be seen, Caso resolves affirmatively the still disputed problem of Mill's positivist lineage. For the English, the question has already been resolved negatively: Mill, their John Stuart Mill, is of purely British and Scottish descent. For continental Europeans, the personality of Mill, though defined before the appearance of Comtian doctrine, influences together with it to produce a philosophical current whose heyday lasts half a century. No one now dreams of classifying Mill as a disciple of Comte: son of the English school, he observed before Comte the attitude of an enthusiastic and learned reader who selects and makes use, never of a follower who submits before an unexpected philosophical revelation.

Mill never avoided criticism, did not sacrifice philosophy to science, did not disdain classical thought: he himself was a spirit of tradition as much as of evolution; and thus his works are classical, are so from the moment they appear.

But if positivism is or should be a complete philosophy, that would begin by studying the problem of knowledge, then the obligatory thing would be to recognize that John Stuart Mill, a classical thinker, is the one who managed to fix the positivist criterion, who, by having sufficient logical audacity, defined the significance of experience as the basis of scientific methods. Mill, declares Antonio Caso, is the most perfect and true positivist for being the most logical. And for being the most logical, he came to place himself, frankly, within the terrain demarcated by the Kantian critique, at the crossroads of subjectivism, of critical idealism.

Not all the truth, however. Positive philosophy, in defining itself in Comte, did not tend toward the idealist criterion, but toward the realist; and in fact, the most vigorous and popular current of positivism did not follow the route marked by Mill, but went to empty into Spencer's realism. Positivism was destined to be a popular philosophy; and every popular philosophy is realist, at least in part.

The idealist thinkers, the aristocrats of positivism — Mill, Taine, Renan — remained all but unknown in their fundamental analyses: they were taken into account only for their services to logic and history.

And it is thus that Mill's criterion, which should have struggled against the realist current, entered into true struggle only in the small circles of thinkers disposed to confront metaphysical discussions. But that criterion is the most exact that came to be formulated within or near positivism, and it survives the others, serving as stimulus to new tendencies. At the end of the 19th century, critics of all camps could affirm that the positivism accepted by the majority of men of science had anchored definitively in the idealist criterion.

"All that we know of objects is: the sensations they give us, and the order in which those sensations occur... Of the external world we know and can know nothing but the sensations we obtain from it": there is the fundamental declaration of Mill's critical idealism (*Logic*, book I, chapter III, § 7). It is true that it leaves open the way of intuition, but it is plain that he concedes to intuition a limited reach.

Mill's name is cited today in relation to the new philosophical tendencies: this fact is due, in my opinion, not so much to the ideas he actually expounded, as to the prestige of his sagacity and his critical prudence. In Comte there are, more than in Mill, germs of pragmatism; but they are never defined except as desires for order and utility: *savoir pour prévoir*. On the other hand, it is understandable that Mill, in placing the epistemological problem at the borders of skepticism, should have aroused in William James the desire to justify knowledge by giving it the value of action rather than of reality. Pragmatism, therefore, is the child of critical idealism.

Mexico, 1909.

## Nietzsche y el Pragmatismo — Nietzsche and Pragmatism

The new philosophy that runs under the popular name of pragmatism preoccupies today the thinkers of both sides of the Atlantic; though it is also often called, by classification, anti-intellectualism; by origin, American philosophy, since North Americans are its principal master, William James, and its precursor, Charles Sprague Peirce; humanism, by the name proposed by Professor F. C. S. Schiller.

But anti-intellectualism was born, in reality, though not complete, with Schopenhauer, and since then has been growing slowly, until producing the new tendencies of recent years: the psychologically-based philosophies of Wundt in Germany and Bergson in France; the pragmatism of James, Schiller, and Dewey, propagated rapidly in the United States, England, Italy, and France, where it has received singular support from many modernist Catholics.

Although the name of Nietzsche has not been mentioned very often in relation to the new doctrines, to him must be attributed, particularly, the agitation that provoked them. With his astonishing perspicacity as critic and psychologist, and his enthusiasm and strength as a writer, he declared war on the classical tables of intellectual and moral values; he wished to make disappear the fixed orientations of Pure Reason and dogmatic morality, and managed to agitate, with a profound perturbation that still reverberates, the philosophical atmosphere of Europe.

In this note I propose only to point out the surprising coincidences between some of Nietzsche's aphorisms and the principal affirmations of James's pragmatism. I say coincidences, because it is a fact that James has not been a follower of Nietzsche, and because the pragmatist affirmations of the German thinker have remained half-hidden beneath his principal ideas.

I will attempt to briefly summarize the central ideas expounded by William James in his book *Pragmatism*, published in 1907 and dedicated to the memory of John Stuart Mill, "from whom I learned," says the North American master, "the pragmatic broadmindedness of thought."

"The pragmatic method," he says in chapter II, "tends to resolve metaphysical disputes that would otherwise become interminable; it attempts to interpret each notion by tracing its practical consequences." The name derives from the Greek word *pragma*, meaning action, practice. The one who first introduced it into philosophy was Charles Sprague Peirce, in 1878, affirming that our beliefs are in reality rules of action, and that to penetrate the significance of an idea, we must determine what kind of conduct it is capable of producing.

Pragmatism comes to replace the old intellectualist methods. "It represents," says James, "the old empiricist attitude, but represents it in a more radical and less censurable manner than the other forms under which it has appeared until now. It is only a method... It disregards first principles, categories; it always seeks things, fruits, consequences." For it, "theories are instruments, not answers to enigmas."

But pragmatism implies, at once as a method, a theory of truth. "For the intellectualists," says James, "truth essentially means an inert static relation." But pragmatism asks: if an idea is true, what difference will it make in action? Its answer is: "True ideas are those that we can assimilate, make valid, verify." Truth, for pragmatism, is not an absolute value: an idea becomes true; its truth is an event, a process: its verification.

As for Nietzsche, the work that clearly indicates his pragmatist tendencies, in the epoch of his plenitude, is *The Gay Science*. What matters, Nietzsche has said, is not that something be true (in the static sense of intellectualism), but that one believe that something is true. "The inner happiness and unhappiness of men," he says in aphorism 44, "have depended on their faith in such or such a motive, not on whether the motive was true. The latter has been of secondary interest."

"In reality, we possess no organ for knowledge, for truth," he says in aphorism 354. "We know, or think we know, what it is useful for us to know in the interest of the human flock..." "We have arranged" (he says in aphorism 121) "for our particular use, a world in which we can live... But this does not prove that such articles are true. Life is not an argument; among the conditions of life, error might figure."

"For long ages" (aphorism 110) "intelligence generated nothing but errors. Some of these proved useful for the preservation of the species... It was admitted, for example, that there exist equal things, that there are objects, substances, bodies, that things are what they appear to be, that our will is free..."

Finally, he preludes pluralism (aphorism 374): "I believe that by now we are cured of that ridiculous immodesty that would claim, from our angle of vision, that perspectives can only be drawn from within our own optical angle. On the contrary, the world has become infinite for us once more, inasmuch as we cannot reject the possibility that it may include infinite interpretations."

And yet, Nietzsche, in the depth of his German spirit, yearned for pure knowledge: "When shall we be entitled to turn again to a pure, newly discovered, emancipated Nature?"

Mexico, 1908.

## La Sociología de Hostos — The Sociology of Hostos

More than a contemplative thinker, Eugenio María de Hostos was a teacher and apostle of action, whose immaculate and astonishingly fruitful life is a truly superhuman example. Born in Puerto Rico, he was educated in Spain, in the epoch of Krausism; he studied not only the sciences, but also classical philosophy, the German thinkers, the positivists and their pedagogy; and when he was beginning to distinguish himself among the intellectual youth of the metropolis, he preferred, to a certain future of triumphs and universal renown, the obscure but redemptive work on behalf of the American land, and launched himself into laboring for the independence of Cuba, for the dignity of Puerto Rico, for education in Santo Domingo.

On dying in 1903, he left published eighteen volumes and in manuscript an enormous material of literary and scientific writings. Only two of his great doctrinal works were published in his lifetime: *Social Morality* and *Constitutional Law*. The *Treatise on Sociology* initiates the posthumous series.

The volume of Sociology contains two treatises: the first, which is the most important, dates from 1901; the second, which is offered as a summary of the former, dates from 1883. These notions were written for the advanced course of the Normal School of Santo Domingo: despite the fact that today many universities still debate whether sociology should be admitted to their programs, Hostos had included it, more than twenty years ago, in the education of Dominican teachers.

It is a work whose importance would be difficult to exaggerate; what it lacks in extension it gains in intensity, and its exposition, as logical and concise as it is rich in data, carries notable advantage over the minutious and redundant exposition of almost all the theoreticians of sociology. The original merit of this work is all the greater in that, at the time Hostos wrote the first Notions, social science was far from its present state of feverish elaboration.

Hostos begins the first group of lessons by pointing out the place that sociology occupies among the sciences, and defines it as an abstract science encompassing all the superorganic order. He then fixes its method ("the inductive-deductive, because its true procedure is experimental"); and, relying on observations of important facts, he formulates six fundamental laws: Sociability, Work, Liberty, Progress, Conservation, and Civilization or the Law of the Ideal, which are productive of the true social order.

The highest merit of Hostos as a sociologist rests on his conception of seven laws governing all superorganic life. The fundamental law of Hostosian sociology is incontestable: Sociability, whose origin he seeks more in necessity than in the admirable concept of "consciousness of species" developed by Giddings and already before sketched by Darwin, who sees in sympathy the basis of the social instinct, itself the basis of the moral sense.

In his fundamental philosophy, Hostos is determinist: he accepts as absolute and necessary the cosmic laws. But in sociology he admits liberty as a product of individual life. "That property we call Liberty," says Hostos, "is the natural mode of doing things... the tendency to impose our own way of being on our way of proceeding"... "The more consciousness we have of the physical and psychic functions of our being, the more vigorously do we adhere to this natural way of doing things."

He considers the human will as a disturbing agent that tends to obstruct the realization of the order that must result from the efficacious fulfillment of the natural laws of society, but an agent that it is possible to reduce, through education and civilization, to the fulfillment of those same laws; and he believes that "man is already an adult in reason and can even be considered an adult in conscience," and, in such virtue, must already begin to govern his individual and collective acts by the interpretation of the truths he has discovered.

Therefore, having been Hostos a thinker who, with all his great love of truth ("Give me truth and I give you the world"), loved the good much more, and esteemed science as "a virtuality tending toward action," which must serve human perfection, it is just that his *Treatise on Sociology* should prove a work of practical tendencies at the same time as of scientific constitution.

His conception of the possible social future is condensed in the paragraph in which he analyzes the probabilities of civilization, after indicating that it never arrives at a definite state, since it is more of a purpose:

"The omnilateral, simultaneous, and concurrent development of all the organs and functions of any society whatsoever would be the only thing capable of producing at one and the same time, as expression, as sign of that development, the three characters we have just analyzed (industrialism, intellectualism, and moralism). Probably, that concurrence of all organs and all functions in social development will be impossible, unless in the course of time, in the increase of common reason, in the increase of will through morality, in the universal predominance of conscience, it becomes possible that collective man be at once a complete worker, a correct reasoner, and a punctual realizer of the virtues of work and reason."

Havana, 1905.
