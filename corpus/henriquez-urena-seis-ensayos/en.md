## Discontent / and Promise

“I will do such things—what they are yet I know not.” The words of the mad king are the motto we have been inscribing, for a hundred years now, on our banners of spiritual revolution. Shall we overcome the discontent that provokes so many successive rebellions? Shall we fulfill the ambitious promise?

No sooner had we emerged from the thick colonial cloud into the burning sun of independence than we shook off the spirit of timidity and declared our lordship over the future. A virgin world, a newborn liberty, republics in ferment, ardently consecrated to the immortal utopia: here new arts, a new poetry, were to be created. Our lands, our free life, demanded their expression.

#### Literary Independence

In 1823, before the battles of Junín and Ayacucho, with political independence still unfinished, Andrés Bello was proclaiming spiritual independence: the first of his *Silvas americanas* is an address to Poetry, “mistress [scan 6] of peoples and of kings,” bidding her abandon Europe—light and misery—and seek on this shore of the Atlantic the wholesome air that suits her native rusticity. The form is classical; the intention is revolutionary. With the “Alocución,” symbolically, Juan María Gutiérrez was to open our first great anthology, the *América poética* of 1846. The second of Bello’s *Silvas*, three years later, in singing the agriculture of the torrid zone, while it shields behind the peaceful imperial shades of Horace and Virgil the “return to nature,” that weapon of the revolutionaries of the eighteenth century, sketches the whole “nineteenth-century” program of material aggrandizement, with culture as its exercise and crown. Nor is that patriarch, that creator of civilization, the only one kindled by the spirit of initiation and prophecy: the signal fire leaps, like Agamemnon’s, from summit to summit, and blazes in Olmedo’s song of victory, in the insurgent cries of Heredia, in the novels and the humanitarian and democratic campaigns of Fernández de Lizardi, even in the *cielitos* and the gaucho dialogues of Bartolomé Hidalgo.

A few years later there arises yet another new generation, forgetful and discontented. In Europe, we heard tell, or saw for ourselves, Romanticism was awakening the voices of the peoples. Our fathers seemed absurd to us for singing the romantic adventure of our independence in classical odes. Romanticism would open to us the road of truth, would teach us to complete ourselves. So thought Esteban Echeverría, a meager artist, save in one or another landscape of straight lines and bare masses, but a clear theorist. “The spirit of the century,” he said, “leads nations today to emancipate themselves, to enjoy independence, not only political but philosophical and literary.” And among the young men he swept along with him, in that Argentine generation which became a continental voice, there was constant talk of “citizenship in art as in politics” and of “a literature that should bear the national colors.”

[scan 7]

Our literature avidly drank in water from all the native rivers: nature; country life, settled or nomadic; the Indigenous tradition; memories of the colonial period; the exploits of the liberators; the political agitation of the moment… The romantic flood lasted long, too long; since, under pretext of inspiration and spontaneity, it protected sloth, it drowned many seeds it hoped to nourish… When the waters began to recede, not after the biblical forty days but after forty years, they left behind them tremendous stretches of tall grass, a few rare shrubs, and two leafy trees, as resistant as ombús: the *Facundo* and the *Martín Fierro*.

Discontent provokes at last the necessary insurrection: the generation that scandalized the vulgar under the modest name of *modernista* rises against romantic sloth and imposes on itself severe and delicate disciplines. It takes its examples from Europe, but it thinks of America. “It is like a family,” said one of them, the fascinating, the dazzling Martí. “It began with imitated affectation and has now arrived at a loose and concise elegance and in the artistic and sincere expression, brief and carved, of personal feeling and of creole, direct judgment.” Creole judgment! Or again: “That is the literature we must move toward: the one that broadens and reveals, that draws from the bloodstained rind the healthy and juicy almond, that strengthens and uplifts the heart of America.” Rubén Darío, if in the prefatory words of *Prosas profanas* he detested “the life and the time in which he was born,” was at the same time founding the *Revista de América*, whose name is a program, and in time became the author of the iambs against Roosevelt, of the “Canto a la Argentina,” and of the “Viaje a Nicaragua.” And Rodó, the enthusiastic commentator on *Prosas profanas*, is the one who later declares, in studying Montalvo, that “only those have been great in America who have developed, by word or by action, an American feeling.”

[scan 8]

Now, thirty years later, there are once more restless youths in Spanish America, who chafe against their elders and offer to work seriously in search of our genuine expression.

#### Tradition and Rebellion

The restless ones of today complain that their forebears lived with their attention on Europe, nourishing themselves on imitation, without eyes for the world around them: they forget that in every generation, for a hundred years now, discontent and promise have been renewed. There existed, yes, and there still exist, the Europeanizers, those who go so far as to abandon Spanish to write in French, or at least, writing in our own language, fit their style to French molds and even ask France for their ideas and their subjects. Or the Hispanizers, sick with grammatical madness, hypnotized by everything of Spain that has not been transplanted to these soils.

But let us dare to doubt everything. Are these crimes really so unusual and unpardonable? Is closed *criollismo*, the nationalist zeal, the many-formed delirium in which men and women even of enemy camps coincide, the only health? Our preoccupation is of a new kind. The Romans, for example, rarely knew it: for them, the arts, the letters, the philosophy of the Greeks were the norm; to the norm they sacrificed, without a tremor or a complaint, every native tradition. The *carmen saturnium*, their “creole verse,” had to yield its place to verse of quantitative feet; the autochthonous sprouts of theatrical entertainment were crushed beneath the wheels of the cart that brought from another’s house its load of plots and forms; even the national legend was retouched, in the aristocratic epic, to link it with Ilium; and if few writers dared to change their language (despite the imperial example of Marcus Aurelius, whose Greek prose is no better than the French of our friends today), the journey [scan 9] to Athens, to the shrunken Athens of the times of Augustus, had the ritual character of our journeys to Paris, and the event was celebrated, as now, with the obligatory banquet, with farewell odes like Horace’s to the ship in which Virgil embarked. The Roman soul found expression in literature, but under foreign precepts, under imitation, raised to a method of learning.

Nor did the Middle Ages look on imitations with shame. On the contrary: all peoples, despite their indelible characteristics, aspired to learn and apply the norms that northern France gave for the *chanson de geste*, the laws of troubadour art that Provence dictated for lyric poetry; and a certain number of themes went to and fro from kingdom to kingdom, from people to people: Carolingian exploits, Celtic tales of love and enchantment, fantastic distortions of the Trojan War and the conquests of Alexander, tales of the fox, dances of death, Christmas and Passion mystery plays, carnival farces… Even language was adopted, temporarily and partially, along with the literary fashion: Provençal, throughout the Latin Mediterranean; French, in Italy, with the epic song; Galician, in Castile, with the lyric song. People did fight, yes, on behalf of their own language, but against moribund Latin, entrenched in the University and the Church, without the blood of real life, without the prestige of the Courts or the popular festivals. As an exception, fourteenth-century England brings down the leafy French tree planted there by the conqueror of the eleventh.

And the Renaissance? The renascent effort devotes itself to seeking, not the characteristic expression, national or regional, but the expression of the archetype, the universal and perfect norm. Italy and France concentrate their efforts on discovering and defining it, relying on the study of Greece and Rome, ark of all secrets. France carried to its maximum development this imperialism of spiritual paradigms. Thus England and [scan 10] Spain possessed systems of dramatic art of their own, that of Shakespeare, that of Lope (a brilliant improviser, but weak in artistic conscience, to the point of begging pardon for writing to the taste of his compatriots); but in the eighteenth century they were bending to the impositions of Paris: the expression of the national spirit could be attained only through international formulas.

At last came the rebellion that assaulted and cast down the classical empire, culminating in a battle of the nations, fought on every front, from Russia to Norway and from Ireland to Catalonia. The problem of the genuine expression of each people lies in the very essence of the romantic revolution, together with the denial of the foundations of all rhetorical doctrine, of all faith in “the rules of art” as the key to aesthetic creation. And, from generation to generation, each people hones and sharpens its nationalist theories, precisely to the degree that science and the machine multiply the uniformities of the world. With each practical concession goes an ideal rebellion.

#### The Problem of Language

Our restlessness is explained. Infected, spurred on, we suffer here in America from a romantic urgency of expression. Sudden fears seize us: we want to say our word before some imminent deluge, we know not what, buries us.

The problem arises in all the arts. But in literature it is doubly complex. The musician could, at the utmost extremity, if he believes he finds in this the guarantee of originality, renounce the tonal language of Europe: to the son of peoples where the Indian survives—as in Peru and Bolivia—the archaic but unfading native system is offered, which from its very pentatonic scale departs from the European. And the man of countries where the creole spirit prevails is master of precious materials, though not strictly autochthonous: music brought from Europe or from Africa, [scan 11] but impregnated with the savor of the new lands and the new life, which filters into the rhythm and the melodic design.

And in the plastic arts it is possible to renounce Europe, as in the Mexican system of Adolfo Best, built upon the seven linear elements of Aztec drawing, with frank acceptance of its limitations. Or at least, if we feel so much renunciation to be excessive, there are suggestions of the most varied kind in the work of the Indigenous artist, in that of the creole of colonial times who made European technique his own (so, with splendor of mastery, in architecture), in the popular work of our own days, even in the stone and wood and fiber and dye that our native lands provide.

In any case, in music and in the plastic arts the parting of the ways is clear: either the European, or the Indigenous, or in any event the creole road, still undecided and laborious. The Indigenous perhaps represents impoverishment and limitation, and for many, to whose cities the ancient lord of the soil never comes, it proves an exotic road: a paradox typically ours. But, strange or familiar, distant or near, the tonal language and the plastic language of Indigenous lineage are intelligible.

In literature the problem is complex, it is double: the poet, the writer, express themselves in a language received from Spain. The man of Catalonia or Galicia need only write in his vernacular tongue to realize the illusion of feeling himself distinct from the Castilian. For us this illusion is forbidden or inaccessible fruit. Return to the Indigenous languages? The man of letters generally does not know them, and the hard task of studying them and writing in them would lead him to the final consequence of being understood by very few, to the immediate shrinking of his public. There were, after the conquest, and there are still being composed, verse and prose in Indigenous languages, because enormous, scattered aboriginal populations still exist who speak a hundred—if not more—native tongues; but that literature is seldom animated by lucid purposes of persistence and opposition. Create languages of our own, children and successors [scan 12] of Castilian? Until a few years ago there was—grave fear of some, mad hope of others—the idea that we were embarked on the hazardous attempt to create creole languages. The cloud has dispersed under the unifying pressure of the constant relations among the Hispanic peoples. The attempt, supposing it possible, would have demanded centuries of digging ditch after ditch between the language of Castile and those germinating in America, resigning ourselves with Franciscan heroism to a crawling, impoverished dialectal expression until the Dante should appear, creator of wings and claws. Let us note in passing that the gauchesque speech of the River Plate, the principal substance of that dispersed cloud, has not in itself diversity enough to raise it even to a dialect like that of León or of Aragon: its slight shade removes it too little from Castile, and the *Martín Fierro* and the *Fausto* are branches no further from the linguistic trunk than the Murcian or Andalusian coplas.

We have not renounced writing in Spanish, and our problem of original and proper expression begins there. Each language is a crystallization of ways of thinking and feeling, and whatever is written in it is bathed in the color of its crystal. Our expression will need double vigor to impose its tonality upon the red and the gold.

#### The Formulas of Americanism

Let us examine the principal solutions proposed and attempted for the problem of our expression in literature. And let no one prematurely charge me with naive optimism for granting them all provisional approval: the end will show why.

First of all, nature. Descriptive literature was to be, we thought for a long time, the voice of the New World. Now the idea enjoys no favor: we have abused its application; there are in our romantic poetry as many landscapes as in [scan 13] our impressionist painting. The task of describing, born of enthusiasm, degenerated into a mechanical habit. But it has educated our eyes: from the conventional picture of the first colonial writers, in whom only now and then the genuine face of the land peeped through, as in the Peruvian sierras of the Inca Garcilaso, we passed little by little, and finally arrived, with the help of Alexander von Humboldt and Chateaubriand, at the direct vision of nature. From much forgotten nineteenth-century literature it would be both justice and delight to extract a lively collection of landscapes and miniatures of fauna and flora. It is enough to pause and recall in order to understand, perhaps with surprise, how we have conquered, stretch by stretch, the pictorial elements of our twin continents and even the spiritual aroma that exhales from them: the colossal mountain; the vast high plateaus of thin air and tranquil light where every outline is sharply cut; the warm lands of the tropics, with their tangles of jungle, their deafening sea and their intoxicating light; the deep pampa; the “inexorable and surly” desert. Our attention to landscape engenders preferences that find vehement words: we have partisans of the plain and partisans of the mountain. And while the former, accustomed to eyes meeting no limit but the horizon, feel oppressed by the neighborhood of heights, like Miguel Cané in Venezuela and Colombia, the others complain of the “too flat” landscape, like the character in Güiraldes’s *Xaimaca*, or else, willing to love it, overcome the initial impression of monotony and desolation and tell how, after traveling the pampa a long while, we no longer see it: we see another pampa that has been made in our spirit (Gabriela Mistral). Or let us approach the spectacle of the torrid zone: for the native it is rich in light, heat, and color, but languid and full of ease; everything dissolves for him in long contemplations, in savory talk, in slow dances, [scan 14] *and in the burning nights of summer*

*the bandola and the long-drawn song*

*that joins its stanza to the murmur of the river…*

But the man of temperate climates sees the tropics through an overwhelming dazzlement: so Mármol saw it in Brazil, in those famous verses, half padding, half the discovery of something lived; so Sarmiento saw it in that brief and total sketch of Rio de Janeiro:

The insects are carbuncles or rubies, the butterflies floating plumes of gold, the birds painted, adorned with crests and fantastic ornaments, the vegetation emerald green, the flowers fragrant and purple, the light of the sky tangible, the air cobalt blue, the clouds gilded with fire, the earth red and the sands mingled with diamonds and topazes.

To nature we add the primitive inhabitant. Go toward the Indian! A program that is born and reborn in every generation, under a multitude of forms in all the arts. In literature, our interpretation of the native has been irregular and capricious. We have added little to that strong vision of the conquistadors such as Hernán Cortés, Ercilla, Cieza de León, and of missionaries such as Fray Bartolomé de Las Casas. They succeeded in defining two exemplary types, which Europe took up and incorporated into its repertory of human figures: the “skillful and discreet Indian,” educated in complex and exquisite civilizations of his own, singularly gifted for the arts and crafts, and the “virtuous savage,” who lacks mechanical civilization but lives in order, justice, and goodness—a character that served the European thinkers so well in creating the image of the hypothetical man of the “state of nature” prior to the social contract. In our hundred years of independence, romantic sloth has kept us from devoting much attention to those magnificent empires [scan 15] whose literary interpretation would require prior archaeological studies; the lack of human sympathy has hindered us from approaching the survivor of today, before these last years, except in cases like the memorable one of *Indios ranqueles*, and in the end, apart from Mansilla’s unique and delightful book, the best works on Indigenous subjects have been written in countries like Santo Domingo and Uruguay, where the pure-blooded aborigine barely persists in remote corners and has dissolved into sentimental memory. “The spirit of men floats over the land in which they lived, and one breathes it,” said Martí.

After the Indian, the creole. The *criollista* movement has existed, intermittently, throughout Spanish America, and has aspired to gather the manifestations of popular life, urban and rural, with a natural preference for the countryside. Its limits are vague; on the Argentine pampa the creole was set against the Indian, the traditional enemy, while in Mexico, in Central America, in the whole region of the Andes and its Pacific slope, there is not always a perceptible frontier between customs of creole character and those of Indigenous character. So mingled, they are reflected in Mexican literature by the ballads of Guillermo Prieto and by Lizardi’s *Periquillo*, the awakening of the novel in our America and at the same time the farewell of the Spanish picaresque. There is no country where creole existence does not inspire pictures of peculiar color. Of them all, Argentine literature, in both the cultivated and the rustic idiom, has known how to take possession of the gaucho’s life in a vision as deep as the pampa. Facundo Quiroga, Martín Fierro, Santos Vega are figures definitively planted within the ideal horizon of our peoples. And I do not believe in the reality of Fierro’s quarrel with Quiroga. Sarmiento, as a civilizer, urged to action, gripped by haste, chose for the future of his country the European and North American shortcut instead of the creole path, still shapeless, long, slow, perhaps interminable, or ending in a blind alley; [scan 16] but no one felt better than he the superb impulses, the acrid originality of the barbarism he aspired to destroy. In such oppositions and such decisions lies the aquiline Sarmiento: the inflexible hand chooses; the broad spirit opens to all the winds. Who understood Spain better than he, the Spain whose bad inheritances he wished to throw into the fire, which he visited “with the holy purpose of drawing up the official report against it,” but which at times made him stir in gusts of sympathy? Who noted better than he the limitations of the United States, of those United States whose constructive perseverance he exalted as an exemplary model?

There exists another Americanism, which avoids the Indigenous, and avoids picturesque *criollismo*, and avoids the intermediate bridge of the colonial era, a meeting place for many before and after Ricardo Palma: its single precept is always to keep to the New World in its themes, in poetry as in the novel and the drama, in criticism as in history. And for me, within that simple formula, as within the earlier ones, we have attained, in happy moments, the vivid expression we pursue. In happy moments, let us remember.

#### The Europeanizing Zeal

Let us now turn our gaze toward the Europeanizers, toward those who, discontented with every Americanism with aspirations to an autochthonous flavor, discontented even with our nature, promise us spiritual health if we keep firm and strong the tie that binds us to European culture. They believe that our function will not be to create, beginning from first principles, going to the root of things, but to continue, to pursue, to develop, without breaking traditions or connections.

And we know the examples they would invoke, the very examples that served us to trace the origin of our nationalist rebellion: Rome, the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, the [scan 17] French hegemony of the eighteenth century… Let us pause before them once more. Might not the classical archetypes be right against the romantic freedom that we use and abuse? Might not the sole secret of perfection lie in holding to the ideal line that the culture of the West has followed since its remote origins? To the *criollista* who defends himself—perhaps for the only time in his life—with the example of Greece, it will be easy to show that the Greek miracle, though more solitary, more original than the creations of its successors, gathered up ancient inheritances: not even miracles come from nothing; Greece, mother of so many stupendous inventions, made use of others’ labor, retouching and perfecting, but, in its own view, trying to draw near to the canons, to the paradigms that other peoples, its predecessors or contemporaries, had sought with confused intuition.<sup>1</sup>

All isolation is illusory. The history of the spiritual organization of our America after political emancipation will tell us that our own guides were, at the opportune moment, Europeanizers: Andrés Bello, who from London launched the declaration of our literary independence, was branded a Europeanizer by the Argentine exiles twenty years later, when he was organizing Chilean culture; and Bello’s most violent censors, once back in their own country, were in their turn to undertake tasks of Europeanization, only to have the devotees of pure *criollismo* hold it against them now.

Let us hasten to grant the Europeanizers all that belongs to them, but nothing more, and at the same time let us reassure the *criollista*. Not only would isolation be illusory—the network of

Let us frankly accept, as inevitable, the complex situation: when we express ourselves there will be in us, beside the portion that is solely ours, daughter of our life, at times with Indigenous inheritance, another substantial portion, if only the frame, that we receive from Spain. I go further: not only do we write the language of Castile, but we belong to the Romania, the Romance family that still constitutes a community, a unity of culture, descended from the one that Rome organized under its power; we belong—in Sarmiento’s oft-repeated phrase—to the Roman Empire. Literarily, since the Romance languages attained fullness of life, the Romania has never lacked a center, successor to the Eternal City: from the eleventh to the fourteenth century it was France, with initial oscillations between North and South; with the Renaissance it shifts to Italy; then, for a short time, it tends to settle in Spain; from Louis XIV on it returns to France. Many times the Romania has extended its influence to foreign zones, and we know how Paris governed Europe, and incidentally the two Americas, in the eighteenth century; but from the beginning of the nineteenth century rival zones define themselves, in open and enduring opposition: the Germanic, instigator of rebelliousness; the English, which embraces England with its colonial empire, now dissolving, and the United States; the Slavic… Even politically we were born and grew up in the Romania. Antonio Caso points out with effective precision the three events of Europe whose influence on our peoples is decisive: the Discovery, which is a Spanish event; the Renaissance, an Italian one; the Revolution, a French one. The Renaissance gives form—in Spain only by halves—to the culture that was to be transplanted to our [scan 19] world; the Revolution is the antecedent of our wars of independence. The three events belong to Romance peoples. We have no direct relation with the Reformation, nor with the constitutional evolution of England, and even the independence and the Constitution of the United States gain prestige among us thanks to the propaganda France made of them.

#### Native Energy

Granted all this, which is all that the Europeanizer may rightly claim, let us reassure the faithful creole by reminding him that the existence of the Romania as a unity, as a collective entity of culture, and the existence of the guiding center, are no definitive obstacles to any originality, because that traditional community affects only the forms of culture, whereas the original character of peoples comes from their spiritual depths, from their native energy.

Apart from fleeting moments when a narrow formula has been adopted with excessive rigor, through excessive faith in rhetorical doctrine, or during periods when a national decline of all energies has struck it dumb, each people has expressed itself with fullness of character within the imperial community. And in Spain, within the central language, without recourse to rivals, the regions at times define themselves with unique profiles in literary expression. So, among the poets, the age-old opposition between Castile and Andalusia, the contrast between Fray Luis de León and Fernando de Herrera, between Quevedo and Góngora, between Espronceda and Bécquer.

The shared language does not oblige us to lose ourselves in the mass of a chorus whose direction is not in our hands: it obliges us only to refine our expressive note, to seek the unmistakable accent. From the desire to attain it and sustain it is born the whole puzzle of a hundred years of proclaimed independence; hence [scan 20] the formulas of Americanism, the promises each generation writes, only for the next to forget or reject them, and hence the reaction, daughter of unconfessed discouragement, among the Europeanizers.

#### The Craving for Perfection

We reach the end of our journey through the confusing palace, through the wearisome labyrinth of our literary aspirations, in search of our original and genuine expression. And at the exit I believe I return with the hidden thread that served me as a guide.

My guiding thread has been the thought that there is only one secret of expression: to work it deeply, to strive to make it pure, descending to the root of the things we wish to say; to refine, to define, with a craving for perfection.

The craving for perfection is the only norm. Contenting ourselves with using another’s discovery, a foreigner’s or a compatriot’s, we shall never communicate the intimate revelation; contenting ourselves with the lukewarm and confused enunciation of our intuitions, we shall distort them before the listener and they will seem to him commonplace. But when the firm expression of an artistic intuition has been attained, there goes in it not only the universal meaning but the essence of the spirit that possessed it and the savor of the land that nourished it.

Every formula of Americanism can render service (that is why I gave them all provisional approval); the whole of those we have tried gives us a sum of useful acquisitions, which make flexible and ductile the original material of America. But the formula, on being repeated, degenerates into mechanism and loses its pristine efficacy; it becomes a recipe and engenders a rhetoric.

Every great work of art creates its own peculiar means of expression; it makes use of earlier experiences, but remakes them, because it is not a sum but a synthesis, an invention. Our enemies, in seeking the expression of our world, [scan 21] are the lack of effort and the absence of discipline, children of sloth and lack of culture, or life in perpetual disturbance and change, full of preoccupations foreign to the purity of the work: our poets, our writers were, most often, and in part still are, men bound to action, to political labor and even to war, and there are not lacking among them the leaders and illuminators of peoples.

#### The Future

Now, in the River Plate at least, the literary profession is beginning to be constituted. With it should come discipline, the repose that permits grave undertakings. And the living and clear collaboration of the public is needed: too long has it oscillated between lack of attention and excessive indulgence. The public must be demanding; but it must take an interest in the work of America. To have great poets, said Walt Whitman, there must be great audiences too.

Only one fear holds me back, and I regret to trouble with a pessimistic note the song of hopes. Now that we seem to be sailing toward the safe port, shall we not arrive too late? Will the man of the future go on taking an interest in artistic and literary creation, in the perfect expression of the higher longings of the spirit? The Westerner of today is interested in them less than the Westerner of yesterday, and much less than the men of distant times. A hundred, fifty years ago, when the disappearance of art was being foretold, the omen was dismissed with easy gestures: “there will always be poetry.” But afterward—a new phenomenon in the history of the world, unsuspected and surprising—we have seen arise into prosperous existence active and apparently happy societies, of Western culture, who are not concerned with artistic creation, who are content with industry, or satisfied with art reduced to industrial processes: Australia, New Zealand, even [scan 22] Canada. Might not the United States have been the intermediate experiment? And in Europe, though artistic and literary production abounds, the interest of contemporary man is not what it was. Art had until now obeyed two human ends: one, the expression of the deep longings, of the craving for eternity, of the utopian and ever-renewed dream of the perfect life; the other, play, the imaginative solace in which the spirit rests. The art and literature of our days scarcely recall any longer their ancient transcendental function; only play is left to us… And art reduced to diversion, however intelligent a diversion, a pyrotechnics of wit, ends in weariness.

…I do not wish to end on the pessimistic note. If the arts and letters do not go out, we have the right to regard the future as secure. We shall turn into a chest of treasures the modest box where we now keep our scanty jewels, and we shall have no reason to fear the alien stamp of the language in which we write, because by then the spiritual axis of the Spanish world will have passed to these shores of the Atlantic.

## Paths of Our / Literary History∗

The literature of Spanish America has four centuries of existence, and until now the only two attempts to write its complete history have been carried out in foreign languages: one, nearly ten years ago, in English (Coester); another, very recent, in German (Wagner). The case of Spain is repeating itself for Spanish America: it was outsiders who first ventured to put order into that chaos or—better—that maelstrom of chaotic worlds. Each group of literary works—or, as the rhetoricians said, “each genre”—offered itself as a “sea never before navigated,” with sirens and dragons, shoals and reefs. Good workers are drawing partial charts: we already move with ease among the poets of the Middle Ages; we know how the novels of chivalry, the pastoral novels, and the picaresque novels developed; we know

The boldest explorers confess themselves at the mercy of unknown winds when they enter the theater, and within it, Lope is a chaos all by himself, monster of his labyrinth.

Why did foreigners risk the synthesis before the natives? It has been said too often that they possessed greater aptitude, greater tenacity; and it is not noticed that they felt the difficulties of the case less. With the natives the proverb held true: the trees do not let one see the forest. To this day, to no great Spanish critic or scholar do we owe a complete vision of the landscape. Don Marcelino Menéndez y Pelayo, for example, devoted himself to describing one by one the trees he had before his eyes; about halfway through the task death betrayed him.<sup>2</sup>

In America we are proceeding in the same way. We undertake partial studies: the colonial literature of Chile, poetry in Mexico, history in Peru… We come to embrace whole countries, and Uruguay has seven volumes by Roxlo, Argentina four by Rojas (eight in the new edition!). All of us in America who feel an interest in literary history have thought of writing our own. And it is not sloth that holds us back: it is, in some cases, the lack of leisure, of sufficient time (life demands other labors of us, imperiously!); in other cases, the lack of data and documents: we know the difficulty, little short of insurmountable, of gathering all the materials. #### Tables of Values

A noble desire, but a grave error when one wishes to make history, is the one that aims to remember all the heroes. In literary history the error leads to confusion. In Coester’s manual, respectable for the long effort it represents, no one will be able to discern whether the eminent historian Justo Sierra deserves more attention than the fabulist Rosas Moreno, or whether Rodó’s significance is much greater than that of his friend Samuel Blixen. What is needed is to put into circulation tables of values: central names and books of indispensable reading.<sup>3</sup>

To leave the mediocre in the populous shadow; to leave in the penumbra those whose work might have been great but remained half done: a common tragedy in our America. It is with supreme sacrifices and even injustices that the constellations of classics are formed in all literatures. Epicharmus was sacrificed to the glory of Aristophanes; Gorgias and Protagoras to the wrath of Plato.

The literary history of Spanish America should be written around a few central names: Bello, Sarmiento, Montalvo, Martí, Darío, Rodó.

#### Nationalisms

There are two nationalisms in literature: the spontaneous, the natural accent and elemental savor of the native land, which no one escapes, not even the apparent exceptions; and the perfect, the highest expression of each people’s spirit, with power of empire, of endurance and expansion. To the perfect nationalism, creator of great literatures, we have aspired since independence: our literary history of the last hundred years could be written as the history of the ebb and flow of aspirations and theories in search of our perfect expression; it ought to be written as the history of the renewed attempts at expression and, above all, of the expressions achieved.

Of the other nationalism, the spontaneous and natural, little would need saying if it had not been turned, needlessly, into a problem of complications and tangles. The confusions begin with language. Each language has its color, summary of a long historical life. But each language varies from city to city, from region to region, and the dialectal variations, however slight, are accompanied by a multitude of diverse spiritual shades. Would it be credible that while every region of Spain defines itself by features of its own, Spanish America should remain a shapeless [scan 27] nebula, and no means be found to distinguish it from Spain? And which Spain would it resemble? The Andalusian? The Andalusianism of America is a structure of slight foundation, long since demolished by Cuervo.<sup>4</sup>

In practice, everyone distinguishes the Spaniard from the Spanish American: even foreigners who do not know the language. Scarcely had an organized population of European origin existed in the New World, scarcely were the first creoles born, when it was declared that they differed from the Spaniards; since the sixteenth century the diversity has been noted, insistently. In literature everyone feels it. Even in Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón: the first impression every reader of his gathers is that he *does not resemble* the other dramatists of his time, although from them he received—already rigid—the mold of his plays: themes, construction, language, meter.

We Spanish Americans constitute diverse regional groups: linguistically, for example, there are five groups, zones. Is it credible that such nuances do not carry over into literature? No; whoever pays attention will soon discover them, and it will be easy for him to tell when the writer is from the River Plate, or is Chilean, or Mexican.

If these plain realities are obscured it is because they are tinged with passion and prejudice, and so we oscillate between two murky tendencies: one that tends to declare us “full of character,” for good or ill, and another that tends to declare us “birds without color, fish without scales,” mere Spaniards who alter the language in its sounds and its vocabulary and its syntax, but who keep unaltered, without additions, the *Weltanschauung* of the Castilians or the Andalusians. #### America and Exuberance

Outside the two muddied currents are many who have taken no side; in general, with a kind of naïve realism, they accept the natural and inoffensive supposition that we have a physiognomy of our own, even if it is not very expressive. But how do they judge? By chance reading: *Amalia* or *María*, *Facundo* or *Martín Fierro*, Nervo or Rubén. On such random reading rest many strange ideas; for example, that of our exuberance.

Let us see. José Ortega y Gasset, in a recent article, recommends to young Argentines that they “strangle emphasis,” which he sees as a national fault. Some months ago, Eugenio d’Ors, on the departure from Madrid of the agile writer and consummate Mexican poet Alfonso Reyes, called him “the one who wrings the neck of exuberance.” He has since returned to the theme, apropos of writers of Chile. America is, in the eyes of Europe—as [scan 29] d’Ors reminds us—the exuberant land, and, reasoning in accord with the usual theory that each climate gives its natives characteristic spiritual traits (“climate influences wits,” said Tirso), we are credited with traits of exuberance in literature. Such opinions (I choose them only because they are very recent) are nothing unusual; they are heard from the lips of Americans too.

And yet I do not believe in the theory of our exuberance. Pushing matters to an extreme, the ingenious might even venture the opposite thesis; there would be no lack of writers, from the sixteenth century to the twentieth, to prove it. My denial hides no defensive purpose. On the contrary, I dare to ask: is it exuberance and emphasis that are attributed to us, and that we attribute to ourselves, or ignorance and clumsiness? Ignorance, and all the ills that derive from it, are not characters: they are situations. To judge our spiritual physiognomy it is well to set aside the writers who cannot reveal it in its essence because their imperfections in culture and in command of expressive forms prevent them. What if they are many? No matter; we shall never succeed in drawing the map of our letters unless we first clear the ground.

If exuberance is fecundity, we are not exuberant; we of Spanish America are not prolific writers. We lack “the vein,” probably; and we lack professional urgency: literature among us is not a profession but a pastime; only in Argentina is the literary profession now being born. Our prolific writers are exceptions; and even they manage to produce only as much as those who in Spain represent the average level of activity; never as much as Pérez Galdós or Emilia Pardo Bazán. And let no one speak of the seventeenth century: Tirso and Calderón are enough to disconcert us; Lope alone produced as many plays as all the English dramatic poets of the Elizabethan age together. If Alarcón wrote little, it was no mere chance.

Is exuberance verbosity? Excess of words does not spring everywhere from the same sources; the Englishman will find it in Ruskin, [scan 30] or in Landor, or in Thomas De Quincey, or in any other of his ornamental stylists of the nineteenth century; the Russian, in Andreyev: excesses differing among themselves, and differing from that which Castelar or Zorrilla represents for us. Besides, in any literature the mediocre author, of poor ideas and scant culture, tends to be wordy; in the Spanish, perhaps more than in any. In America we again stumble upon ignorance; if wordiness abounds it is because culture and discipline are scarce, and not because of any exuberance of ours. *Le climat*—parodying Alceste—*ne fait rien à l’affaire* [climate has nothing to do with the matter]. And on occasion our verbosity draws attention because it is accompanied by a stylistic concern, good in itself, which strives to exalt the power of words, though it lacks the density of thought or the spark of imagination capable of turning tinsel into gold.

Finally, exuberance is emphasis. In the Western literatures, as Romanticism declined, *inspiration*, eloquence, and emphasis—“the finest craft of writing,” as our first poetess nun, Doña Leonor de Ovando, called it—lost prestige. Muted tones came into fashion, and even silence. *Seul le silence est grand* [Only silence is great], it was proclaimed—emphatically still! In America we kept our respect for emphasis as long as Europe prescribed it to us; even today we have three or four *vibrant* poets, as the Romantics used to say. Might they not represent a simple lag in literary fashion? Is not the influence of Victor Hugo being ascribed to the influence of the tropics? Or that of Byron, or Espronceda, or Quintana? True; the choice of masters is already a sign of native inclination. But—leaving aside whatever revealed original character—the emphatic models were not the only ones; beside Hugo there was Lamartine; beside Quintana there was Meléndez Valdés. Neither have we all been emphatic, nor is this our greatest present sin. There are countries of America, such as Mexico and Peru, where exaltation is exceptional. We even have currents and schools of serenity, of refinement, [scan 31] of sobriety; from *modernismo* to our own day, these orientations tend to predominate over the opposite ones.

#### Good America and Bad America

Each country or group of countries—as has been said—gives a special nuance to its literary production in America: the assiduous reader recognizes it. But there exists a tendency, particularly in Argentina, to divide them into only two groups: bad America and good, the tropical and the *other*, the *petits pays chauds* [little hot countries] and the “well-organized” nations. The distinction, real in the political and economic order—save for one or two crucial and extremely difficult points—is neither clear nor plausible in the artistic order. There is, for the observer, a literature of Mexico, of Central America, of the Antilles, of Venezuela, of Colombia, of the Peruvian region, of Chile, of the River Plate; but there is not a literature of tropical America, leafy and emphatic, and another literature of temperate America, all serenity and discretion. And this would be explained—according to the climatological theory on which the attempted split partly rests—by the fact that, contrary to the vulgar belief, most of Spanish America lying between the tropics does not fit within the usual description of the torrid zone. Any geography manual will remind us: intertropical America is divided into highlands and lowlands; only the lowlands are legitimately torrid, while the highlands are cool in temperature, often cold. And Brazil occupies most of the lowlands between the tropics! There is opulence in the spontaneous and delightful baroque quality of Brazilian architecture and letters. But Brazil is not Spanish America… In that which is, in Mexico and along the Andes, the traveler will find vast plateaus that will not give him an impression of exuberance, because those heights are little favorable to the fertility of the soil and abound in arid regions. “The [scan 32] heat of the tropics” is unknown there. Far from being cities of perpetual summer, Bogotá and Mexico City, Quito and Puebla, La Paz and Guatemala would deserve to be called cities of perpetual autumn. Not even Lima or Caracas are types of the tropical city: to find them one must go as far as Havana (an admirable specimen!), Santo Domingo, San Salvador. One cannot expect that the serenity and mild temperatures of the plateaus and slopes should favor “ardent temperaments” or “volcanic imaginations.” Thus it is seen that the dominant character in Mexican literature is one of discretion, of melancholy, of gray tonality (run through the series of poets from the friar Navarrete to González Martínez), and in it the tendency to exaltation never prospered, not even in the periods of Hugo’s influence, except in isolated figures, such as Díaz Mirón, a son of the warm coast, of the lowland. Thus it is seen that the character of Peruvian letters is likewise one of discretion and measure; but in place of melancholy the humorous note sets its particular seal there, an inheritance of viceregal Lima, from the comedies of Pardo y Segura to the present descendants of Ricardo Palma. Chocano turns out to be the exception.

The divergence of the two Americas, the *good* and the *bad*, in literary life does begin to show, and every attentive observer will have noticed it in recent years; but it depends in no way on the division into temperate zone and torrid zone. Its source is in the diversity of culture. During the nineteenth century, the rapid leveling, the similarity of situations that independence brought to our America, permitted the appearance of strong personalities in any country: if Argentina produced Sarmiento, Ecuador produced Montalvo; if Mexico gave Gutiérrez Nájera, Nicaragua gave Rubén Darío. But situations change: the *serious nations* are giving form and stability to their culture, and in them letters become a normal activity; meanwhile, in “the other nations,” where the institutions of culture, both elementary [scan 33] and higher, are victims of political upheavals and economic disorder, literature has begun to falter. Examples: Chile, in the nineteenth century, was not one of the countries toward which the eyes of the lovers of letters turned with greatest pleasure; today it is. Venezuela had for a hundred years, starting from none other than Bello, valuable literature, especially in form: the type of the poet and writer who was master of the language, endowed with *facundia*, abounded. The series of ignorant tyrannies that has been afflicting Venezuela since the end of the nineteenth century—unlike those curious “enlightened despotisms” of before, such as Guzmán Blanco’s—has undone the intellectual tradition: no writer of Venezuela under fifty years of age enjoys a reputation in America.

Everything leads one to foresee that, throughout the twentieth century, literary activity will concentrate, grow, and bear fruit in “good America”; in the other—whatever countries may in the end constitute it—letters will gradually grow drowsy until they fall into lethargy.

II

If literary history calls for selection, it calls also for a sense of *character*, of originality: it must be the history of the new notes—personal accent or flavor of the country, of the native soil—in the living and complete work of the best. In Spanish America, the criterion wavers. Do we have originality? Or are we simple, perpetual imitators? Do we live in everything on Europe? Or shall we put faith in the “new generations” when they proclaim—every fifteen or twenty years, since independence—that *this time* the genuine expression of our America is going to be born?

#### The Eclipse of Europe

I do not know whether we shall begin to “be ourselves” tomorrow at dawn or at noon; I do not believe that Europe’s historic task has ended; but I do know that for us Europe is in eclipse, losing the dogmatic role it played for a hundred years. It is not that we have a compass of our own; it is that we have lost the foreign one.

Throughout the nineteenth century, Europe gave us definite lessons. Thus in politics and economics, the liberal doctrine. There were archaic governments, recalcitrant monarchies; but they yielded little by little to the coercion of example: we noted the slow advances of the constitutional regime and awaited, armed with hope, the hour when it would crystallize definitively among us. Socialism spread; but moderate spirits trusted to dispel it by incorporating its “claims” into the laws: in reality, that is what happened. Now? Every corner, every nook, is a chair of heterodoxy. Peoples distrust their authorities. Governments of force or of compromise prevail; and governments of compromise lack doctrine by their very essence; and governments of force, whatever doctrine they originally aspired to defend, yield as their natural fruit absurd theories. Since light does not come to us from Europe, we remain in the dark and doze lazily; in moments of urgency, obliged to wake, we venture to clarify our problems by our own scanty lights.<sup>5</sup>

#### The Treasures of the Indian

I have deliberately avoided alluding to our Indigenous past before the conquest. That past, long *submerged*, its higher culture undone with the death of its owners and guardians, we could not use consciously: its influence was *subterranean*, but, in the countries where the Indian prevails in numbers (and they are the majority), it was enormous, lasting, powerful in modifying the character of the transplanted culture. The Indian of Catamarca or Ecuador or Guatemala who with his native technique interprets European motifs, or the reverse, knows nothing of their whys. We, most of us, do not know how much of the Indian we have in us: we do not yet know how to think except in terms of European civilization.

After our political emancipation, we have attempted a conscious return to the Indigenous tradition. We have often erred, so often that we have ended by distrusting our treasures: the road of *indigenismo* is full of derailments. Now the musical motif was strung into rhapsodies on the fatal model of Liszt, or at most into transcriptions in the style of Mussorgsky or Debussy; now the plastic motif dissolved into “decorative art”; now the literary motif bore fruit in poems or novels of Romantic cut, strewn with Indian words that required glossary and notes. If the monument to Cuauhtémoc by Noriega and Guerra is beautiful, and so are Zorrilla de San Martín’s *Tabaré*, and José Joaquín Pérez’s *Fantasías indígenas*, and Galván’s *Enriquillo*, the native material influences them only in an external or incidental way.

We could not persist in error indefinitely. In recent days we have begun to penetrate the essence of native [scan 39] art: two cases of success reveal it, the studies on the music of Peru and Bolivia, based on the definition of the pentatonic scale, and on Mexican drawing, with the definition of its seven linear elements. That is the way.

#### History and Future

Our spiritual life has a right to its two sources, the Spanish and the Indigenous: we need only know the secrets, the keys, of Indian things; otherwise, in trying to incorporate them we shall perform mechanical work, without warmth or color.

But the sources are not the river. The river is our life: let us learn to contemplate its current, withdrawing at the opportune hour—without renouncing them!—from the Ilissus and the Tiber, the Arno and the Seine. There is no reason to hasten to define our spirit by enclosing it within narrow formulas and nationalist recipes;<sup>8</sup> let it suffice us to trust that we *exist*, in spite of the slanderers, and to have faith that we shall come to found and to represent the freedom of the spirit.

And in literary history, let us have eyes—I insist—for the images that arose, new to every human gaze, from our wild fields and our anarchic cities: from the “terrible shadow of Facundo” to *Ismaelillo*; even the vision of peace and splendor that we placed at Versailles or in Venice was the intimate dream with which we silenced our disgust at the disorder around us. ## Toward the New Theater

I am an attentive spectator, whom the things of the theater have deeply interested since adolescence, and it has been my lot to know from its origins the complex evolution in which we are still living. When I began to attend performances, realism was law: realistic the drama, realistic the art of the actor, realistic the stage. Ibsen was alive: he reigned. Very few ascended the mystic spiral of his dramas of twilight (Maeterlinck was one of them); the norm of the Western world was set by *A Doll’s House*, *Ghosts*, *The Wild Duck*, *Hedda Gabler*. Even France, apparently aloof, learned from him the lesson of a tight psychology, in which the phrase went step by step penetrating and tightening like a precision screw. The actor took pride in speaking “as in life”; he lost the habit, and unfortunately even the aptitude, of speaking verse. On the stage the aim was the “exact copy of reality.”

Suddenly the signs change. In the year 1903, in New York, it was my lot to attend—and I choose this as a point of departure as I would start from any other—the first [scan 42] performance of *Candida*, which showed that Bernard Shaw would reach the multitudes; his dialogue of ideas was destined for them, because heated discussion is a spectacle that arouses passion. The apostle of the “quintessence of Ibsenism” was working, unwary, against his master. Following his example, men of letters in England lost their traditional dread of the theater: Barrie, the first, gave himself freely to the delights of extravagance. In Ireland, as the native soil was dug into, heroes and fairies sprang from it. In Russia, taking up the thread of Ostrovsky, his drama of scattered action, Chekhov and Gorky reinvented—after Euripides—the motionless tragedy. In Germany, realism was drowning in its own excess in brutal naturalism, or dissolving into poetic deliriums. France, late, and after her Italy, at last joined the tumultuous current on which we sail, at the mercy of its impetus, not knowing where we shall make port.

And we see the material conditions of the spectacle change: stage, scenery, lighting, costumes. Open-air theaters were being born—and reborn. Greek tragedy, the religious drama of the Middle Ages, Shakespeare, reappeared on their original stages. Small platforms arose, with reduced auditoriums, the *chamber theaters*. In Germany, in Russia, in France, in England, there were attempts at reform of the scenery; year after year new experiments were talked of. The theorists—especially Adolphe Appia and Gordon Craig—kept the problem alive. At last the Russian *ballet* burst upon Paris, and, as in the Apocalypse, behold, all things are made new.

Not that realism has died, much less routine; we all know it well. The stages of renewal constitute distinguished minorities. But they suffice for the good spectator, the one who does not want to go to the theater night after night, but with time to savor each thing.

When, after visiting countries of a strange language, or living in them, I return to my own lands, those of the Spanish tongue, [scan 43] I always look for the novelties of the theater, and I find that our novelties are antiquities. I am no more than a spectator (a critic seldom, an author less); but as a spectator I do my duty: in 1920, in Madrid, I called at length for the *renewal of the theater* from the columns of the review *España*; in Mexico, two years ago, and here now, I reiterate my petitions. I shall not ask too much: I shall confine myself to the problem of the stage and the scenery.

#### The History of the Stage

Let us run through the history of the stage as if by airplane. In the Middle Ages, the theater of antiquity being dead (of the classical stock the only survivors were the puppets and the mimes), the drama is born again from ritual, as among the Greeks: sacred performances are born in the church. But if ancient tragedy found easy development in the temple of Dionysus, in the open air, the *mystery play* was hampered within the architecture of the Christian temple, a stage suited only to the schematic ritual drama of the Eucharistic sacrifice. Then the *mystery*, the *miracle*, the *morality* left the church for where all the faithful could behold them: the atrium; thence, to the square, to the street.

In the street the comic farce, usual at popular fairs, joins them; and tragedy and comedy develop slowly, starting from the rudimentary, very brief forms in which they are reborn, at the same time as the stage develops. From the ground, on the level of the spectators, the drama tends to rise, seeks the height of the platform so that all may see better: thus *the scaffold stage* is created. In a circle around it the crowd gathers (the primitive arrangement is perpetuated, in some cases, with the *wagon stage* and its circular scenery); but the actors, to go up or down, need to open a path; very soon it becomes necessary to close off to [scan 44] the spectators one (exceptionally two) of the three sides of the platform: thus the *back of the stage* is born.

But the stage, the *platform stage*, though it already has a back, will take a long time (here more, there less, according to each country) to have free sides, right and left. When the drama, during the Renaissance, enriching itself with the study of ancient literature and renewing its forms, enters the palaces—or at least the courtyard, the *corral*—the spectators are still too close to the stage, or even have seats on it, and leave only the back free. The public theaters, created in the sixteenth century, indoors, or in the courtyards of buildings, or between buildings, put a roof over the stage and little by little move the public away from it. The final stroke is dealt in Italy: the audience, or the greater part of it, is obliged to watch the performance from only one of the three sides from which it could formerly see it; and to make the separation between public and actors definitive, and to increase the freedom of the stage, the *curtain* is created. The stage began to be conceived *as a kind of picture*…

The material elements at the disposal of the modern theater to frame the drama and the actor—costumes, furniture, scenery, light—did not develop in parallel: each has its own development. Costumes and furniture were rich from the time of the liturgical drama, when the resources of the actor or his company permitted; the nineteenth century brought the good desire for historical exactness, but also needless overloading, the excess born of a mercantile eagerness to simulate luxury: the costly has been confused with the beautiful.

The evolution of scenery is long and complex: until the seventeenth century there were theaters that dispensed with it or reduced it to elementary indications; but, at the same time, from the Middle Ages there existed *simultaneous settings*, whose synthetic expression is the triad of the religious dramas: Heaven, Earth, Hell. Shakespeare and Lope de Vega still caught the [scan 45] age of settings at once simultaneous and synthetic; within them they conceived their works, with their frequent changes of place, which give them the variety of the novel. And was not *La Celestina* conceived thus? Afterward, when the front curtain gradually creates the image of the stage as a picture, scenery, with the adoption of pictorial perspective, passes to a new stage of its development, and its future seems incalculable… And lighting came to acquire its full value with the invention of electric light: it represents the appearance of nuance, it permits the suppression of the footlights of the proscenium, with their deplorable effects on the human figure.

With the conquest of light, the *picture-stage* reached its apogee; marvels were expected… The collaboration of painting with the drama would be ever more effective… Why, when its empire seemed most secure, do innumerable protests arise against the modern stage?

#### The Hated Nineteenth Century

Probably the primary cause of such protests is the use that the hated nineteenth century made of the picture-stage. The century of Napoleon III, of Victoria and of William II began by accepting the inheritance of scenery of the academic, *pompier* type, and then combined with it the tyrannical realism of details, the prolix multiplicity of ornaments and furniture on the stage. Academicism and realism joined hands without effort; as well they might, since they represent two phases of one and the same limited aesthetic, the aesthetic of the *imitation of nature*!

More than as a picture, the stage came to be conceived *as a room from which one of the four walls has been removed*. There remained for the stage designer with imagination the scenes of forest, of garden, and even those of streets and historic halls… But routine seized upon these too.

[scan 46]

To appreciate how short a flight the nineteenth century took in its scenic conceptions, recall Wagner’s clumsiness, his mania for reducing to puerile realism, in plastic representation, the marvels of Teutonic myth and Christian legend: the more complicated the contrivances employed to produce the illusion, the poorer the effect obtained. Is there a greater absurdity than to present to us as real the ride of the Valkyries, the dragon of *Siegfried*, the swan of *Lohengrin*, the moving earth of *Parsifal*?

Or recall Sir Henry Irving in his interpretations of Shakespeare: profusion of costumes, of furniture, of drops, in which the brown note abounded, very serious, very *Victorian*. Imagine the Venice of the *Merchant*, the Venice of the Bellinis and of Crivelli, filled with brown blotches! “I made a fortune and spent it on the propaganda of Shakespeare,” said Irving in his old age. “Not so,” affirms Bernard Shaw; “Irving made a fortune with Shakespeare’s plays, and spent it on scenery.”

The realist delirium ended by abandoning at times the drops and the painting, carrying them over to interior settings, which by dint of exactness turn into furniture: solid, massive, of wood and metal. They are exact, yes, but inexpressive, cumbersome, and extremely costly.

#### What Is Realism For?

What is realism for? The realism of the picture-stage serves for *A Doll’s House*, for *The Weavers*, for *Lady Windermere’s Fan*, for *La Parisienne*, for *El gran Galeoto*. For dramas of modern interiors, realism is a conquest that should be made use of: prudently, to be sure, with simplicity.

But is it enough, or does it even fit, in *When We Dead Awaken*? In *La nave*? In Claudel? In Dunsany? In Tagore? In Maeterlinck, who began by writing for marionettes? Is it enough, strictly speaking, [scan 47] for *Los intereses creados*, for *Las hijas del Cid*? And what is to be done with the Greek and Latin, English and Spanish classics, who *did not write for stages like the present ones*? What to do with Racine and Corneille, with Molière’s best comedies, which hardly require a stage? And what to do with so many works that are not performed but are performable, contrary to the vulgar opinion, from *La Celestina* to the *Comedias bárbaras* of Don Ramón del Valle-Inclán?

It is plain to see: the modern stage forces us to reserve for the library the greater part of the great dramatic works of humanity, and in exchange condemns the assiduous theatergoer to contemplate interminable exhibitions of mediocrity, which do not even offer any novelty. Thus in Spain, for lack of renewal, the theater has been reduced to a few types of dramatic work: the drama and the sentimental comedy of the people of Madrid; the comedy of the country or the village, preferably with an Andalusian setting; the tragedy of workers and peasants; farces and *sainetes*, generally grotesque; the detective drama, and, as an exception, the poetic drama, revived by Marquina. The Argentine theater is even narrower: dramas and comedies, of uniform cut, about the *elegant world* of Buenos Aires; comedies about the families of the poor bourgeoisie; dramas of the outskirts, with the typical *conventillo* or tenement house; rural tragedies—all subject to realist technique. And the Argentine is the only national theater of full development in our America.<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Since 1925, the attempt to break with routine has taken on a copious impetus in Spain. The drama, which was becoming the monopoly of men of little letters, is again the pastime and concern of genuine writers: Azorín, Baroja, d’Ors, the Machados, Araquistáin; they reappear in Unamuno and Valle-Inclán. In Argentina, with less force, similar signs are observed.

#### The Artistic Solution

Various solutions present themselves. Most of them, and the best, are simplifications: there is agreement in affirming that the modern stage is overloaded with useless things.

There are those who replace realism with fantasy: the *artistic* solution. Their arguments are interesting. They not only protest against the pretensions of photographic exactness, against minuteness of detail, but attack the essential structure of the modern stage. The realist stage may pass when it reproduces small interiors, like a Dutch painting; but for reproducing large halls—save in exceptionally vast theaters—and above all for the open air, modern methods are more mistaken than those of the Middle Ages. When a forest is to be simulated, a few trees are distributed on the stage and a landscape painting is placed at the back; the eyes pass abruptly from the real perspective of the isolated trees to the fictitious perspective of the landscape. And the illusion is claimed to be complete! There is no illusion: there is only the lazy habit of accepting that as scenic realism. Why, even when the solid trees are missing, the disproportion alone between the real human figure and the fictitious perspective of the background destroys all illusion of truth!

But it is not enough to suppress the absurd mixture of two perspectives that do not fuse. One goes further. Is deception a purpose of art? The concept would be paltry… Why pretend that the landscape simulates an enormous colored photograph? Whom is the painted landscape to deceive? Whom does the photograph deceive? Away with pretensions of realism! Since the object of the scenery is not to deceive but to suggest, to indicate the place, let us make the indication not photographic but *artistic*, the child of the painter’s imagination, which will know how to vary, according to the works, the style of the scenery, from the opulence of color that [scan 49] befits *The Thousand and One Nights* to the somber tones that harmonize with the atmosphere of *Macbeth* or *Hamlet*.

Thus the *artistic* stage is born. Of it there are two principal types: one, which serves as an architectural or pictorial background for the actor, and is even reduced to the foreground, with synthetic scenery, as Fuchs and Erler do; the other, in which the actor is conceived as a mere element of a vast plastic and dynamic ensemble, according to the practice of Max Reinhardt in a good part of his scenic inventions. The *artistic* stage chooses as points of support now the drawing and color of the scenery, now the resources of light. The Russian *ballet*, under the inspiration of Léon Bakst, is the best-known example of the new riches of form and color. Appia and Craig turn to architectural suggestions and delight in making us conceive inaccessible heights, deep spaces. Appia has been, besides, the evangelist of light.

#### The Trojan Women

I remember *The Trojan Women* of Euripides, under the direction of Maurice Browne, an English devotee of the gospel of light. It was in Washington, during the Great War; Browne’s chamber-theater company was then traveling in the cause of peace, presenting the tragedy that Euripides wrote, according to the historians, against the injustice of war. That afternoon—a strange coincidence—the *Lusitania* had just gone down. Before the curtain rose, a young man appeared before the public, pale, trembling, to say a few words to us about the war; his first gesture was to unfold before the audience the newspaper extra announcing the sinking of the monstrous ship: LUSITANIA SUNK…

In that lugubrious atmosphere the performance began of the most lugubrious of the great tragedies. The stage is plunged [scan 50] in darkness: deep night… Presently a wall is dimly outlined, broken in the middle. Out of the night come the Trojan women, the chorus gathering around Hecuba the queen. The endless lament begins… Day rises slowly upon their tremendous desolation… Cassandra, the prophetess, passes by in delirium; she knows she is to die in the catastrophe of the house of Agamemnon. Andromache arrives, the young and strong mother, leading by the hand Hector’s only son, in whom faint rays of hope take refuge. But war is implacable: Talthybius comes to tear the child from his mother’s hands; the Argives have decreed that he be put to death by being hurled from the heights. The despair of the Trojan women spreads in pathetic waves from the dark stage to the dark house of the audience. The women weep… For a few brief moments, in full day now, the radiant figure of Helen, adorned in gold and crimson, passes before the procession of the conquered women wrapped in mourning cloaks. Behind her, the angry Menelaus. “Sacrifice her!” is Hecuba’s cry. Helen goes off toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans. Will she die? Her powers are mysterious… Talthybius returns to deliver the shattered body of Astyanax. While womanly pity shrouds the corpse and anoints it with loving tears—how Euripides felt the pathetic poetry of children!—red glares of fire rise behind the broken wall. Troy is burning, its proud towers fall, and the *commos*, the clamor of Hecuba and the Trojan women, who sing their farewell to the heroic city, rises and rises together with the flames… It dies away in a long moan as night falls: toward the night the captive Trojan women file and vanish.

#### The Historical Solution

Others say: let us give each work a stage equal or similar to the one it had at its origin; thus we shall understand it better. The *historical* solution. Hence the resurrection of the Greek theaters in the open air, [scan 51] with growing success, which even tempts people to stage modern creations in them, for which the ancient setting proves propitious. Shakespeare and his contemporaries are restored to their *Elizabethan* stage; thus the plays are reborn whole, uncut, alive and swift in their original *tempo*, free of the odious intervals “to change the scenery.” When shall we see Lope and Tirso, Alarcón and Calderón restored to their own stage?<sup>2</sup>

#### The Radical Solution

The radicals say: let us set aside the problems of painting; let us have nothing to do with architecture; there is no need to dream of the fusion of the arts when what one wants is, strictly, to perform dramatic works. The simplification must be complete: everything accessory gets in the way, distracts from what is essential,

And the English-speaking peoples likewise did not hear Shakespeare’s real text during the nineteenth century: the state of affairs toward the end may be seen in the two succulent volumes of Bernard Shaw’s *Dramatic Opinions and Essays*. Even in 1914, seeing Mantell’s *King John*—Mantell an actor of the old school, a “Shakespearean specialist”—I could observe that the *version* performed reduced the tragedy to less than a third of its length. Thus the second act, an ample epic rhapsody, all noise, color and movement, with its “alarums and excursions,” with its verses resounding with the voices of clarions and the notes of bells, was cut down to fifteen minutes. And the *version* was the work of William Winter, spokesman of academic criticism in the New York press, an enemy of modern literature, but incapable of respecting the old. Adaptations, abridgments, or versions extraordinarily disrespectful to Shakespeare were still abundant. But they seemed acceptable beside the frightful versions of the Italian actors; of Shakespeare’s words, of that marvelous purple mantle of hendecasyllables spangled with resplendent metaphors, not even rags remained; the drama was reduced to elementary phrases and to the crude matter of the primitive tale, and we know well that the plot was not Shakespeare’s invention: so that we came to see on the stage the *Romeo and Juliet* of Bandello or the *Hamlet* of Saxo Grammaticus, with the sole addition of the well-thumbed soliloquy. Let anyone unfortunate enough to have seen it recall the monstrous arrangement of *The Merchant of Venice* that Novelli offered. [scan 52] which is the drama. And the first obstacle that must disappear is the scenery. Away with scenery!

The greatest apostle of the radical solution, of absolute simplification, is Jacques Copeau. And his successes at the Vieux Colombier bear witness to the validity of his theories.

#### The Best Solution

There are those who do not dare so much, and adopt mixed solutions. There are those who speak of *synthesis*, and of *rhythm*, and of other notions that they employ with desperate vagueness: not all the reformers have in their ideas, or at least in their expression, the French clarity of Copeau. Strictly speaking, the mixed solutions incline for the most part to the artistic type, and at times sin through profusion and overloading like the scenic realism they aspire to banish. Among these solutions there are all kinds, even some that reach extreme complications, like Gémier’s “grand spectacle,” with interludes of athletic exercises, a reminiscence of the Italian Renaissance.

The best solution is to take advantage of all the solutions. The *artistic* is one of those that impose themselves and can give us incomparable delights. The *historical*, on the contrary, succeeds with difficulty: it requires the utmost tact in stage direction, so that history may not smother the life of the drama.

I confess my immoderate love for the *radical* solution, for simplification, relative or absolute. I know nothing better than Sophocles, Euripides, Shakespeare, Racine, without scenery or with mere schematic indications of place. And nothing confirms me in my fondness like the *Hamlet* of Forbes Robertson. I saw it first with scenery, and it seemed to me what everyone conceded: the best *Hamlet* of its time. Years later I saw it again without scenery. Forbes Robertson did not belong to any reforming groups. He was retiring from the stage by traveling through all [scan 53] the English-speaking countries, on a tour that lasted three years, devoted to *Hamlet*; the last performance took place on the day that commemorated the third centenary of Shakespeare’s death. On this tour, in which the city changed very frequently, if not daily, the scenery seemed a nuisance, and it was suppressed, replaced with dark green hangings, according to the plan advocated in England by William Poel. The effect of this *Hamlet* was something unique in contemporary art. The lack of cumbersome accessories left the tragedy bare, giving it a stupendous severity, and the method Forbes Robertson employed of identifying the spiritual conflict, keeping the actors grouped at a short distance from the protagonist, produced the impression that the drama took place entirely “inside Hamlet,” in Hamlet’s head. Never did I better understand Mallarmé’s idea: the characters of *Hamlet* are like projections of the protagonist’s spirit. This *Hamlet* was no longer merely the best of our day: it is the most extraordinary production I have seen upon the stage.

With the renewal of the stage and of the forms of performance, all the great works return to life; the drama ceases to be mere diversion of the moment. The assiduous theatergoer in France, Germany, Russia, England, the United States, for the past four lustrums has enjoyed extraordinary privileges; he sees reappear, beside the tragedy of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, the *new comedy* of Athens and Rome and even the pantomime of Sicily; with *Everyman*, the allegorical *morality* of the Middle Ages, and with *Maître Pathelin* the comic farce; the distant Orient sends him its treasures: India, the ancient poems of Kalidasa and the modern ones of Tagore; Japan its *Noh*, its synthetic drama; China, for now, only its stylized methods of performance. Even the *Book of Job* and the dialogues of Plato take on stage life!

[scan 54]

#### In Spain

Might the times not be ripe, in the Spanish-speaking countries, for the renewal of the theater? I believe they are. More than thirty years ago Don Marcelino Menéndez y Pelayo said that *La Celestina* perhaps could not be performed “within the conditions of the present theater, much narrower and more paltry than it seems.” But he added: “Who assures us that this work of genius, whose author… glimpsed an almost perfect dramatic formula, will not become, in the course of time, capable of being performed in a theater that tolerates an amplitude and a development not known until today?”

So far, few attempts at renewal are made in Spain. One or another, timid, trying to reconcile the gods of Olympus and those of Avernus, taking care not to frighten the routine-bound mass of the Madrid public, is due to the companies of María Guerrero (with Tagore’s *The Post Office*) and of Catalina Bárcena. Benavente, whose occasional good intentions make one forgive his many sins, deserves mention for his experiments in children’s theater: to one of them *La cabeza del dragón*, the delightful comedy by Valle-Inclán, owes its birth. Unamuno’s *Fedra* at the Ateneo of Madrid, with simplified staging, must be recalled. And the frame of the stage was skillfully broken, though without reform of the scenery, by Cipriano Rivas Cherif, distributing between the stage boards and the auditorium the characters of the assembly act in *An Enemy of the People*, when the Madrid socialists organized a performance of that tragicomedy of individualism (1920).

A great devotee of utopia—of utopia, which is one of the grand spiritual creations of the Mediterranean—Azorín has created (on paper!) the theater to which modern Spain aspires. And if it were not already perfect, like all utopias, [scan 55] his project might even deserve to be called useful as well as delightful, because it contains a precious anthology of dramas.

The enchanting language of the emotions in the prose of the tragedies imitated from the Greeks by the master Pérez de Oliva!

#### In Our America

In Mexico we discover one or another attempt worthy of attention. Greek theater has been tried, in the open air, in Chapultepec Park, with Margarita Xirgu and her Spanish company performing *Electra* (1922); unfortunately, the platform that was erected was of the modern type, and the work chosen was none of the classical tragedies, but the frenzied melodrama of Hugo von Hofmannsthal. Better still, an effort has been made to draw upon the popular art of the country: sometimes outside the drama, in the short pieces of the Teatro Lírico, since 1921, or in the ephemeral and ingenious Teatro Mexicano del Murciélago (1924), of the poet Quintanilla, the painter González and the musician Domínguez, spurred on by the Russian example; at other times in the drama, in the theater of the Indians, begun by the playwright Saavedra at Teotihuacán, beside the Pyramids, and afterward transported to other sites (1922): the stage was of the *artistic* type; the natives were the actors, at times with the utmost delicacy. And Mexico has given the international movement the contribution of Miguel Covarrubias, author of the sets for the uproarious *Revue Nègre* of Paris, and for Bernard Shaw’s *Androcles and the Lion* and Aeschylus’s *Seven Against Thebes* in New York.

In Argentina there are favorable signs; the Asociación de Amigos del Arte harbors among its projects one of dramatic spectacles; Señor Piantanida, in the columns of *Martín Fierro*, makes excellent observations on the prospects [scan 56] of the “art theater” in Buenos Aires; Greek theaters are being built, which might well win over the public, to the surprise of the skeptics… Meanwhile, since 1919, the group *Renovación*, of La Plata, has been organizing from time to time performances, with curtains painted in a new style, of modern dramas and old comedies (Lope de Rueda, Cervantes, Molière, Goldoni); the company *Arte de América* has also adopted, for its tableaux of popular dances and songs, *artistic* curtains, inspired by motifs of the New World; the intelligent curiosity with which the resurrection of *Juan Moreira* and of *Santos Vega* is received, in their primitive and perfect setting, the circus ring, which breaks with the custom of urban spectacles, is a sign of maturity of taste; and even in opera, that venerable church of routine, the beak of the *Golden Cockerel* has just split tradition in two.

The desire for renewal is in the air. To fulfill it in our countries we shall have to begin, as everywhere, with special performances, to which only the devotees are admitted, previously constituted as a society, and unnecessary spectators are excluded. But university students ought also to work in this renewal; precious contributions are owed to students in other countries: there are cities in the United States where the best dramatic spectacles are those offered by the young people of the university in their *Little Theatres*.

Let us hope that attempts will soon multiply. If Spanish America is to fulfill its aspirations to artistic originality, it is its duty to abandon the beaten paths and seek new routes for the theater.

## Don Juan Ruiz / de Alarcón

Within the unity of Spanish America, there are in literature traits proper to each country. And not only in the works where the creole or the Indigenous character is sought, the description of local life and things. No; any practiced reader discerns without great effort the nationality, for example, of the poets. The great artists, like Martí or Darío, often form an exception. But observing by groups, who does not distinguish between the *facundia*, the *difficult facility*, the Venezuelan elegance, at times superficial, and the metaphysical lyricism, the transcendental orientation of Colombia? Who does not distinguish, beside the slow and measured march of Chilean poetry, the brilliant impulses and daring of the Argentine? Who does not distinguish Cuban poetry, eloquent, rotund, more reasoning than imaginative, from the Dominican, similar to it, but more sober and freer in its movements? And who, finally, does not distinguish, among the manifestations of these and the other peoples of America, this peculiar character: [scan 60] the veiled feeling, the discreet tone, the twilight shade of Mexican poetry?

As the landscapes of the plateau of New Spain, sharpened and cut clean by the thinness of the air, made arid by dryness and cold, are covered, under pale blue skies, with gray and yellowish tones, so Mexican poetry seems to ask of them its tonality. Discretion, sober measure, melancholy, twilight and autumnal feeling accord with this perpetual autumn of the heights, quite different from the eternal fecund spring of the torrid lands; an autumn of discreet temperatures that never offend, of soft twilights and serene nights.

Thus we discover Mexican poetry from the moment it defines itself: poetry of soft tones, of discreet emotions. Thus we see it, a little before independence, in the *Ratos tristes* of Fray Manuel de Navarrete, effusions poured out in notes that attain a crystalline delicacy; afterward, in José Joaquín Pesado, whose fine landscapes of the Atlantic slope, *Sitios y escenas de Orizaba y Córdoba*, though they required a more vigorous brush, reveal a pictorial world of extraordinary fascination; in the mystic songs of the religious poets of the mid-nineteenth century; in the stoic philosophy of the tercets of Ignacio Ramírez; in the longings that fill the verses of Riva Palacio; in the grave classical inspiration of Pagaza and Othón; in *Pax animae* and *Non omnis moriar*, the most penetrating and profound accents of Gutiérrez Nájera, the most autumnal of poets, “autumn flower of Mexican Romanticism,” as Justo Sierra aptly called him; lastly, in the delicate emotions and solemn meditation of our best-loved poets of today, Nervo, Urbina, González Martínez. There are exceptions, of course: in Gutiérrez Nájera (*Después*) and in Manuel José Othón (*En el desierto*) we find intense notes, passionate cries; they would not be the great poets they are if these were lacking. The poets [scan 61] born in the lowlands, like Carpio and Altamirano, have given us burning landscapes. And above all, you will tell me, Díaz Mirón. Ah, yes! Díaz Mirón, who is among the Mexican poets born in torrid regions, gathers in his great odes the impulses of the hot country and in the pictures of the *Idilio* the reverberations of the tropical sun. But even to him the pacifying breath of the plateau descends: to him we owe delicate songs like the *Barcarola* and the melancholy *Nox*; serene philosophy in the ode *A un profeta*, and sad landscapes, tinged with twilight emotion, as in *Toque*:

*Where is the creeper that does not spread*

*its dark green like a plume*

*over the gray wall? The ivy fastens*

*its sad rag to the ulcerated wall.*

If the Mexican landscape, with its gray tonality, has entered into poetry, how could it not have entered into painting? Once, in one of the interminable rearrangements that the galleries of the Academy of Fine Arts undergo in Mexico, Spanish painters and modern Mexican painters came to face one another on the walls of a hall. Among those Spaniards, none recalled the long and deep tragedy of the Castilian tablelands, but rather the strong life of the Cantabrian coast, of Levante, of Andalusia; among the Mexicans, all gathered notes from the plateau. And the contrast was abrupt: on one side, the warm opulence of red and gold, the violent blues and purples of the sea, the cheerful light of the sun, the vivid flowers, the flesh of women, in the canvases of Sorolla, of Bilbao, of Benedito, of Chicharro, of Carlos Vázquez; on the other, the black cloths, the melancholy faces, the pale flowers, the gray atmospheres, in the canvases of Juan Téllez, of Germán Gedovius, of Diego Rivera, of Ángel Zárraga, of Gonzalo Argüelles Bringas.

[scan 62]

Thus, amid the opulence of the Spanish theater in the Golden Age, amid the abundance and prodigality of Lope, of Calderón and of Tirso, the Mexican Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón y Mendoza strikes a note of discretion and sobriety. It is no mirage of distance. Let us turn to his contemporary Don Juan Pérez de Montalbán, and we shall see what he tells us in the *Memoria de los que escriben comedias en Castilla*, at the end of his miscellany *Para todos* (1632): “Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón arranges them with such novelty, wit, and strangeness that there is no play of his that does not have much to admire and nothing to reprove, which, after so much has been written, is a great proof of his most fertile store.”<sup>1</sup>

If Alarcón’s singularity was noticed from the first, how is it that no one afterward attempted to explain it? The fact is that Alarcón had served as a subject, as a rule, only for works of an academic kind, in which the curiosity of psychological inquiry barely shows itself. Academic criticism—and especially its most illustrious representatives in this matter, Hartzenbusch and Fernández-Guerra—took it for granted that Alarcón, traditionally counted among the leaders of the national theater, must be as Spanish as Lope or Tirso. And metropolitan disdain, even when unconscious and without malice, abetted by sloth, forbade any search in Alarcón’s nationality for the roots of his *strangeness*. How was the distant colony to engender a true *wit of the court*? The homeland, in this case, turned out to be a mere accident.

[scan 63] in Spain, the Spanishness of the Senecas and of Quintilian, of Lucan and of Martial, of Juvencus and of Prudentius?

Alarcón was born in the city of Mexico around 1580. He went to Spain in 1600. After five years in Salamanca and three in Seville, he returned to his country in 1608, and took the degree of licentiate in Civil Law at the old University of Mexico. From there, Fernández-Guerra supposed, he had returned to Europe in 1611; but the Mexican researcher Nicolás Rangel has shown that Alarcón was still in Mexico in mid-1613, when his famous biographer imagined him premiering plays in Madrid. We do not find him at court until 1615. At thirty-four years of age, more or less, he left his homeland for good; in Spain he lived twenty-five more, until his death. A proud man, but discreet, he might perhaps not have been the victim of the bitter literary customs of his time, had it not been for his physical deformity and his condition as an outsider. He can have written for the stage for only some ten years. He published two volumes of plays, in 1628 and 1634; they contain twenty, and in separate editions three more are attributed to him: these are all that are rigorously authentic and exclusively his. With all the doubtful attributions and the works in collaboration—including the ten in combination with Tirso that the Frenchman Barry supposes to be his—the total would barely come to thirty-six; Lope, by contrast, must have written more than a thousand—even cutting back his own exaggerations and the still greater ones of Montalbán—Calderón nearly eight hundred, and Tirso four hundred. Outside the theater, he produced only occasional verses, very rarely. Surely he began to write plays before 1615, and perhaps he composed some in America; of one of them, *El semejante a sí mismo*, it is judged [scan 64] probable; and, in truth, both that one and *Mudarse por mejorarse* (there are many curious similarities between the two) contain words and expressions that, while remaining pure Castilian, are used more in Mexico today than in any other Spanish-speaking country. He had the possibility of having them performed in Mexico, for a theater was built around 1597 (that of Don Francisco de León) and there were customary

*festivity and new plays every day,*

according to the testimony of Bernardo de Valbuena in his luxuriant poem *La grandeza mexicana* (1604). He probably collaborated in the years 1619 to 1623 with the master Tirso de Molina, and if *La villana de Vallecas* is a product of that collaboration, both authors will have combined in it their memories of America: Alarcón, those of his homeland; Tirso, those of the Island of Santo Domingo, where he was from 1616 to 1618.

Montalbán’s curious observation, cited a thousand times, never explained, suggests at last to Fitzmaurice-Kelly the posing of the problem: “Ruiz de Alarcón,” he says, “is less genuinely national than all of them (Lope, Tirso, Calderón), and the true individuality, the *strangeness*, that Montalbán noticed in him with a certain perplexity, makes him better appreciated by foreigners than in his own country (Spain).”

Less Spanish than his rivals: nor did the eminent Wolf fail to perceive it, though he was content to indicate it in passing.

The Spanish theater of the Golden Age, which seeks its definitive formula with the schools of Seville and Valencia, attains it in Lope, and imposes it for a hundred years of splendor, until it exhausts it, until its death in the ill-starred beginnings of the eighteenth century. It is not, doubtless, the most perfect formula of dramatic art; it is not simple and direct, but artificial: the *comedia* claims to live by itself, to be sufficient unto itself, to justify itself by its power of attraction, [scan 65] of diversion, in short. Within it there is room for great divine and human cases, and there were such: its profound reality does not always overcome artifice, and the *auto sacramental*, in which the loftiest conceptions found a place, is subject to the complicated allegorical fiction.

The need for movement: that is the characteristic of Spanish life in the golden centuries. And that movement, which spills over into wars and voyages, which undertakes grand religious and political enterprises, is what in literature makes of *La Celestina*, of the *Lazarillo*, of the *Quijote* the initial examples of active realism, with a vitality superior to that of merely descriptive or analytical realism; what in the *conceptistas* and *culteranos* exercises itself in unforeseen ways, consuming itself in perpetual effort of invention, and what, finally, in the theater gives life the appearance of a rapid and ingenious mechanism.

No one could match Lope de Vega in mastering that mechanism, in good part his own invention, and subject it to every sort of combination, thus multiplying the models that all Spain immediately adopted. Within Lope’s mechanism there was room for everything from the most puerile subjects, treated with the vivacity of the cinematograph, to the most vigorous humanity; seldom, and then without seeking it, the ethical or philosophical problem. In Tirso, in Calderón, at moments in other dramatists, like Mira de Amescua, those problems entered the Spanish theater and sent it soaring in dizzying flights.

In the midst of this artificial but rich and brilliant theater, Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón displayed a singular personality. He entered as an apprentice upon the paths Lope opened, and tries his hand equally at grandiloquent tragedy (in *El Anticristo*) and extravagant comedy (in *La cueva de Salamanca*). He wishes, then, to know all the resources of the mechanism and to measure his own strength; the day comes when he realizes his true aptitudes, and then he cultivates and perfects his enclosed garden.

[scan 66]

He is not rich in the gifts of the poet: he lacks lyrical virtue altogether; he versifies cleanly (except in the hendecasyllables) and even with elegance. He is not bold and prodigal like his master and enemy, Lope, like his friends and rivals: he is discreet—as a Mexican—writes little, polishes much, and proposes to give his plays a clear meaning. He does not, in appearance, modify the formula of the national theater; for that reason, superficially, he is not distinguished among his emulators and may be supposed as Spanish as they; but inwardly his formula is a different one.

The world of Alarcón’s comedy is, outwardly, the same world as the school of Lope: noble gallants who woo, against others of their rank or higher, frequently princes, ladies watched over not by mothers, who never exist, but by fathers, brothers, or uncles; entanglements and intrigues of love; conflicts of honor over feminine decorum or the rivalry of gentlemen; thoughtless love in the man; fickle affection in the woman; the solution, whatever turns up, with marriages handed out even when unnecessary or unsuitable. But this world, which in the work of the Spanish dramatists lives and stirs dizzyingly, tying and retying conflicts as in a complex dance of figures, in Alarcón moves with less rapidity: its march, its development are more measured and more calculated, subjected to a stricter logic (except in the denouements). Hartzenbusch already noted in him “the brevity of the dialogues, the constant care to avoid repetitions, and the singular and rapid manner of sometimes cutting off the acts” (and the scenes). Judged comparatively, he does not go to excess in entanglements; much less in words; he reduces the monologues, the digressions, the lyrical outbursts, the long talks and disputes full of sparkling and brilliant plays of wit. Only the narrations tend to be long, from excessive desire for explanation, for dramatic logic. Upon the impetuosity and prodigality of the European Spaniard who created and spread the mechanism of the [scan 67] *comedia*, there has been imposed, as a moderating force, the prudent sobriety, the discretion of the Mexican.

And the gifts of observation, too, are those of a Mexican. Malicious and sharp observation, made in a satirical spirit, is the privilege of no people; but if the Spaniard expresses it with abundance and effrontery (what better example than the interminable diatribes of Quevedo?), the Mexican, with his habitual reserve, keeps it slyly in store to launch it in a concise formula at an unexpected opportunity. Brief observations, unforeseen retorts, epigrammatic formulas abound in Alarcón and constitute one of the attractions of his theater. And for this argument it would be enough to compare the embittered attacks directed at him by Lope, and Tirso, and Quevedo, and Góngora and other eminent wits—if on this occasion petty ones—with Alarcón’s sober replies, by way of allusion, in his plays, particularly that one, no longer satirical but bitter, in *Los pechos privilegiados*:

*Blame him who, forgetting*

*the defects of his own soul,*

*jests by nicknaming*

*those another has in his body.*

The observation of characters and customs is Alarcón’s fundamental and constant resource, while in his emulators it is incidental: observation, not the spontaneous reproduction of customs nor the free creation of characters, in which he does not surpass them. The purpose of incessant observation is subordinated to a higher one: the moral end, the desire to give an ethical truth the convincing aspect of artistic reality.

Within the old Spanish theater, Alarcón creates the species—solitary within it, without qualified antecedents or immediate succession—of the comedy of manners and of character. Not only the [scan 68] creates for Spain, but helps create it in France: imitating him, translating him, not only into a different language but into a different artistic system, Corneille introduces into France with *Le menteur* the high comedy that was to become, in Molière’s hands, a fine and profound labor. That comedy, extending its empire over the whole eighteenth century, with the influence of France over all European literature, reenters Spain to attain a new apogee, somewhat pale, with Moratín and his school, in which another Mexican of discreet personality significantly figures: Manuel Eduardo de Gorostiza. Thus moral comedy, in the modern era, runs a cycle that starts from Mexico and closes again in Mexico.

But nationality can never explain the whole man. Our dramatist’s gifts as an observer, which coincide with those of his people, are not all his artistic wealth: what is superior in him is the transmutation of moral elements into aesthetic elements, a gift rarely granted to creators. Alarcón is singular for that reason in Spanish literature.

In him misfortune—his physical deformity—sharpened sensibility and stimulated thought, leading him to an attitude and a conception of life strongly defined, even excessive in its definition. Proud and discreet, observant and reflective, hard social experience led him to form a code of practical ethics, whose precepts reappear at every step in the plays. It is not an ethics in open disagreement with that of the hidalgos of the time; but it does indicate particular directions that entail modifications. He thinks that, in the classic expressions, *what one is* is worth more than *what one has* or *what one represents*. Virtue is worth more than talent, and both more than titles of nobility; but these are worth more than the favors of the powerful, and more, much more, than money. It is plain to see: Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón y Mendoza lived a long time in scant fortune, and only in maturity attained the economic position he longed for.

[scan 69]

But he had been born and had grown up in a country where the recent conquest made class distinctions deep, and his titles of aristocracy were excellent, since he descended on his father’s side from the Alarcóns of Cuenca, ennobled since the twelfth century, and on his mother’s from the most illustrious family of the Mendozas, which had given the greatest number of eminent men to Spanish arms and letters. Alarcón tells us in every form and in every play—or nearly—the incomparable nobility of his lineage: a weakness known in him in his own day and which Cristóbal Suárez de Figueroa censures in his affected and venomous style.

Honor, of course! Honor must be the careful concern of every man and every woman; and it must be opposed as a superior principle to every social rank, even royalty. Moral notions cannot be repealed by any man, even a king, nor for any motive, even the most legitimate passion: love, or self-defense, or punishment out of family duty, a survival of barbarous ages. Among the virtues—how lofty is mercy!—Alarcón goes so far as to speak out against the duel, and especially against the desire to kill. Moreover, the virtues of the prudent man are particularly dear to him, the virtues that may be called logical: sincerity, loyalty, gratitude, as well as the practical rule that must complement them: discretion. And there is a lesser virtue that he esteemed highly: courtesy. The conquistador found in Mexico populations with deep-rooted habits of complex courtesy, in the Asiatic manner, and the life of the colony was impregnated with it. The courtesy of New Spain was proverbial from the times of our dramatist: “courteous as a Mexican Indian,” says Vicente Espinel in the *Marcos de Obregón*. Shortly before, the Spanish physician Juan de Cárdenas celebrated the urbanity of Mexico by comparing it with the manners of the Peninsular newly arrived in America. At the end of the seventeenth century the venerable Palafox, in his *Virtudes* [scan 70] *del indio*, said: “courtesy is very great.” And in the nineteenth century, was not courtesy one of the traits that best caught the shrewd eyes of Madame Calderón de la Barca? Alarcón himself was very courteous: Quevedo, maliciously, calls him “fly and flatterer.” And in his plays one notes an abundance of expressions of mere formal courtesy that contrasts with their frequent omission in his contemporaries.

Love is a great thing; but—Alarcón thinks—is it possible to attain it? Woman is fickle, inconstant, false; she falls in love with a fine figure or a pompous title or—worse—with money. Above all, the abominable, the petty woman of Madrid, who lives dreaming of being treated to gifts in the silversmiths’ shops. Friendship is a more disinterested, firmer, surer affection. And how could his personal experience have been otherwise!

The greatest interest offered by this set of conceptions about human life is that one sees them constantly appear as motives of action, as stimuli of conduct. There are in Alarcón no theses that are posed and developed syllogistically, as in the dramas with a *raisonneur* of the modern French; nor do they spring up abruptly on the occasion of exceptional conflicts, as in *García del Castañar* or *El alcalde de Zalamea*; for the theater of the European Spaniards, apart from extraordinary cases, is content with conventional norms, on which no long thought is bestowed. No; the moral ideas of this man who was a moralist among men of imagination circulate freely and normally, and are incorporated into the fabric of the comedy, without weighing upon it or turning it into methodical dissertation. Usually they appear in brief, concise form, as incidents of the dialogue, or else are embodied in examples: such are the Don García of *La verdad sospechosa*, and the Don Mendo of *Las paredes oyen* (examples *a contrario*), or the García Ruiz de Alarcón of *Los favores del mundo*, and the Marquis Don Fadrique of *Ganar amigos*.

[scan 71]

The gift of creating characters is the third of Alarcón’s great gifts. To develop it he was well served by the ample movement of the Spanish theater, whose cinematographic freedom (like that of the Elizabethan English) allowed characters to be shown in all situations of interest to the action; and so, under the principle of logical unity that he imposes on his characters, they enjoy a wide margin in which to reveal themselves. Their creator treats them with sympathy: the women less so (in contrast with Tirso); the male characters, yes, even the vicious ones whom he punishes. At moments one would say that in *La verdad sospechosa* Alarcón is on Don García’s side, and we even expect him to burst out in a praise of lying, in the manner of Mark Twain or Oscar Wilde. And what character is there in all the Spanish theater of so curious a physiognomy as *Don Domingo de Don Blas*, in *No hay mal que por bien no venga*, apologist of logical conduct and of the simple and comfortable life; paradoxical in appearance, but profoundly human; a character worthy of English literature, in Wolf’s opinion; worthy of Bernard Shaw, shall we say today?

But, besides, in Alarcón’s world the turbulent life of perpetual struggle and intrigue that reigns in the drama of Lope and Tirso is sweetened, just as the life of the colony was much calmer than that of its metropolis; people are more in the house than in the street; there are not always duels; there is more discretion and tolerance in conduct; human relations are easier, and the affections, especially friendship, show themselves in a more normal and intimate way, with less apparatus of conflict, of exception and of trial. Alarcón’s moral purpose and meditative temperament illuminate with a pale light and tinge with melancholy gray this aesthetic world, drawn in clear and firm lines, more regular and more serene than that of the Spanish dramatists, but without their riches of color and form.

[scan 72]

All these qualities, which derive partly from his own genius, original and irreducible, partly from his experience of life and partly from his birth and education in Mexico, placed within the frame of the Spanish literary tradition, make of Alarcón, as Menéndez Pelayo masterfully said, the “classic of a romantic theater, without breaking the formula of that theater or diminishing the rights of the imagination for the sake of a narrow code of precepts or an ethical dogmatism”; a dramatist who found “by instinct or by study that almost imperceptible point at which moral emotion becomes a source of aesthetic emotion and, without pedagogical apparatus, while it moves the soul and kindles the fancy, instructs the understanding as in a school of virtue, generosity and courtesy.”

An artist of classical spirit, the designation being understood in the sense of a sober and reflective artist, he is so also by his fondness for the literature of Latium, by his affinity, so often remarked, with the sober and pensive muse of Terence. But his spontaneous discipline never kept him from appreciating the value of the art of his time; not only did he adopt Lope’s dramatic system, and put into it his new orientation, but he observed with interest and critical spirit all the literature of that day: there are in him reminiscences of Quevedo and of Cervantes. His aristocratic inclinations and preoccupations keep him from the song and ballad of the people, while Lope and his school had an extraordinary and fruitful fondness for them.

There are in his work experiments that do not belong to the type of comedy he developed and perfected. Of these the most important is *El tejedor de Segovia*, a novelistic drama, of extravagant romantic subject, but beneath whose picturesque brilliance Alarcón’s own muse is discovered, preaching against killing and defining supreme nobility. Nor should *El Anticristo* be forgotten, a religious tragedy inferior to those of Calderón and Tirso, of at times monstrous plot, but in which, by her hieratic attitudes, [scan 73] the figure of Sophia stands out and in which are found some of the most eloquent passages of its author, those that come closest to the heroic tone (for example the one that begins: “Babylon, Babylon…”).

Comedy has two great traditions: the poetic and the realistic; which might also be called, trimming the sense of the words, romantic and classical. The one gives itself up disinterestedly to the imagination, to the joy of living, to amiable emotions, to the desire for simple ideals, and sometimes borders on the idyll and on utopia, as in Aristophanes’s *The Birds* and Shakespeare’s *The Tempest*; the other wishes to be a mirror of social life and an active critique of customs, keeps close to the exact observation of habits and characters, and often approaches the task of the psychologist-moralist, like Theophrastus or Montaigne. Greater geniuses have favored the former: Aristophanes and Shakespeare, Lope and Tirso. The representatives of the other are more limited artists, but admirable masters of their domain, fine and perfect cultivators. Menander is the patriarch of its tradition: to it belong Plautus and Terence, Ben Jonson, Molière and his numerous following. Alarcón is its representative of genius in Spanish literature, and Mexico should count among its own blazons having furnished, with elements of national character, the foundations for the constitution of that singular and eminent personality.

## Enrique González Martínez

…You yourself are the road. Such is the spiritual route of this poet: it departs from the manifold vision of things, from the wealth of images necessary to the man of art, and, going inward along the way, arrives at his philosophy of universal life. His poetry takes on a double character: of individualism and of pantheism at once. The monads of Leibniz enter the universe of Spinoza thanks to the miracle of aesthetic synthesis.

I

Of the greatest interest, for the spiritual history of our time in Spanish America, is the formation of the poetic current to which the verses of Enrique González Martínez belong.<sup>1</sup> This poetry of transcendental concepts and subtle emotions is the last

<sup>1</sup>This essay was written in 1915, as a preface to the book of verse *La muerte del cisne*; in reading it, the limitation of time must be borne in mind. The poet, since then, has not stood still. Neither has the world. [scan 76] transformation of Romanticism: not only of interior romanticism, which belongs to every age, but also of Romanticism as a historical form. As in every triumphant revolution, in the Romanticism of the neo-Latin literatures the grave dissensions were the internal ones. In France—which the peoples of the Castilian tongue have followed for a hundred years as sole mistress, for better and for worse—alongside the pure romantic poetry, that of Hugo, Lamartine and Musset, the naked expression of every individual disquiet, an impetus that flooded, to overflowing, the channels of a new rhetoric, there arose Vigny with his praise of silence and his aristocratic disdains; there arose Gautier with his hedonistic curiosity and his aristocratic irony. The *Parnasse* rises at last as a protest against the excess of violence and nakedness: its aesthetic, poor by reason of its negative attitude, or at least its limiting one, remained bound and tied to that of Romanticism by its very purpose of contradiction. After the romantic thesis, which engenders the Parnassian antithesis, there appears, and still endures, the synthesis: Symbolism. Neither so much violence nor so much impassivity. Everything has a place in poetry; but everything is treated by symbols. Everything is purified and ennobled; it also becomes more or less abstract. Hence now the abstract lyricism, the danger that is engendering the reaction, the antithesis contrary to the present symbolist thesis under whose empire we live.

This is, meanwhile, the force that dominates our poetry: Symbolism. We have been, in America, classical, or often academic; we have been romantic or at least disheveled; we never managed to be fully Parnassians or decadents. Our *modernismo*, years ago, seemed to take from French Symbolism only formal elements; little by little, without noticing it, we have entered into its atmosphere, we have adopted its attitude toward the essential problems of art. We have arrived, at last, at the spiritual position of Symbolism, accommodating ourselves to the lyric tone that it has given to French poetry.

[scan 77]

II

So the work of Enrique González Martínez shows; so shows the cult he arouses among the young. Although many in America do not yet know him, González Martínez is in 1915 the poet whom the intellectual youth of Mexico admires and prefers; abroad, he is beginning to be imitated in silence.

Seldom will anyone come to know the tables of literary values of Mexico who does not visit the country; for criticism is exercised far more in the coterie than in the book or the newspaper. Who, in our America, does not know the collections of verse, popular among women, by Mexican poets who flourished before 1880? Are their names not repeated as representative names among moderately informed readers? But the opinion of the coteries declares—and truly—that Mexico had no poets of quality in the two centuries between Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz and Manuel Gutiérrez Nájera. He, thinks Antonio Caso, is the most influential literary personality that has appeared in the country. From his work, deceptive in its air of lightness, incalculable directions proceed for verse as for prose. With his appearance, which historically is always a sign, though it may not always have been an influence, the group of the major gods begins to form.

Six major gods are proclaimed by the voice of the coteries: Gutiérrez Nájera, Manuel José Othón, already dead; Salvador Díaz Mirón, Amado Nervo, Luis G. Urbina and Enrique González Martínez.

Each of the earlier poets had his hour of influence. González Martínez is the poet of the present hour, the one beloved and preferred by the young who are initiated, as in the warmth of some strange hothouse, into the intense activity of art and culture that survives, cloistered and secret, amid the threats of social dissolution.

This poet, to whom the select souls of his country pay intimate homage, arrived in the capital scarcely four years ago.

[scan 78]

He was welcomed with solicitous enthusiasm by the representatives of tradition, in the Academy; by the representatives of modern culture, in the Ateneo. He already brought four books: the fourth, *Los senderos ocultos*, admirable. He came from the provinces, where he had spent his youth.

#### III

…What worlds of experience did this poet traverse, capable of so many, in the twenty years that passed between impressionable adolescence and youthful maturity? His poetry conceals every trace of outward and daily existence. It is, from the beginning, spiritual autobiography; a work of symbolic art, composed not of native materials but of the ideal essence of thought and emotion.

The poet was, from his awakening, kindled with intimate longings and anguish. But he observed what lay around him; the prestige of forms and colors seduced him, the marvel of sound:

*I loved only the crimson twilights,*

*the clouds and the fields, the shore and the sea…*

*From the garden the jasmine and the rose attracted me*

*(the blood of the rose, the snow of the jasmine)…*

*The voices of the birds delighted my ear,*

*the ballad of the wind, the song of the shepherd…*

Then the inevitable descriptive sonnets are composed; Virgil is consulted; themes are begged from the decorative Greece of French poets; Leconte or Heredia is translated.

But beside the smiling mythological scenes, amid the landscapes of the *Mexican school* (the one that begins with Navarrete and culminates in Pagaza and Othón), romantic reminiscences float: archaic invocations to the ocean wave and the lightning of storms; voices [scan 79] confusedly troubling the desired harmony. In this ensemble that aspires to Parnassian repose, strange notes already sound; modulations of Verlaine’s flute slip in. Alas for him who has heard this *poignant* sound!

In the traditional wood, two symbols attract the poet: the majestic tree, the hidden fountain. From them he learns, after his first deliriums, the lesson of recollection and temperance. They will free him from two intoxications, dangerous if they persist: the inner one, the metaphysical pain of an adolescence tortured by sudden disillusions; the outer one, the dazzlement of youth before the pomp and delight of the physical world.

He finds his discipline, his norm: the perfect enjoyment of beautiful things asks for “attentive leisure, sweet silence”; and the enjoyment of high emotions asks for the stilling of inner tumults, asks for temperance:

*You will go over the life of things*

*with noble slowness…*

*Let everything leave in you a kind of trace,*

*mysterious, intensely engraved…*

For this secrecy, this temperance, now lead him far from the cult of impassive idols; they lead him to scrutinize beneath the sumptuous veil of appearances. The decorative image of the swan is succeeded by the spiritual symbol of the owl, with its air of taciturn interrogation.

*I loved only the crimson twilights…*

*When the note died away, when the star went out,*

*oh shadows, oh silence! you too were slumbering…*

No; now he strives “not to disturb the silence of life,” but he refines his soul so that it may “listen to the silence and see the shadow.”

[scan 80]

His poetry acquires exquisite virtues; its character is defined as one of solemn meditation, of contained and discreet emotion; its atmosphere of contemplation and reverie; its clear crystal melody; its delicate lacustrine harmony. Serene ecstasies:

*Seek in all things a soul and a hidden*

*meaning; do not confine yourself to vain appearance…*

*In all beings there is a gentle smile,*

*an ineffable sorrow or a somber mystery…*

“Everything is revelation, everything is teaching,” says Rodó, “everything is treasure hidden in things.” Everything is symbol:

*At times a leaf fallen*

*from the height of the trees, a weeping*

*of the waters that pass, a sonorous*

*trill of a nightingale, trouble my life…*

*…For I know not whether I diffuse myself in all*

*or all penetrates me and goes with me…*

Thus, after steering clear of the danger of youthful intoxications, the poet attains the supreme and tranquil intoxication of pantheism.

But the old romantic sap was not extinguished; the experience of sorrow, always personal, always intimate, is perhaps what stirs it, like that ancient sadness which interrupted his forgetful happiness:

*I pruned my orchard and sipped my wine…*

*And the old sadness stopped beside me*

*and I heard it say softly: Have you forgotten?*

*From my eyes still clouded with pleasure and festivity*

*a mute tear was the only answer…*

[scan 81]

Disquiet bids him look inward:

*You are deceived: you have not lived while your faltering step*

*furrows the glooms of your interior, groping…*

He finds his road. He stands before the gates of maturity. He has won his equilibrium, his autarky:

*And I can melt into the prayers of the landscape*

*and the miracles of the twilight light…*

*But in my subjective realms…*

*a soul stirs with its exclusive joys,*

*its own impulse and its particular sorrow…*

IV

The lyric autobiography of Enrique González Martínez is the history of a perpetual ascent. Toward greater serenity, but also toward greater sincerity; toward a more severe and profound conception of life. Mirror of our struggles, voice of our longings, this poetry is fully of our century and of our world. Terrible tempests lash our America; but Nemesis keeps watch, ready to punish every faintness, every wavering. Nor let us seek to forget, amid frivolous games and ingenious dalliances, the duty of building, of constructing, that the moment imposes. Our creed cannot be hedonism; nor can the golden pheasant or the silken swan be the symbol of our ideal preferences. What do Rubén Darío’s *Prosas profanas* signify, whose paths begin in the flowering garden of the *Fiestas galantes* and end in the sculptured hall of *Los trofeos*? A momentary diversion, a youthful digression where the strong spirit rested before intoning the *Cantos de vida y esperanza*.

[scan 82]

The youth of today thinks that those were “too many swans”; it wants a more complete artistic interpretation of life, a more devout respect for the need of interrogation, for the desire to order and construct. Art is not a passing flattery destined to oblivion, but an effort that aids in the spiritual construction of the world.

Enrique González Martínez gives voice to the new aesthetic aspiration. He does not speak to the multitudes; but through select souls his word of faith travels, his counsel of meditation:

*Wring the neck of the swan of deceptive plumage…*

*Look at the wise owl…*

*He has not the grace of the swan, but his restless*

*pupil, which pierces the shadow, interprets*

*the mysterious book of nocturnal silence.*

V

Beneath the poet’s solemn contemplations the old disquiet lives, with threats of tumult. So, beneath the triumphal harmony of Shelley, archangel whose sword of flame points out summits to perennial longing, the note of faintness moaned, momentarily.

The poet thinks that one should “weep, if one must weep, like the hidden fountain”; he should purify sorrow in art and, according to his aesthetic religion, transmute it into symbol. More still: the symbol must be *catharsis*, must be a lesson in fortitude.

But life, cruel, does not always give strength against every disaster. And then the artist chisels with somber delight his cup of bitterness, whose tragic splendor seduces like a philter of enchantment. In the pages of *La muerte del cisne* the two impulses struggle, that of faith and that of hopelessness, the sobbing voice of the *useless days* and of the *closed garden*.

[scan 83]

The times are hard. Let us hope… Let us hope that the tumult will subside when the turbid tide of the hour goes down. Then the wisdom of meditation will prevail, the serenity of autumn.

#### Postscript∗

To complete the historical perspective of this study, I shall recall some “Notas sobre literatura mexicana,” which I wrote in 1922 and which were not continued, stopping precisely at González Martínez.

From 1800 to our own day, Mexican literature divides into five periods. The first is characterized by the academic style in poetry; its beginning might be fixed in 1805, with the appearance of Fray Manuel de Navarrete in the first *Diario de México*; Pesado and Carpio represent its apogee and its decline. Coinciding with academicism in poetry is the savory popularism in prose of Lizardi, of Bustamante, of Padre Mier, whose heirs afterward are Cuéllar and Morales.

After 1830 Romanticism enters Mexico: for us the era of careless verses and lurid, overgrown novels. The harm done in America by our childish interpretation of the romantic doctrines can never be lamented enough! Literature was to be a work of inspired improvisation, without hindrances; but in fact none of our poets enjoyed the happy ignorance and the virgin eyes that are the supposed

All were city men and, for better or worse, educated in books and in schools; but fleeing discipline they gave themselves up to the hazards of bad culture; they did not read books, but devoured newspapers; and so, when they thought they were expressing intensely personal ideas and feelings, they repeated others’ formulas left lodged in their disordered memories.

Between 1850 and 1860 the period of the Reforma begins, in which the eminent figures of Altamirano, Ignacio Ramírez, Riva Palacio and Guillermo Prieto hold sway. Men of high talent and good culture, they would have been great writers had their literary work not been born in hours stolen from political activity. Even so, there are pages of Ramírez that count among the best Castilian prose of his century. And in the labor of others of his contemporaries, investigators or humanists, there is permanent value: in the historical and philological works of José Fernando Ramírez and Manuel Orozco y Berra; in the formidable reconstruction undertaken by García Icazbalceta of the intellectual life of the colonial period; in Alejandro Arango y Escandón’s book on Fray Luis.

Shortly after 1880 there opens, to end around 1910, the period usually considered the golden age of Mexican letters, or at least of its poetry: it is illustrated by the names of Justo Sierra, Díaz Mirón, Gutiérrez Nájera, Othón and Nervo, and is linked with the lively flowering of letters throughout Spanish America, where Darío and Rodó were the central figures. It is the age of the *Revista Azul*and the *Revista Moderna*. With political activity reduced to a minimum under the Díaz regime, writers have leisure to cultivate themselves and to write: there is room to refine the work.

From 1910 onward, literature again loses its atmosphere of tranquillity with the fall of the old regime, and is produced, in the Horatian phrase, “amid alarming things”: sometimes in a country in tumult; sometimes in exile, [scan 85] voluntary or forced. Like France after the Revolution, Mexico possesses its literature of émigrés. All this was bound to give, and has given, a new vital tone to the literature, in contrast to the air of dilettantism that it was acquiring during the epoch of Porfirio Díaz.

In 1910 fifteen years had passed since the death of Gutiérrez Nájera, in whom the contemporary poetry of Mexico had officially begun; Manuel José Othón had died four years earlier; Salvador Díaz Mirón wrote little and published less. The poets of earlier generations—though they might write admirable things, like Bishop Pagaza—were hardly read. The poets then in vogue were about forty years old: Amado Nervo, Luis G. Urbina and José Juan Tablada.

Of these, Nervo was to survive only nine years, during which he added nothing new to his work in themes or in form; but he did go on perfecting himself in the deep purity of his conception of life and acquiring a definitive simplicity of style: there are in *El estanque de los lotos*many of his most sincere and clearest notes.

Nor is there any important variation in the work of Urbina: *El poema del Mariel*, for example, is the same combination of landscapes and melancholy sighs as *El poema del lago*. Outwardly, yes, the themes change: the landscapes are not Mexican but foreign. And he has new notes, of picturesque realism, in the *Glosario de la vida vulgar*.

Shall I dare to say that in Tablada, too, there is no essential change? Tablada has always been the most restless of the Mexican poets, the one who strives to “keep up to date,” the reader of new things, the master of all exoticisms; it is not strange that in twelve years there should be so much variety in his work: types of poetry brought from the Far East; echoes of the various revolutions that from Apollinaire down to now ruffle the surface of literary Paris; and at the same time, Mexican themes, from Indigenous religion and legends [scan 86] to present-day life. In a large part of this labor there is more wit than poetry; but when poetry asserts itself, it is of fine quality; and in any case Tablada will always be a beneficent agitator, helping the good to purify themselves and the bad to plunge headlong.

There were other poets in 1910: for instance, those of the intermediate group, of transition between the *Revista Moderna* and the Ateneo. Their representative poets, such as Argüelles Bringas, belong by the volume and character of their work to the Mexico that ends in 1910 and not to the one that was then beginning.

There were, finally, two poets of importance, but still situated in the half-light: Enrique González Martínez and María Enriqueta.

The literary reputation of María Enriqueta dates from after the Revolution: toward the end of the old regime the belief abounded in Mexico that woman could have no possible role in culture. And yet her first book of poems, *Rumores de mi huerto*, is of 1908. Her inspiration of deep and contained tragedy is without precedent in Mexico, and, for now, without sequel and without influence; but through it, and in spite of her childish moments, María Enriqueta is one of the most singular artists.

Enrique González Martínez—who by age belongs to the group of Nervo, Urbina and Tablada—was to be the central poet of Mexico for a good stretch of the last twelve years. In 1909 he publishes his first book of great interest, *Silenter*, from the provinces; in 1911 he comes to the capital; in 1914 he is the poet most read; in 1918 he is the one most followed by the young. ## Alfonso Reyes

At last the public is convinced that Alfonso Reyes, above all, is a poet. Casual news items are beginning to call him a poet: a good sign. Good and reassuring for those of us who for a long time defended, amid alarms, the thesis in whose support the poet voluntarily left us defenseless.

When Alfonso Reyes appeared, twenty years ago, in a precocious, luminous and explosive adolescence, he was acclaimed a poet in generous and fervent youthful coteries. He was full of lyric impulse, and his verses, leaping from his lips with the quiver of arrows, went to lodge themselves in the memory of his eager listeners:

*The imperative simplicity of song…*

*That country of the golden cicadas,*

*where the mountains are of marble…*

*I love life for life’s sake!…*

*To me, who feel the ground’s voice wherever I tread,*

*what do you tell me with your silence and your prayer?*

[scan 90]

That happy moment for Mexican youth—the moment of the review *Savia Moderna*, of the Society of Lectures—soon passed. With more spirit, with greater solidity, the Ateneo would come (1909); the age of dream and unconsciousness had ended: the Ateneo lived amid struggles and was, in the order of pure intelligence, the prelude to the gigantic transformation that was beginning in Mexico. The Revolution was going to knock at every door and mark every man on the forehead; Alfonso Reyes, one of the first, saw his patrician home, on the summit of the mountain, dismantled by the hurricane that was being born:

*Ah, my great house, my only house!*

The poet hid his song before the storm. Song is autobiography; his went wholly in symbol and cipher, and he still took pains to conceal it. Afterward keeping it hidden became a habit. It was

*a muffled little song, sad…*

*a slave’s song, that tastes*

*of the fruit of prohibition…*

Wholly in symbol and cipher; rich in complex images, in subtle figures, with hermetisms of ancient lineage or of newest invention, but transparent to affectionate attention. A song charged with sentimental resonances: while the eyes follow the rainbows of the lyric torrent, the ear reconstructs from the resonances the intimate history, the history of a soul intense in emotion and in passion. And so, in the *Fantasía del viaje* the amazement at new spectacles (“I have seen the sea!”) fuses with the tragedy of the paternal home, of the native landscape left behind, with its forges of metal and its dusty fields. The odyssey begins: beneath the Homeric mask sounds the lament of parting, the “Elegía de Ítaca”:

[scan 91]

*Ithaca and my memories, oh friends, farewell!*

And the man who tastes the salt flavor of another’s bread makes his way amid impulses and faintings. Falling and rising, he ends by entrusting himself to life:

*Oar in tempest,*

*wing in hurricane:*

*the very fury that lashes me*

*is what will sustain me.*

Life grows hard; but in the midst of the storms pure days arrive, halcyon days, of limpid sky, of mild air, without the murmur or the ardor of spring:

*If I wake now to new festivals,*

*I remember others with a sudden tear…*

The lamps of the new home, laboriously lit in a foreign land, are at last signs of peace, by whose light one discovers in the valiant companion “the silver vibration—purest thread—of the first gray hair” and savors the “child’s voice wrapped in air” and the “clear impersonal kiss” of the son to his parents.

Afterward life gives him back part of the stolen gifts and fulfills promised triumphs; resurrected youth recovers its voice, now with new resonances: above the warm notes, from a bird’s breast, dominates the metallic timbre of irony, rust of the years… But it is irony without gall, which pursues the winks and fancies of things instead of human weaknesses; a caper of ideas, a dance of wit. The eyes treat themselves to festivals and journeys; the cities, reduced to cubist syntheses, file past in unreal processions: as for every traveler of intense gaze, they shrink for him into magic signs with which the spirit of the place is evoked.

[scan 92]

With the years, every lyric poet, laden with contradictory life, with complex emotions, tends to become a dramatic poet. In Alfonso Reyes the drama has come: his central work, in which he has concentrated the essence of his life and of his art, is his tragic poem *Ifigenia cruel*.

In the moment we are passing through, Greece has entered the penumbra: we do not know whether for a passing eclipse or for definitive shadow. There are illustrious exceptions (Santayana! Paul Valéry!), and they are rare. But in the times when Alfonso Reyes and his friends were discovering the world, Greece was at its apogee: never did it shine better! The Greece of all the classicisms having been buried, even that of the Parnassians, another had arisen, the agonistic Hellas, the Greece that fought and strove, seeking the serenity it never possessed, inventing utopias, giving reality in the works of the spirit to the dream of perfection that in its embryonic life proved impossible. The tempestuous wind of Nietzsche still blew, swollen with the duel between the Apollonian and the Dionysian spirit; in Germany, prolific erudition was being aired with the ingenious hypotheses of Wilamowitz; among the English-speaking peoples, the public was electrified by the sacred tremor and irresistible choral surge of the tragedies, in the extraordinary versions of Gilbert Murray, while Jane Harrison rejuvenated with the oil of “creative evolution” the old machinery of myth and rite; in France, while Victor Bérard reconstructed with picturesque researches the world of the *Odyssey*, Charles Maurras, a passionate pilgrim, pursued the transmigration of Athens into Florence.

On that living Hellas we were nourished. How many times afterward have we recalled our readings of Plato; that reading of the *Symposium* in the architecture studio of Jesús Acevedo! That living food would turn into our blood; [scan 93] and the myth of Dionysus, that of Prometheus, the legend of the house of Argos, would serve as vessels for our own conceptions.

The *Ifigenia cruel* is woven, like the songs, with threads of intimate history. The canvas is the legend of Iphigenia in Tauris, saved from the propitiatory sacrifice for the sake of the Trojan War and consecrated as priestess of the feral Artemis among the barbarians. In Alfonso Reyes’s work the tragic maiden has lost the memory of her earlier life. When Orestes arrives in search of her, she refuses to accompany him, contrary to the tradition recorded by Euripides. Orestes, spurred by the ritual urgencies of his expiation, which is the expiation of his whole race, carries off the statue of Artemis. Iphigenia remains in the foreign land. In Alfonso Reyes’s original conception, Iphigenia set about carving a new idol, a new Artemis, to replace the one Orestes and Pylades tear from her. In the definitive version of the tragedy, it is enough for her to cling to her new homeland.

Whoever knows Alfonso Reyes’s life will feel the personal accent of his *Ifigenia cruel*:

*I go about wary of myself,*

*watching for the fall of my feet,*

*in case I guess where I am going…*

*For I claim my intoxication,*

*my patrimony of mortal joy and sorrow.*

*Strange to me are so many human festivals*

*that you traverse with the gaze of the soul!…*

*There is one who lost her memories*

*and is consoled already…*

*And trades the dream of the eyes*

*for the dream of her heart…*

[scan 94]

Alfonso Reyes made his debut as a poet; but from his beginnings he could be seen overflowing toward prose: his culture exceeded the margins of what in our infantile America we think sufficient for poets; his intelligence spilled out in keen observations and concepts, if not cumbersome, at least useless for pure poetry.

His culture was in part the fruit of the severe discipline of the ancient and illustrious Preparatory School of Mexico; in part a reaction against it. To be a “preparatoriano” in the Mexico before 1910 was a badge comparable to being a “normalien” in France. That teaching was the privilege of a few, and perhaps for that reason a scant good for the country: to those whom it reached it gave foundations of unsurpassable mental solidity. In accordance with the positivist tradition, the scale of the sciences occupied the center of that edifice; men of robust mental fiber, disciples of Barreda, the founder, watched over and directed the student’s gradual and rigorous ascent of that scale. Most were impregnated with positivism forever by their passage through those classrooms. But Alfonso Reyes was one of the rebels: he accepted wholly, gladly, all the science and all its discipline; he rejected the reigning philosophy and set out to seek on the compass rose whither the spirit was blowing. When he left his *alma mater*, in 1907, germs of doctrinal revolution were seething among the youth impassioned for philosophy. Three, four years more and positivism vanishes in Mexico, as the old regime vanishes in politics.

In the work of Alfonso Reyes the influence of his School is felt in the poise, in the fullness of foundation. At first it reached further, even against his wish; still in *El suicida* (1917), alongside pages of fine originality, there are pages of a “preparatoriano,” with traces of the peculiar scholasticism of that positivism.

[scan 95]

Outside his School, forgetful or sparing of the humanities, he had to seek his literary orientations too. A voracious reader, but a sure one, without errors of choice; impetuous, never denying his impulses but seeking the best channel for them, his concern was to know nothing by halves. He made—we made—long excursions through the Spanish language and literature. The excursions had the dangerous excitement of forbidden hunts; in America, all Spanish tradition was interpreted under the watch of academic spirits, stationed in their eighteenth century (rules! genres! schools!), and the young fled from old Spain, thinking any attempt to revise values or meanings useless. From those excursions came Alfonso Reyes’s first works on Góngora, explaining him by the lyric impulse which in him tended “to fuse colors and rhythms into a superior manifestation,” and on Diego de San Pedro, defining his *Cárcel de amor* as a novel perfect in its choice of focus, the author placing himself within the work only as spectator. And from Spanish themes he extended to Mexican ones; in one of his studies, unfinished and now buried among inaccessible pamphlets, *El paisaje en la poesía mexicana del siglo XIX*, he set down precious observations on the relations between literature and the physical environment in America.

From those excursions he could go on, in 1913, to hold the first chair of Spanish philology that ever existed in Mexico, in that quixotic venture in which, without official help, we created the advanced humanities courses at the University; in Madrid he could become one of the workshop hands at the Center for Historical Studies and the *Revista de Filología Española*, under the wise, firm, kindly hand of Menéndez Pidal, with the cordial encouragement and exemplary discipline of Américo Castro and Navarro Tomás.

He gave himself wholly to those labors; from 1915 to 1920 he publishes in turn his studies and editions of the Archpriest, of Lope, of Alarcón, of [scan 96] Calderón, of Góngora, of Quevedo, of Gracián, his version of the *Cantar de Mío Cid* in modern prose. And from him, from those works, comes an interesting portion of the notions by which the interpretation of Spanish literature has been renewed in our day: from the medieval comic use of the “I” in the Archpriest to the significance of Alarcón’s theater as a “measured protest against Lope.”

In those Madrid years it was not only the researches into the literary past that absorbed him; above the dark and honorable mountain of index slips there still rose the weekly page of *El Sol*, with disquisitions on history (from which he has been able to select the ingenious volume of *Retratos reales e imaginarios*); there rose, finally, the grove of translations—Sterne, Chesterton, Stevenson—for the publishers of Madrid were living the most feverish period of their frenzy for launching foreign books.

Alfonso Reyes threw himself wholly into his labors, because he does not know how to give himself otherwise to anything; but he sighed for the free pen, for which he had only brief moments left. The work of the investigator, the scholar, the philologist, imprisons and devours; in his letters—opulent, overflowing letters—he complained of the growing tyranny of the “philological slippers.” He might have added, like Henri Franck in a similar plight: “But I dance in slippers!”

And from his furtive dances, in stolen moments, came the verses, the tales, the essays, the minimal and keen notes. With them, adding them to earlier writings from Mexico or from Paris, the free books keep coming out: *Cartones de Madrid*, *El suicida*, *Visión de Anáhuac*, *El plano oblicuo*, *El cazador*. Afterward, in years of freedom, come the volumes of verse and the *Ifigenia*, the *Calendario*, the five series of *Simpatías y diferencias.*

[scan 97]

In Alfonso Reyes, the writer of the free pen is of a type unusual in our language. Seeking to define him, to classify him (old mania!), people call him an essayist. And he does resemble, in truth, English essayists; not the grave family, philosophical and moralist, of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, nor the family of polemicists and critics of the nineteenth, but that of the free essayists of the romantic period, such as Lamb and Hazlitt. English literature made him familiar early with those paths of freedom. But his freedom does not come only from the English example; it is broader. He had the singular fortune of living from adolescence with spirits open to every novelty, for whom every road deserved the honors of trial, every fantasy the honors of realization. Among such friends he could conceive, write, argue the most unforeseen literature; he thus acquired, after overcoming the heavy inheritance of the “long paragraph,” an extraordinary ease; Antonio Caso, one of the friends, defined it as the power to give literary form to every kind of “bright idea.” His essays turned into certainty Goethe’s paradoxical saying: “Literature is the shadow of good conversation.” New concept, psychological glimpse, observation of things, unexpected comparison, fantastic invention, everything had room and found expression, crystallized into an agile, audacious style, of quick and luminous touches.

In the earliest of his free pages, alongside the easy mastery of expression, one still feels the weight of reminiscences: it is natural for the young man to complete life with books. Among his tales and dialogues in *El plano oblicuo* there are some, like the episode of Achilles and Helen, laden with literature—of the best; but there are also round, new creations, like “La cena,” where the characters move as if outside every plane of gravitation; there are spacious backgrounds of life and traits of swift tenderness, amid pirouettes of wit, in “Estrella de oriente,” in the memoirs of the German merchant and philologist. A pity that the storyteller has not persevered in Alfonso Reyes!

[scan 98]

The man of imagination, of avid and fine senses, has given us at least the *Visión de Anáhuac*, “a poem of colors and of men, of strange monuments and heaped-up riches,” says Valery Larbaud, a colorful reconstruction of the spectacle of Aztec Mexico, center of the civilization spread over that majestic high plateau, “the most transparent region of the air”; the observer has given us the *Cartones de Madrid*, sketches of the renewedly Goyesque spectacle of the Spanish capital, within the Castilian high plateau, bare, energetic, bristling with peaks and edges. Those two plateaus, similar to the superficial glance, opposed in their deep essence, preoccupy the writer: in them lie the roots of the enigmatic spiritual life of his homeland.

For in Alfonso Reyes everything is a problem or can be one. His intelligence is dialectical: he likes to turn ideas inside out to discover whether there is deceit in the weave; he likes to change the focus or point of view to verify relativities. Before, he pursued subtle relations, unsuspected oddities; now, convinced that everyday things are swollen with complexity, he is content to point out the invincible antinomies we stumble upon every minute. “Before, I collected smiles; now I collect glances.”

But the conviction that the universe is antinomic leads him to no radical form of pessimism; the fatalism of his people takes no hold of him; he will never be a fatalist, but an agonist, a fighter. As an artist he knows that the antinomies of the universe resolve themselves, for the sense of spectacle, into harmonies, and a morning of light, after a night of rain, gives us faith, if only momentary, in the essential balance of things: “the unfading face of the world shines as on the first day.” And he knows that in artistic creation the lyric impulse imposes rhythms on discord.

He conceives the lyric impulse—his youthful theory, which we long debated, but which never received its final garb—as a form [scan 99] of the ascending energy of life. He knows, he feels the values of the vital impulse, of intuition, of instinct. But he does not entrust himself to them alone; he knows they can falter, betray.

When, in opposition to positivism, the triumphant philosophies of intuition spread, bent on reducing intelligence to a mere useful and servile function, it might have been thought that Alfonso Reyes would find in them the justification and enlargement of his theoretical endeavors and even of his temperament. It was not so; deeply interested in them, like his friends, he resisted better than others the fascination of irrationalism. Impulse and instinct, in him, call upon reason to order, to channel and to lead to a happy end.

Like his artistic vision, his confidence in disdained reason keeps him far from pessimism. Reason, educated in the pursuit of truth, prepared never to rest in the seats of error, never to lose itself in the fog of vague ideas, to guard against the fictions of selfish interest, is a light that does not go out. Every other illumination, perhaps more intense, is subject to the unknown will of the gods. Alfonso Reyes, poet of deep emotions, man of imagination and wit, essayist whose freedom comes to wear the appearances of arbitrary caprice, is the reverse of the improviser without compass and the eccentric without norm: he preaches—and exemplifies—for his homeland fidelity to the one firm, if modest, light. Beneath his complexities and his fantasies, his digressions and his ellipses, one discovers the devotee of the just notion, of clear orientation, of “reason and the idea, masters in the whirlwind of all subconscious things.”

## The Argentine Friend<sup>1</sup>

I

I met Héctor Ripa Alberdi in Mexico in September 1921, and he was for me the intimate revelation of Argentina. Until then I had known, alongside the Argentina of international fame, the Argentina that its writers reveal; I had always observed how the vigor and the brilliance that give the country its character in our time, and that are attributed to its recent development, had existed long before; I found them in Echeverría, in Mármol, in Sarmiento, in Andrade. But Argentine literature, with its mere hundred years, does not reveal the whole of the national life; if it is possible, let us say, to know through its writers the character of the English or the French people in all its detail, no people of America has yet attained in its literary creations to such a *chorography*. There is a great part of our life, above all of our daily and domestic life, that the mere reader, even the assiduous reader, cannot know

<sup>1</sup>Preface to his works, soon to be published. [scan 104] with certainty; and the more so if one considers that, beneath many apparent similarities, and amid many profound ones, there exists a curious variety of spiritual nuances among the peoples of Spanish America. Ripa Alberdi, with his companions of 1921—Orfila, Dreyzin, Vrillaud, Bomchil—revealed to my eyes the spirit of his land with the traits of cordial strength and intimate delicacy that I had wished for. If Argentina is like this, I thought, we may now trust that our America will come to deserve that the words of Hostos, humorously repeated in conversation by Antonio Caso, no longer be applied to it: Half-men, half-civilizations…

Even before I knew him familiarly, Héctor revealed to me aspects of Argentina that were then new to me. He introduced himself in Mexico by addressing the public in the amphitheater of the Preparatory School: there, where in 1912 the strange and moving funeral of Justo Sierra took place, which Vasconcelos calls, with rare aptness, the culminating act in the spiritual life of the country; there, where in 1922 the mural painting of Diego Rivera arose, opening a hard-fought battle of art that still continues. Chance had brought me there, to the first International Congress of Students, in which the singular idea of the shrewd author of *Miniaturas mexicanas*, my loyal friend Daniel Cosío Villegas, took on reality; the students of my country, lacking one of their own who could make the journey to Mexico, decided to give me their representation so that there would be someone to recall the unjust fate of Santo Domingo, and in particular the fate of its schools, many of them closed as the invader’s petty revenge against the popular protest at the demands of Wall Street. When the Congress opened, on September 20, 1921, the numerous Argentine delegation aroused interest; we knew that it carried the representation of the movement that had renewed the universities of its country. The first to speak was [scan 105] Ripa Alberdi. And within a few moments we realized how many veils he was drawing back.

If we were to judge by him, Argentine youth had abandoned the pedantic jargon that was in fashion twenty years earlier and expressed itself in limpid Spanish; it had abandoned positivism and invoked Plato. Those of us who ten years before, in the Ateneo of Mexico, had been nourished on the word of the master of Athens now felt that the cult of Greece, so rare in the Spanish-speaking countries, united us with the new Argentina.

Better still: the youth of that great and prosperous country, a country of enterprise and drive, was turning with generosity and disinterestedness toward the study of social problems, and what concerned it was not success or wealth, though these were claimed as national characteristics, but justice and the good of all. One could think that our America is capable of preserving and perfecting the cult of the things of the spirit, without being dazzled by its own conquests in the order of material things. Rodó had not preached in the desert.

By singular good fortune, the work of the whole Argentine delegation only confirmed the impression left by Ripa Alberdi’s opening speech. Mexicans and Argentines dominated the Congress with their ardent devotion to the ideas of social regeneration and imposed the generous *Resolutions* adopted at the end and published as the fruit of those assemblies. During the close and active collaboration we established there, lasting friendships were formed. When the meetings ended, many of us felt the wish that that Argentine delegation, all understanding and enthusiasm, should not carry away from Mexico, as its only baggage, the debates of the student Congress and the festivities of the Centenary: we wanted them to see the country, at least in part; the remains of its formidable past and the efforts of its restless present. We succeeded: for my part, [scan 106] I offered my house, a bachelor’s then, to Ripa and Vrillaud. There began a series of excursions to exhumed Indigenous settlements, to colonial cities, to historic places, to picturesque spots. More than once the young Argentines coincided with another most dear guest of Mexico, Don Ramón del Valle-Inclán: none of them will forget that delightful journey from the capital to the Pacific Ocean, with stops in venerable and tragic Querétaro, merry and flowering Guadalajara, rustic Colima.

I came to know then Héctor’s clear and fine intelligence, his capacity for study and self-improvement, his firm and discreet character; and from our talks arose the plan of writing in collaboration a brief history of the literature of Spanish America. We began a correspondence. The following year I saw him again in his own country, where we were able to learn of the cordial propaganda he had made, with his friends, on behalf of things Mexican. When I was expecting that we would finally meet in Argentina, the news of his death reached me (1923)… Some days later it fell to me to say a few words at the ceremony that the Secretariat of Public Education of Mexico dedicated to his memory, in precisely the historic amphitheater where we had met him.

II

When death abruptly cuts short a life that was beginning to flower in abundance, like that of Héctor Ripa Alberdi, his friends, unreconciled to the unexpected blow, gather to consider how they will perpetuate the memory of him who departed untimely. In Héctor’s case, the natural thing is to collect and reprint his work.

Then doubt assails us: are we going to give, with these sketches, a just idea of the one who is gone? Héctor was like a tree in blossom; the fruits were only in promise: can those who did not know him catch the fragrance of the flower now dried?

[scan 107]

More than in his written work, Héctor lived intensely in the struggle for culture and in the stimulus of friendship. Of the exceptional virtues of the friend—manly, loyal, discreet, an *animator*—Arturo Marasso gives a clear idea in his article *Mis recuerdos de Héctor Ripa Alberdi*: a page that tells the noble story of a friendship, with the disorder and ardent force of a pen charged with emotion. Of the university fighter, who labored so much to impose the renewing orientation, many will bear witness. The insurgent student of 1918 had reached the professor’s chair by 1922; but not in order to compromise with any form of reaction, whose germ so often hides in spirits that temporarily or partially adopt advanced positions, but in order to combat it. In spirits of pure temper, neither age, nor power, nor wealth, nor honors create fear of free ideas; rather they reaffirm faith in the radical concepts of truth and the good. Neither Socrates nor Tolstoy was made conservative or renegade by age.

#### III

Those who did not know him and read only his written work will not learn all that Héctor Ripa Alberdi was; but let us not exaggerate the fear: they will learn, if not the breadth, the quality of his spirit. His spirit was serenity and strength. In his verses he deliberately wished to put only serenity; in them one reads his limpid soul, his clear thought, his firm and tranquil character. From early on he aspired to be a poet of solitude and repose; to join the master singers, like Arrieta, like González Martínez, who preach a gospel of serenity in our restless and discordant America, like the Greek who, amid perpetual agitation and public quarrel, raised *sophrosyne* to an ideal of life. Nature was transformed in his eyes into symbols of sweetness and light; the images of the countryside, of his native countryside, [scan 108] fresh, damp, luminous, murmuring, are those that fill his verses. With them he peoples the jealous solitude of his room; among them he places the figure of the woman loved or awaited. At times his voice rises, goes in search of distant souls, pure as his own. Or the souls he seeks live in the past, in the Greece that dazzled him, in the Spain of the mystics. Only for moments is that peace disturbed by strange presentiments: those of premature death…

So his first book, *Soledad* (1920), revealed him. On reading the second, *El reposo musical*(1923), in which those notes persisted, I thought it was time he let loose in his verses the force that lived in him, and I told him so. There was no time for the reply.

There was an occasion, however, when he came out of his retreat to sing, drawn along by his companions, the clamorous song of the youthful multitude. And he never composed a better song. In the meditative poet of musical repose there lay hidden the master of the noble popular choruses.<sup>2</sup>

IV

That tranquil spirit was a strong spirit: for that reason he joined, to the deep peace of his inner life, the frank integrity of his public life. I believe the best of his written work remains in his speeches, because they represent a part of that public life. The man of study was revealing himself in the brief pages of criticism. In them he always expressed his disdain for fashion, his devotion to the eternal norms. His themes were things of our America. In his last months he had written his first

His articles in the first number of the handsome review (*Valoraciones*, of La Plata) that he had just founded with his friends, shortly before dying, show the ease and pointed liveliness his pen was acquiring: he even wielded, in good humor, without rancor, the weapons of satire.

His speeches and his articles on university questions tell us better than any other effort of his pen what ideal guided and preoccupied him; he began by thinking of the renewal of the Argentine universities; from there he passed to the longing for a national culture, shaper of a higher fatherland. These aspirations were joined to others: on the one hand, national culture could not become a clear reality unless one thought of the lot of the *submerged* people, of man exploited by man, for whom democracy has been an incomplete redemption; on the other hand, the Argentine spirit does not live isolated in the New World: the fraternity, the moral union of our America, faith in the “great fatherland,” are necessary imperatives of every national development. Possessed by those truths, inflamed by those enthusiasms, Ripa Alberdi’s word took on lofty eloquence. “In the midst of these stirrings,” he said, referring to the university revolution, “the Argentina of the future is germinating.” And on another occasion he affirmed: “In the soul of the new Argentine generation, sympathy toward the sister nations has begun to expand…,” calling this fact a “kind of expansion of nationality.” An expansion without dreams or greed of empire! He goes so far as to offer Mexico Argentine blood for the defense of its territory… And in Lima, with noble indiscretion, facing with serene courage the hostility of a part of his audience, he preaches the sacrifice of sterile rancors on the altar of the America to come, which will see “the emancipation of the arm and of the intelligence.”

## Contemporary / Argentine Poetry

Julio Noé’s anthology<sup>1</sup> is, barely launched, an indispensable work of its kind. Anthologies of Spanish America, whole or partial, are constantly turned out; but this labor, which in France, England, or Germany is thought proper to discreet men, has with us sunk into the mire of vile trades. It takes courage, literary heroism, to raise it from there, when the decorous work must jostle and struggle in the public square with the deplorable wares of Barcelona. “The arrangement of an anthology,” believes Julio Noé, “is not among the most arduous undertakings.” Modesty, perhaps? His effort was not easy; I know it well. In America it stands beside that of Genaro Estrada in *Poetas nuevos de México* (1916).<sup>2</sup> Here, as there, the collection confines itself to the period that starts from *modernismo*, a moment of irruption and assault on romantic disorder and indolence; here,

The work invites one to draw the political map of contemporary Argentine poetry. Julio Noé’s starting point is the year 1900; before that, among the poets of the anthology, very few had verses in volume; of those few volumes, only one was important: *Las montañas del oro*, by Lugones (1897). But the official inauguration of contemporary poetry in Argentina is the publication of the *Prosas profanas*, by Rubén Darío, in Buenos Aires (1896). Darío represented then the revolutionary wing of literature in the whole Castilian language. Soon after, with Lugones, an extreme left stands out, especially since *Los crepúsculos del jardín*, whose wide circulation in reviews, from before the beginning of the new century, provokes an epidemic

Simultaneously, with *El cencerro de cristal*, by Ricardo Güiraldes, the newest extreme left was announcing itself, still arousing few suspicions; in 1925 we see it leafy and arrogant in the “vanguard reviews,” *Proa*and *Martín Fierro*. Borges believes there are too many names in the anthology; where eighty-seven are handed to me, I am not going to haggle over the quality of four or five. I do regret, with Borges, the omission of Norah Lange, a fundamental note in the vanguard’s bugle and the only active woman of the left; most Argentine women poets, faithful to the law of their sex, take shelter in the conservative wing; only Alfonsina Storni attempts intermittent audacities. With Francisco Piñeiro, deceased, an exception could have been made to the golden rule of the “published book”; was it not made with Emilia Bertolé, God keep her?

I think it inexact to attribute to him “notoriety prior to the *modernista* movement.” And his *Poemas* (1896) are essential and typical of the era of *Prosas profanas*, *Las montañas del oro*, and the *Castalia bárbara*(1897), by the Bolivian Jaimes Freyre, then resident [scan 114] then in Buenos Aires. ## Panorama of / the Other America

#### “Twenty Years of Literature in the United States”

[scan 119]

## Twenty Years of Literature

## in the United States<sup>1</sup>

I

During the twenty years that run from 1907 to 1927, literature has been transformed in the two Americas, the English and the Hispanic. The transformation is greater in the United States than in Spanish America: an abrupt and total upheaval between 1910 and 1915. Our great upheaval occurred earlier, between 1890 and 1900. Since then we have changed much; we have even adopted positions frankly contrary to those of 1900; but at a slow pace or in short leaps. There are in our environment forces capable of sudden acceleration and of creating decisive variations: the nationalist zeal, for example, whether on the Indigenous road or on the creole path; or the function of the image in style. But everything is in process.

<sup>1</sup>I shall study groups, movements, orientations; to enumerate and judge individual authors would prove tiresome to readers little familiar with them: those I mention will serve me as examples, as illustrations of moments or directions in literary life. Omissions do not necessarily imply an unfavorable opinion.

[scan 120]

As the new century opened, literature in the United States suffered stagnation. There were barely a hundred years of national work. After the timid beginnings—Cooper, Irving, Bryant—came the heroic cycle whose center was Concord: Emerson, Hawthorne, Lowell, Holmes, Thoreau, Longfellow, Prescott, and near them, in rebellious zigzags, Melville, Poe, Whitman. The North American spirit finds living expression, which Europe welcomes as a revelation laden with promise. Afterward, the channeling and diffusion of literary currents typical of the nineteenth century: realism, tempered by Puritan scruple (William Dean Howells); the psychological novel (Henry James); regionalism (Bret Harte); humor (Mark Twain).

But in 1900 the great names, the dominant names, were those that had emerged around 1870: Howells, James, Bret Harte, Mark Twain. Referring to the country, Rodó said in *Ariel*: “The wings of its books have long since failed to reach the height at which it would be universally possible to descry them.” True: apart from the four great names, literature, most abundant in quantity, unfolded like an interminable pampa without eminences. Stories and novels of prudent, minute realism, with a preference for the regional setting; humor in masses; essays that displayed culture, wit, discreet observation; abundant theater, but null in quality; poetry, academic in some, adorned with light graces of symbolism in others (as in the facile and fine Canadian Bliss Carman), but little of substance: the liveliest shoots of personality—Emily Dickinson, Stephen Crane—had vanished in premature deaths.

The best things were in the novel and the story: Mary Wilkins, or Gertrude Atherton, or Frank Norris, new then, in whom greatness was drawing near; but the public preferred pleasant books, inoffensive ones, to strong ones. And the warlike adventure of 1898 had ebbed back into an epidemic of mediocre novels [scan 121] of chivalry. The tastes of the reader, as to the plots of novels and their development, were those that the naive moviegoer has inherited.

Upon that gray multitude the impatient Gertrude Atherton, whose disorderly talent has the aroma of the great airs and the wild days of California, hurled like stones the epithets “bourgeois,” “timorous,” “small-minded.”

The stagnation came about as a fatal fact: the exploits of struggle and mastery over nature, or of invention and economic power, seduced the energetic spirits; in intellectual life culture was scarce, moral prejudices and religious taboos abounded. Upon literature weighed the national law of compulsory optimism: the country rolled along the rails of success, and skepticism, discussion, even a halt to reflect, were sins of lèse-patrie. The mediocre tone of the intellectual world stifled original impulses. The only path of salvation was rebellion. But there were no rebels.

Van Wyck Brooks has described with bitter prolixity the domestication of Mark Twain<sup>2</sup>: the poor humorist, after spending his youth among his rough compatriots of the Middle West hiding his literary inclinations as shameful, saw his work, his free expression, oppressed and trimmed according to the canons of excessive decency that he found in the cities of the Atlantic. His wife, his friend Howells, were frightened by coarse words, by popular speech, by backwoods manners, and crossed out and retouched his manuscripts. And once on the slope of intrusion, the habit of correcting grows, becomes a mania: Mark Twain’s work suffered alterations of various kinds, and lost part of its freshness. Meanwhile, that ultra-decorous environment knew how to give him, and to demand of him, neither culture nor discipline

<sup>2</sup>*The Ordeal of Mark Twain*, New York, 1919. [scan 122] of a higher order. In his last years, Mark Twain wrote the synthesis of his ideas on the universe and human society; he left the book for posthumous publication, fearful of the scandal his audacities would provoke: but those that would have been audacities for the Boston or Philadelphia of 1870 turned out to be commonplaces for the New York of 1910, hardened and seasoned on Nietzsche and Shaw.

II

What was happening precisely twenty years ago, in 1907? The mediocrity of 1900 prevailed; but it was beginning to crack: rare outbursts announced changes.

William James was deafening the world with the mechanical din of his brand-new pragmatism. His book, titled like his theory, *Pragmatism*, was published exactly in 1907. The United States seemed to be giving birth, at last, to its philosophy, the metaphysics of the practical sense, the theory of truth as function, as a useful but variable resource, judging and justifying it by its consequences in action. James abandoned the tradition of spiritualist idealism—which Josiah Royce, his venerable colleague at Harvard, still represented then—and seemed the living voice of his country. If the world was becoming Americanized in material things, here it would find the formula of Americanization for the spirit. But the world, when it recovered from its stupor, rejected pragmatism: a mere infertile variation on the familiar theme of the limitations of human knowledge. Neither by age nor by orientation did William James represent the longing of the new generations. His pragmatism is not the first philosophy of the twentieth century: it is the last of the nineteenth. Does not the dedication of the book to the shade of John Stuart Mill reveal it? The philosopher was, to be sure, an admirable psychologist and an admirable stylist, and any page of his is exciting, juicy.

[scan 123]

A colleague of William James in the teaching of philosophy at Harvard was also, by 1907, George Santayana, the Spaniard. His work up to then was chiefly that of a poet (*Sonnets and Other Verses*, 1894; *Lucifer*, 1898; *A Hermit of Carmel*, 1901) and of a philosophical treatise-writer (*The Sense of Beauty*, 1896; *The Life of Reason*, 1905). The essayist—perhaps the greatest thing in him, extraordinary in depth, in subtlety, and in humanity—had scarcely revealed himself in his *Interpretations of Poetry and Religion* (1900), which includes that much-discussed essay on the *poetry of barbarism*, exemplified in Browning and in Whitman. He had not yet written his best works, which begin in 1910 with his book on *Three Philosophical Poets* (Lucretius, Dante, Goethe). In 1912 he moved definitively to Europe, to which he had always belonged in spirit. He received part of his education in America; but—says Joseph Warren Beach—“he is in truth a product of English and Spanish culture; there is no reflection whatever in his work of the distinctive color of North American thought.” In the five excessive volumes of his *Life of Reason* there were nevertheless enclosed the germs of the philosophical conception that was to define itself as characteristic of the twentieth century in the United States (and in England): critical realism. His influence on the philosophical movement of the country he has exercised chiefly in absentia, from Europe. In literature his trace is little perceived: where he receives the most ardent suffrages is in England, among lovers of refined thought and impeccable style.<sup>3</sup>

<sup>3</sup>After the eminent Harvard trio—Royce, James, Santayana—philosophy does not recover in the United States its literary richness of expression. Neither in John Dewey nor in the coryphaei of realism are there great virtues of style. Among the exceptions: Baker Brownell, upon whom Santayana does exert an influence. Among the young of 1907—or the “still young”—no personality equaled Edith Wharton’s. She knew of the novel all that England and France could teach her; she handled style with stupendous mastery, combining the precision of steel with the brilliance of crystal; among her natural gifts she counted command of the play of motives that makes and unmakes human lives, the sense of character, incisive observation, irony, and, on occasion, the force of *pathos*. She had two or three novels, one of them a resounding success, *The House of Mirth* (1905); many short stories roundly perfect. She would later write novels of vital breath: *The Custom of the Country* (1913), *The Age of Innocence* (1920). But her fine intelligence, formed in the light of noon, did not divine the direction of the dark currents: her work suffers from excessive fidelity to the molds of her formative period (realism, psychologism) and from excessive attachment (as in George Meredith and Henry James, as in Bourget and in Proust) to the world of those favored with wealth. The youth after 1910 has been unjust to the great woman novelist, in whom it admires only the brief story of *Ethan Frome*, strong and desolate as a Russian tale.

The one who did observe the change of the times was Henry Adams. Born in 1838, but in Boston—which was equivalent, he said, to being born a hundred years behind—he was approaching seventy in 1907 and was known in letters only as a researcher in national history. He had written, in whole or in part, and only his friends knew them, his two masterpieces: *Mont-Saint-Michel and Chartres: A Study of Thirteenth-Century Unity; The Education of Henry Adams: A Study of Twentieth-Century Multiplicity*. If I were obliged to decide which is, for me, the most important book written in the United States, I should say without hesitation: *The Education of Henry Adams*. It is the book of modern life as crisis, a perpetual crisis in which each cyclone of ideas lays waste fields and cities, and there is never time to sow and build on firm ground because another cyclone is approaching. The crisis affects [scan 125] thoughts and acts alike, science and politics, art and conduct, religion and business. Henry Adams, favored by fortune and by culture, tries to educate himself for himself and for the world: according to the classic mandate, he wants to serve; but each time he thinks he is setting the course of his education, the world changes and forces him to begin anew. At seventy, with all the learning of Faust, he declares that he abandons the Tantalean struggle of his education: he leaves it unfinished…

#### III

William James, George Santayana, Edith Wharton, Henry Adams: if they represented an age of transition, and if transition must be followed by fulfillment, an age of incomparable fulfillment would have arrived, as much in the power of talent as in the breadth and firmness of vision, of discipline, of culture. But mere coincidence brings together, at their zenith, around 1907, those stars of irregular orbits.<sup>4</sup>

The new era opens around 1910, not with masterly figures but with restless, rebellious, destructive multitudes, who, if they believe in discipline, do not respect tradition, at least the short intellectual tradition of their country; who, if they seek culture, ask it to be efficacy and not luxury. Breadth and tolerance will be sacrificed, if need be, to intensity; narrowness will be preferred, if with narrowness vigor is attained.

The discussion of national life, which was kindled with difficulty amid the optimistic prosperity, became frank and boisterous when the European War forced the Americas to look within themselves. Since then it has kept growing. It has organs of its own, where every line written carries purpose and judgment: *The New Republic, The Nation, The American Mercury, The Dial*. It penetrates magazines that were conservative: *Harper’s Magazine, The North American Review*, even *The Atlantic Monthly*. And one may suppose how the socialist publications help: there are excellent ones, such as *The Liberator* (formerly called *The Masses*), *The New Masses, The Survey*. The discussion is condensed in an organic work, *Civilization in the United States* (1921), written by “thirty Americans”: the general staff of the rebel army reviews thirty fields of activity and, with few exceptions, finds fault with everything.<sup>6</sup> Everything is discussed with tremendous energy in magazines and books: from the religion and ethics of the Puritan forefathers to the artistic taste of the modern “tired businessman,” from the imperialism that plunders and offends Latin America to the mercantile tyranny that demoralizes the universities.

But these narrownesses do not hinder one who is satisfied with life because he has won comfort and luxury or because he hopes to win them. The mental nakedness in which the country’s tradition and customs leave a man makes him unable to face with discernment the unforeseen splendor of material existence. The acquiescence of the thinker or the artist would mean accommodating himself to the optimism, half naive and half cynical, of the merchant who believes the universal problems solved because he has managed to reconcile official Puritanism with his instinctive hedonism.

To the army of rebels the United States will owe its moral and intellectual salvation, unless it is defeated by the powerful army of the Philistines, which keeps in its iron strongboxes all the gold of the world. The struggle is undecided.

IV

Of the battalion of essayists, the one who most disquiets the public is Henry Louis Mencken. For the “good patriots,” the untamable nightmare, the genius of evil, the supreme corrupter. He defies, with burlesque courage, all angers, and sees to it that no one fails to hear his blasphemies. In his hatred of national absurdities he has gone so far as to write: “When the Japanese conquer the United States and the republic descends into hell…” He is master of the epigrammatic style, sparkling, crackling, seasoned with learned words and relishing popular ones; fertile in the invention of humorous hyperboles, grotesque definitions, crushing scorn. Tireless in the hunt for the Philistine, he pursues him to his tabernacles of respectability and turns into terms of opprobrium the names he is proudest of: *homo americanus*, Rotary Clubs, lodges, congresses, alfalfa universities… He has compiled, with George Jean Nathan, another agile [scan 129] essayist and critic, the dictionary of national dogmas, from common errors in astronomy or meteorology to the formulas of fearful incomprehension in politics.<sup>7</sup>

But Mencken accompanies each blow of the pickax with song and laughter. The gladness of his battering announces reconstruction: one destroys in order to replace. One of the initiators of the era of demolition and reconstruction, Randolph Bourne, aspired to found “a new fellowship in the youth of America as the principle of a great and revolutionary departure in our life, a league of youth, consciously framed with the purpose of creating, out of the blind chaos of American society, a fine, free, articulate cultural order.”

Among the reconstructors, a numerous and tireless legion. Waldo Frank wins our sympathy, because he has deeply felt the attraction of the Hispanic world and seeks in it treasures whose secret he will carry with him to enrich his native land. In his *Virgin Spain* (1926) he turns into the song of a mystic lover the fury of a prophet, warm in indignation, intense in vision, which dictated to him the book dedicated to his country: *Our America* (1919).

Discussions of national life swarm in literary criticism, turning it into social criticism. The typical representative of the tendency is Van Wyck Brooks, with his studies on Emerson, Henry James, Mark Twain. Many follow the example: so Lloyd Morris, with his severe book on Hawthorne, *The Rebellious Puritan* (1927). Against the censors rises the group of apology and defense. VI

At the beginning of the twentieth century, the curse that undid it in England for a hundred years weighed upon the drama in the United States: the theater was not, did not want to be, literature. The impresarios forbade it, under the pretext of the public’s taste: that eternally slandered unknown quantity. Amid the mass of melodramas and farces, scarcely rising, with aspirations to cleanness, were the mediocre realistic comedies of Augustus Thomas and Clyde Fitch. From Fitch an occasional scene is salvaged, such as the opening of *The Climbers*, where a family returns from the father’s funeral declaring it a social success. There followed attempts at poetic drama (Percy MacKaye) and isolated works of vigorous breath, such as *The Great Divide*, by the good poet William Vaughan Moody, with a scent of the desert in its opening scenes, or of lively wit, such as *The New York Idea*, by Langdon Mitchell (1907). Regeneration came, at last, from the little theaters, from the amateurs, ready to perform good drama and to reform the methods of the stage, especially the scenery. In 1914 the Washington Square Players arose in New York, whose founders would later scatter and organize new groups, such as the Greenwich Village Theater and the Theatre Guild. They presented short plays, such as Susan Glaspell’s *Trifles*, Alice Gerstenberg’s *Overtones*, *Helena’s Husband* by Philip Moeller, who from there made his way to greater things. Independent groups appear in various cities: the Provincetown Players in New York; Maurice Browne’s Little Theatre in Chicago; the Portmanteau Theatre, itinerant; later, in New York, the Playwrights’ Theatre, of revolutionary writers, the Civic Repertory Theatre, and so many more, in continual appearance and disappearance. University students make good theater all over the country.

[scan 136]

Greek tragedies are offered to the public, dramas of India and Japan, European farces of the Middle Ages, Irish, Russian, Austrian, German, Spanish novelties… There arise, at last, the impresario of marvels, Arthur Hopkins—whose example Winthrop Ames and Jed Harris follow—and the dramatist of color and shadow, Eugene O’Neill. O’Neill is the first complete dramatist the United States has given. He starts from realism—his realism is a bitter criticism of the modern world, but animated by deep compassion for the wretched, the oppressed, the dispossessed—and rises to poetic fantasy, always in a somber tone.<sup>13</sup> After him there is no one comparable to O’Neill, but there are good authors of dramatic literature, who try to push the purveyors of trivial successes off the boards. Among the best: Edna St. Vincent Millay, whose stage poems win clamorous triumphs; Sidney Howard, creator of strong dramatic situations; George Kelly, sharp and lively in comedy; Zoë Akins, whose irresistible *Papa* transposes elegant life into cynical paradox; Booth Tarkington, whose sweetishness as a bourgeois novelist is transformed at times, on passing to the theater, into delicate ingenuousness.

In poetry the problem of form is victoriously solved: there is a good number of poets whose expression is effective and, for their ends, perfect. What is more: during the last twenty years it is in the United States, more than in England, that English-language poetry has sought and found new forms. Since Harriet Monroe founded the magazine *Poetry*, in Chicago, in 1912, and opened a campaign in favor of all renewals, the United States has been turning, according to Enrique Díez-Canedo’s paradox, into “the country where poetry flourishes.” Hundreds of poets, thousands of readers. Every year, anthologies, panoramic collections, critical studies. There are systematic florilegia appearing annually, like Braithwaite’s. Amid so much abundance there is much dead leaf, much puerility; but little charlatanry. High purposes animate the poets. Two chief ones: one of form, refined expression and free rhythm; the other of content, the longing to give voice to the soul of the land, to the national spirit.

The renewal of forms is due, above all, to the *Imagists*, an international school, whose masters reside in Europe and in America. They are: H. D. (initials with which Hilda Doolittle signs, wife of the English poet Richard Aldington); John Gould Fletcher, poet of clear lines; Ezra Pound, active, quarrelsome, nourished on ten literatures (he is a good translator of Spanish verse); Amy Lowell, rich in imagination as in culture, equally curious to observe the forms and colors of a flower and to scrutinize the secrets of the word in Keats, to whom she devoted her last and formidable book. The group’s fondness for free verse, for variable rhythm, which from them has spread to poets of other tendencies, takes its example from French symbolism and rests on the tradition of Whitman. Their technique, *imagism*, tries to express sensations and feelings in rapid and firm images, but woven with subtle, sometimes remote elements. It attains its perfection [scan 139] in the crystalline, diamantine poems of H. D., in whom the study of ancient arts is evident, of the brief poetry of China and of the Greek *Anthology*. Close to the *Imagists* one must place T. S. Eliot, whose concentrated poetry aspires to the classical perfection of the Mediterranean.

If the *Imagists* interested and influenced literary life widely, the nationalist poets interest the country. Two contrasting groups divide attention: the one, of the Atlantic coast; the other, of the interior. The titles of their books reveal their geographical ties: *North of Boston* is the name of one by Robert Frost; *Chicago Poems*, one by Carl Sandburg. Face to face: New England, taciturn, dry, aged, with its rural towns mad with taboos, with solitude, with snow; the center of the country, the Middle West, with its sustaining pampas, with its fierce industrial cities, black with iron and coal, black with moral hardness. New York, a complex sum, expresses itself in its novelists (Waldo Frank, John Dos Passos) better than in its poets, in spite of repeated attempts. The South, always in lethargy, scarcely murmurs. And only the enormous Southwest is beginning to babble in English, with its landscapes of bare geology or gigantic forests, with its marvelous surviving Indians, with the magnetic network of roads and architecture that the dominion of Spain and Mexico left in it.

Two poets personify New England: Edwin Arlington Robinson and Robert Frost. Traditionalists in form, severe in style, they draw near to the soul of the lean grandsons of the Puritans who were the country’s hard masters and gather up the testament of the lineage in its decline. In New England, says Robinson, conscience always has the rocking-chair and joy sits knitting, huddled, shivering with cold. In Frost’s poems (he has returned to the short narrative poem in blank verse), somber figures file past: the old man who lives alone and walks through the empty house on a [scan 140] winter night, the *house he cannot fill*, the servant who comes back to die at his former masters’ house, adopting it instinctively as home, because home is the place from which we are not to be thrown out when we go to die…

Very different is the spirit of the Chicago poets: Edgar Lee Masters, Vachel Lindsay, Carl Sandburg. The funereal note sounds like an organ point in Masters’s famous work, the *Spoon River Anthology:* in each poem he tells the life of one of the inhabitants of the town of Spoon River, who sleep in the cemetery. But in Spoon River life is not decrepit as in the towns north of Boston: amid its narrownesses, it bustles with activities and hopes.

With Vachel Lindsay, poetry returns to song and dance, with popular breath. The poet writes so that his verses may be recited with fullness of rhythm, and at times chanted, or sung, or even danced. He himself shows how they should be rendered, by speaking them in public; once he traveled, reciting them before ingenuous audiences, and, in passing, wrote his *A Handy Guide for Beggars*. But he has not stopped at the easy and equivocal triumphs that novelty bestowed on *The Congo* and *The Firemen’s Ball*: he has made sweet and severe poetry, with the background of loving irony that is the essence of his soul.

And in Carl Sandburg we hear a deep voice like a torrent, a torrent of sap in the *prairie*, the pampa swollen with wheat and corn; a human torrent in the city swollen with labor. He is the poet capable of outcry: he cries out like Whitman, but knows better how to rein in the shout; his rhythm is sure (his free verse, read aloud, always convinces of its rhythmic balance); his word is just, to say or to suggest. If he errs, it is only in tonality, when he confuses planes of expression. Forced emphasis, never, though he lets out all that the throat gives, clean, resistant. And he knows all degrees of force, down to the whisper.

Outside the essential nuclei, whose innovations imply grave and resolute renunciations, there are many poets who prefer variations on familiar themes and rhythms, or who divide their time between the warm protection of the ancestral house and excursions of curious investigation. And even very good poets, like Wallace Stevens, like Ridgely Torrence, like Edna St. Vincent Millay.

But the soul of the United States, its spiritual salvation, is embodied in men like its major poets, Sandburg, Masters, Lindsay, Frost, Robinson, like its best novelists, Theodore Dreiser, Sherwood Anderson; men who refuse repose, comfortable acquiescence, and go on, with their life of faith, of effort, even of simple poverty amid so much blind prosperity, with their preaching and their art, hewing stones for the house of light.

## Closing Words

[scan 145]

The six long works that I gather here, under the title I owe to my good friend and publisher Samuel Glusberg—*Six Essays in Search of Our Expression*—, and the two Argentine notes that follow them, are united by the fundamental theme of the spirit of our America: they are investigations into our expression, in the past and in the future. Over fifteen years the theme has persisted, defining and clarifying itself: the complete exposition will be found in “Discontent and Promise.” I do not pin my faith in our genuine expression on the future alone; I believe that, however imperfect and poor we may judge our literature, in it we have engraved, unconsciously or consciously, our spiritual profiles. By studying the past, we may glimpse features of the future; we may point out orientations. For me there is one essential: in the past, our friends have been laziness and ignorance; in the future, I know that only effort and discipline will yield the work of pure expression. We men of yesterday, and in part those of the present, have an excuse: the milieu offered us only backward and fragmentary culture; our time was stolen from us by urgent undertakings, sometimes lofty, sometimes humble. And yet, until the end of the nineteenth century our best literature is the work of men occupied with *something else*: liberators, presidents of republics, educators of [scan 146] peoples, combatants of every kind. The calamity has been the idle: those romantic poets, whose only known trade was making verses, but who were incapable of putting seriousness into the work! And what was formerly seen in the Romantics, is it not seen now in their descendants, under different designations? The modern writer, when attacked for his lack of seriousness, defends himself at times with the outlandish notion that art is not to be taken seriously. If so, I do not speak with him; there is nothing to talk about. But why are magazines founded and battles fought over things that are not serious?… Art as sport? But the masters of sport, the Greeks, the English, have always held that sport is a serious matter.

As a term of comparison, I add, at the end, the literary panorama of the United States in the twentieth century: there too, as among us, the orientation of literature is a national problem, under restless, incessant discussion.

Of the nine works that make up the book—six essays, two Argentine notes, and a panorama of “the other America”—three were lectures: “Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón,” the oldest, delivered at the Librería General, in Mexico City; “Toward the New Theater,” at the Asociación de Amigos del Arte, in Buenos Aires; “Discontent and Promise,” at the Sociedad de Conferencias, of Buenos Aires, whose talks are read at the premises of the Amigos del Arte and published in the daily *La Nación*. Two works were prefaces: “Enrique González Martínez” and “The Argentine Friend.” The “Notes on Mexican Literature” that serve as a postscript to the study on González Martínez appeared in the review *México Moderno*, of the Mexican capital. The study on “Alfonso Reyes” was published in *La Nación*, of Buenos Aires; the article “Paths of Our Literary History” [scan 147] and the note on “Contemporary Argentine Poetry,” a bibliographical review of Julio Noé’s anthology, in the review *Valoraciones* of La Plata. The work on “Twenty Years of Literature in the United States” was written especially for the anniversary (twentieth) number of the review *Nosotros*, of Buenos Aires.

The lecture “Toward the New Theater” lay in embryo in the article that in 1920 I gave to the review *España*, of Madrid, on “The Renewal of the Theater”; but that embryo constitutes less than half of the present work. By contrast, the study on “Don Juan Ruiz de Alarcón” is reprinted much reduced: the ample introduction on the national spirit in literature disappears, along with an occasional later paragraph and the extensive notes. All that served its ends in the first two editions (Mexico, 1913, and Havana, 1915) of the lecture, when my thesis—the Mexicanness of Alarcón—was new and required defensive armament. Afterward the thesis has enjoyed good fortune: frequently commented upon in all the countries where the history of Spanish-language literature is of interest, it circulates in reviews and manuals; and Alfonso Reyes, in his prefaces to the editions of Alarcón in the *Clásicos castellanos*, of *La Lectura*, and in the *Páginas escogidas* of the Calleja series, has reconstructed the figure of the dramatist in a new spirit, adding to the reconstruction all the material of data and documents. My purpose having been fulfilled with unexpected success, the work could be lightened and reduced to its essence.

The book goes in search of the fervent spirits who are concerned with the spiritual problem of our America, who suffer the longing for our pure and full expression. If it manages to interest them, I shall believe that it will not be wholly useless.

La Plata, August 1927
