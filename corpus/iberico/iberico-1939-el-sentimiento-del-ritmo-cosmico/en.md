## SEGUNDA PARTE — PART TWO

## EL SENTIMIENTO DEL RITMO CÓSMICO — THE FEELING OF COSMIC RHYTHM

What is rhythm? It is generally defined as a perceptible division of time, subject in each case to a given mathematical law. This conception encompasses the following definitions:

"Rhythm is a determinate ordering of durations." (Aristoxenus of Tarentum).

"Rhythm taken in its most general sense is the division of time, by means of phenomena perceptible to the human senses, into periods whose total durations are equal to one another and which repeat themselves in accordance with a simple law." (E. D'Eichthal).

"Rhythm is to time what symmetry is to space." (The same author).

"Rhythm is the property of a series of events in time which produces upon the mind of the observer the impression of a proportion between the durations of the various events or groups of events composing the series." (Sonnenschein).

"A series of phenomena occurring at intervals of duration, whether variable or not, but governed by a law, constitutes a rhythm." (Francis Warrain).

"Rhythm is perceived periodicity." (P. Servien).

"Rhythm consists of a measured impulse of the soul, answering to a number always the same that obsesses and sweeps us along. It is a kind of poetic dance that implies connection to a certain numerical composition, at least approximately." (P. Claudel).

According to Lipps, who approaches the question primarily from the standpoint of poetry, rhythm is the periodic ordering of groups of elements under the dominance of similar units.

These definitions, however, suffer in our view from three deficiencies: 1st, By contemplating the phenomenon of rhythm from an exclusively aesthetic or artistic standpoint, they bring out its purely formal qualities and neglect the characters we would call material, which are precisely those that concern the purpose of this essay; 2nd, All these definitions translate an excessively subjectivist conception of rhythm, which seems to exist only insofar as it is consciously perceived, experienced, and measured — which is inaccurate; and 3rd, They confuse rhythm with mere chronometric regularity, with simple meter.

In contrast to this formalist and subjectivist conception of rhythm we find that of Ludwig Klages, who establishes at once the cosmic, universal, and objective character of rhythm and what we might call its vital immanence — something that, beyond reflective consciousness, presents itself as the deepest and most mysterious structure of life.

We shall briefly summarize Klages's ideas on the essence of rhythm, both because we believe them true and profound and because we judge that knowing them will serve as a useful introduction to the central theme of this work.

Above all, rhythm is a vital phenomenon. Rhythm does not occur in the mere mechanical repetition of a metronome, nor in the monotonous mathematical periodicity of engines. Rhythm occurs, on the other hand, in dance, in verse, and in the thousand forms in which the innumerable appearances of nature alternate, come and go, are born, flourish, and perish, only to be born again, flourish, and perish once more.

Having affirmed this vital character of rhythm, we see with perfect clarity that rhythm is not a mere periodicity mathematically measurable and divisible according to any law whatsoever, but a periodicity whose frequency marks the ebb and flow of the infinite ocean of appearances and that scans, as in a poetic recitation, the universal confidence. And here we see how this character, while serving to establish the metaphysical pre-eminence of rhythm, also serves to differentiate it from other, lower forms of periodicity with which it is commonly confused or under which it is erroneously subsumed. And so Klages writes: "Rhythm is a general appearance of life, in which man participates as the living being he is; meter, on the other hand, is a work of man; rhythm can in its most perfect forms appear in the total absence of meter, whereas meter cannot appear without the cooperation of rhythm."

In determining the essence of rhythm more precisely, Klages formulates the following proposition: Rhythm is articulated continuity. He illustrates it with a symbolic example, describing the back-and-forth of waves — a rhythmic alternation in which the descending wave literally prolongs the movement of the ascending wave, itself then prolonged in the ascending curve of the next wave, and so on to infinity, the undulating line the waves trace never breaking, the continuity of the liquid medium in which they move never lost.

But it is not enough to say that rhythm is articulated continuity in order to capture its mysterious essence. It is necessary, further, to complete this statement with two other notes implicit in the concept of life but not immediately given in the mere periodicity of the continuous: incessant renewal and polarity.

"Meter reproduces; rhythm renews," writes Klages. And he adds: "No wave reproduces the one before it, no shrub the parent tree, no young animal its mother, no leaf of a tree another leaf, no strand of hair another strand. The current of life causes the new to spring from the new; but the various shoots of its innumerable branches resemble one another."

We might say something equivalent with respect to music and poetry. Indeed, in melody as in verse, no two sounds are ever identical, no two pauses chronometrically equal, no two accents of the same intensity. In this way, in nature and in art, something always returns — but that something which returns is new! And here lies the true, deep mystery of rhythm: it is return, but not the return of the "equal" and the "same" — for neither in nature nor in art do those categories exist — but the inexpressible reappearance of the similar, the kindred, something like the magical secret of the eternal youth of life.

And finally, rhythm is polarized continuity — that is to say, an alternation of opposed terms that, precisely as they stand opposed, require and complete each other. Every verse, every truly artistic prose, is an ebb and flow, a swaying held in terms of harmony and grace. And all music is a periodic passing from silence (vibrant) to sound, from sweetness to force, from affirmation to negation — a beating, a breathing, a something that gives and withdraws like the waves of the ocean.

In like manner, the life of the cosmos — which is rhythmic life — presents itself to us, essentially, as the opposition of night and day, darkness and light, summer and winter, death and birth, and so forth: polar extremes, states, phases that succeed one another, also like waves upon the ocean, without the vital continuity in which they alternate ever breaking or being lost.

Rhythm is not only temporal; it is also spatial. We have all followed with our eyes the rhythm of sand dunes, in which the back-and-forth of the sea seems to grow still, the rhythm of a decorative line, the rhythm of a statue. Klages explains the existence of spatial rhythm through the deep kinship between time and space, through the indissoluble polar relation between these two forms of the reality of the world. Space is the sphere of expression of time; time is the soul of space — so much so that everything that happens in time also happens in a certain space, and conversely, whatever occurs in space also occurs in a certain time. By a natural inclination of the soul we give sounds the spatial qualifications of high and low, and in speaking of melody or literary style, which belong to the order of time, we refer to the melodic line or the line of style. And conversely, in every distance, every line, every spatial form, we see the expression or configuration of a movement that, as such, belongs to time. "Space and time are in the appearance of reality two inseparably united poles, such that in the auditory image the spatial pole depends on the temporal pole, while, inversely, in the visible image the latter depends on the former. Consequently no temporal articulation occurs without an articulation in space, just as no spatial series occurs without its correspondence in the order of time, and the essential observations can indifferently be discussed in relation to the forms of space or to the current of time — rhythmic articulation in space together with rhythmic articulation in time contains within itself the rhythmic change of state that we find in every natural symmetry." This explains the admirable correlations in the life of the world between the temporal successions of its states and the spatial disposition of its elements and essences, and gives us the key to understanding the emotion of nature in both the ancient philosophers and the Romantics, who identified the feeling of cosmic structure with the feeling of its rhythmics.

In sum, rhythm is at once the deepest breath and the most authentic expression of life in its splendor and mystery. Continuity, it is also in a certain way interruption, punctuation; renewal, it is also return, cycle, in which eternal creation recreates itself and, through incessant death, like a blossoming spring, is reborn. Contrary and antagonistic to the mechanical regularity of machines, it is nevertheless periodicity, and consequently number. Polarity, it is yet a profound vital unity, in which contraries alternate and accord, and in which literally, as Heraclitus says, harmony results from the composition of opposing tensions, like the bow and the lyre.

We propose to contemplate certain cosmic rhythms and to show the correlations in which the organic unity of the world is manifested. We wish likewise to study their repercussion on the human soul — or more precisely, the emotional participation of the human being in the universal rhythm of life. A participation that, as is readily understood, implies a feeling of vital continuity, but one that can be combined — and in fact is combined — with intellectually based emotions when rhythm is felt as symmetry, harmony, proportion in the dynamics or in the structure of the world.

The rhythms whose experience is given to us most immediately are the heartbeat and breathing. Of the former it has been said that they are the "inner metronome" in poetic measure, the pump of our life, the fundamental iamb — one weak beat and one strong; of breathing, that it is "the rhythmic function par excellence, with tension, relief, and repose; the reflex and accompaniment of the affective waves, whose sonorous expression are prosodic or musical rhythms."

Setting aside what we might call the aesthetic significance of the heartbeat and the respiratory wave, it is possible to affirm that through them, in the individual organism, manifest themselves — God knows what — mysterious and yet deeper pulsations of universal becoming; and in any case, that both rhythms are expressions of that law so well known to the old philosophers of nature, which explains the entire cosmic process through the eternal alternating succession of expansion and contraction, of union and separation.

Every living pulsation is rhythmic, and every rhythm is a rhythm of the cosmos. That is why we have mentioned the physiological rhythms of the heart and of breathing, despite the apparently narrow sphere in which they occur. Let us now study other, vaster rhythms in which the life of creation renews itself in an integral and visible manner.

The alternation of night and day constitutes one of the most mysterious, admirable, and fundamental rhythmic changes in cosmic life, because the passage from night to day and from day to night is not a simple astronomical phenomenon but something far deeper — an integral vital phenomenon that envelops in its ebb and flow the secret pulsation of all potencies and seeds, that implies, with its innumerable influences on body and soul, the passage from sleep to wakefulness and from wakefulness back to sleep — which is ultimately the symbol and eternal expression of metaphysical polarity and, as Jaspers would say, of the existential polarity of the soul.

In mythic conceptions — where, as is well known, the feelings of vital continuity with nature are translated and expressed — night appears as something primordial, prior: a maternal and fecund principle from whose womb life and light spring forth together. And according to these same conceptions, a mysterious kinship exists between night, earth, and vegetation. All three symbolize the same dark germinal secret of life and at the same time that tropism — that as-it-were unconscious aspiration of all living things to rise toward the luminous and ethereal.

But there is more: night is sleep and the world of visions that people the space of the sleeping man. Through sleep, animals and humans reintegrate into the zone of vegetative existence, and it can likewise be said that they breathe in time with the universal breath. And in the visions that people the space of the sleeping man, the mystery of the participation of the archaic, pre-logical soul is revealed — the soul that lives in a kind of undifferentiated communion with all beings. Night is thus the eternal "before," the humus that nourishes from its remote and pulsating depth the sap of life.

All of which might be summed up, perhaps, by saying that night is the unconscious — provided that word is given the deep and cosmic meaning the German Romantics attached to it. For them the unconscious was neither a mere sum of forgotten or repressed contents of consciousness (Freud), nor a latent consciousness (Leibniz), nor simply a dark and dangerous region, but the primitive, pre-individual ground of psychic life — or better, as Albert Béguin puts it, interpreting the philosophers of Romanticism: "the point of insertion of the human being into the vast process of nature," with the consequent harmony between the soul and the system of astral and telluric rhythms that are integrated in the cosmic process.

"The unconscious is, according to Carl-Gustav Carus — in whom the psychological conception of Romanticism is organized and refined — the subjective expression designating what we objectively know under the name of nature." This simultaneously deepens and clarifies what we might call the Romantic sense of night: not simply a state of the cosmos nor a state of the soul, but a mode of being that is at once permanent and alternating — the present and unacknowledged ground of activity and consciousness, and, in its meaning both direct and symbolic, of light.

Darkness gives way to light; night to day. Light and day are consciousness and wakefulness. Light is, above all, a principle of individuation. Thanks to it, things are distinguished from one another and take their place as separate, impenetrable objects in cosmic space. But at the same time, light is a principle of order: by provoking the appearance of the abstract, it also awakens the conscious perception of the similar, offering to the gaze and to action alike, along with a system of geometric relations, a mental system of logical conditions, of which the former are the visual and mathematical representation.

But let us return to mythic wisdom, which, as we have said, reflects and symbolizes what we call feelings of vital continuity with nature. For the ancients, the sun and day do not succeed night by virtue of a simple astronomical mechanism; rather the sun — and with it day, as light, brightness, splendor — is the child of night. Its child, but also its conqueror and adversary: for while night represents the maternal, dark principle, day represents the paternal principle, the principle of ordering and distinction, both in the space of nature and in the sphere of the soul. In this way the ancient soul, affirming the fundamental kinship of opposites, fulfills the rhythmic law of polarity and alternation, and lives in the very splendor of day the nostalgia for the nocturnal, maternal shadow.

And as one readily understands, the sun — and therefore the day it engenders — never ceases to be the child of night, destined to reintegrate into the maternal abyss and be reborn each dawn like a child. Thus the ancients — Greeks, Egyptians, Peruvians, Aztecs — and with them the German Romantics, in whom so much of the archaic feeling for nature was reborn, saw in this alternation of darkness and light, of night and day, a kind of biological and metaphysical pre-eminence of the night. So Novalis says: "a limited duration was granted to the day, but the dominion of night knows neither duration nor space." And so finally Klages, in whom the cosmic feeling of the ancients and Romantics is prolonged and renewed, writes with admirable poetry and beauty: "Night has its blaze of stars, and every sun is no more than a star in the night."

Crossing the solar rhythms are the rhythms of the lunar revolutions, which offer to contemplation two categories of modifications: one relating to changes in the form of the illuminated region of the body, and others, correlative, which are modifications of its position in the firmament. Among all peoples, profound influence has been attributed to these modifications or phases upon the life of living beings — principally in plants, in the menstrual cycles of women, and in the excitation or depression of nervous and mental patients. Folklore is full of stories, beliefs, and prescriptions of this kind, all of which are pervaded equally by a feeling of vital continuity and by analogical or symbolic thinking — essentially rhythmic thinking — that relates the destiny of beings and their role in the cosmos both to the rhythmics of their movement and to what we might call their physiognomy or expression. Thus the boat-like appearance of the crescent moon leads the primitive mind to regard it as the ship that carries the souls of those about to be born and bears away the souls of the dead; the moon is also compared, by its form and by the action attributed to its various phases, to the sickle, the cradle, the sieve; and poetically the Egyptians saw in it the gondola of Isis. It should be noted that these analogies — or metaphors, as a modern mind would call them — always refer to the connection of the moon with the ebb and flow of life and death, linking its physiognomy to the dynamics of its activity in space and in the cosmos.

"The vital rhythms of living beings are without doubt different from one another and moreover occur according to genera and species; this does not mean, however, that they present no conformity — one due especially to the connection that exists between the proper rhythm of each living being and the rhythm of the surrounding cosmos. Not only does the change from wakefulness to sleep, experienced by humans and many animals, occur in harmony with the change of day and night, but the vital melody of the world of plants likewise follows that rhythm. And not only do plants accompany with their growth, flowering, and fruiting the turning of the seasons, but humans do the same, with periodic cycles of growth in height and breadth, with an annual rhythm in the duration and depth of sleep, with phases in states of mood that, according to the testimony of large-scale statistics, show the same annual curves for all peoples with respect, for example, to crimes against morality and to suicides. The molting of reptiles and amphibians; the change of plumage in birds, the change of coat in mammals, the long flights of migratory birds; the so-called winter sleep of many species, mating seasons, periods of torpor and germination; migrations of salmon, eels, herring; swarming seasons of dragonflies and butterflies — all these transformations and others, and still innumerable more, we see fitted and incorporated into the rhythm of the cosmos."

As this brilliant page of Klages expresses in synthesis, all the alternating changes of living beings are included and incorporated in the rhythm of the cosmos. But it is undoubtedly in the rhythmic change of the seasons that one can experience a collective sensation more intense, comprehensive, and profound of cosmic rhythmics — for in the succession of the seasons we observe, along with the changing climate and the variation of landscape, correlative and synchronous modifications in the vital process of all animals and plants. In the changing of the seasons, it is no doubt the plants that present the most easily observable modifications, but all the life of nature renews itself as they do. Insects follow their cycle in the process of their transformations, changing from the larval and subterranean life of winter to the ardent, luminous, winged life of summer; migratory birds leave the countries that winter is beginning to claim in search of warmer ones rich in insects; all life, finally, like a sea, ebbs and flows — following, one would say, the oscillations of the sun in space. And thus the rhythmics of the seasons gives us, as no other rhythmics of the cosmos does, the symphonic impression of universal life.

Winter is the cold season in which nature seems to withdraw into itself, and in which, beneath a blanket of snow, life pursues its germinal, subterranean work. Spring is the symbolic season of hope, in which the green of the fields returns as an expression of vegetation's unconscious longing for the light. Summer is the season of vital fullness, in which all creation seems to vibrate in a frenzy, and in which at the same time something ecstatic, something panic seems to infuse a mysterious stillness into every animate form. Autumn is the season of nostalgia, in which, even as the leaves scatter, the fruits ripen to fall to the earth and, reintegrating into its fertile womb, die. To die in order to be reborn in a new spring. Thus the succession of the seasons is a circle of birth, flowering, fruiting, and death, recommencing endlessly. A cycle that the pagan religions celebrated with mysterious rites, symbolizing in the great figure of an agrarian god, dead and risen (Dionysus, Adonis), the wintry death and the springtime rebirth of vegetation.

We do not claim to describe all the wondrous correlations, all the rhythmic correspondences that fill us with wonder in the vast symphony of the cosmos. We shall mention only, among those innumerable correspondences, the migrations of animals — movements in which the organic, rhythmic unity of the vital process of the world is revealed in an evident and visible manner. Fabre has described the migrations of many insects and birds; Roule and others, the migrations of fish; in pages full of poetry Chateaubriand has described the migrations of the bison across the savannas of Louisiana and New Mexico. What interests us to retain from these descriptions and from the abundant folklore relating to the journeys of living beings is that always and at all times migrations have represented for humankind sure signs, announcements, harbingers of integral changes in nature. "Pay heed," says Hesiod, "when you hear the voice of the crane launching from the heights of the clouds its annual cry. It brings the signal for sowing and announces the coming of the rainy winter." And this is undoubtedly because migrations do not obey the voluntary decisions of animals but are merely the first phase of a collective movement that, as it brings the earth toward the winter solstice or the spring equinox, stirs that living tide of birds or fish that flows southward in the "rainy winter" and returns announcing the joyful arrival of the spring season.

"The year," writes Otto J. Hartmann with justice, "is not an abstract period of time but a great organism that incorporates within itself all the domains of nature and human life." The seasons mark the vital rhythm of that organism. Let us see how through them the human being participates in that rhythm. Winter is the season of clear, cold thoughts, like snowflakes. Spring is the season of desire, with its natural psychological accompaniments: hope and illusion. Summer is the Dionysian and erotic season, in which, as in a dream, as in a siesta, the imagination and the senses are at once lulled and exalted. Autumn, finally, is the season of ripening, of fruiting, in which, under a melancholic light, both in nature and in the soul, the possibilities of life attain their plastic form and supreme savor.

"In winter," says Gracián, "plants take root; in spring they flower; in summer they fruit; and in autumn they ripen and are perfected." Words that can be applied equally to the vegetative life of nature and of the soul.

Many ancient peoples (Hindus, Chaldeans, Aztecs) believed in the existence of vaster cycles encompassing the whole life of the cosmos, with an alternation of birth and death, of creation and total annihilation. For the Brahmans this cycle takes the form of an alternation of Manvantara and Pralaya — that is, of a period of life lasting a prodigious number of terrestrial years and a period of dissolution in which all that exists will be reabsorbed by the supreme soul. "It is thus that through alternating waking and repose," say the laws of Manu, "the immutable being causes this whole assembly of mobile and immobile creatures to revive eternally." The Chaldeans had the Great Year — an immense period of time at whose end creation would be annihilated, only to be reborn in a rhythm without end. According to the Aztecs, the existence of the world had a beginning, will have an end, and is divided into periods at the close of each of which the world is destroyed alternately by the four elements and by hunger, in this order: water (flood) after four thousand years of existence; air (wind) after another four thousand years; fire (universal conflagration) after a somewhat longer period; then by hunger five thousand years later; the current period to end through the action of earthquakes.

Under Eastern influence, and obeying above all the cyclic tendency that characterizes the cosmic feeling of the Greeks, Empedocles believes in a universal rhythm of love and strife that will cause the world to pass successively from harmonic unity to destructive dispersal, returning, through the dominion of love, to the original state of harmony and immobility. In the nineteenth century, Nietzsche — influenced perhaps by Brahmanist thought, perhaps also by the Greeks and by certain hypotheses of modern science, but led principally by his own feeling for life and destiny — affirmed with an exaltation at once tragic and jubilant the eternal return. "How could I not be drunk with love of eternity?" exclaims Zarathustra. "How could I not desire the ring of rings, the wedding ring of eternal return? Until now I have found no woman from whom I would want children, save this woman whom I love: for I love you, O Eternity." As we said, the cycle of cosmic transformations in Empedocles obeys a certain feeling of nature, which is not the case in Nietzsche, whose eternal return is nothing but the subjective, lyrical expression of a solitary soul.

The primitive soul is a rhythmic soul, not only because its natural life runs according to the great alternations of cosmic becoming, but because all the forms we might call germinal of culture — those that begin their development in the humus of the archaic soul — are determined by a fundamental rhythmic tendency. Myth reflects in its images the ebb and flow of the cosmos, with its central themes of night and day, death and resurrection. Art, as is well known, is rhythm — not only in its lyrical manifestations: poetry, music, but in its plastic ones: dance, sculpture, painting, architecture. And finally, manual labor — as K. Bücher has shown so completely — is, in both its individual and collective manifestations, essentially rhythmic, and is intimately connected with music: not only through the songs that accompany it but through the varied rhythms that mark its cadence. By this means primitive labor, as a human repercussion of cosmic life, presents itself to us, like poetry and music, with its various sounds and noises, as a voice of nature in unison with the accents of man and occurring in time with his movements and exertions. "Each season," says Bücher, "has its distinctive work-sound; each kind of work its characteristic music. At the end of autumn the flax paddle sings its merry song in our villages; in winter one hears the crack of the flail in the barn, the dry, muffled sound of the fodder-chopper coming from the stable next door; in spring one hears the sound of paddles washing clothes in the brook; in summer, the whetting of scythe and sickle in every house."

The soul of the great cultures has also been rhythmic, because they knew how to preserve the fund of morning freshness in life and because their cosmologies did nothing but stylize — or rather prolong — the great lines of mythic conceptions. By this means those great cultures gave to humanity, alongside a concrete image of the cosmos, an emotion through which they participated in the solemn rhythm of its life. Without this, on the other hand, preventing those great cultures from attaining their highest elevations, their most rarefied and refined abstractions, their most subtle and most immaterial constructions — for all those magnificent objectivations of their mentality obeyed no simple constructive or destructive caprice, but rose like the crowning achievement of the integral edifice of life, or better like the rare and perfect fruit of their vigorous and fecund tree.

The ancients possessed a closed, astrobiological cosmology, at once plastic and musical. A conception of such a nature that it allowed them to live in one undivided feeling the symmetry of the cosmic elements in space and the melody of their transformation in time.

The ancients — Asians, Greeks, Aztecs, Peruvians — had an astro-biological and plastic cosmic conception. The cosmos was for them an organism whose elements and members were perfectly distributed among regions of space, whose situation was not considered relative but absolute. On the other hand, between the positions of the stars — not only the nearby ones: the sun, moon, Venus, but the distant ones: the polar star, Sirius — and the rhythm of animal, vegetable, and human life, there existed an intimate and fundamental bond. In this way the ancient soul had, at any moment, a vivid feeling of the form of the cosmos, and could likewise read at any moment in the signs of the sky the revelation of the biological — or rather psychic — state of the world. Within this conception, the aspect of the celestial vault was at once an influence and a kind of physiognomic expression of the cosmos.

To this must be added, if we are to understand what we might call the psychology of that conception, that the ancients — principally the Greeks — perceived the world as a harmony. A harmony from which neither the contrast nor the drama of life was excluded, but in which both were, in a certain way, dominated and absorbed. By a marvelous feeling of the correlation of space and time — between the distribution of elements and the order of their successive transformations — the ancients perceived in each moment of cosmic life the totality of the world as a chord.

And that harmony in simultaneity was at bottom the derivation of a melodic feeling of existence. Time was not a mere indifferent medium, but the principle and law of changes — something like the soul or the secret pulsation of life. Something that presided jointly over the motionless splendor of the starry night, the alternation of night and day, and the cyclic march of the seasons.

The medieval soul likewise possessed a vivid and productive feeling of cosmic rhythm. As is well known, its cosmology was in its broad outlines the same as that of the ancients — so well suited to the perception, admiration, and poetic emotion of rhythm. And in addition, the childlike, morning soul of the Germanic peoples exerted a powerful influence on the medieval feeling for nature.

The Renaissance marks what we might call the crisis of the soul. During this epoch three principal movements arise with respect to the interpretation and feeling of nature: a tendency toward plastic configuration, whose most illustrious representatives are the great Italian masters; a tendency that in general terms we might call mystical, expressed in certain great conceptions concerning nature, the soul, and God (Giordano Bruno, Nicholas of Cusa, and Böhme). And finally a distinctly scientific movement, whose principal representative is Galileo, which on one hand tends to abolish sensible reality (the doctrine of the subjectivity of sensible qualities), and on the other, to interpret all objective relations in quantitative and mechanical terms — thereby substituting for the varied and living world of the ancients and the Middle Ages a uniform and dead world.

Of these three movements, the first two are compatible with a vivid feeling of cosmic rhythm and even imply it. The last, on the other hand, is contrary to it, because, as we know, rhythm is life, and the mechanical conception of nature is by essence antivital. The tendency inaugurated by Galileo has prevailed inarguably in Western culture; and thus, apart from the local and fugitive parenthesis of German Romanticism, from the seventeenth century onward the human spirit, in its relation to the cosmos, has erected mechanism, quantitative reduction, geometric and abstract figuration not only as the criterion and goal of scientific explanation but as the norm and discipline of practice — thereby enthroning at the eminent place in the hierarchy of values the form of existence most adverse to rhythm. It is worth noting that this mechanization of life, this reduction of quality to quantity, is not limited to men of science but is spread among the masses through industrial civilization and the reign of machinery — which are the economic and technical consequences of Galileo's conception.

The recent theories of physics, and especially the tendency toward a geometric interpretation of the universe represented by the theory of general relativity, judged from the standpoint of their psychological repercussion, signify in reality nothing other than the prolongation of the movement inaugurated by Galileo, directed toward an extremely abstract and purely mathematical and relational conception of the universe. Hypotheses that, whatever their theoretical value, imply an emotional resonance antagonistic to the true feeling of rhythm.

Modern science describes and studies numerous cyclical phenomena: periodic movements of the planets, vibrating plates, electromagnetic oscillations, and so forth. It should be noted, however, that the majority of the cyclical phenomena studied by science — principally by physics — are in reality incompatible with rhythmic return, for two principal reasons: 1st, because the mathematical regularity of their periodicity is opposed to that living freedom of the rhythmic process; and 2nd, because if one accepts the hypothesis of the energetic degradation of the universe, one would have to conclude that no truly cyclical phenomena exist, and that in the physical and biological world we are witnessing a growing retardation in the cadence of cosmic oscillations.

But it is not the theories themselves that principally contribute to the abolition of rhythmic feeling and of respect for the deep laws of vital maturation — it is their applications in the field of industrial production, of education, and, taken as a whole, of individual and social behavior. In the field of industrial production, the application of mechanical theories has suppressed the direct work of the old artisan — which was in essence a work of art and as such an expression of cosmic life — and replaced it with mass production, whose monotonous uniformity suppresses rhythm. In education, an itch for acceleration and performance, similar to the industrial and factory itch, attempts to burn the stages of children's and adolescents' development, in ignorance of the intervals proper to each age and to the detriment of the normal evolution of life. Finally, in the widest circle of individual and social activity, scientific and technical presumption has generated an excessive programmatic frenzy, whose most typical expression is the famous five-year plans — inspiring action with considerations of mere immediate productivity, which at the same time breaks the connection between humanity and natural rhythms, and ends by killing the truly creative spontaneity of real time, of concrete and living duration.

Neither in the city nor in the country do the noble and joyful sounds of ancient labor still vibrate; nor are the voices of landscape and soul still heard: street cries, songs, bells, sheep bells. Only the din of machines spreads, or there extends a silence that is not one of recollection or meditation but of fear or death. And thus, without intending to, we think of the prophets of the Old Testament who, when they sought to describe with force the decadence of a city, made the voice of the millstone and the song of the grape-treaders fall silent within it.

## CONCLUSIÓN — CONCLUSION

It would be false to say that the intention of this work is to advocate what is usually called "the return to nature" — the abolition of culture and history, the reintegration of humanity into the most primitive and archaic stage of evolution. We do not, of course, believe such reintegration to be possible, for life is irrevocable, and though the past is preserved in the depths of time, it does not return, because it is past. And moreover, even supposing such reintegration were possible, we would not deem it desirable, for we love culture and know that our own feeling of nature is nourished by a certain historical and cultural atmosphere. Nor do we form, like Rousseau and the Romantics of his school, an idyllic image of the state of nature. We therefore do not propose the return to a lost paradise, which is the work of poetry and nostalgic transfiguration. And finally, as is readily understood, neither do we advocate a simple tropical abandonment that would suppress initiative and cut off the creative impetus of the will.

What is at stake is simply the reviving of cosmic feeling, of the emotion of universal life — and, within the limits indicated by the primarily psychological character of this study, the idea that the cosmos is a life and not a machine, a symphonic unity and not a mere aggregate of elements; and finally, to use the magical word of Jalal al-Din, a soul.

And with this we might consider the present essay concluded. However — given the connection of its subject matter with the problems of the spirit and of culture — we believe it indispensable to add a few words briefly defining our position in regard to both these important questions.

We do not, of course, suppose that the soul considered as splendor and rhythm, as authentic and visible life, is opposed to the spirit understood as a world of intemporal and absolute essences and values. For the spiritual values that are the supreme forms of activity and emotion are, without doubt, above life — but they do not negate it; on the contrary, they promote and elevate it.

We judge, on the other hand, that a profound enmity exists between the soul and the mechanizing intellectualism that determines, on the one hand, a science lacking all criterion for understanding what is organic, original, and creative in life, and on the other, a philosophy of practice, purely economic in nature — one that either proclaims as its supreme ideal immediate utility and pleasure, or consecrates and pursues large-scale production, yield, and acceleration as the most excellent and desirable things in existence.

We therefore believe it necessary to oppose to this philosophy — which regards humanity as a mere exploiter of an inert world — a conception that, while consecrating the pre-eminence of the spirit, also gathers the message of poetry and magic that nature sends us: a philosophy in which the human being appears as the metaphysical mediator destined to make life into spirit and to embody spirit in life; a conception, in short, that promotes, together with respect for the intentions and vital rhythms of the cosmos, the true spiritual creation that is obtained not as the result of a technique but as a gift — at once merited and gratuitous — received through the soul's fidelity to the universal principle of animation and appearance.

And it seems to us that this philosophy has its foundation not only in superior subjective demands but in the most profound cultural and historical reality. Indeed, artists, saints, and heroes — who are noble and complete expressions of culture — have in every age accomplished the mysterious mediation between spirit and life, or if we prefer, between spirit and soul. A mediation in which life transcends itself and in which the spirit sets the seal of the enduring and perfect upon the fleeting appearances of life.

But if great creations in all times and places can be cited as illustrations of this principle, few, in our judgment, are as significant and exemplary as these three admirable realizations — at once of spirit and of soul — that we take from medieval civilization:

The cathedrals were life — popular life and cosmic life — promoted, elevated to the highest spirituality. As is well known, the cathedral was essentially a collective work, a work of the popular soul: a magnificent plant, deeply rooted in the soil, growing slowly toward light and space. "The cathedral," says Elie Faure, "vibrated with the noise of the workshops and the forges. The villagers sowed their wheat there, harvested their sheaves, pressed their grapes and their apples. The horses, the donkeys, the oxen traced their furrows there, drew their carts, and the rams and goats were not surprised to find, around a pillar, an elephant, a rhinoceros, a hippopotamus, or a wise king on his camel. A murmuring, confused life, full of birdsong, the sound of fountains, and the rustling of creatures beneath the moss, awoke or slumbered there." In the end it was a vast germination of forms unified by the deep solidarity of the same sap.

Saint Francis of Assisi, by contrast, is a manifestation of spirit in life. The poor man of Assisi knew and realized the spiritual values of love, selflessness, and dedication of self to an ideal. But he realized them with enthusiasm and joy. And above all, he placed at the service of his spiritual ideal an enchanted and poetic soul — the soul of a child — thus presenting himself to us as the palpable proof of the mysterious alliance between the spirit and the freshest, most spontaneous, and most innocent forces of life.

The chivalric ideal also constituted a realization of spirit in life. Chivalry was, of course, vital impulse, poetry. But it was at the same time submission to the heroic duties of existence — duties whose fulfillment did not exclude but on the contrary presupposed the biological and psychic bond of the human being with the cosmic, germinal foundation of tradition and the earth.

And here is a concept of culture that we believe it legitimate to deduce from historical observation, especially as it relates to the great cultural circles of the Classical Age and the Middle Ages: Culture is the sphere of mediation between spirit and life. That is why every authentic expression of culture — and in an eminent way, the genius of the individual — is symbolic, or what amounts to the same, a vehicle and configuration of meaning. That is why meaning, the spiritual ideal of existence, is not a mere concept, a mere conception of the mind, but an archetype: a complex of vision and emotion in which the temporal longing of the soul is realized in intemporal and eminent form. And that is why, finally, when cosmic life and ideal are separated, the symbolic feeling of culture vanishes at once, and what remains falls like the scattered debris of a fire — on one side, a material, mechanical, and inert world; on the other, the mere structure of abstract forms that is still given — even now — the name of spirit.

Thus spirit and life, spirit and soul — or to use the language of Frobenius, the ideal and the genius — can be allied. What is difficult, and perhaps impossible, is to abolish all feeling of participation in cosmic life, to reduce the world to a mere mechanical composition of forces, and to serve the spirit effectively. Every work of life is born of life, and in the end the spirit is the flower and the distant sun of life.

And thus, if humanity is to emerge from the present chaos toward a new horizon of harmony, it will not be through a progressive mechanization of life but through a new mediation — a new rebirth of the soul that appears with all the freshness of the gratuitous and new, and all the mysterious majesty of the eternal and profound.

The cosmos is a work of God. But it is a living work. Let us not, then, follow the separate path of those who, interpreting creation in terms of inertia and death, turn away at once from God and from life.
