There is an idea that, in a certain sense, guides and orients the speculation of the seventeenth-century philosophers, at least the most representative ones — Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz — and that is the idea of "clarity," which holds not only a gnoseological importance, since the clarity and distinctness of knowledge serve as a guarantee of its truth, but also a significance that we might call axiological: in the hierarchy of actions and feelings, those whose reasons and foundations are perceived with the greatest clarity are considered the most worthy and elevated — those pleasures that Leibniz called, in an expression at once technical and poetic, "luminous pleasures." And more still: this idea of clarity, with the genuine spiritual cult devoted to it, implied at its core an entire metaphysics, since its admission as a criterion of certainty and its establishment as a guide for the moral life presupposed a true ontological adequation between the clarity of conscious intuition and the deepest structure of reality and being. It is for this reason that the study of that idea holds a vivid speculative and historical interest.

Naturally, in these brief pages we do not claim to expound in full the philosophy of the great thinkers we consider the representative philosophers of the seventeenth century, nor do we propose to examine in depth the value of the principle that grounds the truth of clear and evident ideas or propositions — a principle that some critics reject, maintaining that the clarity of ideas does not reveal but rather conceals the effective complexity and mysteriousness of the real, while others contest it only partially, granting its validity with regard to formal truth — logical and mathematical — but denying it with regard to material truth, that is, the adequation of things in themselves to the intellect. Our aim is limited to showing, in the conception and development of the principle of clarity — whose meaning we shall attempt to fix — a certain typical form of metaphysical thought that, by broadening and elevating the significance of that idea, promotes it until it assumes not merely the representation of being but the supreme representation of perfection and divinity.

In other words, we propose to contemplate briefly the admirable destiny of an idea that came to contain within itself not only an entire philosophy but, in a certain way, to represent and stimulate a form of contemplation of God that, by virtue of the elevation and spiritual purity proper to it, could with justice be called mystical.

Could it be that beneath the name of clarity one finds simply the idea of reason? Perhaps — provided that reason is considered not as a mere mechanism of deduction, a bare discursive activity, but as indubitable vision, as direct intuition of the presence of being to the soul. In any case, there is more of intellectual intuition than of deductive mechanism in the idea of clarity that, under the influence of Descartes, the philosophers of the seventeenth century employ, and something like an erotics that seeks in the shadowless presence of intellectual objects a satisfaction that belongs not to mere knowing but to the highest and most noble forms of affectivity.

Looking more carefully, we find that the establishment of clarity as a criterion — as the very substance of certainty, as a touchstone at whose contact thoughts and ideas reveal their truth and their adequation — amounted in essence to the following:

1. What is true is what the mind comprehends, or rather, what the mind perceives with precision, distinctness, and fullness. That which it does not comprehend because it is obscure or confused is an error, or contains elements of error that, as such, in Descartes's expression, partake of nothingness.

2. Clarity is none other than the name for the direct intuition of truth and being, and it cannot be deceptive because it carries within itself the internal condition of its own validity. Clarity is the foundation of evidence and is consequently an absolute and ultimate criterion, since the very divine veracity to which Descartes appealed in order to guarantee the truth of our clear and evident ideas was, at bottom, merely an attribute derived from the idea of perfection whose clear and distinct perception constituted the most decisive proof of the existence of God.

3. Clarity is not, then, mere faith in logic. It is that and something more: it is the admission that there exists something like a metaphysical vocation or necessity of revelation, of self-disclosure in the deepest ground of the real.

4. Finally, invoking what we might call Husserl's reading of Cartesian evidence — which is nothing other than the criterion of clarity — we might say that clarity is, at bottom, nothing less than the total and explicit presence of the object of knowledge to the gaze of the mind, of the ego. Clarity is, formally, the property of a vision that perceives the object directly with all that is inherent to it, and with the articulations that simultaneously distinguish it from and relate it to other contents of the mind. And thus, as evidence that, above all doubt, establishes the absolute certainty and formal necessity of mental structures, clarity comes to be something like the form or the consciousness itself of apodicticity.

From a historical point of view, the equation clarity-truth determines the entire destiny of Cartesian speculation and that of its great continuators Spinoza and Leibniz. Once the equivalence or inherence of rational clarity in truth, and of truth in being, was established, nothing remained to prevent clarity from being elevated until it constituted not merely a simple attribute but, in a certain way, the constitutive essence of God himself, as the summum esse. With this, metaphysical feeling and theological meditation were transfigured into something like a mystical ecstasy before the luminosity of ideas and before the supreme harmony of the intellectual order of the world.

And thus the philosophy of these great thinkers was a diurnal philosophy; it stood in ecstasy before the harmony of ideas, before the admirable order it believed it could discover in the world. And in the intellectual love of God — which was nothing other than the clear vision of eternity — it placed the supreme elevation of the soul, both in the order of knowledge and in the sphere of spiritual perfection and beatitude. But in that diurnal philosophy — with the possible exception of Leibniz — night does not exist; or rather, night and obscurity could arise only as inferior forms of what those philosophers called, with a devaluing intent, passion. And in this way that philosophy disregarded or failed to study the action of nocturnal elements in the dynamics of the inner life.

We know that Heraclitus of Ephesus was called "the Obscure." This could be attributed to one or both of two causes. He might have been called obscure because some of his formulas were or seemed difficult to understand. Perhaps one of them is encapsulated in Fragment 52, which says: "Time is a child at play, moving pieces on a board: the kingdom of the child." He might also have been called obscure in reference to the darkness of Heraclitus's own intuition of things, to his sense of the mysterious, nocturnal, and unfathomable character of reality. And perhaps, among others, the judgment would be justified by Fragment 45, which says: "Do not try to traverse the limits of the soul; it is very deep."

In any case, Heraclitus is obscure — he is "the Obscure." And this relation between Heraclitus and obscurity, while instructing us about the character of the Heraclitean conception, also serves to determine what might be considered the negative aspects of the philosophy of clarity, principally from the gnoseological point of view.

The Heraclitean conception is a philosophy of becoming and of opposition, and it is obscure because its intuitions do not stand out or fix themselves like geometrical figures in the space of the mind. Rather than visions reducible to eidetic or conceptual formulas, they are intuitions of immersion, expressible only in symbolic forms and filled, in themselves, with a feeling of unfathomability and mystery. And perhaps they are obscure because they challenge the principle of non-contradiction and violate — if one may speak thus — the principle of identity.

If the philosophy of becoming and opposition is the philosophy of obscurity, then the philosophy of being and identity is by essence the philosophy of clarity. The intuition of what exists statically must of necessity be clear in order to be accepted as valid. The intuition of what is cannot be confused, because if it were, it would not yield to us the notion of what it contains. The conception or intuition of the immovable must be clear and complete, because otherwise it would not be an immobile perception of the eternal but a restlessness, a striving — a dark dissatisfaction contrary to philosophy's proper object and opposed to the ethical, religious, and aesthetic aspiration to attain, in repose, the pure contemplation of that object.

These approximations and distinctions may help us attain the final aim of these pages, which consists not in formulating an exhaustive critical assessment of seventeenth-century philosophy but in fixing the meaning of the idea that served it as a guiding star, with the scope and limitations proper to it.

To be sure, the philosophy of clarity, which marks with a sign of inadmissibility every intellectual or intuitive content that is not clear, explicit, and non-contradictory, lacks any sense of internal dynamics and, above all, of the unfathomability of the real. The unfathomable is by essence obscure, since in the intuition and experience proper to it, the intentional object is not something immediately seen or perceived but something that lies beyond, like an absence that is at the same time a presence, yet indefinable and obscure.

Naturally, every aspiration toward knowledge contains the tendency to achieve intellectual possession of its object — or, more precisely, its conceptual definition. But this does not justify the systematic disqualification of every cognitive content that resists a clear intellectual configuration. Let us recall the truths of the heart of which Pascal speaks, which belong not only to the sphere of mystical experience but to other spheres of knowledge and spiritual life, such as those of philosophy and aesthetic contemplation.

The principle of clarity has proved fruitful and useful as a methodological principle because it combats laziness, encourages rigor, and stimulates intellectual honesty and truthfulness. As a gnoseological principle it is a principle of apodicticity that grounds the evidence of essential connections. It has proved pedagogically effective in promoting spiritual elevation and the contemplative illumination of conduct. But it is gratuitous insofar as it postulates something like a metaphysical vocation of the real toward conceptual clarity, and narrow insofar as it leads to the disqualification of the obscure zones of mystical and aesthetic experience. Finally, it is excessively abstractive and dogmatic insofar as it inspires the psychological and ethical conception of the affective life, which has its own modes of apprehension, irreducible to intellectual clarity.
