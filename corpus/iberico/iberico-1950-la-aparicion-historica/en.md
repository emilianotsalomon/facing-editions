THE HISTORICAL APPARITION Mariano Iberico

## DE LA GÉNESIS Y LA VOCACIÓN DE LA HISTORIA — ON THE GENESIS AND VOCATION OF HISTORY

There is a prehistory of history, and that prehistory is myth. This fact invites us to compare these two forms of expression and human realization with one another, in order to see whether they illuminate each other reciprocally and whether, in doing so, they allow us to obtain an adequate notion of their psychic nature and spiritual significance — above all as concerns the spiritual vocation of history.

Like history, myth makes reference to the past. Only the mythical past is not, as the historical past is, something irremissibly gone; it is, on the contrary, something living and actual. It is not absence, as the historical past is, but rather presence — notwithstanding that it refers to a very remote epoch in time, since it is an initial, originary instant. But principally, the mythic account is distinguished from the historical one by its lack of density, of material concretion. The mythical past is more like something fugitive that penetrates the whole of life but that, as time passes, loses its force and thus evolves toward mysticism or toward pure poetry.

The images of myth are archetypal — not only in the sense that they are experienced by archaic humanity as the supreme, unsurpassable expressions of the human and divine world they configure, but also because, as recent studies in depth psychology demonstrate, those images possess universal representative validity as natural, spontaneous symbols of the fundamental modes of psychic reality.

Finally, myth implies a unity felt at the deepest level of life between the human being and the Cosmos — a unity whose images express with vivid intensity — and it likewise implies the reality and feeling of a human community in which individual personality either does not exist or exists only as a germ suppressed by an impersonal world that encompasses all aspects of human sensibility, imagination, and practical activity.

But at a certain indeterminate moment, a new feeling of life arises and asserts itself, defined by a double secession: the secession of the human being in relation to the cosmos, thereby breaking the vital continuity that myth embodied, and the secession of the individual in relation to the totalitarian community that incorporated and sustained them. This feeling, psychologically speaking, is nothing other than a feeling of solitude.

We shall not attempt to find the causes of this state of spirit, because we do not believe it possible to insert them within a local determinism, since we regard it as the expression of a global movement originating in the intrinsic possibilities and fatalities of life. According to this, the double secession would belong not to the order of causality but rather to the order of destiny. Nor do we believe it possible to deduce the consequences of the feeling of separation as one deduces the corollaries of a theorem. But we do consider it legitimate to affirm that within this feeling — or if one prefers, within this psychological situation — there is contained, among other things, the germ of historical consciousness and feeling. And this for two reasons: first, because history contains a vision of becoming that is exclusively human, acosmic, and according to which individual personality possesses interiority and autonomy; second, because the separated human being attempts to fill with the spectacle of a new, polychrome and vibrant life the void left by the absence of myth, and to rediscover, through sympathetic understanding and the emotionally charged experience of human vicissitude across time, the lost community of souls — even if what the human being ultimately finds in history is in fact a new community, different from the mythic one, founded on the dynamic feeling of adventure, risk, and the immanent and eternal drama of existence.

It is curious that the consciousness of personality — or more precisely, the consciousness of the individual self, emancipated from the totalitarian solidarity of the mythic state — should be joined, in a fundamental way, with the sense of rational universality. And so we encounter this paradox: individuality is solitude, and ontologically presupposes the irreducibility of this individual being into other beings, or essences, or categories, as one may wish to call them — yet the feeling of individual personality can only affirm itself in solidarity with the universal reign of reason, which annuls all concrete differences and, so to speak, dissolves them in its abstract system of forms and norms. The reign of that reason replaces the mythic participation of affective basis with a new form of participation — through intelligence — since to know rationally is to include the object within a category or essence that the mind conceives. The object of knowledge thus participates in, or is absorbed without remainder into, a unity of reason that the mind can conceive; and the personal mind, in turn, participates in the knowable intimacy of the object by absorbing it into the rational entity the mind can conceive. But if rational participation were complete, without remainder, then the individual diversity of things would vanish, and above all the psychic diversity of the human would be annihilated within a reductive and homogeneous comprehension. There must be in the person an unknowable ground, a redoubt of solitude, a mystery that confers upon what exists its reality and its depth. How to reconcile this truth with the simultaneous emergence of rationality and the feeling of personality?

History as the spiritual vocation of the human being appears contemporaneously with the rationality that dethrones myth in order to erect its own empire. But rationality, essentially temporal, is ahistorical; it categorically denies history. Then how is it to be explained that history appears alongside rationality? Clearly, the anti-mythic revolution that rationalist activity represents simultaneously implies a secession of the individual human being in relation to the primitive group — a group dissolved as a unit of totalitarian co-participation. This secession indicates the emergence of subjectivity, and with it, the emergence of time as irreversible succession and of memory as the possibility of preserving the past in the image. And without doubt, from the feeling of the fugacity inherent to subjective time and from the urge to overcome that fugacity, history is born — replacing myth, though on a new plane — insofar as it represents the retention of the past and, paradoxical as it may seem, the victory of the awakened human being over the time that, in a certain sense, the human being has created.

In sum, the triumph of rationality over myth, though it institutes the empire of a universal formality that excludes the ontological solitude of the soul, in fact produces — as the prestige of myth dissolves, along with the human community founded and sustained in myth — a new solitude: of the human being who has lost the intimate co-participation with others, yet has acquired the possibility of an interior life of their own. And thus personality would appear as a form — not logical but existential — of alliance or dialectical conflict between historical subjectivity and the spiritual objectivity of ahistorical reason. Rationality triumphs over myth and favors or provokes the resurgence of history, which in turn rises up against the empire of atemporal rationality.

The opposition between the continuity of reason and the existence, in time and space, of irreducible individual units is analogous to the opposition between waves and corpuscles that so preoccupies the theorists and epistemologists of physics. If the corpuscles were absorbed or eliminated, the waves representing continuity would triumph; if concrete beings were absorbed by rational abstractions, reason would triumph. Only this would be a triumph of death — as if the homogeneous continuity of the desert submerged the isolated oases in which life pulses. Pure science aspires to suppress — theoretically — the concrete individuality of beings. Against it, history vindicates the ontological and phenomenal variety of life. And thus an opposition takes shape between science, which consecrates the abstract, and the historical contemplation of the concrete — terms that, in their very opposition, complement each other in the dialectical and creative unity of existence.

From all of which it follows, in the end: first, that history appears to us as the adversary and yet the heir of myth — adversary because, allied with reason, it contributes to dethroning myth; heir because it uses, in the service of the concrete and factical, the urge toward evocation and the instinct of imaginative configuration proper to mythic activity. Second, history appears likewise as both contrary and allied to reason — contrary because history represents what is concrete and living; allied because, in concert with reason, history effects the secession of the solitary and rationalist human being from the psychic sphere that encompasses human life and cosmic life in a common atmosphere.

And since every spiritual movement of importance contains a tendency of return to origins, in history too there regularly appears, in a spontaneous manner, a movement toward the archetypal configuration of personages or events, and a certain psychic proclivity to contemplate those archetypal forms, or to invest them with a splendor and a kind of supra-historical, mythic irradiation. Historical personages such as Caesar, Alexander, Napoleon, Bolívar, and events of universal history such as the French Revolution, classical antiquity, and especially those that bear decisive significance in national histories, are examples of this archetypal transfiguration that converts them into myth, on a plane that transcends the merely historical succession and temporality. This imaginative and affective transfiguration must not be confused with the act of attributing a spiritual meaning to historical events that transcends pure historicity. In general, historical contemplation tends toward a symbolic order that already passes beyond the strict limits of history. It is rather the effect of historical narration upon the souls of peoples, poets, and thinkers, in whose domain historical events acquire epic or lyric resonances and become charged with meanings whose reach extends beyond time and times. In reality the past also runs its adventure, its risk, and its possibility of glory when laid on the table of historical analyses, syntheses, and polemics.

When we think of history, it is well to distinguish it from historiography, and thus to dispel the equivocation into which some German schools fell in the last century. Historiography may be defined as the discipline, or ensemble of disciplines, whose object is to realize history. According to this, history is in part a work of historiography, but it transcends it. And so when we refer to history, we are alluding neither to the formless past in its crude materiality nor to historiography as a merely literary or scientific work: we speak of history as an eminent ontological vocation of the human being, whose ideal is not pure knowledge but the assumption of the past into one's own spiritual life — though on a higher plane of reality and meaning.

To explain the "historical appetite," it is not sufficient to subsume it under the concept of curiosity. There is something deeper in that appetite — something (as Schelling saw) that is joined to the metaphysical exigency of the past, which impels the past toward apparition, so that the inventive appetite of the historian converges with the ontological appetite of the past in the construction of history. And thus the function of the historian consists not merely in provoking the apparition of the past for the purposes of theoretical knowledge, but with a view to aesthetic contemplation and the psychic and spiritual enrichment of the human being: it is the Reintegration of the abolished being to existence in the domain of vision, admiration, and anguish.

The past as such — or if we prefer, the being of the past, the being of what-was — obeys the universal law of apparition; and so it must appear, and in fact does appear, through an effect of a kind of metaphysical convergence between the ontological vocation of the past toward presence and the provoking act of the historical contemplator, the historian.

History, in its domain, would be a case of the universal law that prescribes to being its exteriority — that is, prescribes that being make itself present to the soul. An exteriority that is the language of the internal, an expression, a symbol, and that in history shows itself to us as the incessant appearing and disappearing of something that, even as it changes, subsists, and that, even as it annihilates and dissipates itself at every instant, remains in the incorporeal and inexplicable inexistence of the "was" with which the past passes into its mysterious eternity.

In this way, history presents itself to us — like cosmic life and like poetry — as one of the great forms of universal appearing. Cosmic life, with the splendor of its images and the rhythm of its great alterations, invites us to participate in an originary fecundity at once obscure and radiant. Poetry, with even greater efficacy, guides us along the path of return to the intimacy of being and allows us to live it simultaneously in its musical and plastic exteriority. Finally, history — both as intrinsic becoming and as the intentional knowledge of the past — by showing us the infinite prodigality of life, teaches us that being is "thus": concrete and living, and not according to the tautological formulae with which the abstractive urge of logical thought presumes to define and conceive it.

History is the apparition of human being in human time. But being is not time, nor is it something separate and indifferent that projects an apparent succession onto the running ribbon of time. The being of history is neither the Heraclitean becoming nor the merely rational, desert-like immobility of Parmenides. The being of history is something that changes while remaining itself, and remains itself while changing — always new, yet recognizing and exalting itself in that novelty, in that continuous, unforeseeable, uncontainable appearing.

To understand this — not in a causal or final mode, but through an illumination, let us say Delphic, that makes us see subsistence within change, alterity within unity, and to feel and taste the inexhaustible prodigality of life, which overflows yet does not shatter the vessel of the present — this is the great ontological lesson of history and, at least for me, the spiritual fruit of this meditation.

Regarding this transition from mythical consciousness to the form of spiritual life that in general terms may be called history, Jaspers, in the book Origin and Goal of History, advances the thesis that the crisis in question took place in what he calls "axial time," situated around 500 BCE, within the spiritual process contained between the years 800 and 200 BCE. "In that span," the philosopher tells us, "the most significant movements in history were realized; it is there that the human being with whom we live today has its origin." In this era there appear the great reformers, thinkers, and inspired figures who inaugurated a new feeling of life: in China, Confucius and Lao Tzu; in India, the Buddha lives and the Upanishads arise; in Iran, Zarathustra teaches; in Palestine the prophets appear, from Elijah to Deutero-Isaiah; in Greece we find Homer, Parmenides, Heraclitus, Plato, the tragedians — brilliant founders and great illuminators through whose action the human being became conscious of being itself, of personal spirituality, and at the same time, of the universality of spirit.

## FORMAS Y PERSPECTIVAS DEL PASADO — FORMS AND PERSPECTIVES OF THE PAST

In Bergson's doctrine of memory there are two perspectives on the psychological past that bear interest for the ontology of that region of time. According to one of those conceptions, the past persists, in a certain sense, in the moment or psychic state that follows it, and, accumulating and condensing itself into ever richer totalities, incorporates itself in the present — which in part derives its originality from the more or less considerable mass of past that condenses within it. The past is thus an element, more precisely, a factor in the dynamics of the spirit: we might call it an impulse, a virtuality tending toward actualization, which in this way promotes both the conservation and the incessant renewal of life.

The other conception is founded on the assertion that "the past, by hypothesis, is what no longer acts." A past essentially impotent, yet preserved in the integrity of its content as a latency that, under certain circumstances, can present itself again — with the freshness of its first appearing — to memory. It is a static past, from which can emerge the recollection that collaborates with action, but which, in itself, in its ontological purity, only exists and does not act. According to Bergson's doctrine, the past is not exhausted in its representation; it is not a mere imaginative species, but exists "in itself," independently — consequently — of whether it is apperceived or not. It thus constitutes a certain mysterious region, the region of what was and is no longer, but which in some indefinable manner is preserved with the totality of its modes and details of appearing and of experience.

There was, then, reason to consider a past that in a certain way alters itself as it accumulates upon itself and as it contracts or expands in accordance with the demands of action. And one would also have to admit a complete past, existing in itself and unalterable. In this way there is discovered — unexpectedly — through the apparent Heracliteanism of Bergson, at the very bottom of psychic life, an element of permanence and immobility: the past as a ground upon which consciousness may project itself like the light of a lantern that roams over the invisible yet real totality of what has been lived — or, if one prefers, like a hidden ground of images that can appear through an act of revelation comparable to the one that makes figures emerge from a photographic film, where they existed hidden yet whole and, so to speak, eternal.

It is not the intention of this work to study the strictly psychological aspects of the Bergsonian doctrine of memory. Its purpose is to highlight its ontological significance — a significance that Bergson does not examine specifically, but which appears with clarity from his theory, and more still, which constitutes a necessary presupposition for understanding, within that theory, the possibility of memory. If the past — or if we prefer, if the psychological event — does not engrave itself in the brain cells, and yet is remembered, it is that, inevitably, the past, which is the matter of memory, is not abolished but subsists independently of the body, whose sole function consists in serving as an instrument for consciousness in practical action, or as an organ of communication between the latent content of interior life and the fully conscious actuality of evocation. And so the past — all the past of our personal history — follows the body, envelops it like a nimbus, and we might even say that it penetrates and impregnates it without ever being confused with it: the past itself as ontological reality, and not memory as mere activity of evocation.

For our purposes it is irrelevant that this past is psychological, because psychic facts, insofar as they belong to universal happening, possess as such an objectivity, a materiality as perfect as the phenomena of cosmic life or the external contents of historical becoming. And in this way we can erect this subsisting past of the soul as an example or a simple case of the subsistence of the past in general, whatever it may be. The psychological past is past, simply, and subsists independently of and outside memory. That is what is decisive from the point of view of the metaphysics of the "was."

In the doctrine and therapeutic procedure of psychoanalysis, it is easy to perceive these two possibilities or modes of existing and acting of the past. It is well known that according to psychoanalysis, certain experiences, traumas, or emotional shocks from childhood can engender complexes of repressed tendencies that, from the subconscious, act as factors of imbalance and psychic disturbance.

In this concept, the past acts dynamically, provoking, from its shadowy hiding place, various disturbances in the economy of psychic life. But on the other hand, by employing appropriate techniques, the psychiatrist can awaken the memory of the disturbing event and, through that illuminating apparition, overcome the disturbance and reestablish equilibrium. Here the past no longer acts as an energy but as a light. And so, alongside a dynamic past there would exist a representative past — which presupposes that beyond the dynamic subconscious there exists the hidden plane where memories subsist without major contamination.

Psychoanalysis does not provide us with an ontology of the past, but its theory implies the existence of two irreducible forms of the psychological past: one that mingles with the present and can act as a pathological element; another that remains in itself and only manifests through the effect of a provoked revelation. Perhaps we could summarize the perspectives offered by these doctrines — Bergson, Freud — by saying that alongside or above the dynamic subconscious past there exists another past, merely representative, that does not act directly as a force, but only through the effect of its illumination in full consciousness.

In reality no theory of memory that denies the recording of perceptions in the brain cells can elude the necessity of admitting the existence of a "past in itself," the matter of memory — a region from which certain images return like phantoms, or into which we may venture as a way of evading the present or of seeking motives for aesthetic creation or retrospective delectation.

And so, if the psychological doctrines do not give us — nor do they claim to give — a metaphysical solution, they do make us see how memory constitutes one of the deepest, most mysterious, and most apt problems for awakening speculative emotion and unease. Because the problem of memory is not only psychological. When psychologists, after having refuted the hypothesis of the recording of memories in brain cells, assert that the question "where do memories remain?" lacks sense, since memories occupy no space — no doubt they are stating a truth, but a truth on a certain plane, because on another plane that "where" does not refer to any place in geometric space but to some metaphysical place, to some region of being into which the past with its images would fall. When I evoke the memory of some eucalyptus leaves that I would tear off in childhood in order to breathe their aroma, I cannot avoid asking myself where those images might be waiting for me to perceive them again. And then I realize that that "where" is a metaphor — that it does not allude to the material place where they would be enclosed, but to the mysterious destiny of those images that are not yet memories, and that, being past, draw their substance from their futurity of appearing. The problem of memory is thus the problem of the past itself — of that abolished being which nonetheless envelops and follows us, and expands like a gas, while what we call the present (in which we concentrate all the intensity of existence) is nothing but a line, a transversal cut without thickness in the flow of time.

We consider the admission of this ontological status of the past as an indispensable presupposition for any attempt to found an epistemology of historical knowledge. The following reflections on history take this fundamental postulate into account, and from it derive the inferences relative to the possibility of objective knowledge in history and, more generally, to the metaphysical and human meaning of the historical.

Contemplating the past in temporal flow, we can also discover two perspectives. There is a past that is antecedent — practically identifiable with cause, origin, preparation, or simply as a segment of the temporal line that precedes the present. This is the anterior antecedent past. And there is a subsequent, posterior past that follows the abolition of the present, or that can also be defined as the conversion of the present into the past, of the "is" into the "was." When I read a novel and arrive at the denouement — which is the final present of the book — the anterior past is constituted by all the pages read, whose content is actualized and at the same time dissipated in the present. In contrast, once the book is read and I close the volume, the whole of it falls into a new past — the past of the denouement that now enters the sphere of the "was" and of memory. Every present, as it abolishes itself, engenders a past that survives it — paradoxically, in death. Psychologically, memory follows perception as the phantom of a vanished body. And so one might perhaps say that all presents fall into a kind of Hades where the images of all that was accumulate, until an Ulysses, an Orpheus — perhaps a poet or a sage — descends into that depth of being and draws from among that innumerable people a few presences to exhibit in the light of contemplation: whether in memory or in history. The content of these two pasts is the same, and the only difference is that while the antecedent past is a past in the making, the consequent past is an already-made past. And perhaps the great difficulty for the historian who faces this surviving past consists in trying to relive, or to reconstruct with the elements of an already-made past, a past in the making. Psychologically, the vision through memory of this finished past is discontinuous and tends to obey a tendency toward spatialization — whence derives, according to Bergson, the fact that in retrospective evocation a spatial superposition of elements replaces the creative continuity of interior life. Mutatis mutandis, the same might be said of historical construction and of the risk the historian runs in trying to reconstruct with dispersed elements the indivisible becoming of the historical process.

Finally, leaving for later the study of the question relative to the distinction between history and myth, let us briefly address the difference that exists between tradition and history — which also imply a duality of perspectives on the past. Tradition is the accumulation of the collective past, in part unconscious, which manifests itself as an underlying feeling in the economy of psychic and social life, and principally in the repetition — accompanied or not by explicit evocation — of forms of external life and modes of psychic reaction. It is the past that prolongs itself in spiritual valuations, in decorum and customs, in the subjective modes of personal experiences, and so on. History, by contrast, is the clearly conscious commemoration of the past, and its retrospective vision may or may not be accompanied by the affective elements inherent to the traditional experience: love, piety, veneration toward the past, and so forth. Traditional experience is effective and practical, sometimes aesthetic. Historical experience is essentially theoretical. Tradition is more present than past; history cannot avoid the feeling of the unbridgeable distance that separates the present from the intentional object of its contemplation.

## HACIA UNA NOCIÓN DEL PASADO — TOWARD A NOTION OF THE PAST

The concepts that metaphysicians and ontologists employ to explore the mystery in which we are and move are, in general, wholly inadequate to their object — and we might even say that they systematically elude it. Those concepts are typically those of existence, reality, and being, which are supposed to encompass in their vastness everything that can be conceived, affirmed, or supposed. But it happens that those concepts are exclusive. The concept of existence excludes non-existence, that of reality excludes the ideal, and that of being excludes non-being, nothingness. And since non-existence, unreality, and nothingness have a fundamental importance in the composition of our questions, doubts, and hopes, it turns out that those concepts, with their limitations and restrictions, do not deliver what they should. Therefore, perhaps, defying the principle of the excluded middle, we must admit between the two opposing extremes of the traditional dichotomies, zones of participation that contain both the yes and the no that constitute the sign of the separated and rigid concepts.

Following the thread of that meditation, it seems to us justified to affirm that around the petrified entities of a merely formal ontology there extend zones constituted or populated by rarefied forms of being, by impalpable emanations of nothingness. Possibility, risk, destiny, absence, the past, the future — all inhabit those vague horizons that Mallarmé, perhaps the only one, once dared to explore in Igitur and in A Throw of the Dice Will Never Abolish Chance.

The past is a horizon of absence to which withdrawn presences have access. That horizon sometimes attracts us toward itself, arousing nostalgia; at other times it is we who attempt to draw the past toward the present — when we make history, or when we lean over the river of our personal time to rescue one or another image from its inexorable current.

But this, in reality, is more of an impression, and therefore we need to subject to a more rigorous examination the problem relative to the notion of the past — a problem of equal interest to ontology and to history.

What is the past? If we say that the past is a non-existent, we shall no doubt state a truth, but it will be an incomplete truth, because there are many non-existents that do not partake of the nature of the past. Thus, for example, a square circle is a non-existent, but it is neither past nor present, because it does not belong to time. We must therefore add to non-existence some quality that defines it as past, and we may perhaps achieve this if we say that the past is a non-existent that owes its non-existence to an act of abolition, of suppression. In so doing, no doubt, we define the past, in a certain tautological manner, by the past itself — but it is also evident that this provisional definition gives us a sufficiently clear view of our object and thereby leads us to the broader perspective of time, where we may perhaps attain its most adequate notion.

According to this, the past is constituted from the matter of presents that were and are no longer. But the past may also be considered as cause, origin, preparation of the present — and in that case it is not inappropriate to say that the past expires in the present in which its final vocation is fulfilled. Whence arises an apparent duality of directions, difficult to perceive clearly from within time. On one side, the past aspires toward the future that is the present; on the other, this present immediately turns toward the past and is carried, ever further, toward the unfathomable depths of time. So the past at once engenders and devours the present. The present is the future of the past, and the "was" is the inexorable future of the "is" — childhood is the past of youth and aspires toward it; youth is the past of maturity and tends toward it; and finally maturity, through old age, tends toward death, which cancels life and precipitates its totality into that mysterious Hades that is the great future where the past will repose forever.

It is a pity that the ontology of the verb "to be," in its tenses and moods, has not been more deeply explored. The philosophical study of those accidentals would illuminate us — not only about the grammatical possibilities of the verb, but about the structure of the human being, subject to the moral and temporal variations expressed in the accidentals of this part of speech. "Being" — a word in which the verb and the noun, the two great categories of existing: act and substance, are symbolically and allusively unified.

Employing an inexact comparison, as every spatial figuration applied to time must be, we shall say that the past is the segment of the line of time that terminates in the point called the present — which, moment by moment, incorporates itself into that segment and pushes it toward confines ever more remote from the new presents that arrive and, in turn, sink.

The relation between present and past reveals itself in historical time as a feeling of distance between what was and the current moment. But it is a unilateral relation. The historian can reach the past and contemplate it, describe it and interpret it. In a word: the past is the intentional object of historical knowledge, but the present cannot be the object of knowledge for people of the past. In contrast, the past can act to a certain extent in the present, while the present cannot in any way act upon the past. Socrates can influence Heidegger, but Heidegger cannot influence Socrates.

Klages says that the past is the distant in itself. This we might perhaps interpret in the sense that the past is withdrawn from all possibility of physical apprehension by the contemplator. In space we call "near" what is within our reach; "far" what lies beyond our grasp. But in time, no past is within our reach; therefore all past is far — and ever farther. By which reason, between every present and every past there interposes an unbridgeable distance.

That distance is the very condition of history. No doubt the historian attempts to traverse it — but knows that they must not succeed, because if they did, the past would be present and would thereby lose its historicity. History, the science of the past, must maintain distance from its object, and yet must abolish it.

The notion that emerges from these indications does not aspire to be formulated in the logical terms of proximate genus and specific difference. It claims to be only what we shall call a functional notion, because it refers to the past as a region of time in the different perspectives of its appearing to consciousness and in its diverse modes of action in life. In the end, it aspires to constitute a knowledge of its object rather than a mere concept of it.

The nothingness of the future — that space of indetermination and possibility — being suppressed, the human being disappears. And so we can assert that there exists an absolute incompatibility between the integral knowledge of the future and the ontological structure of the human being. The ignorance of what is to come is a part of the being of a human being who only lives because they do not know, and who would disappear — not with biological death but with ontological death — if the totality of happening were revealed before their eyes once and for all, like a novel already read.

No doubt owing to this profound metaphysical necessity of ignorance, uncertainty, and doubt, prophecies are almost always enunciated in symbolic form. Perhaps because the symbol, being susceptible to several meanings, offers in its very indetermination the margin of uncertainty necessary for the efficacy — or better, for the existence — of the living interiority of human action. The human being may catch a glimpse of the future, but cannot, and must not, see it directly. The symbolic form of prophetic expression preserves the essential negativity of the future and maintains the uncertainty necessary for the foresight to be incorporated into the economy of psychic life without breaking the fundamental structure of the human.

"The past is only a belief," asserts the Faust of Paul Valéry, adding: "a belief is an abstention of the powers of our spirit, which repudiates forming all the conceivable hypotheses about absent things, as it repudiates giving them all equal force of truth." A thesis that at bottom amounts to saying that the past lacks objectivity, lacks being in itself — that it is a mere creation of our subjectivity, and that the version of it we consecrate as true is only one possibility among many others — equally fallacious and uncertain — of conceiving, writing, and in sum believing in what was. A brilliant thesis, perhaps, but one that harbors a great equivocation: the equivocation of confusing the belief in the past, which is a psychological phenomenon, with the past itself as such — which is the intentional object of belief. A fallacious thesis, therefore, but one that, expressed as it is by the French poet, allows us to highlight an equivocation and an error that can corrupt the entire epistemology of historical knowledge.

The soul — or at least certain subterranean depths of the soul — is like a Hades where the shadows of things that were roam.

Those shadows, withdrawn from necessity and abandoned by hope, do not exist but only seem to exist. And that absence of being, of necessity, and of hope is no doubt the mysterious cause of the strangely peculiar light in which the figures of that world move — a light that is not light, a brightness that one would say is darkness — the light of inexistence, comparable to the light of mirrors, which does not illuminate being but only the image.

This is perhaps why, in his Orpheus, Cocteau has made mirrors the symbol of the passage from life to death, from the world of corporeality and necessity to the world of shadows.

## DE LA MATERIA DE LA HISTORIA — ON THE MATTER OF HISTORY

When we read the history of the great archaeological discoveries — Sumer, Chaldea, Egypt, Greece, Peru, and so on — especially those in which the decipherment of writing permits the establishment of at least an approximate chronology, we see how, before our imagination and astonishment, ever deeper (that is, ever more ancient) layers of the past arise. And one would say that those impalpable strata of the past — which ruins, vessels, and inscriptions allow us to glimpse — were there, latent, in themselves, awaiting the gaze, the decipherment, the interpretation of future people. And so one would say that each leaf of time joins itself to the other leaves of time, and that all remain forever, like a book, in a space that is no longer time but perhaps eternity: the eternity of what has passed. Incorporeal archives that we can perceive through the documents that the diligence of scholars or archaeologists places before our sight and deciphers for our instruction, thus incorporating the past into history.

A theorist of historical knowledge may naturally profess a subjectivist or neo-Kantian doctrine about this form of knowledge, but they will inevitably encounter the difficulty that, while Kant had nothing to do with the noumenon, the historian must necessarily describe and interpret a certain "in itself": the past — whose insolence was already glimpsed by Dostoyevsky when he wrote that "nothing is more insolent than a fact," thereby contradicting, perhaps without knowing it, Goethe's formula that "the fact is already a theory." Whatever one may make of this opposition between Dostoyevsky and Goethe, what is certain is that the historian must seek full objectivity even knowing how difficult it is to attain. The historian will justly seek to eliminate the elements of subjectivity, passion, or interest that may — in themselves or in other investigators — veil or distort their vision of the past. And so the deformed or confused vision will in any case be a result of the human condition's tendency to infuse the personal subjectivity into what one sees or studies — but it will not be an effect of the scientific intuition of the historian or of the intrinsic structure of historical knowledge. The historian wants to reach the past. Therefore, the past is there: it has a Dasein that fixes its situation in time and the intrinsic objectivity of its presentation.

Historical vocation is a form of the universal human vocation toward transcendence — that is, toward an alterity that surpasses mere existence. But at the same time, history satisfies another tendency that apparently contradicts but in reality complements the first: the tendency to seek in everything a kind of prolongation or amplification of one's own life, one's own personal interiority. History aspires to find in the other the same, and in the same the other. History aspires to transcend the present in an alterity that exists in itself, and at the same time history offers the human being the possibility of living their own life in a dimension without limits. And so its vocation is to reach, through transcendence, immanence. Two contradictory and complementary tendencies in the human being: toward alterity and toward unity.

The existence of a past in itself guarantees the alterity that is the condition of transcendence, and the universality of the spirit founds the possibility that the human being may recognize themselves — even in what, as in history, poetry, or mysticism, overflows, transports, or elevates them beyond themselves.

The historical world must be essentially an open world — without limits, open to the possibility of difference, variety, and heterogeneity — and it is subject, a priori, to the necessity of spiritual unification. To establish precisely the conditions of that unity which synthesizes without destroying, and maintains vivid and fresh both qualitative differences in simultaneity and in succession, is the task of the epistemology of history and the ideal — at once descriptive and constructive — of the historian.

Whatever the objective factors that intervene in historical vision, what is certain is that the historian (Ranke) aspires to express their own personal historicity and at the same time to attain an authentic vision of the historicity of the past. The historian is immersed in history. And no doubt their yearning for authenticity and objectivity is achieved, at least in part. If this were not so, in reality history itself would not exist — only the history of historiography, or better of the successive versions of the past; and in this way the essential vocation of history would be frustrated.

In history, as in nature, not everything is form. In history, we do not merely reconstruct or configure the past imaginatively — we also, and essentially, discover it. Not only the document but the content and significance of the document — which presupposes the admission of something preexistent and absolute that the document reveals but does not create. And so this conviction of the possibility of discovering the past in itself constitutes perhaps the deepest ground of historical consciousness — one that is not content with knowing the versions of the past but aspires, rightly or not, to a direct contact with it.

No historical subjectivism could exorcize this phantom of the past in itself, which is there as a presence irreducible to any alchemy, to any historiographical fabrication. And there is in the historian's work something analogous to the portrait in which the painter, while submitting to the model, transfigures it and creates its image. But with these differences: first, the artist's creation is intentional; in the historian's, involuntary. Second, the painter imitates what they see; the historian must to a certain extent see what they describe. Third, the artist paints from a present model; the historian describes an absent model.

History is the great science of absence. It seeks to confer a presence upon absence. For the physical sciences nothing is present or absent — it is as if they acted in a kind of abstract ideality, one that can nonetheless find an experimental verification in the effective world. For history, on the contrary, whose vocation is concrete and living reality, everything is absent — and its fundamental aspiration is to make it present, without it being possible for history, as it is for abstract disciplines, to obtain experimental verification of its presuppositions.

From all of which one may infer the following conclusions relative to the specificity of historical matter and consequently of the knowledge that pertains to it: first, the intentional object of historical knowledge is something withdrawn from the historian's sensory perception, which can only be evoked and described by means of imagination and through the technical procedures of critical inference. Second, this something forms part of a series whose last member is situated in the moment in which the historian writes history. Third, history studies "concretes," not "abstracts." Fourth, each historical concrete is unrepeatable — historical pluralism. Fifth, it is presupposed that what the historian narrates is not fantasy but reality, which implies the admission of an object to whose demands of fidelity, truthfulness, and respect the historian must submit.

None of this is new, but it gathers in clear synthesis the indispensable presuppositions for an epistemological work that determines to what degree or within what limits the historical vision can deliver to us the authentic reality of history. "Nothing returns of what has been, just as nothing that has been can be abolished," says Croce in his book Theory and History of Historiography. Which means that if "what has been cannot be abolished," it is because the past enjoys a certain privilege of indestructibility — without our being able to argue that this privilege belongs to everything that is not, since such an objection would distort Croce's thought, which concerns the past as something "having-been" and not as simple non-being or pure nothingness.

## DE LA VIVENCIA HISTÓRICA — ON HISTORICAL LIVED EXPERIENCE

In what does historical lived experience consist? In the first place, it consists in the evocation or representation of the region of time we call the past — which, no longer perceptible by the senses, must be represented or reconstructed through the technical procedures of historiography, with the indispensable aid of the faculty of imagination and the sometimes divinatory intuition of the historian.

Historical lived experience requires, in order to attain its fullness, intelligibility — that is, the integration of the fact, personage, or institution that is the object of historical treatment into the web that links the various contents of a more or less extensive span of time. And this requirement seeks its fulfillment either through the way of immanent or transcendent teleological comprehension — methods whose characteristics we have briefly studied — to which should perhaps be added the mode of historical intelligibility advocated by Croce — akin to and strictly reducible to the immanent teleological mode — consisting in "understanding the quality, and thereby the mode of unfolding and operating, of the fact taken as object of consideration, distinguishing it from facts of different quality or order and treating those, now as matter offered to the former, now as obstacles against which the former must struggle."

Historical lived experience is, in large part, a living of someone else's psychic states. This participation does not exercise itself only in the strictly psychological aspects of individual or collective reality. It also functions in the historical comprehension of the realizations of objective spirit: philosophical ideas, law, art, and so on — which call not upon the mere intellect but upon other psychic tendencies and possibilities. A certain Einfühlung is necessary for the total capture and internal assimilation of the creations of culture, except naturally when one deals with works of mere abstraction such as mathematics or logic. The nimbus, the aura of cultures and epochs of which Ariès speaks — which the historian must intuit — is something beyond abstract intelligence and calls upon the capacity for aesthetic sympathy of the spirit.

Every historical lived experience presupposes evaluation — that same evaluation performed with a transcendent criterion when facts are judged according to atemporal, ethical, aesthetic, or juridical norms that are in reality non-historical; or with an immanent criterion when facts are judged according to the historical moment in which they occur. Historicism is the expression of this criterion of radical immanence in the judgment of historical happening. It implies a total relativism that ignores the force of the eternal in time, and is therefore combated by those tendencies that confer a final meaning upon the historical totality — that, so to speak, suspend the significance and value of becoming in the majesty of an atemporal absolute.

What is the content of truth — or if we prefer, of objective knowledge — in historical lived experience, as it emerges from our summary analysis? This question we shall try to answer across the following chapters.

Within this conception, we shall briefly refer to Simmel's doctrine of historical knowledge — a doctrine that, by its roots in critical philosophy, seems to us useful for the clarification and discussion of this speculative position in the sphere of historical knowledge.

Simmel attempts in his book a critique of historical realism analogous to the critique Kant had carried out with respect to knowledge of nature. The difference is, obviously, that history is already spirit and not nature, and that consequently, in its domain, "the categories that intervene, with their general autonomy and the demands they impose upon the matter, do not stand out as sharply from the matter itself as in the case of natural science." With this — that is, with consideration of the formative power of the spirit vis-à-vis history — there dissolves, as in the case of nature, what we might call the constraint of history.

But perhaps the difference would be more fundamental than appears to follow from Simmel's formulation. And that difference would consist in the fact that while the thing in itself of the Critique of Pure Reason is an X entirely lacking in determination, the thing in itself — or if we prefer, "the in itself" — with which the historian is confronted is, whether we like it or not, the past. Whence it follows that historical Kantianism would need to superpose upon — or systematically link to — the categories of understanding in general (the transcendental concepts of the Critique of Pure Reason) the specifically historical categories, namely those that condition knowledge of history not as mere natural happening but as happening in the domain of spirit. By which the problem of historical knowledge is more complex and obscure than that of the mere epistemology of natural knowledge. Since the historian must depart from the paradoxical situation of seeking beyond themselves something they know to be unfindable, since it is in itself, and which at the same time they posit as susceptible of verification.

It is evident that history is, in large part, a work of subjectivity — and if one prefers, a work of history itself, that is, of the human and cultural moment in which the past is contemplated. But it is also true that theoretical culture strives to reduce that subjectivity to a minimum and, precisely, to restore to the past its full and essential objectivity. With which, history truly does what every cognitive discipline does: pursue knowledge of things as they are — not in the Kantian noumenal sense, but in the sense of a supra-individual phenomenology, and in that sense, objective, and therefore, in this precise sense, in itself.

Clearly the great historical a priori is unity — that is, every historical content is historical only insofar as it is conceived within a form in which all material elements are inserted as members of a certain dynamic and synthetic structure. This unity presupposes the institution of a certain hierarchy among the contents of happening, in which some elements are affirmed with greater emphasis and play a subordinating role in relation to the others. And it also presupposes a certain discriminatory and abstractive sense that effects separations, classifications, and determines privileged spaces of investigation and description in the indeterminate flow of happening. It must be borne in mind that, since the contents of human history are primarily psychic, the reconstruction of the past is, in large part, a work of psychological comprehension — which presupposes a supra-logical historical intuition and a genuine affective projection of the historian's self onto the human matter of their study. There is thus in history, both by virtue of the essential nature of its contents and by virtue of the reconstructive, descriptive, and configurative vocation proper to it, a ground of identity with art — since the historian, like the artist, does not reproduce the past photographically but reconstructs it according to forms and modes inherent to their personal mode of being and to the circumstances of the cultural environment in which they live.

The objectivity to which history aspires is different from that to which the physical sciences aspire. For the latter, objectivity means coherence with the general system of the sciences and universality in the validity of their laws. For history, objectivity means concrete reality — more precisely, adequation or fidelity of historical description or interpretation to the singularity of concrete cases of happening. The epistemology of history must therefore study the possibilities of this objectivity and, if we wish, the foundations of historical truth. For the sciences of laws, there is neither past nor present — all that exists are regularities ultimately reducible to relations of quantity. Nor do presence or absence exist: lived experiences. History, when it treats exclusively these lived experiences in their concrete and irreplaceable reality, sometimes fails to comprehend them.

From all of which it appears that Simmel's subjectivist doctrine is true, but only in part — true insofar as it affirms, but not insofar as it appears to deny the ontological foundation of history; insofar as it ignores the great aporias of historical knowledge, and above all the principal one: which consists in the necessity of establishing the objectivity of a past inaccessible in itself, because it is past, and of aspiring nonetheless to describe and interpret it without affecting its authenticity. We must therefore seek our orientation by another path.

From what has been set forth it follows that historical lived experience does not consist in the mere conception of intellectual structures or of other psychological states or contents, with omission of all reference to their projections in the world of appearing — or if one prefers, of sensory exteriority — since that projection, while defining its regularity, establishes its situation in the line of time. That is, those projections establish the historicity of the facts that constitute the matter of lived experience. History does not move in the world of the purely invisible, because it is not a science of the abstract, but a comprehensive vision of the concrete — which is inconceivable without appearing and temporality. And so the words of Denise de Rougemont in his book Love in the Western World: "if it is true that the mutations of the heart are prepared and worked in the unconscious, they date, in fact, from their epiphany in written, plastic, or musical expression" — these words confirm us in our idea about the content of lived experience and the meaning of the historical perspective that constitutes itself as an order of presentation of its objects in the epiphany of happening.

## DEL CONOCIMIENTO HISTÓRICO — ON HISTORICAL KNOWLEDGE

If, taking as given the various theories of knowledge in general, we pose the epistemological problem in the specific domain of history, we find immediately that the fundamental intention and primary impulse of historical investigation is the knowledge of the past as it effectively was — according to Ranke's famous formula: Wie es eigentlich gewesen. Whether that intention is or is not realizable, whether the possibility corresponds to the intention — these are themes open to gnoseological meditation. A supremely difficult question, because the past is, in itself, ungraspable, and because historiography must therefore seek and find a criterion of certainty that guarantees it the full objectivity of a notion that is in reality unverifiable. Let us study that difficult question.

Clearly, the first idea that presents itself to the spirit when the question arises of whether the possibility corresponds to the theoretical intention of history, is whether the specificity of the knowledge inherent to this discipline is or is not absorbed within the theory of knowledge as it appears from Kantian speculation — or whether it is, on the contrary, a specific theory, not reducible to the generality of knowledge.

The fundamental effort and endeavor of historical investigation consists in rescuing the "was" from oblivion — in converting its absence into presence. And since to say presence is necessarily to say present, it follows that history aspires to live the past incorporated into the present, with all that this incorporation implies as experience and responsibility — and this not only as the subjective vision of the investigator (erudite historiographer, archivist, and so on), but as something belonging to the universal objectivity of the spirit. By which, no doubt, Croce is right when he asserts that all history is contemporary. Only one would have to add that this contemporaneity does not, and must not, erase the feeling of properly historical temporal distance in the domain of contemplation. José Antonio Maravall, in his interesting book Theory of Historical Knowledge, grants that history is a science of the present — but of a present constituted by a living past. And he writes: "what is certain is that the historian postulates and even demands knowledge of that past as such — that is, insofar as it is in a certain measure detached, distanced from us."

Every objective knowledge is proven through verification. And so historiographers ask, responding to this epistemological anxiety so acute in history: "What is the criterion of historical verification?"

For our part we think that the task might be facilitated if we classify historical contents according to the following categories: first, facts we shall call material, perceptible by the senses: an earthquake, a battle, and so on. Second, facts and forms of life corresponding to objective spirit: works of art, institutions, and so on. Third, facts psychological in nature and internal: intentions, motives, emotional reactions, and so forth. Fourth, connections — causal or final — that respectively give rise to these two goals of historical investigation: explanation and comprehension. Fifth, spiritual transcendence.

The degree of certainty — or if we prefer, the degree of verifiable objectivity — diminishes as we pass from the first to the last classes of this enumeration. Without doubt it is easier to establish the date of an earthquake or the duration of a battle than the intentions of a general or a leader — and easier than to describe the evolution of a style than the meaning of the general historical evolution of the West. One must, however, make the following clarification: this classification is more theoretical and taxonomic than real. Since in historical life these planes are not presented in isolation or merely superposed upon one another, but integrated into concrete totalities — into complexes that, in themselves, may contain various degrees of certainty, yet which, as such tonalities, present themselves as objectivities, whether in the investigative spirit of those who write history, or in naive historical experience. Historical consciousness is nourished by acceptances and rejections, but always on the basis of belief in the essential objectivity within which it moves.

Moreover, it is evident that there exist one or several possibilities of historical verification. No doubt for certain limiting cases a technique may exist that participates in or coincides with the technique of the physical sciences, or that makes use of it: for example, establishing dates by reference to the positions of the stars, and so on. But in general, especially for processes of a psychic or spiritual nature, there is no possibility of verification of the scientific type. It is the knowledge of others' mentality, the sagacity, the penetration, the psychological instinct of the historian, that allows one to reach a conclusion that may be indemonstrable yet on occasion possesses great internal force. For the creation of history as a form of human knowledge, there appear from time to time brilliant personalities capable of intensely reliving the past and incorporating it — without inflicting upon it distortion or harm — into the spiritual economy of the present.

One might say that the possibility of strictly logical certainty in the domain of history is in inverse proportion to the spiritual depth and importance of the significance of the happening. High significances, the profound and enigmatic determinations of destiny are not of the order of the apodictic but of the order of interior assent, of what Croce would call internal verification. An assent that the historian grants to these obscure evidences after having exhausted their information and critically scrutinized with rigorous scrupulousness the information at their disposal.

The historical world is primarily a psychological and spiritual world — one that, by its nature, cannot be explored or understood by strictly scientific and technical methods, but only through divinatory intuition, through the personal and untransferable sagacity of the historian. The historian must configure their knowledge — which is not an object of perception, nor properly of memory — by means of imagination; and in this way history participates in poetry and art. This does not, however, imply the arbitrariness of historical investigation, because there is something that guarantees its quality as a cognitive discipline: namely, the historian's intention, which is not to invent as the poet does, but to know as the scholar does. It has been said, as Aron remarks, that historical facts do not exist before the historian — which the philosopher of history may admit and profess, but not the historian, whose vocation is precisely the reconstitution of the past and not its creation.

There are four theoretical possibilities for historical work: (a) Photographically reproducing the past; (b) Interpreting the past according to successive categories (analogous to or derived from the Kantian ones); (c) Total invention of the past; (d) Spiritual verification. The problem consists in knowing whether history can be at once an authentic, objective version of the past and a contribution of the spirit. The historian cannot live the totality of the past, but only sectors, parts, limited spheres.

Hence these three observations inherent to historical work: first, Selection — according to a criterion of significant importance. Second, Configuration — form and synthesis. Third, Interpretation.

There is an anterior past, which is cause, preparation, and antecedent; and there is a posterior past that follows the abolition of the present as death follows life. Example: reading a book — the pages read lead to the present; the current page, once finished and turned, falls and becomes past. Here the past does not precede but follows the present.

Selection. One cannot know the totality of the past. To know it in its totality would be to live it, and to live it would be contrary to its contemplation — that is, to its being history. The historian makes the selection of a theme from the more or less indeterminate mass of the past. The historian chooses the object of their study and future description, guided by interests that are not always theoretical, though their final intention is theoretical. And those interests are axiological, or derived from circumstances, or proceeding from personal formation, from political, national, social preferences, and so forth. Within the theme itself, the selection of facts is made on the basis of their significant importance. By all of which one sees that selection, by implying a delimitation of historical material, already implies a beginning of configuration: first, because it detaches or abstracts from the fluid continuity of the past a theme that is itself already a form; second, because this form disciplines the material of the past according to a definite intention.

Configuration. This is the operation by which the historian traces the geometric contour of the fact or facts being studied. It obeys a principle of organic unity, and its ideal is to fix the masses of time that constitute the terrain of the investigation. The historian attempts to penetrate, beneath the fragments that are visible, to the interior lines that unite them, to the inanimate currents that run not only from one to another but that also envelop and, so to speak, simultaneously carry along other elements that would appear scattered to a superficial, immediate attention.

Necessarily, configuration is a work of signification and, to a certain extent, of fixation. It is, necessarily, a stylization — and this in a double sense: as signification, fixation, and immediately apprehensible figure of happening; and as "will to form," whether that of the individual historian or as the expression of a general tendency of historiography. This must not be understood, however, as an arbitrary improvisation or as the imposition of a preestablished design upon the historical material. The historian is forbidden to solicit historical material, and it is not legitimate to do history in the service of a non-historical interest. Garcilaso de la Vega configured the historical reality of the Inca Empire motivated by high ideals of knowledge and homage, and what he wrote emanated from a conviction of his spirit and from no subaltern interest.

On Interpretation. Interpretation is the intellectual operation that consists in discovering the meaning of a text. Historical interpretation consists specifically in discovering the meaning of happening and translating it into conceptual language.

For configuration, a clear example of stylization is offered by the Royal Commentaries of Garcilaso de la Vega. Inspired by his love for the Inca past — which he knew principally from the accounts and descriptions of his relatives — imbued with the currents of Renaissance thought, principally insofar as they reflected or translated the Platonic influence, the Inca was naturally inclined to configure the material he knew about Inca history in a form very close to ideal perfection. It is not that Garcilaso solicited the material in order to adapt it to a preestablished mold, but that spontaneously he arranged, interpreted, and composed that material into a figure whose continuity filled the intervals that tradition left vacant, and that conferred upon the whole a harmony of contour and a vividness of expression in which throbbed the lyric accent of nostalgia and a kind of filial reverence.

"At bottom, the difference from one epoch to another approaches the difference between two paintings or between two symphonies: it is of an aesthetic nature."

The true awareness — or better, "the true object of history consists in the awareness of the halo that particularizes a moment of time": an act of aesthetic nature.

Much has been said in the epistemology of history about the principle of unity, but insufficient emphasis has been placed on the other essential principle of historical consciousness: the principle of alterity. A principle that might be formulated, perhaps too sharply, as follows: the past is irreducible to the present — not only in circumstances or external elements that are integrated into each moment of happening, but in the very psychic reality of the present. One must insist on the fact that it is precisely in that irreducibility that the historicity of the past consists. If the past could be assimilated without remainder to the present — whether material or psychic — the essential heterogeneity of history would dissolve into a desert-like homogeneity, and along with it, for lack of true content, history itself would disappear. Something of that sort happens with the ascendancy of rationalism, which suppresses the heterogeneity of life, sacrificed on the altar of the universal reductive unification of concepts — a triumph that would not only ruin history but all aesthetic interest and all sensual attraction of existence. It is necessary, therefore, that the past should possess, with respect to the present and to the subjective unity of the contemplator, a radical alterity that allows it to be considered as a historical moment — therefore unique and unrepeatable in the temporal flow. Physical phenomena have no history, because they are theoretically the same and can repeat themselves indefinitely. Each historical present falls into the past without possibility of return, and this quality confers upon the time of history, together with its tragic sense by virtue of the constant abolition realized within it, the unlimited possibility of offering to the curiosity of historical knowledge an inexhaustible variety of presences and themes.

The irreversibility of the time of history implies the unity of each moment of the past, and so, while according to the principle of unity the historian grasps historical matter according to forms and modes that determine its final identification within the psychic or spiritual substance of the investigator, according to the principle of alterity the investigator — or better, historical consciousness — must posit the ontological uniqueness of the past as a member of an irreversible temporal succession.

Thanks to the evocation of history, historical time, without ceasing to be irreversible in itself, unfolds the succession inherent to it into a kind of spatial simultaneity. Just as in space we can walk in all directions, in historical evocation we can live the new before the ancient, or contemplate two epochs in the same instant. We can superpose new rhythms and new times upon the real time of happening.

History teaches us that life is always the same and essentially other.

History — or better, the historian — oscillates between the tendency to homogenize happening as a necessary condition for its full assimilation by the present, and the contrary tendency to vindicate the heterogeneity of that same happening as a no less necessary condition of its historicity.

Historical evocation is an apparition — an apparition of the second power. It is a reviviscence, a re-apparition, and this is what distinguishes historical representation from mere literary narration. When I read The Three Musketeers by Alexandre Dumas, my attention and contemplation dwell on the characters, actions, and meanings — I have no interest in knowing whether the Three Musketeers actually existed. By contrast, when I read a history book — say, about the Persian Wars — it is not enough for me to admire the mere figures of the heroes: I need to know that they existed. And so, while the intentional object of literary narration consists solely in the significant image without reference to its factual reality, in historical narration the intentional object of lived experience presents itself, so to speak, on two levels: one, the immediately apprehensible level of images and significant relations; another, the level of the real ontological strata of those figurations. The object of historical lived experience has in this way a density that the mere literary narration lacks — whatever may be, moreover, the depth and aesthetic and human excellence of the work of art. Historical life unfolds on a plane of reality; aesthetic life on a plane of trans-reality or unreality.

By virtue of the very mobility and fecundity of the time of history, one can affirm that just as the becoming of human life gives rise to capacities for artistic and scientific creation, so it also gives rise to capacities for historical intuition that, so to speak, make it possible for the spirit to transpose the interval of times and to contemplate, evaluate — in a word, to live — the past or certain privileged regions of it in their real and fresh authenticity. It is no doubt for this reason that Tovar speaks of inspiration as the mainspring of historical intuition. Thanks to the spiritual capacity to transpose the limits of time, the past incorporates itself into the psychic economy of the human being — not by virtue of a kind of external unification, but as a contribution of what has already been lived to the lived experience and to what we shall call the poetic richness of the actuality of life. It is a rescue and, like all that is the fruit of inspiration, a creation.

From all the preceding considerations there emerges as an inevitable conclusion the necessity in which the epistemology of historical knowledge finds itself — if it truly wishes to fulfill its cognitive intention — of admitting a certain dogmatism that affirms the possibility of grasping the past in its authentic reality. Which likewise implies the admission of a certain metaphysics underlying historical work that establishes, alongside heterogeneity, the universal force of life as individuation and at the same time as the possibility of spiritual and psychic communion. And for this reason, even at the risk of employing a theoretically objectionable expression, we might perhaps affirm that the epistemological attitude proper to the historian is that of a critical realism.

## DE LAS GRANDES APORÍAS DE LA HISTORIA — ON THE GREAT APORIAS OF HISTORY

The first great aporia of history consists in the fact that it must work on something that does not exist (the past), yet must affirm its existence as a sine qua non condition for its work to have an object and meaning. History must depart from the affirmation of the existence of a past in itself, as the condition of its own existence, and must deny the existence of that past — since if the past existed, it would no longer be past, and consequently history would no longer have reason to exist. And so history must effect something like a Hegelian synthesis between the thesis that posits "the past is" and the antithesis that denies: "the past is not." An aporia, contradiction, and synthesis that originate all the problematics of historical knowledge and the principal tendencies of its ontology and metaphysics: realism, which affirms the existence of the past in itself, and subjectivism or idealism, which denies the existence of that past and considers history as a mere construction of the spirit — more precisely, of the historian — who uses documents and data that in reality no longer belong to the past, since as mere material objects they stand outside psychological temporality and can be incorporated indifferently into any present.

The other great aporia of historical knowledge concerns the concept of an irreversible and unrepeatable time inherent to historical consciousness. If time were not unrepeatable and irreversible there would be no history, since history is founded on the concept that the past is past — definitively abolished. But without a certain repeatability and reversibility, there would be no historical comprehension either.

"Thus conceived, history, to be lived, requires the existence of fundamentally different structures — so different that one does not pass from one to the other by imperceptible degradations."

The third great aporia of historical investigation concerns the concept of an essentially unrepeatable happening and a radically heterogeneous time inherent to history. If human happening could repeat itself, the past would not exist — because it would be a possible present, and because in the end it would reduce to the category of physical phenomena and thus be absorbed without remainder into the uniformity governed by the laws of those sciences. If it were not radically heterogeneous — if the passage of time were a pure monotony — both the matter and the incentive of history would vanish. It is well known that the historian explores the unknown and seeks the unedited; well known, too, are the views of Fustel de Coulanges, who directed his research toward "bringing out the radical and essential differences that distinguish forever (à tout jamais) these ancient peoples from modern societies." And yet something, much, of the past must repeat itself for history to be revived. If everything disappeared from the past, nothing of it would remain — not even memory. And it is also necessary that a certain fundamental homogeneity make possible the participation of the various moments in a spiritual community that renders them intelligible and interpretable. We must not attenuate this contradiction. The historian must affirm the originality of each moment of happening (its originality is its historicity) and at the same time must incorporate that fact into one or another spiritual or psychic constant.

The historian must be realist insofar as they posit a past in itself and attempt to write it, yet must result as subjectivist insofar as they configure their knowledge and confer a meaning upon and interpret the historical happening.

"When Ranke," says Simmel, "expresses the wish to extinguish his self in order to see things as they were in themselves, in fulfilling this wish he would suppress precisely the result he expects." And he adds: "without the self, there can be no comprehension of others." Which is true — as is also the fact that the self, if it does not wish to isolate itself in a desert-like solipsism, must posit, admit, and respect the irreducible reality, the impenetrable intimacy of others. Here the problem of history is confused with the problem of all relational life, which must posit the intrinsic reality of others and at the same time subject that reality to the dispositions of interpretation and capture of one's own self. The presence of the self is a condition required by every kind of appearing — not only the historical. And so Simmel's objection, which errs by proving too much, does not invalidate Ranke's methodological principle — one that evidently does not refer to the higher form of subjectivity that makes possible the clear contemplation of the object, but to its lower forms: passions, interests, programmatic aims, that ignore or deliberately falsify or muddy the true objectivity of history.

## DE LA INTELIGIBILIDAD DEL ACONTECER Y DE LA CAUSALIDAD HISTÓRICA — ON THE INTELLIGIBILITY OF HAPPENING AND HISTORICAL CAUSALITY

According to the principle of causality, the same causes produce the same effects. And here is the great difficulty that arises when one attempts to apply this principle to historical facts. In history one can speak neither of the same causes nor of the same effects, since each historical fact is by definition unique — and since, being so, the historical procession offers an incessant novelty and is, literally, a creative procession. Causality implies lawfulness, and the institution of rigorous historical laws, if it were possible, would in reality suppress history by converting it into sociology. There is in historical contemplation, as intentional activity, something that withdraws it de facto from causal consideration: the fact that history aspires simply to see the passage of time with the things, people, and events that its incessant flow carries.

If causality is identified with the principle that the same causes produce the same effects, then no doubt that principle is not applicable to the historical becoming, since the facts, moments, or phases that constitute it are by definition unrepeatable. There can be no other burning of Rome with Nero, nor another storming of the Bastille on July 14, 1789. Nevertheless, historical lived experience requires the conception of a nexus, a link between the various states or moments of that becoming. It cannot regard them as gratuitous, discrete apparitions devoid of explanation and meaning. And so the historical mind posits, in one way or another, and therefore seeks in the concrete unfolding of life the causal connection. How to resolve that difficulty, that aporia?

Perhaps the distinction between microscopic causality and macroscopic causality can help us toward a solution.

Historical facts — or if we prefer, historical objects — are unrepeatable individual totalities, but analysis can discover within them partial sequences, elementary relations, and lines of natural causality: physical, biological, and so on. And it is evident that within those partial relations clear causal nexuses can be established — and this for three reasons: first, because these phenomena are isolable and repeatable; fire is the cause of the destruction of the city; the mechanical action of an exploding projectile can be the cause of the sinking of a ship. But these are not properly historical but physical causes. The physical cause of the Reichstag fire was an electric spark; the historical causes of that event were of a political, social, and perhaps at bottom psychological order. With which physical causality turns out to be transposed to a plane of complexity in which it appears, in a certain sense, as the effect of a movement that transcends it, a vaster dynamism. The physical cause of the terrible tragedy of Hiroshima is the explosion of the atomic bomb; its historical causes, however, reside in the general situation of the world war, in the evolution of physical theories about matter and the structure of the atom, in the calculations and technical procedures aimed at realizing the mere theories in the sphere of practice, and above all in the intention to use the results of those studies and labors for purposes of destruction and intimidation.

And here arises the temptation to erect psychological causality as the true historical causality — a temptation that must be resisted, because against a merely psychological causality one can adduce, though with lesser intensity, what was said with respect to physical causality: namely, that it may arise in reality as a result or terminus of a more complex process of economic, social, and other factors. In any case, psychological causality in history would have to be interpreted as something that in many cases overflows mere individual determination, since it corresponds to totalities in which the individual intervenes — even without losing their autonomy — in situations not definable as merely individual.

Perspectivism: isolation of certain sequences according to the historian's criterion.

Here intervenes the mentality — or if we prefer, the spiritual situation — of the historian. Since it is impossible to know and embrace in its totality the infinitely complex movement of historical becoming, it is inevitable to establish certain perspectives, certain lines, certain privileged directions of investigation. These lines and isolations — determined by the necessities and inevitable limitations imposed upon investigative work by the vastness, complication, and in reality the infinity of historical matter — depend, in their content, on the historian's interest, whether an affective or a theoretical interest, and in one way or another they break — whether horizontally or transversally — the undivided continuity of happening. And it turns out that in these more or less abstract lines, in these partial sequences, the historian seeks and finds causes — only that those causes lose their concrete efficacy as the interplay of factors grows more complex and the richness of the becoming increases, until finally, in the whole, it is rather uncertainty and apparent indetermination that prevails. A police officer will certainly discover the material author of a crime; the judge will have more difficulty fixing the responsibility of the delinquent; the sociologist will confront a problem of greater complexity in explaining it as a case of a general sociological law; and finally the historian of civilization who wishes to integrate that crime, in its typicality, into the totality of a historical moment, will have to complicate their investigation and may only arrive at the probable. In summary: the more one encompasses, the more hypothesis functions and the less empiricism, the more deduction prevails over induction.

Until finally the historian will engage in work akin to the artistic if they wish to achieve, within this innumerable and sometimes chaotic profusion of elements, a coherent and intelligible picture of historical reality.

And so we arrive at the proposition that is almost a commonplace among epistemologists of history: that history is a creation, a construction of the spirit. Only the following two things must be borne in mind: first, the materials for that construction are given by the past, and those materials are resistant and do not allow themselves to be absorbed without remainder into the plasticity of construction. Second, the historian professes as the essential formula of their vocation the respect for the past — which they regard as something in itself, and not as the mere product of their work of invention (a word that must here be taken mainly in its legal sense of discovery, a "finding").

Differential psychic causality.

"In the final analysis, every history has as its content successions of psychic events" (Simmel). Historical events would recognize this individual causality.

However, every historical singularity is a totality, a universe. Which means that the reduction of the field — whether for monographic reasons or by the spatial or temporal extent of its comprehension — does not necessarily imply a simplification of the problem of historical causality. If in order to find the causality of any given fact we had to analyze it in its partial causalities down to its ultimate elements, we would undertake an endless task, since one would never know whether one had arrived at what is truly primary and ultimate. In reality this enterprise would transcend history itself into metaphysics, since only metaphysics could know where the ultimate elements, the ultimate — or if we prefer, the first or most elementary — sequences are to be found.

If we admit with Simmel, and to simplify, "that the individual soul is the element of historical events beyond which no simpler element exists," we might perhaps attempt an analysis of historical causality by reducing it to its psychological sequences. But this reduction would not allow us a scientific reconstruction of historical causality for any given fact: first, because the psychic sequence is transposed to a historical plane that is no longer simple but complex; second, because historical structures are not decomposable since they are organic totalities. A psychological law can explain without remainder a laboratory experience, but it cannot explain without remainder human conduct, complete historical facts. The variation in sensations of weight can be explained without remainder by Fechner's law, but no abstract law alone could ever explain Napoleon's decision to leave the island of Elba in order to reconquer Imperial power. For historical facts are by nature collective and complex — not only because many wills generally intervene in them, but because they integrate an inexhaustible multiplicity of factors that cannot be expressed by a simple schema. Finally, the most important reason why historical facts cannot be expressed by any simple sequence is that historical facts are global organic totalities that surpass the mere accumulation of their elements and that possess a meaning. In any case, historical facts always display a margin of indetermination that constitutes the privilege and mysteriousness of life. Of all life.

What has been said about psychologism could be said, mutatis mutandis, of all theories that attempt to erect an abstract element of individual or social life into an explanatory principle monopolizing historical becoming. And especially it could be applied to historical materialism, which is either a debatable sociology or a necessarily deductive philosophy of history, without comprehension for the essentially spiritual factors of human evolution. Croce's critique of pragmatic history for the abstractive vice that invalidates it could be applied to all one-sided theories of history: "The explanation of history is truly such when it coincides with its own development; where explanations by way of abstract causes are essayed in history, they are nothing but a breaking of the process and an effort — the living being dead — to obtain life again by spatially fastening the fallen head back onto the decapitated trunk." Au regard de la pensée causale, il n'y a pas de terme universellement privilégié.

There is an irresistible human tendency to seek the explanatory cause of all that exists. But there also exists the human tendency to live the past as an expression of the essential creativity of life — as something new, irreducible to its antecedents, in short as something unrepeatable, that is, historical. According to which, alongside the instinct of repetition — that satisfies the yearning for universal explanation — there would exist in historical consciousness, with no less insistence, the instinct of irrepeatability, which corresponds to the intimate feeling of human time and to a kind of necessity for adventure in the journey of the spirit.

But historical becoming is not chance, nor can the historian regard it as such. The human being tends, by the essential demand of their spirit, to understand what they see — to find a meaning in the spectacle of all appearing, especially of human appearing — to decipher, in sum, the symbols, to read in the interminable flowing of physiognomies, forms, and acts that compose history. And since causality appears, from a scientific point of view, insufficient to account for human becoming and as inadequate and heterogeneous to the demand for spiritual comprehension and psychic decipherment of happening, historians have sought and created certain categories — not Kantian — of unification of historical material. Those categories are, among others, those of destiny, rhythm, form, cycle, and so on. They, in denouncing the impossibility of assimilating history to science, imply as a whole a conception of life in which the causal order is subordinated to creative freedom or to the mysterious fatality — at once tragic and triumphant — of a destiny that is not mechanism but religious necessity.

History is above all becoming, and becoming is above all changing apparition. But history is something more than becoming — it is, in the Bergsonian sense of the word, duration: that is, a reality whose idea is not exhausted in the mere kaleidoscopic succession of apparitions replacing one another, but is rather an indefinable permanence of the gone, a presence of the absent in the present, in the living actuality that receives, absorbs, and surpasses it. With which, history presupposes a creative becoming that, enriching itself with what has already been acquired, adds to all the past that pulsates within it an irreducible novelty. With which, finally, the principle of causality — if not totally abolished — suffers a fundamental limitation in the sphere of historical reality. And so at last we must see in history, as in universal life, the creative action of an élan vital that incessantly gives rise to new apparitions, new forms of life, possibilities and also problems that history itself resolves or dissipates in the course of its eternal becoming.

## DE LA COMPRENSIÓN HISTÓRICA — ON HISTORICAL COMPREHENSION

Comprehension is a mental operation very different from causal explanation. The latter is an operation of derivation or identification of the given case to the law, principle, or general theory. Comprehension, in contrast, is a semantic operation that seeks and finds meaning. Before the sight and sound of lightning in the forest, a man of science will seek the physical, meteorological law that explains it as the effect of such-and-such causes. Primitive humanity, on the other hand, will interpret it as a sign of the will of the gods or of destiny. The civilized person has a causal mentality; the primitive person has a symbolic, semantic mentality. And so, just as there are valid explanations and false ones, there are profound comprehensions and others that are fallacious and puerile.

As is readily inferred, comprehension concerns immediately the decipherment of documents (hieroglyphics, quipus, cuneiform signs, and so on) and the apprehension of their intellectual content. But in its more general sense it refers to the apprehension of the intentional content of human acts, to the final direction of historical facts — in a word, to their meaning.

What is past for us was present for those who lived it. And if it was present, it constituted an experience, an intimacy, an interior life. And clearly, the object of history consists in reliving that experience — that abolished present — as authentically as possible. Or perhaps also it consists in contemplating and judging that experience from outside, panoramically, at a distance. No doubt it consists in both. And so history would be a mixture — in whose domain what was and what is would be united in a living unity — a mixture not impure (Schelling), whose finality and essence consist in triumphing over the mere successiveness of time with the simultaneity of a contemplation in which consciousness convokes and evokes, in an indivisible act, the totality of what has happened.

In general, to comprehend is to insert an entity — a sign, a fact, a personality — into a world (or if we prefer, into a system of thoughts, ideas, feelings) in such a way that it does not appear as a foreign body but as an element of a whole, with its place, its function, its finality — according to a correspondence and connection with the ensemble that is the foundation of its significance.

The entity into which the object of comprehension must be inserted in order to be comprehended is a whole — that is, a being endowed with unity and form, i.e. with limit. A whole that, moreover, can itself be a member of a larger world. Thus, a cuneiform sign is comprehended when we can insert it into the system of signs and meanings of Assyro-Chaldean writing. A battle will be comprehended when we can interpret it as decided by a strategic plan that it simultaneously realizes and expresses.

Croce's doctrine of historical comprehension may be qualified as qualitative and functional. For him, understanding a historical fact consists in defining it specifically, differentiating it from the others, and studying its appearance as a member of a qualitative development. And since development implies expansion, the unfolding of a nucleus toward its final plenitude, strictly speaking Croce's doctrine turns out to be included within the teleological position, which regards the elements or stages of a process as instances and at the same time as results in the progress toward the fulfillment of a final form.

A higher degree of comprehension — above or completing mere intelligible comprehension (Aron) — is comprehension by affective participation, or if we prefer, by sympathy. Historical personages are human beings and their deeds are human actions. And our reactions toward them and their deeds — esteem, disesteem, admiration, disapprobation, sympathy in the strict sense, antipathy, and so on — are the same reactions with which we respond to the personality and acts of our present-day contemporaries. In this way history creates a society that surpasses the limits of space and time. But at the bottom of all these phenomena there is something more subtle — the object of recent studies — consisting in affective participation, or better in a kind of affective, divinatory identification that lives in the soul of another sometimes with admirable depth and authenticity. This affective participation (see Scheler) is no doubt at the base of historical comprehension, to the point that sometimes we can comprehend the conduct of a personage not through the way of intelligence but through the ways of the heart — whose reasons, according to Pascal, reason as a mere faculty of subsumption and logical deduction ignores.

Comprehension according to Aron: "Saisie d'une intelligibilité objectivement donnée" — the grasping of an objectively given intelligibility.

In any case, historical comprehension is an introspective capture of the lived experiences of others, and a reminiscence of them in the field of knowledge, of the contemplation of the past.

We comprehend, by mere intelligibility, the purpose of the Inca terraces. We comprehend, by affective participation, the gesture of Napoleon I — offering his breast to the soldiers who attempted to intercept his march on Paris upon his return from the island of Elba.

If we recall Pascal's famous thought and Bergson's profound observation that intelligence is characterized by a radical incomprehension of life, we shall see the great psychological problem of the relation between intelligence and the heart (instinct) — and consequently between the two forms of comprehension we have indicated — posited in all its intensity. Do they exclude each other, or can they collaborate? Are they heterogeneous and parallel? It seems to us that this problem can be contemplated, and in a certain measure resolved, from two perspectives: first, no doubt there are depths of interior life that intellectual light does not reach; but perhaps a long commerce with souls (teachers, professors, poets) can cause intelligence — not to replace the heart, certainly, but to help clarify human situations, thus facilitating the essential capture of the heart. Second, no doubt pure, theoretical intelligence is heterogeneous to feeling; but in the concrete life of the soul, intelligence is charged with affectivity — with instinct, if one wishes — as Bergson suggests in speaking of the fringe of instinct that surrounds the intellectual function. And in this way, from this intelligence animated and sustained by irrational forces, we may avail ourselves to comprehend and decipher the enigmas of life.

Interpretation — which consists in determining the meaning of an expression — is a form of comprehension in which causality does not intervene.

## DE LA ESTRUCTURA — ON STRUCTURE

With the purpose of resolving the problem of historical comprehension without exclusive recourse to causality — whose principle has been shown to be inefficacious as a universal mode of intelligibility — there has been devised, by Dilthey and other students of the spiritual sciences, the principle of structure, which consists in regarding historical phenomena as organic totalities whose constituents — which with certain reservations might be called elements — are not isolable but only exist in function of the whole. By which it is illegitimate to study those parts, elements, and factors in abstraction from the others that are integrated into the concrete reality of history. According to the principle of structure, historical realities are treated as complex and concrete wholes, as systems of reciprocity among components and of functional subordination in relation to an end toward which all conspire — an end that we may better call meaning, whether taken in its telic acceptation or in its semantic function.

There can exist — and do in fact exist — structures from which causality is absolutely absent: mathematical, logical, and artistic structures, whether plastic or rhythmic. These we shall call formal structures, because in them there predominates the vocation to fix themselves in configurations capable of repeating themselves as a form. As for the properly historical dynamic, the structures that configure or interpret it may include causality, but they incorporate it into the whole and thereby confer upon it a meaning that promotes it above mere mechanical impulse. All of which does not mean, however, that historical becoming is incompatible with the formal structures that are, by essence, foreign to causality. On the contrary, these forms — which may be defined as expressions of objective spirit — emerge in the course of history as condensations of the creative movement, and as such, they undergo a certain contamination from the atmosphere proper to the time in which they appear. But if they attain ideal perfection, they transcend the merely historical and offer to contemplation, to curiosity, or to human endeavor a horizon of eternity. Art historians know this very well, for when contemplating a perfect work they necessarily have to forget its mere historicity in order to appreciate it in its pure values of expression and form. Hence Elie Faure writes with justice: "The artist fixes eternity in its momentary form."

And even more: there exists a history of forms as such — only that this history, as is well understood, is foreign to the causal principle, provided one does not make abusive use of the notion of cause by identifying it with other notions such as origin, tendency, destiny, and so on. The evolution of forms is governed by other principles, among which we may cite filiation, tendency, will to form, and fate. Filiation consists of a non-causal link that relates successive forms according to a mode that, for lack of a better name, we shall call linear continuity — by comparison with the kind that obtains, for example, between the forms composing a spiral, which is so fundamental and intimate that those curves might justly be regarded, despite their different positions, as constituting a single line. In succession there can be a similar link, such that all phases of the evolution of a form might seem to unfold in an ideal space that is, in the imagination, the static representation of time.

The principle of structure means that the different historical phases of forms appear to direct themselves toward a final, eminent, invisible form that orients and confers unity upon the development. One would say that this final form is contained, germinally or virtually, in those that precede it — and it is in reality the meaning of the historical succession of forms. For which reason the principle of tendency might also be called the principle of meaning. The following propositions of Worringer illustrate this point of view: "The contrary of matter is spirit. To dematerialize stone means, therefore, to spiritualize it. Thus there stand face to face the two tendencies: Greek architecture tends to sensualize; Gothic architecture tends to spiritualize."

In that same admirable book from which we have extracted the preceding quotation, we find interesting developments relative to other principles of historical intelligibility irreducible to mere mechanical or physical causality. Among those principles we have the will to form — which seems to us intimately connected with the principle of tendency — and which designates a certain purpose or expressive intention that, through all the moments of an evolution of forms, asserts, enriches, and refines itself.

Finally, historiographers and philosophers of history often employ the notion of fate as a principle of intelligibility of happening, especially in the sphere of the great cultural expressions. The notion of fate is vague and even — at least as employed by the scholars who apply it to historical interpretation — contradictory. It certainly implies fatality, since it presupposes predetermination of happening; but — and this is what is important — it does not absolutely exclude the spontaneous creativity of life, especially of spiritual life. Rather, maintaining life within certain limits and certain directions, one would say that fate places at the disposal of creative genius only certain possibilities and not others, and that the will to form, in conformity with its fate, will choose only that which consecrates and confirms it. This philosophy of historical fate — whose most eminent and brilliant representative is Oswald Spengler — integrates with other presuppositions of a vitalist type and posits the existence of great wholes or cultural individuals that, at each moment of their development, imprint a certain meaning upon the form of the soul and upon the feeling of life.

We do not propose to apply these criteria to the concrete becoming of history — that is the work of historiography. What we attempt is solely to show the existence of formal possibilities of intelligibility that surpass or encompass causality and that interpret historical realities as beings of expression, whose treatment allows us to perceive — in the very appearing and in the beyond that it suggests — a psychic and spiritual ground of meaning: whether in the creations of individual genius, or in the great complexes of historical movement: cultures, epochs, the formation and decline of nationalities, and so on. A mode of interpretation of the nature of the organic that at bottom appeals to symbolic thought — which, as Huizinga asserts, "does not seek the union between two things by following the hidden paths of causal connection, but finds it suddenly, by means of a leap: not as a union of cause and effect, but as a union of meaning and finality."

We shall end this brief chapter by observing that all these categories, in reality, appeal to and in the end reduce themselves to a more encompassing principle: the principle of structure, which regards historical becoming not as a mere happening included in the course of inorganic nature and therefore abandoned to the blind play of mechanical forces, but as a manifestation of life in which the entelechy of finality and form prevails.
