## LA UNIDAD SUCESIVA — THE SUCCESSIVE UNITY

A Synopsis on the Theme of Time

Having consulted the doctrines of the great thinkers of time and having listened closely to our own feeling for life, we come away with the impression that it is not possible to enclose in a formula that living reality which is time — that time is not a concept that can be subsumed under another concept and thereby incorporated into the system of pure intellectual knowledge. So that only a description might perhaps be capable of transferring its intuition into the forms of outward language. But what would this description describe? Time, no doubt. But how would it describe it, if we did not already possess a sense, a certain original knowledge of its intrinsic reality? And indeed we do possess that knowledge, whether veiled or evident; only we cannot express it in the words of ordinary language, built chiefly around space. Like Saint Augustine, when no one asks us we know what time is, and when we are asked we do not know. And indeed, in the descriptions, approximations, and suggestions concerning time — the time of life, not abstract time — we find this ineluctable distance between experience and its expression, an expression that is always, one might say necessarily, inadequate. Perhaps only poetry, and above all music, could speak, in the language of sensible forms, the language of time. A language that ought to give us something more than the mere fluidity of its sound or its images: the possibility of reconstructing, though on a plane clearer and higher, what, still unknowing it at bottom, we already knew.

[p. 110] But this would amount precisely to attaining an explicit knowledge of that implicit and profound knowledge; for the mind does not cease in its effort to find an intellectual equivalent for the obscure lived experience of time. And thus an interminable interrogation is prolonged, a never-attaining, a longing that perhaps corresponds to the intrinsic essence of reality, which in that very interrogation is at once reflected and sought — in other words, the being of time: suspension, tension, possibility. There would thus be an interrogative ground not only in the sphere of its theoretical knowledge, but in its very root. Time would be, within this concept, the eternal question, the eternal dissatisfaction of the real, the universal restlessness that drives change, because it aspires to a light and a plenitude it does not attain. "Our civilization," writes Malraux, "is set apart from those that preceded it, because it is not affirmative but interrogative" (1). This would explain, at least in part, the dominant importance of the theme of time in the modern era, since it is perhaps in that very theme that the interrogative aspect of existence is marked with singular intensity.

But the intelligence is formal by its very nature; it does not live within the intimacy of the real, but merely orders it, classifies it, and to a certain extent constructs it according to "a spirit of geometry" that renders it conceivable and, moreover, manageable. The intelligence only defines aspects, that is, abstractions, but it cannot define the concrete, nor can it mint the word that, like a coin, would bear the image of time. "Life is not a vessel but a flame," said Philo of Alexandria, in terms that, in their affirmation and their negation, might be applied to time — not to define it, but to suggest symbolically the undefinable reality of its being.

This metaphor of the illustrious Philo turns our thought toward the idea of becoming, that mode of being of existence so profoundly bound to time that it is at times confused with it. And thus we come to say: time is the becoming in which what is abolished subsists and in which what has not yet come casts itself forward, hypostasized, [p. 111] into the future. What is abolished and what has not yet come are two nonexistents, two absences that combine with the presence of the present in a mobile simultaneity of three members: time. And thus, in stating these ideas, we realize that only thanks to time can we conceive of becoming. According to which time does not derive from becoming; rather, on the contrary, and phenomenologically speaking, it is in truth the original phenomenon.

Absolute change — that is, one in which appearances and abolitions bear no relation whatsoever to one another — is inconceivable, since in every change we introduce, inevitably, the relation of succession that already belongs to time. And since we not only conceive of becoming as succession, but live it as the reciprocal implication of its states, it turns out that, in reality, time thereby founds becoming itself as a stringing-together — melodic or syntactic — of elements. That is to say, both the succession of changes and the inner continuity of sense that transcends them and, essentially, governs them, are in the end resolved in time. Resorting, inevitably, to a contradictory language, we might say that time is the being that ceases to be by being. It is the being that is born and perishes without ceasing to be what it is. Time would thus constitute an intermediary between pure being, which merely is, but which neither was nor will be, and pure becoming, which pulverizes existence into an infinity of moments without connection. Time introduces becoming into being and being into becoming, and thus, contradictorily, founds the real. "Time is fixed, and yet time flows" (2), writes Husserl from a phenomenological standpoint, though his formulation may legitimately be transposed to the plane of metaphysics.

Psychologically, continuity in temporal succession would arise from the reciprocal implication of the states that compose it, whereby the past persists in the present, [p. 112] now as cause, now as remembrance, now as melodic resonance, as background, as grammatical or logical connective of discourse, and so on; and the future announces and prepares itself and even intervenes in what occurs as formal cause, finality, hope, and so on — such that each moment of duration finds itself, so to speak, in tension between a past that retains or propels it and a future that attracts or repels it, in such a way, finally, that no moment is a separable, spatially definable entity, but rather the dynamic result of two opposing intentions of life. Looking at the matter more deeply, we think that one should perhaps not speak of the continuity of time, but of the continuity of life or of vital duration — a perspective within which the continuity of human time would be determined by the unity between the two extremes that, constituting the limits of life, are nonetheless indissolubly bound together: birth and death; or more clearly: the continuity of human time would consist, as Heidegger holds, in the ceaseless running of the existent, assuming in each case the open and never-concluded totality of what it is, toward death.

The human being — the being that endures — is lost and recovered in a dialectic of yes and no, in which each yes gathers up the no and affirms itself within it. And that creative alternation is the return: novelty in the repetition and retention of what has been abolished, according to a formal law that determines, throughout the whole of our passage through the world, the accents and the pauses of our life. Each yes of the temporal dialectic gathers and chooses — that is, it includes a no within its affirmation — and thus time advances, abolishing, retaining, and creating, ultimately fashioning a destiny according to a rhythm of its own that counts out the steps of its progressive oscillation.

We live, or we are, this indefinable unity that is time: a unity in which succession is transfigured into a fleeting simultaneity of what is coming, presenting itself, and passing. And that ever-renewed lived experience, never still, yet thanks to which we do not lose ourselves in the current but, on the contrary, find ourselves again — being what we were and pointing toward what is to come — is the [p. 113] present, the privileged place from which the perspectives of temporality open out, and whose notion must accordingly be clarified both in itself and in its relation to the other horizons that are descried from it.

In a broad sense, the present is equivalent to presence. In this sense, present are the affections of our body and, in general, everything that is matter for outer perception; and present too are the memories currently evoked, however much their intentional object be the past; present are the images of our fancy, and in the end our hopes and expectations belong equally to the present, in this broad sense, however much their intentional object be the future. The present is thus the total, though unstable, panorama of our life — a panorama that can be divided simultaneously at every instant of existence, and to which what was and what will be converge, constituting more or less distant and distinct horizons. And in this way the present, as vision, perception, apprehension, is nothing less than consciousness itself. Wherefore, in reality, nothing that occurs in time escapes the jurisdiction of the present, which, as the universal locus of appearing, assumes an ontological quality of central importance: in the present the real is revealed in its being and in its appearing; the present is, in short, what "is." A quality comprehensive of the totality of time, which Saint Augustine perceived with genial intuition, expressing it thus: "There are three times, past, present, and future; or more properly one should say: a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future" (3). The present possesses, then, a quality of privilege among the regions of time, insofar as it constitutes what Merleau-Ponty calls a field of presence — a designation the thinker himself clarifies when he affirms: "In my present, if I grasp it still living and with all that it implies, there is an ecstasis toward the future and toward the past, an ecstasis that makes the dimensions of time appear, not [p. 114] as rivals, but as inseparable: to be in the present is to be so always, and to be so forever" (4). Within this same circle of ideas, and on the terrain of historical epistemology, Benedetto Croce holds that if the understanding of the past is to be living and intense, the act of understanding will necessarily incorporate the past into the actuality of the spirit, and that consequently "all true history is contemporary history" (5) — history of the present.

Consciousness plays an important role in the determination of the present, and with it, the notion of simultaneity. Simultaneous are the members or elements of a plurality that occur within the same present. So that the characteristics of simultaneity would be: plurality — there is no simultaneity except of the plural — and presentness: there is no simultaneity except of the present. And it follows that between these concepts, simultaneity and presentness, there exists an essential relation of reciprocity: only the present is simultaneous, and reciprocally, the present, as a mode of time, is consciousness and apprehension of simultaneity. My present is the perception of space and of my body as situated within it; but my memories are so as well, insofar as they are elements of the plurality of impressions and evocations captured by my consciousness. We might say that the present is the privileged zone of time in which becoming is rendered simultaneous. And we shall not say that the present is the synthesis of time, because the notion of synthesis is artificial and inadequate to express the nature of true vital unities; but we shall say that the present confers upon the intrinsic successiveness of becoming a certain simultaneity, at times confused, and at other times a clear panoramic successiveness.

A restricted conception of the present is that which identifies it with action, understood as the disposition and the set of movements — whether deliberate, instinctive, or automatic — by means of which an end is pursued that [p. 115] may be foreseen or unforeseen. And since the body is, par excellence, an instrument of action, it follows that, in the end, the present comes to coincide with the disposition in which action is carried out. But the body is space and acts upon space, wherefore action, and with it the present, may be regarded as the insertion of our activity into the plane of space. In such a way that, in the present so understood, these three elements would be conjoined: action, corporeality, and spatiality.

This spatiality characteristic of the present in the restricted sense is integrated into the spatiality we shall call ideal, belonging to the present in the broad sense, and which, as we have already indicated, consists in the panoramic vision or in the global and indivisible lived experience of our inner world.

The present that we shall call panoramic allows us to contemplate temporal distance and to evaluate, subjectively or by chronological reference, the intervals that lie between the moments of accentuation of life or of history. Generally we live, or imagine, the distance that separates us from the past: 20 years, 50 years, 1000 years, and the feeling of that distance is contemplative, perhaps nostalgic, a feeling that tends to join with those of fleetingness or slowness, depending on the mood with which we contemplate the procession of time. But we also tend to evaluate the distance that separates us from the future, sensed or glimpsed: the examination, the arrival, death. And the feeling of that distance is dynamic, full of a tension that is now one of delay, wishing to "brûler les étapes," now one of acceleration, arousing the desire to halt time before the threat or the imminent catastrophe that seems to be rushing forward with dizzying speed. And thus the feeling of distance, together with the feelings of acceleration or delay inherent to it, implies a certain spatialization of time — a spatialization that is not, however, of the Bergsonian type: measurability, geometry, inertia — but a vital operation inseparably connected with a feeling of so radical a temporality as fear, hope, anguish, longing, and so on.

[p. 116] "Distance is not merely an interval; it is a medium, a field of union," says Georges Poulet profoundly (6) — a thought that helps clarify not only the nature of the present, but the general vision of psychic time that unites what it separates, and that thus, in separation itself, in distance itself — just as in the contemplation of the remote Galaxies — establishes the profound ontological unity of the is, the was, and the will-be as distinct yet solidary horizons of time.

In whichever of its senses, the present is a place of confluence and separation of two perspectives: the past and the future. To the past belong all the real or virtual contents of lived experience, whose intentional objects are irrevocably inscribed within the context of existence — a context that can disappear into oblivion, though in its depths such contents continue as latencies or possibilities of recollection and, consequently, may reappear in the present as second presences, given that they are not direct presences of the object but images that the object projects from its ontological absence. What is to come is given as a lack of being or, better, as the vocation of not-being toward being. And we, in our total present, confront that vocation and in a certain way provoke its fulfillment — whether by receiving the impact of things, of stimuli, of "the blows" of which César Vallejo would speak, which spark the explosion into being and appearing of what was not; or by gathering and kneading the second presences for the metamorphosis in which chance, anguish, and the creative longing to fill with an unprecedented presence the void that awaits us, or that comes to meet us, set their uncertain sign.

Time, in sum, would be a mode of being of life, of existence, or of being, characterized by the fact that within it something we shall call the present is incessantly abolished, but in such a way that it leaves a certain prolongation of itself, which links it to a new present that supervenes only [p. 117] in turn to be abolished and retained within a continuity of arisings, abolitions, and retentions. Considering time in this way, two horizons present themselves: something that is abolished, yet prolongs itself into the new present and may reappear as memory in the dimension of the was; and something that does not exist, but has a certain right to existence and to being: the will-be. So that the present is the point of separation, or of metamorphosis, between what comes and arises and what passes and departs. What is coming becomes is, and what is becomes was, becomes been. And thus the is of the present is a bridge between two nonexistents: the "will-be" that is not, yet aspires to, or has a right to, being; and the "was" that is not, yet can no longer cease to be, insofar as it was.

The duration of a temporal object that does not change (Husserl's unveränderte Zeitobjekt) would seem to fall outside this description; a closer look, however, dispels this apparent inconsistency. Suppose the blast of a factory whistle, producing a homogeneous sound, one that would seem immutable (unveränderte). Faced with that sound, our primary impression is not of something static, but of something that, while remaining the same, passes. And this may be explained by the fact that our sensory impression (present) goes on prolonging itself while at the same time turning into a psychological state that is no longer sensory, but that clings to the sensation that supervenes, which will in turn become an echo of itself; and so on until the whistle ends. Looking in another direction, we expect the sound to continue or to end, and thus our present is no longer purely sensory, but is integrated with the retention of what passes and the protention of what will be. And in this way, finally, the present acquires an extension, and is not a point but something like the segment of a line. This extended present we call duration. In short, to be in time is to pass, to change position, or to disappear, or to renew oneself, or "to go on having been." Always, even in cases where the temporal object apparently does not change, an intimate change occurs in the ontological status of the matter that flows within it. When we contemplate a star [p. 118] motionless in the firmament, we have the impression that a kind of continued creation maintains it in existence — a creation whose continuity gives us precisely the sensation of becoming. "Thus," writes Husserl, "sound in its duration is given as something that does not change; but within this very duration the unchanging sound undergoes an alteration that does not concern the content, but rather the mode in which this content is given within duration" (7).

The present is a limit; one might say it is like a shore where, instant by instant, the wave of life comes to expire. In reality, at every moment, the present in which we live could be the last, could mark the final term of time, the end of everything. Why not? And this possibility instills in us a profound feeling of precariousness, as though existence, at every instant, faced the abyss. But immediately, before the incessant, gratuitous arising of the new, before the inexplicable yet evident persistence of what flows and passes, we are overtaken by a sense of miracle, and we are exalted by the idea that the source of existence is inexhaustible, that an infinite production of being will go on filling, always, the void of what is not yet.

The great difficulty in achieving a clear intellectual representation of the structure of time consists in the fact that it necessarily presupposes becoming, while at the same time, immanent within that very succession, there is a quality that surpasses it, inasmuch as it implies the retention of what passes and something like the recovery of what is lost within the global orientation of what endures. Thinking along these lines, Bergson compares real duration to a melody. But we consider that Bergson did not complete this musical comparison, because he did not grant due importance to rhythm — "system of instants," a scheme of the temporal figure in which duration renews itself while at the same time modulating itself according to certain frequencies, thereby confirming and, in a way incomprehensible to pure logic, surpassing the irreversible flow of life.

[p. 119] And in this way we come to the consideration of rhythm as a mode of being inherent in temporal succession.

There is in everything temporal a characteristic to which neither the immortal Bergson, nor the existentialists, nor the phenomenologists have granted sufficient attention or interest — a characteristic that overflows mere immediate psychological experience, that affects the very essence of original temporality, of time in itself, and that might be defined as a kind of need for the periodic breathing of what is going on having been. A characteristic that reveals itself, in the end, in the universal psychic phenomenon of rhythm.

We hold, then, that an essential character of time is pulsation, or more exactly, rhythm — which must not be interpreted as a mere repetition of elements at more or less measurable intervals, but as a true figure of succession. Rhythm is a formal element of time that disciplines its flowing matter and links together the distant moments of duration, while nonetheless maintaining the intervals and thereby establishing an order that is no longer pure succession but a distribution of its moments. This conception of rhythm, which Bachelard develops in his admirable book La dialectique de la Durée (Paris, 1950), allows us to understand certain concrete lived experiences in which several rhythms are superimposed, composing a veritable thickness of time, and also the possibility that the gaps interrupting the flow of human time may be overcome by means of a higher formal continuity: the formal continuity of rhythm, which incorporates the voids, the silences, the pauses within a unitary lived experience. There is no time without pause, and the pause is a vibrant void in which what has occurred comes to rest, and the advent of temporal arising is prepared. Poetry is a succession of accents and pauses, a dynamic dialectic, in short a rhythm that, while governing the course of words as external objects, regulates without rigidity the ebb and flow of the emotive wave. Whereby we see that rhythm performs a function of mediation, or if we may put it this way, that rhythm effects a kind [p. 120] of osmosis between the sensory — in this case sound, with its intensional reference to the external — and the strictly subjective inferiority of life, of the soul.

The foregoing observations, especially those concerning rhythm, allow us to understand the reason that justifies applying the word time to apparently heterogeneous objects. We speak of the time of spring, of the time of the waltz; recalling Ecclesiastes (III, 1-8), we say: "There is a time to be born and a time to die, a time to plant and a time to reap, a time to weep and a time to laugh, a time of mourning and a time of celebration," and so on, and we call time the span of duration granted to our occupations, obligations, and pleasures. And this is because "time," in all these cases, is equivalent to life — as season, rhythm, occasion, tension, and so forth. Wherefore the heart beats within life, which symbolically rises and falls, and whose incessant throbbing — the fundamental iamb, according to Claudel — sustains the agonic continuity of our bodily becoming; it is the great metaphor of life, and with it, of time.

The journey is another good metaphor of time, and not solely because a journey is change. It is so because it allows us to perceive, in their taut simultaneity, the three members of the temporal structure: the was, the is, the will-be. On a journey, every city, every port, is both a goal and, at the same time, a step. As a goal attained, it contains the past within itself and brings it to a close; as a step, as a transit, it is possibility in the process of realizing itself, and is therefore a future that is coming to be. In this way every present is an arriving, a pausing, and a going on. And, in the end, the journey is an accumulation of "souvenirs," not only as material objects that are preserved, but as spiritual objects reserved for future remembrance. So that the great future of journeys is not the new city to which the train carries us, but the future of the past, in which memories will fill the space of evocation with the resonance of nostalgia. And thus, in a journey, past, present, and future are nothing but the components of that changing actuality that moves between two absences: [p. 121] the one created by the abolition of the present, the other dissipated as it becomes material along with it.

This metaphor of the journey, whose spiritual efficacy transports the mind beyond mere immanence, opens onto another plane of meditation in which, perhaps, we may be granted to contemplate what Gabriel Marcel (8), moved by this same image, calls, in inspired language, "fixed stars in the sky of the soul." And here we find ourselves at the decisive moment, in which we must pass, or leap, from the world of subjectivity to the sphere of extra-conscious objectivity — a speculative or spiritual step that no thought aspiring to grasp somehow the intrinsic reality of things can avoid. But here too we encounter the fatality and the contradiction inherent in all knowing. To know is to go out of oneself in order to apprehend things as they are (we might say it is an ecstasy); but it is also an operation of reduction, or of assimilation, of the object to the representation of the subject. Pure realism (9) is enraptured in the perceived objectivity; phenomenological or critical reductivism confines itself to reduction, to mere subjective intuition. And both schools disappoint us — not in what they affirm, but in what they forget or exclude: realism forgets that the one who knows is, in the end, the self; reductivism systematically excludes the sense of reality inherent, implicit in the very intentionality of its intuitions, and contents itself with the description of forms or contents that isolate its existential possibility. And so neither realism nor reductivism satisfies the cognitive longing of man, who, as a being at once internal and external, aspires to know what is in itself and, at the same time, to grasp it as something that can be incorporated or assumed into his inner world as nourishment, as a stimulus, or as a resistance. The soul needs the other in order not to become absorbed in solitude, and needs [p. 122] to transform the other within the inner unity of life, so as not to feel itself lost in the desert of inanimate otherness.

Man possesses interiority, but has a vocation toward the external. And for this reason, in knowledge, as in every authentic existential possibility, he must, if he wishes to live the act of knowing with metaphysical fullness, assume both extremes, both poles of life. Whereby it can be seen that, in reality, the terms internal and external, in the phenomenon of authentic knowledge, are two abstractions that the spirit must overcome, living jointly the two extremes of its possibility.

The growth of plants, the alternation of the seasons, the succession of geological periods — all this gives us an intense impression of time; it is time. But it will be objected that this is an apparent time, arising from the projection of subjective temporality onto the outer world. It will be said that spring returns because we saw the previous spring, that the Tertiary terrain is prior to the Quaternary because we interpret the spatial order of geological strata in terms of temporality. But if it were true that cosmic time is a projection of our subjectivity, then we should have to attribute to the universe the memory and the expectation that are essential components of human time. And that is not what happens; rather, we directly perceive exterior cosmic time as change, heterogeneity, and periodicity. If the evidence of that perception is denied, it is by virtue of a parti pris of solipsism, of a zeal for isolation that insists on separating human reality — which is essentially consciousness — from the universal context in which it arises. If consciousness is regarded as pure subjectivity and the universe as pure objectivity, it is evident that there is between them a fundamental ontological difference; it is a situation of confrontation. But if we think that consciousness, or, if we prefer, conscious life, the soul, is an existent included within general existence, then it will not seem strange to us that consciousness participates in and registers the movement and the pulsation of universal time. And more than that: one might say that the elementary, primitive time of consciousness is modeled upon [p. 123] the rhythmic alternation of cosmic changes. Hedwig Conrad-Martius writes with admirable concision, referring to the universal and psychic existence of time: "It is not the self that walks along a world temporally at rest; rather it is the universe — which normally also includes all human consciousness — that advances from one moment of actuality to the next" (10).

We do not intend to develop the question of knowledge at length; that is not the object of this study. But it seems to us indispensable to fix certain points for the better understanding of our ideas. The idea that time is a mere mental synthesis, a subjective form foreign to the reality of things in themselves, leads directly to idealism, and from idealism to its most extreme expression: solipsism, which implies a peremptory denial of the outer world. But it turns out that beneath the apparent simplicity of solipsism there stir the very problems it seemed to have eliminated, and, most curiously, exteriority reappears in a form that might be called heavier still, since the sensations, ideas, and images that the solitary self perceives are not pure consciousness but objects of consciousness — objects that, insofar as they are independent, insofar as they are presences, are essentially exteriority, an internal exteriority if you will, but one that arises as a spectacle existing in itself before the pure contemplation of the self. If, on the contrary, with a saving decisiveness, we admit the existence of something outside consciousness, yet in relation to it, we shall not say that the ontological mystery disappears, but we shall say that many of the difficulties arising from the reductive zeal of gnoseological idealism are dispelled. It is precisely this revealing iteration that makes possible union, and still more, participation through knowledge, emotion, and action in spiritual and cosmic reality. And thus, in general, and not only on the question of time, it is legitimate to think that consciousness can attain adequate apprehensions of the intrinsic structure of the real, and that it need not be fascinated by the mirage of its own [p. 124] projection into the void — we might say, into the desert of nothingness.

And here lies precisely the drama of absolute idealism: it has begun by abolishing the otherness of those entities that lay claim to an autonomous existence with respect to the pure subjectivity of the self. But it turns out that this abolition is of practically no use, because the self finds that these entities, whatever they may be, resist the self's all-encompassing dominion, whether in the domain of knowledge or in the domain of action. And idealism then finds itself obliged to restore the state of otherness — of objectivity, if we prefer — of the entities it had thought to abolish by absorbing them into the domain of subjectivity. In other words, the limits of the self reappear in fact, though not in the manner of the void surrounding the roundness of the earth, but under the guise of resistance, and even more, of opposition — which, however, as such, offers the self the possibility of conquest, and consequently the possibility of incorporating the opposed into its own dominion.

Something similar happens with time as happens with the representation of the sensible world. We do not doubt that ferns, with their color and their forms, already existed in the Devonian period, 350 million years before us, and we hold this conviction notwithstanding that no one could then have beheld those plants; likewise, we do not doubt that the pre-Cambrian period was prior to the Cambrian, notwithstanding that in those ages there existed no geologist at all. It is a natural movement of the mind to conceive the intrinsic temporality of that relation, and if we attempt to abolish it by regarding it as a mere mental synthesis, we shall arrive only at confusion and at nothingness, since time, which implies a true change of being, cannot be eliminated, not even in our thinking of the history of the world. Either we think that history according to intrinsic modes of succession, and hence of temporality, or we cannot think it at all. Doctor Honorio Delgado speaks to us, with unerring intuition, of the "temporality of external facts," of the "time of the world" [p. 125] (11). And this is because, in short, as Jean Wahl puts it so well: "No doubt time cannot be perceived unless one is a person; but there is an order of before and after that is independent of the individual" (12).

It is not, then, exact to say that our conception of cosmic time is constituted by way of a projection of subjective, human time. The cosmos is life, and therefore rhythm, time. We, insofar as we are living beings possessed of natural life, participate in numerous cosmic rhythms: the alternation of day and night, lunar cycles, the seasons of the year, and so on. Psychic and spiritual life emancipates itself from these rhythms and tends to create new frequencies, new rhythmic figures, in the work of the intellect, in art, in mysticism, in the unfolding of historical life. But it is by means of the images of cosmic alternations that we symbolically express — poetically, one might say — the stages, the tensions, the situations of our inner life and of our historical experience. Day, night, dawn, dusk express, not only in literature but in the immediate feeling of existence, and with intense veracity, psychic and spiritual states. And this profound aptness, this admirable veracity of the symbol, derives from the fact that between cosmic images and the soul there is a profound connection of time — as though the rhythm of the universal oscillations contained something like a poetic possibility in which the cosmos and the soul take part (1).

If time is a structure of becoming, according to which what becomes is not utterly annihilated but in a certain manner persists and constitutes itself as a member of a series and [p. 126] of an order; if time is change subject to rhythm, then it is evident that in the cosmos there exists time and, even more, that cosmic life is essentially time. According to which, in the end, time, by means of rhythm, would be the bridge whereby the external gains access to the internal, and whereby, conversely, psychic interiority participates in the poetic renewal of the world.

In the end, we have sought to sum up and to signify the content and the final intention of these pages by saying that time is successive unity; and in doing so we make use of a notion that is logically imperfect, since unity, as a mere logical species, thereby excludes both division and successiveness. Yet it happens that life is division, time is succession, and neither of these great realities of existence can be thought — still less effectively lived — apart from the idea and the feeling of unity. As for time, were it merely succession excluding unity, it would be confounded, even more thoroughly, with pure becoming as kaleidoscopic change, whose instants bear no reciprocal relation of any kind whatsoever. And that is not what happens with time, in which each moment — or rather, each present — even as it in a certain way repels the present that precedes it, takes that present up so as to insinuate itself into the one that follows, and is carried along with it in its fall; and so on, in an indefinite process. Wherefore we think that in concrete time there exist no true instantaneous presents, but only extensions of time within which moments succeed one another, the phases of duration, while at the same time coexisting, in a manner difficult to describe, the horizons of the temporal triad. Those extensions of time are units of time which, as they pass, articulate with the other successive units in the great implication of vaster times, since every time is composed of times, and because times are always times of time.
