## LA MEMORIA EN LA CONCIENCIA MÍTICA — MEMORY IN MYTHIC CONSCIOUSNESS

Memory, considered from the domain of mystical consciousness, takes two principal forms: one consists in the recollection of images belonging to the pre-human origins of the world and of life; the other, in the personal recollection of successive existences which, according to certain religions, the human being traverses in the course of time, across the centuries. It is worth noting that in both cases the word and notion of memory presuppose a certain extrapolation onto the extra-psychological plane of a religious order of experience, though in its fundamentals the concept of memory as the faculty of re-actualizing the past — whether in image or in action — retains its validity both in everyday psychology and in the perspective of mythic consciousness. No person in our present civilized society could remember the moment when Prometheus stole fire from Jupiter, or the moment when Pallas Athena sprang forth armed from the head of the Father of the Gods. Perhaps not even the post-Homeric Greeks themselves considered such recollection possible, for to them the myth had descended to the rank of a mere literary fiction of symbolic character. But the archaic man does believe himself capable of remembering certain events from his cosmogonic legends, and more still: he believes it possible to repeat the divine or heroic exploit through the efficacy of rites performed according to formulae of a magical character.

To all of this, and by way of both complement and clarification, it seems pertinent to add the following: beyond the two forms of memory we have mentioned, which are representational in kind, there exists a third which, by reason of its predominantly emotional character, we shall call pathic memory — thanks to which there arises in the soul a certain stirring, a sense, or rather a re-experience, confused yet profound, of the feeling of life proper to the originary world and to the archaic mentality, that is to say, the essentially mythic mind.

The individual type of mythic recollection which we study first for methodological reasons may be regarded as a kind of existential pluralism, since it admits of a plurality of successive existences capable of being evoked — so that death is nothing other than a passage from one world to another, or more precisely from one existence to another, from past life to present life, which will in turn pass into another, and that into others indefinitely: a manner of conceiving the succession of human existences that forms the spiritual foundation of great religions such as Buddhism, Brahmanism, and Orphism, and that poses difficult and profound questions to the ontology and psychology of human reality. As an example of this belief, we shall cite Diogenes Laertius, in whose book on "The Lives of the Most Illustrious Philosophers" we find the following references to the various incarnations of Pythagoras: "that while he lived he remembered all things, and after death preserved the same memory; that in time after his death he passed into the body of Euphorbus and was wounded by Menelaus. That being Euphorbus, he said he had at another time been Ethalides, and had received from Mercury the gift of transmigration of the soul, and that he effectively migrated and circulated through all manner of plants and animals; foreseeing moreover what his soul would suffer in Hades, and the rest detained there. That after Euphorbus died, his soul passed to Hermotimus, who, wishing likewise to confirm this, went to Branchidae, and entering the temple of Apollo, showed the shield that Menelaus had consecrated there, and declared that when returning from Troy he had consecrated his shield to Apollo. That after Hermotimus died he passed to Pyrrhus, the Delian fisherman, and remembered all things anew: how he had first been Ethalides, then Euphorbus, then Hermotimus, then Pyrrhus, and finally Pythagoras, and he recalled all that we have mentioned."

As for the Brahminic religion, whose mythology is somewhat confused, we have little to add, since it is amply known that the transmigration of souls is one of the most fundamental and widespread beliefs of that religion, so that what we have noted, following Diogenes Laertius, with respect to Pythagoras, may be applied mutatis mutandis to the beliefs of the Hindu religions.

It therefore does not seem superfluous to refer here to the figure of the Buddha as an eminent and highly significant embodiment of the philosophy of reincarnation, proper to the Oriental — and especially Hindu — feeling for life. In Buddhist doctrine, the Buddha possesses memory not merely of some but of all his previous existences, unlike other sages and yogis who may be acquainted with a considerable number of their reincarnations, but not the totality. The Buddha would thus be omniscient, for he enjoyed a universal knowledge; and to know is to remember, since it consists in recognizing, point by point, all the intellectual and affective experiences lived through the course of time.

The question of whether men exist capable of having experiences comparable to memory and related to human or pre-human origins — more precisely to the universal cosmogony: creation, the struggles of gods and elements, and so forth — is an insoluble question for us, civilized Westerners who regard myths as mere symbolic fictions of the collective imagination, or, at best, if myths are an integral part of certain religions (Orphism, Hinduism), as objects of meditation and nothing more.

Nevertheless, as an important qualification of the above, we may cite the case of Pythagoras, who would have been in one of his numerous incarnations Hermotimus, a possible witness to the Trojan War; by which reckoning the philosopher, through Hermotimus, might have been able to recall — centuries later — the ruin of the Asiatic city, and to evoke Achilles, Helen, and even certain Olympian gods who personally intervened in the conflict. A possibility that might prompt the objection that the Trojan War is not the origin of the world but a historical event confirmed by recent archaeological discoveries. An objection we can readily dismiss, arguing that even though that war was a historical fact, it was laden with mythic elements constituted above all by the repeated intervention of the Olympians.

And here seems the fitting place to insist that in this essay we do not claim to affirm the existence of a memory that recalls events that never occurred, but only to study the psychological character of the lived experience (possible above all in archaic man) whose intentional object is those fictitious events supposed to have taken place at the origin of time. The existence of peoples such as India, which believe with absolute certainty in transmigration, confirms us in the view that such experiences are possible; for if man can regress in time indefinitely, why could he not evoke some of his remote existences in which he was witness, for example, to the already mentioned Trojan War or to the sacrifice of Iphigenia at Aulis? Unless it be that certain myths lack any narrative sense and are mere symbols or parables of philosophical intent, consequently inactual in character — as, for example, the famous burning of the Khandava forest, interpreted by some Indologists as the representation of the struggle between the infra-conscious and the supra-conscious, or as the timeless exploit of the divinity in abolishing the duality that the forest represents, and causing to prevail at last, over the abolition of all variety, the sublime, the supreme total unity.

In treating the type of evocation which, for lack of a better name, we shall call cosmogonic, the first idea that presents itself to the mind is that of similarity, not to say identity, between the mode of mythic figuration — whose intentional, imaginary object is the past — and artistic figuration, more precisely poetic figuration, so much so that one would be tempted to say that myth is a form of art. But one resists this assimilating temptation, reflecting that mythic figuration, however fictitious it may be, contains a fund of belief in the factual, not to say historical, reality of its subject matter, whereas artistic consciousness attributes no factual reality to its images, but regards them as mere metaphorical symbols of subjectivity. This thesis, concerning the intimate relationship between myth and art, is corroborated when one studies the original documents in which mythic conceptions are set forth, especially those that constitute the subject of the poetic narratives of Homer and Hesiod. According to the Theogony of this poet, the true father of Greek mythology, Mnemosyne — who is memory itself — is the mother of the Muses, those benevolent and inspiring heavenly divinities of artistic creation. Gracious and celestial divinities who undoubtedly inherited from their mother the marvellous recollections of mythic times, and who inspired — through this memory of the marvellous — the tales of the Theogony, according to which before all else, that is before time, there was the Abyss, from which were born Erebus and Night, who in turn engendered Aether and the Light of Day. To these elements we must add Love, Eros, "the most beautiful of all the immortal gods, who in the heart of every God and of every man subdues the heart and the will to his dominion."

And here, having established the bond between mythic memory and the Muses, the moment seems ripe to declare that myth is a collective creation and that, despite the meritorious labors of ethnologists and sociologists, we still do not know how the creative imagination of the collective soul works, how it operates in the depths.

Another important similarity, and an important difference as well, bearing on the understanding of mythic consciousness of the past, are those that exist between history and myth. Both forms of spiritual knowledge are projected toward the past, and speaking in very broad terms one might say that myths are history: the history of the Argonauts, the history of Dionysus Zagreus — histories that naturally unfold in a mythic time. Only history takes as its object the reality, let us say material, of events, whereas myth is fiction, a special type of fiction, never wholly assimilable or reducible to genuinely historical fact. Treating the theme from the perspective of time, Ernst Cassirer writes: "The true difference in the treatment of time as between myth and history consists in the fact that for myth there is an absolute past which, as such, neither needs nor is susceptible of any extensive elucidation, whereas in history the event that stands out in the chain of becoming contains in itself no privileged or absolute point, but on the contrary, every point is susceptible of being related retrospectively without limit, so that in the past there is offered a regressus in infinitum. Myth for its part also establishes a separation between being and becoming, between present and past, but it reposes definitively in the latter, in such a way that, once attained, the past is considered as subsisting in itself and withdrawn from all problematic."

"In Greek mythology" — writes Mircea Eliade — "sleep and death, Hypnos and Thanatos, are twin brothers"; and this is understandable. To sleep is equivalent to a transitory death, while to die is nothing other than definitive, eternal sleep. To these dualities a third must be added, perhaps the most important, namely that which in the great mythologies assimilates death to forgetting. The death-forgetting duality interests us in a special way by reason of its close relation to the object of our study, since, in reality, forgetting is also a twin brother — though an enemy — of both memory and death. To remember is to live, and consequently to die is to forget. And if it is true, as Bergson believes, that consciousness (life) is memory, and that it is materiality (let us say death) that instills forgetting in us, then it will not seem strange to us — on the contrary it will seem entirely logical — that archaic man should identify death with forgetting, above all when he contemplates the lifeless materiality of mortal remains.

Now again the fear assails me that I am engaged in a labor of Sisyphus: for if, as depth psychologists and ethnologists affirm, a certain mythic state is prior to the exercise of the fabulating or figurative function, can one speak of memory at all? What would that memory recall? We believe, nonetheless, that it is legitimate to speak of mythic memory — not in the sense of anecdotal or historical recollection, such as that of a soldier remembering the Battle of the Marne or a sailor the Battle of Angamos, but of a memory of another kind that would possess the capacity to clothe the pure nakedness of the image in the illusory garment of recollection. It believes it remembers what it invents. It appears that the mythic figurations arising from the collective mentality are gathered and stylized by poets, priests, and mages, and that they then migrate across the centuries, not as mere repetitions but fresh and authentic, as though carried beyond time by the imperishable spiritual wind of the archaic soul.

I remember once having heard a song in a small village in Cajamarca, sung by an indigenous woman of rare inspiration. It was a song so intense, of so mysterious a depth, that it produced in me an indescribable impression. It was like a mystical lament, something akin to nostalgia, a reminiscence, a memory. A memory of what? I do not know. But in all of it there was something of memory, a recollection of something I had not personally lived, yet which existed within me like a relic, like a vibrant echo of the past. I would say that it was a crypto-memory, buried yet alive since the most remote origins of humankind, whose recollections stirred confusedly before the magical enchantment of the song — and so this experience makes me perceive with clarity that while historical memory is above all an image-memory, mythic memory is a pathic memory; and for this reason, when we recall a historical event pathically, we convert it, without realizing it, into myth — that is, into an archetypal and trans-historical figure, more than historical, situated in a time which by its vital significance already belongs to the world of origins.

The great German poet Hölderlin possessed a kind of pathic memory that made him feel, or re-feel with solemnity and passion, the psychic life of archaic Greece — that is to say, mythic Greece.

The poet translated that feeling, in reality inexpressible, in marvellous form, and his descent into madness was perhaps nothing other than a return to the original darkness, which thus became not only the source but something like the final refuge of his mysterious inspiration.

Analyzing the Hypnos-Thanatos equation, and placing it in relation to forgetting conceived as a prefiguration of death, we accede to a level that already transcends the purely psychological theme of forgetting and raises the soul toward planes of contemplation higher and more transcendent. The Greeks assimilated forgetting (amnesis) to death, and speculating between life and death they arrived — above all within the occult, mystery-oriented societies and eschatological doctrines — at certain conclusions that disconcert our mentality and psychology somewhat: it turns out that these sects come to assimilate what we call life to ignorance — a synonym for forgetting — and to affirm that death is the true awakening to memory and to the living experience of truth and light. Wherefore our body would be, as the Orphics said, a tomb in which the soul lies buried and in darkness.

So we witness a kind of spiritual conversion in which life appears as death and death as true life; and what clashes most sharply with our psychology and our traditional feeling for life is that our earthly existence — with its urgencies, difficulties, and desires — appears as a form of forgetting, while forgetting itself (or death) appears as the awakening from life's deceptive dream.

Plato regards the knowledge of ideal archetypes (Ideas) as reminiscences — an attenuated way of saying recollections — which evoke a metaphysical past in some sense prior to time. A doctrine that implies the conception of a crypto-memory functioning beneath the surface memory, where images are stored, or sleep. Images that may be awakened by virtue of the dialectic or of the erotic ecstasy that draws a man out of himself and raises him to the sphere of transcendence and the absolute.

But in this myth-dream-forgetting relation, a phenomenon occurs which we might call the sublimation of the concept of forgetting — in numerous philosophical and mystical schools of Greece, Egypt, and Asia (Orphics, Neoplatonists, Gnostics) — consisting in this: that forgetting is no longer the amnesia of external perceptions or of the anecdotal or properly historical aspects of life — memories of youth, of travels, of readings — but forgetting of the self, forgetting of one's own ontological intimacy; so that what we call life is equated with forgetting, while death is consecrated as the passage toward light, and in reality toward the remembrance of the world of ideal and supreme essences.

Thus through these pages, initially dedicated to mythic memory, we arrive at the thought of death — which is the fatal reality par excellence, that is to say the least mythic thing conceivable. Yet we do not know for certain what death is, nor what life truly is, since according to the Platonists death is the access to memory, while life — "what we call life" — is the fall into forgetting, that is, into death. The Spanish poet Rufo already said as much, in verses that retain their emotional force despite their austere laconism:

Life is a long dying, / And dying the end of death.

And the Orphic poet Shelley leaves us these enigmatic lines from his marvellous poem "Prometheus Unbound," which produce in us a confused impression of hope and anguish:

Death is the veil which those who live call life: / They sleep, and it is lifted;

This forgetting identified with death, which we are considering as an outgrowth of our study of mythic memory, appears at first glance to be an incoherent and therefore senseless sequence from the original premises of this work. But it is not so, because if — as the Orphics and Neoplatonists maintain — death is forgetting, or if we prefer, the supreme remembrance, then there is nothing strange in including the thought of death within the theme of memory, since forgetting is, as one might say, a twin brother though an enemy of memory. To die is to forget, but it is also, on a different plane, a form of recollection — whether in Hades or through transmigration, which is a forgetting of previous existences or a discontinuous memory that erases the lethal transitions. In this regard it is fitting to recall the figures of the Buddha and Pythagoras who lived and died in memory, while also bearing in mind the unprivileged remainder of mortals who live in a punctiform present and who, at death, forget all, or rather forget themselves — whether in the light of ecstasy or in the absolute darkness of total absence.

I have had no puerile pretension of exhausting in these short pages the multiple sociological, ethnological, and metaphysical aspects of the theme of mythic memory. I have confined myself to describing, within the limits of my knowledge, the perspective and the imaginative horizon of consciousness in its relation to myth; and in so doing my endeavor has been more interrogative than illustrative. And in truth I do not know whether I have attained any teaching through this inquiry. But I am certain of having arrived, through the thought of death, at the edge of the abyss of which Hesiod speaks, inspired by the Muses — an abyss that is not merely cosmological, a synonym for chaos, but of psychic nature: the abyss where ignorance, divination, and anguish dwell together in confusion. And perhaps something more: that "desperate hope" experienced by don Miguel de Unamuno.

I also wish to place on record that I have not sought to invade the religious sphere, except naturally when it was unavoidable by reason of its at times unbreakable connection with the world of myth. Religion is a doctrine of salvation oriented toward the future; myth is a phenomenon of symbolic vision whose point of departure, or rather whose foundation, lies in a certain feeling of the originary past. Myth is a remembrance that bears upon the earthly destiny of man; religion is a hope that concerns itself essentially with the beyond.

In sum, if the abyssal ground of the soul is constituted by the tragic tension of the opposites, life and death, which transmute reciprocally each into the other, I believe the most adequate way to close these interrogative meditations will be to recall Fragment 88 of Heraclitus (Diels), which we do not know whether it lifts or leaves suspended, as in a theater, "the veil that men call life." And that fragment reads thus:

"It is the same thing to be living and dead, awake or asleep, young or old; for by change the one is the other, and by change the other is in turn the one."

In summary, in our view: if by memory is understood the psychic capacity to represent to consciousness, in more or less faithful images, what has already been perceived, then it is evident that one cannot speak in the strict psychological sense of "mythic memory," since myth is a fiction and, as such, belongs not to the sphere of memory but to that of creative imagination. But if, in a broader and deeper sense, memory is understood as the capacity to relive — without imaginative representation, and purely in an affective mode — the psychic states of the past that may have affected consciousness in more or less remote ages, then we can indeed speak of mythic memory, and maintain that its experiences may contain confused but in a certain sense historical experiences of mystical tendency and mythic import, as attested by Pythagoreanism and its spiritual derivation, Orphism. And it is in this sense that we have spoken of mythic memory in the foregoing pages — pages whose utility, we believe, may consist, within their limitations, in showing that by way of myth the soul may reach planes of singular significance and rare spiritual depth.
