## DE LA CAUSALIDAD Y EL SENTIDO — ON CAUSALITY AND MEANING

If we compare the causal relation to the relation of meaning, we find that the first is a relation from before to after, from prior to posterior, a relation that metaphorically we may call impulsive; the second is an inverse relation, let us say attractive, in which the future, the posterior, the yet-to-come seems to discipline the phases, the moments, the stages of the evolution that lead to its final realization. The target, which is the meaning of the projectile, attracts it in a certain sense, while the detonation of the explosive impels it. The direction of traffic disciplines the traffic, and does so not by mechanical impulsion but by telic attraction. In any case, meaning, like causality, constitutes one of two categories of intelligibility that allow us to comprehend — that is, to incorporate the fact into a rational, vital, or psychic structure.

If we are concerned with the movement of an automobile, the causal principle will allow us to explain it by analyzing the mechanisms involved in the functioning of the machine, including as mechanical elements the impulsions imparted by the driver. But the causal principle that explains the functioning of the clutch and the transmission cannot explain the rules of traffic. These belong to a different order. The arrows indicating the direction, the meaning [sentido] in which the vehicle must travel, erect themselves, above mechanical causality, upon a new plane of determination: intentional, telic. And so this example, in its schematic simplicity, enables us to understand how in history there are two orders of determination: one causal, which implies a certain quantitative and therefore calculable equivalence between cause and effect, and another of final attraction which — like the traffic arrow — guides from its transcendence the march of things toward intentional realizations. And let us not forget, finally, the third principle, which at once illuminates and keeps in mystery the dynamics of historical becoming: the principle of creativity, which constitutes the great, the ultimate enigma of history and of being, because it implies what is incomprehensible to pure logic, namely, that something should arise from nothing.

"In all that comes to us through tradition, above all through written tradition, the whole resides in the foundation, in what is internal, in the meaning and direction of the work; there lies what is primordial, divine, efficacious, intangible, and indisputable, nothing being capable of damaging that intimate, primigenial essence — at least no more than physical illness can damage a well-formed soul." According to Goethe himself, the written work must be regarded "as the body of a spiritual work."

"Let the Gospels contradict one another at will, provided the Gospel does not contradict itself."

In history, causality is not sufficient to produce the intelligibility of the facts of the occurrence that it studies. And this for two reasons that are conjoined: a) The historical occurrence cannot be attributed to a single cause nor, consequently, explained entirely by it. b) This being so, it is likewise impossible to exhaust the multiplicity of causes, factors, physical, psychological, social, and other circumstances that may converge in the production of a historical fact; apart from the fact that — and this is fundamental — as a human fact, it possesses an ineliminable coefficient of indetermination. One may perhaps determine by strictly causal criteria the elements that intervene in the psychic aspects of what Bergson would call the superficial self, but the deep self remains withdrawn from its influence and therefore cannot be an object of this analysis.

Consequently, if we wish to understand historical facts as we understand physical facts, we should have to renounce their comprehension in depth. But we have at our disposal the category of meaning, and so what is causally ungraspable may become intelligible through the intuition of its meaning. I may be ignorant of the mechanical and physical laws that propel automobiles, yet I understand why they move in one direction or another because I know the rules of traffic. We do not know the psychophysical laws that determine the movement of the pieces in a game of chess, but if we know the rules of the game, we understand the moves — not precisely by their causes, but by their meaning.

In any case, meaning implies stylization and the overcoming of causality. Amid the inexhaustible, confused complication of the causes (psychological, social, biological, physical, and so forth) of occurrence, we establish a few lines of sequence and disregard a multitude of other possible causal series; we proceed as if the river of history were leaving on its banks a multitude of objects, originally comprised within its course, but which did not enter its central and dominant current. We treat causes not in relation to their intrinsic force, but according to the degree of collaboration we assign them in obtaining an already known result. Our history books enumerate the causes of the French Revolution, of the independence of the Spanish colonies, and so on; but in reality, in the first place, one must consider that those causes are themselves historical facts with their respective causalities, and in the second place that those enumerations are influenced by the meaning — or, if we prefer, by the end to be attained — which induces us to select the appropriate causes so that the event may appear as the natural fruit of a process, as a fruit that in reality already pre-existed in our minds. Whereby meaning simplifies occurrence and institutes, above mere materiality, the intentionality of the spirit.

In every evolution that culminates in a process of human transcendence, the so-called causes are essentially members of a process of signification, offered to reading as a text whose signs interest us not for their physical or chemical nature but for their meaning, and insofar as their formal connections illuminate the inner current that animates them. Only that which is a sign belongs to the category of history; and for this reason an object is historical only insofar as it directly or symbolically expresses something human. A stone, so long as it is merely a stone, lacks historical character; it acquires it if it is a piece of flint that may be a primitive axe.
