## DE LA VOCACIÓN DE LA CULTURA — ON THE VOCATION OF CULTURE

The word vocation bears a twofold meaning, or rather there exists an intrinsic polarity in vocation as a spiritual and psychic phenomenon. Indeed, in vocation there is present at once an inward impulse of the soul, an appetite toward a determinate acquisition or realization, and on the other side, at the opposite pole, a call that issues from an authority superior to mere immediate experience and that summons the powers of the soul toward an anagogical promotion — that is, that invites them to rise, to ascend, transcending the limits of the imperfect and confused, toward the realm of the sublime and perfect.

By which token, in vocation there would be two movements that at one and the same time oppose and complement each other: one of impulsion, another of attraction; one of pure psychological spontaneity, another of obedience, of submission to the sway of transcendent forms and norms which from the spiritual world orient and direct the effort we shall call plastic, or the effort of giving configuration to life.

Vocation in its twofold meaning, in its polarity of impulsion and attraction, is an essential category in every existence that assumes true human dignity — in the individual as in society, in the singular person as a relatively solitary and unique being, and in culture as the universal domain of human vocations and realizations. And this idea constitutes the fundamental thesis of the present chapter.

So it is that vocation is a phenomenon of Aristotelian type, in which we find the appetite of matter for form, and the sovereignty of form that draws matter to itself and promotes it to the plane of configuration and value.

All these ideas acquire evident clarity when they are considered in connection with the cultural phenomenon, which, reciprocally, acquires a clear meaning when it is studied as a phenomenon of vocation — as a historical realization and mode of being of the human endowed with permanent and exalted significance in the world of spiritual values.

But before proceeding it is fitting to enter a caveat: in this chapter we shall concern ourselves only with the formal aspects of the cultural phenomenon and shall consequently set aside, as far as possible, merely material and historical facts, which will serve us principally as examples to illustrate the schemata of its essential characters.

Within this criterion, it seems to us that the principal characters of culture are the following: Cultural worlds are organic totalities, complexes of mutual impregnation; the cultural phenomenon is a phenomenon of mediation between nature and spirit, the internal and the external, the temporal and the eternal; in connection with this character, every true culture has an archetypal vocation and consequently implies, in the Platonic sense, a certain erotic; cultural activity is an activity of configuration and symbolization.

The mere enumeration of these characters makes us perceive that culture is a phenomenon of dual, or if we will, polar structure — a duality whose terms or extremes are unified in the living reality of cultural creation. Were we to schematize in a simple formula the whole ensemble of dualities and oppositions that contrast and unify themselves in cultural life, we might perhaps say, employing Latin words [words of Latin] metaphorically, that culture is the domain of mediation between humus [humus], which represents the maternal and fecund earth, and lumen [lumen], which is the light that creates forms. Wherefore the image of the tree presents itself to us as the paradigmatic symbol of culture, since the tree sinks its roots into the maternal earth, dark and nocturnal, and rises expanding its branches, its flowers, and its fruits in the solar and form-bestowing clarity of space.

On the basis of these premises we shall study succinctly what we call the configurations of culture, which represent so many possibilities of cultural vocation.

We call configurations of culture those manifestations of spiritual significance in which the creative activity of civilized man — as individual or constituted in social community — reveals itself to contemplation or to action. In each of these configurations there is naturally revealed a specific tendency, but all have in common the fact of constituting directions of one and the same human aspiration toward the realization of archetypal forms that at once limit, promote, and channel the restlessness of man.

The principal configurations of culture would be, in my understanding, the following: myth, religion, art, language, science — under which denomination we embrace the totality of theoretical knowledge — economic structures, and the ethical and juridical order. Configurations that we shall not study in detail, but only insofar as they contribute, as exemplary forms, to illuminate the idea of culture that we propose to develop in this chapter.

And with this intention we shall concern ourselves, though briefly, only with myth, religion, art, and language.

Myth is a symbolic configuration in which metaphysical entities, or the cosmic powers that constitute the germinal ground of the real, or the elementary tendencies of the soul, or the great collective experiences that appear clothed in a marvellous garb and are projected toward a certain region of the past that one would call anterior to time, are personified and presented under imaginative guises. One must bear in mind what perhaps constitutes the most important trait of mythical thought, namely its archetypal sense, since mythical figurations are not realistic but are images in which the dark germinal powers of life are idealized and stylized for admiration, love, or terror.

Myth is not religion, even though many religions are constituted with legendary and mythical elements. Religion is far more than myth; it is essentially a sentiment of the soul, a mode — which we would call ontological — of human reality, and in this sense equally, at its most radical depth, religion is more than a mere cultural configuration. But if this is so, it is likewise true that the ritual and aesthetic forms — plastic, literary, musical — in which religious feeling expresses itself are subject, at least to a certain degree, to the variations of styles and cultural currents. Yet there is a deep bond between religion and culture, and it consists in the sense of mediation that belongs to the former and that is, as it were, the paradigmatic model of every authentic cultural attitude. Indeed, in religious life there is manifested, better than in any other form of culture, the sense of mediation between nature and spirit, for religion, by its global action upon the soul, while it defines singular human reality and establishes and consecrates the love of community, draws all that is human toward the higher instances in which one might say that the cordial pulsation of man expires and at the same time is made eternal.

Art is a figuration of the being of things and of the soul that extracts the ontological intimacy of existence and unfolds it upon a surface that is not surface alone, but communication, language, symbol. The law of style fulfils itself in art with singular evidence, both as regards the individual artist and as regards the total ensemble of artistic works belonging to a determinate circle of culture. The style of the man of genius translates his most intimate personal predispositions by subjecting them to a law of form; the style of a culture — Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque — impresses upon all the creations of artists, however diverse the directions of their inspiration and genius may be, a common seal, an atmosphere, a certain something, as though above the diversity there imposed itself, without constraining liberty, a common will to form. For this reason all great cultures constitute a unitary world, albeit rich and varied; and for this reason doubtless, recalling the immortal aesthetic law of unity in variety and rightly assimilating cultural creation to the poetic work, Elie Faure was able to say that every civilization worthy of that name is a lyric phenomenon.

And it is in the cultural phenomenon of art that there is revealed in the most certain and most luminous manner the oceanic nature of the time in which the great movements of spiritual life unfold. One wave of time brought the Hellenistic style; another wave of time brings the Gothic; yet another wave brings the Baroque. The ocean of time raises a crest of brilliant foam, sustains it a moment, and then dashes it down. But the time of culture and of art possesses a privilege that the time of common life lacks, and it consists in this: that this spiritual time bears within itself an eternal message, so that the vision of the coming and going of cultures through time presents itself to us, principally by virtue of art, as a series of eternal instants in which, once and for all, above all precariousness, the absolute of perfection and of plenitude is revealed.

Language is a cultural configuration that fulfils two important functions: an expressive function and a communicative function. By means of the expressive function language externalizes psychic interiority, and does so generally through poetry which, availing itself of melody and rhythm, at once supplies the imperfection inherent in verbal signs and confers upon language a power of contagion and of magic. By means of the communicative function language transmits to others, for theoretical or practical ends, thoughts, desires, requests, commands, and the like, which requires a certain depersonalization of signs so that they may circulate, endowed with a precise signification, from mind to mind, as coin circulates. We need not say that to the manifestations of language in its expressive function the considerations we have formulated with respect to art apply without restriction.

There are, among others that we do not consider necessary to mention, two great classical conceptions of man that express themselves in two lapidary Latin formulas: Homo Sapiens [the knowing man] and Homo Faber [the making man] — conceptions which we regard as true insofar as they affirm, and false insofar as they implicitly deny or exclude. It is true that man has the vocation of knowledge; it is equally true that man has the fabricating and more precisely utilitarian itch; but it is likewise evident that these characteristics do not define the whole man, nor do they even express what is fundamental in the human, wherefore it would be desirable to possess a broader and more profound definition. We do not know what Latin formula we might institute to absorb or replace those we have enunciated and which, to employ Pascal's language, we would venture to call partialities: the theoretical partiality, the pragmatic partiality. And therefore, without proposing a formula in two words, we would say that what defines man in his most significant humanity is his being a being of vocation, a being that oscillates between matter and form, between what he is and what he ought to be; in other words we would conclude by saying that man is a being who seeks perfection, who wills to ascend — an anagogical being who lives in the creative tension between what he possesses and what he seeks.

And culture is the domain of that vital tension.
