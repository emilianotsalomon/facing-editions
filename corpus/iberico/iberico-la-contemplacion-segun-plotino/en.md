## II

## LA CONTEMPLACION SEGUN PLOTINO — CONTEMPLATION ACCORDING TO PLOTINUS

When one reads, in the Enneads, the pages devoted expressly and directly to the study of contemplation (III, 8), a first impression of bewilderment is unavoidable. For it would seem that, according to Plotinus, everything is contemplation: nature, the soul, reason, intelligence. And thus this concept, on account of its enormous extension, seems to lose its specificity. But this impression vanishes, and meditation replaces it with a more fitting and higher feeling: admiration before the depth and the vast reach of a doctrine that is at once a psychology, an axiology, and an ontology of contemplation.

What appears with immediate clarity throughout the Enneads is the supreme excellence of contemplation which, understood as the mode of existence in which being rests in itself, rising above every form of alteration and change, presents itself as the fullest and at the same time purest form to which the soul can aspire in its relation to being. Whereby, within the sphere of human life, contemplation is superior to action, and in universal life contemplation is nothing other than being itself in its most august permanence and plenitude. We find, then, a clear appraisal of contemplation, a sense of veneration for this mode of existence and of spiritual life, an estimation that can serve us as guide, as key, as clue for positively grasping its metaphysical meaning and its religious and mystical projection.

Perhaps the supreme mainspring, the most intimate psychological fiber of the metaphysical tension in the thought of Plotinus, is his love for the unity hypostasized in the Pure One. The One is the Good, the Good is the One. Supreme eminence toward which mystical nostalgia and longing tend, origin and sum of love and of being, in which the meditation of the great Alexandrian at once grounds itself and comes to rest. "Intelligence, he asserts, is the first act of the good, the first being. Outside intelligence, and circling around it, is the soul; the soul looks into intelligence and, contemplating it even in its inmost intimacy, sees, through intelligence, the Supreme God" (I. 8,2). Having thus glimpsed the goal, and having marked out the zones of existence that are inferior to it and dependent on it, the way of return is offered to the soul. And in this manner contemplation affirms and exalts itself, both as an end in itself and as the way of access to the Supreme.

Although the treatises of the first Ennead were doubtless written by Plotinus at a late period, in them we can find a kind of general introduction to the study of the work, and especially to the study of contemplation — both because sensible beauty, which is the principal subject of this Ennead, is the gateway to the contemplative life, and because from its pages emerges a vivid overall impression of the meaning and end of the mystical ascent. An ascent that is in reality a return, a spiritual odyssey in which the soul, purifying itself of everything material and sensible, rises toward its native homeland, situated beyond not only the world of the senses but intelligible beauty itself.

Following the Platonic inspiration, but prolonging, so to speak, the lines or directions of the speculative and mystical intent of the dialogues, Plotinus develops in the first Ennead a doctrine of the beautiful in which we find the ascensional sense of Platonic aesthetics together with a metaphysics in which pure Platonism is surpassed by a vision, or more exactly by a higher philosophical and mystical intuition. The musician, Plotinus states, offering us an archetypal example, rises from the mere perception of sound to that of rhythm and melodic figure, so as to ascend to grasp the beauty of proportions and of harmonies, and finally to arrive at intelligible harmony and at beauty itself, which is set above every particular loveliness.

Sensible things, bodies — why are they beautiful? All agree that a combination of colors and harmonious, symmetrical lines, or a musical chord, or a rhythmic succession of words or of mere sounds, are beautiful. But why are they so? Whence comes their beauty? Occupations and actions too can be beautiful; science is beautiful. But whence does it come that all these things — bodies, occupations, science — are beautiful?

Deferring the answer to this question, we realize at once that there is a scale in beauty. Its first rung is constituted by sensible beauty, symmetrical, measurable; then come beautiful occupations, the virtues, in which symmetry is no longer visible, though it may perhaps exist, and intelligence. Along this scale we see that the soul cleaves to and loves beautiful forms, beautiful occupations, the virtues, intelligence, and how this ascent is accompanied by the rejection of the ugly, the formless. This makes us understand that beautiful things are in accord with the nature of the soul, and that our love for them is grounded in the fact that both beautiful forms and our soul participate in an Idea.

Bodies possess a form imprinted on them from on high, and this imprint, this divine, illuminating reason, makes them beautiful. This form, this reason, this idea unifies their parts and harmonizes and combines them into a whole. This is why bodies are the more beautiful the closer they stand to the incorporeal. And here, Plotinus says, giving expression at once to a philosophical idea and to a feeling of lofty poetry: "The beauty of a simple color comes from a form that masters the darkness of matter and from the presence of an incorporeal light that is reason, or idea. Whence it follows that, among all bodies, fire is beautiful in itself: among the other elements it holds the rank of an idea; it is the most elevated by its position, and the lightest of all bodies, because it is closest to the incorporeal; it alone does not admit the other elements into itself, while the others admit it into themselves; for they can be heated, while it cannot be cooled. It shines and gives light because it is an idea" (I. 6,3).

In passing from sensible beauty to higher beauties there is something in the doctrine of Plotinus worth noting, because it reveals with clarity the spiritual intent of his aesthetic metaphysics. Sensible beauty is lovable, since it comes from the Idea, but it must be surpassed by purer and nobler forms of contemplation — an ascent whose breath, whose attraction, whose flame are constituted by love, which at each degree of the soul's life carries it to an ever more spiritual, more diaphanous height and — to use a word that captures the sense of the aesthetic, intellectual, and mystical vocation that inspires the whole of Plotinus's speculation — ever more divine height.

On the path of contemplation, sensible beauty invites us to enter another sphere, supra-sensible yet immediately joined to sensibility: the sphere of relations, proportions, figures. But the soul must not linger at this level; it must rise to the beauty of higher objects still: beautiful occupations, the arts, the virtues, the sciences. And this elevation will be accomplished through the work of love guided by dialectic — an admirable science that defines objects, classifies them, determines in each case whether existence belongs to them or not, and which, in the end, possesses not merely the theorems but the realities themselves. Professor García Bacca will call this dialectic a "transcendent technique," one that lifts us out of the condition of creatures toward the supreme solitude, firmness, and permanence of the absolute.

Beauty is, ever more, purification, elevation, and light, according to this description of the soul's steps. "Following an ancient discourse," says Plotinus, "temperance, courage, every virtue, and prudence itself are purifications" (I. 6,6) — forms, we might say, of liberation from the sensible, still more from the body; and in the same passage the philosopher adds: "The soul, once purified, becomes form, reason; it becomes incorporeal, intellectual; it belongs wholly to the divine, where the source of beauty is, and whence come all things of that same kind" (I. 6,6). Intellectual beauty is thus a higher beauty because it partakes of the divine, and further because it occurs within the soul itself, "truly isolated" from all exteriority, and because, being the intelligence's contemplation, in it the soul is identified with real being.

Thus, according to the dialectical doctrine of Plotinus, each of the degrees in the contemplation of the beautiful receives its value and its meaning from a light, from an instance higher than all of them. They are degrees of illumination, of participation in an eminent light, ranging from the beauty of beautiful bodies up to the intelligence, where the beauty of the Ideas resides — a beauty which, however lofty, is not the absolute, supreme end; for beyond the beautiful stands the Good, which is the beginning and the end of all contemplation. And the Good is the One.

The Good stands above intellectual beauty. But it is beautiful with an incomparable beauty whose contemplation is supreme happiness. "It is necessary, then," we read (I. 6,7), "to ascend still further, toward the Good, toward which all souls tend. If one has seen it, one knows what I mean and in what sense it is beautiful. As the Good, it is desired, and desire tends toward it; but only those obtain it who rise toward the higher region, turn back to it, and strip off the garments they put on in their descent — as those who ascend toward the sanctuaries of the temples must purify themselves, leave behind their former vestments, and go up unclothed — until, having abandoned in this ascent everything foreign to God, one sees, alone with the Alone, in its isolation, simplicity, and purity, That on which all depends, toward which all things look, by which is Being, life, and thought; since it is the cause of life, of intelligence, and of being." Reading this page we recall the nakedness of which St. John of the Cross speaks. And seeing further on how this union is filled with wonder accompanied by pleasure, and how the soul that loves this ineffable beauty is filled with awe, we think of the admirable intuitions of Rudolf Otto on "the Holy," and we glimpse the profound sense — not only metaphysical but religious — of this mysticism.

To attain this vision, or rather this union so lofty that it transcends all sensible beauty, one must flee every lesser form of delight and seek, like Ulysses, our true homeland. "As Ulysses, they say, fled from the magic of Circe and Calypso — that is, he did not consent to remain near them, despite the pleasures of the eyes and all the sensible beauties he found there. Our homeland is the place from which we came, and our father is there. What, then, is this journey and this flight? It is not with our feet that we can accomplish it, since our steps only ever carry us from one land to another; nor is it necessary to prepare a chariot or a ship, but rather one must cease to look and, closing one's eyes, exchange this way of seeing for another, and awaken this faculty that everyone possesses but that few make use of" (I. 6,8). And how is this to be achieved? By closing the eyes to the exterior and exercising "the inner eye" that sees within the innermost part of the soul itself, consummating union with itself. A state whose reality, intimacy, and loftiness the master expresses in words of one illumined, when he says (I. 6,9): "Then you will be a vision; trust yourself; even while still remaining here, you have ascended; you no longer need a guide; fix your gaze and see."

And here the principle of likeness becomes a principle of identification. To contemplate the sun, the soul must be like the sun. To contemplate the beautiful, the soul must be beautiful. To contemplate God, the soul must be divine. Ideas that anticipate, and perhaps inspired, the Goethean thought rendered in the famous verses:

War nicht das Auge sonnenhaft, Die Sonne könnt' es nie erblicken; Läg' nicht in uns des Gottes eigne Kraft, Wie könnt' uns Göttliches entzücken? ("Were the eye not sun-like, it could never behold the sun; were the god's own power not within us, how could the divine enrapture us?")

In sum, purification, elevation, and illumination would constitute the three ideal moments that combine in the ontological and psychic reality of contemplation — a state in which the soul passes from likeness and image to identity with Being and with the One.

And now, with this overview in mind, and starting from the semidarkness of nature, let us follow Plotinus along the itinerary of his ascent toward the fullness of light. And let us do so bearing in mind that the philosopher's thought is, to a great extent, cyclical — that is, at each step of his meditation he repeats themes already touched, though he repeats them by incorporating them, dialectically, into a new and higher sphere of perfection and of being. Plotinus establishes that nature — the first rung of the ascending scale of beings, understood as seminal reason, as form imprinted upon matter — is contemplation. It is contemplation, he asserts, because, being form, it does not go outside itself, nor change, nor move as matter does; whereby immobility appears to us as the first essential condition of contemplation. But within this immobility there is something whose consideration can raise us above mere nature; and it consists in this: that, insofar as it is reason, contemplation is at the same time contemplation and production. How so?

There is something enigmatic and, at first sight, incomprehensible, in these pages of Plotinus; but from them there emerges something like an infinite perspective, and through his formulas an extraordinary depth is discerned once they have been intensely meditated upon. Nature is a reason, a form, a logos, which comes from a higher reason, one which at the same time produces it and contemplates it. Nature is therefore produced as an object of contemplation and is contemplated in the very act that engenders it — in the way a geometer produces geometrical figures by the mere fact of contemplating them. Insofar as it is reason, nature is therefore an object of contemplation, the being of contemplation, and contemplation itself. "For nature, to be what it is, is to produce; now, it is contemplation and object of contemplation, since it is reason; for, because it is contemplation, it is object of contemplation and reason; and for that reason it alone produces" (III, 8.3). By identifying contemplation and production in this way, Plotinus directs our thought toward the deep essence of contemplation as he conceives it; for if to contemplate is to produce, to contemplate is to be — in intimate communion of life, of activity, and of substance — the very object of contemplation.

Looking upward we shall see how nature proceeds from the soul; we see that it is a soul that proceeds from another, earlier and higher, soul. But the higher soul is itself contemplation. In this way Plotinus considers a soul that dwells on high and a soul that, so to speak, descends. The higher soul dwells in the intelligible; it is therefore contemplation, though of a loftier kind, since while the contemplation of nature is silent and somewhat vague, the contemplation of the soul is given, on high, in an eternal plenitude and illumination.

Making precise the direction of his thought, which coincides with the ascending gradation of reality, Plotinus says: "Contemplation is a progress from nature to the soul, and from the soul to intelligence; it unites ever more intimately with the being it contemplates; in the wise soul the objects known already become identical with the knowing subject, because it aspires to intelligence, where subject and object are but one" (III. 8,8). Thus in intelligence contemplation reaches its greatest perfection, its plenitude; because in it the contemplating subject is no longer distinguished from the contemplated object, and because in it — that is, at this ontological level — "being and thinking are the same thing." A living contemplation, because in it the object is not distinct from the subject, and because thus it possesses, in its full manifestation, all that is manifested life.

There is, then, according to Plotinus, a fundamental identity between being and contemplation. Being is contemplation, contemplation is being. Being contemplates itself, and that contemplation is its being. So that in the act in which the soul contemplates, an ontological operation is accomplished — an identification that suppresses the distance between subject and object and that, in this way, places the being of the contemplator within the very object of contemplation. "Socrates contemplating does not know whether he is an intelligence or a soul." "All our activity is directed toward the object contemplated. We become the object. We are no longer ourselves except in potency" (IV. 4,2).

But intelligence, however lofty it may be, presupposes otherness — between object and subject, between idea and idea — and thus presupposes also a higher, absolute principle in which the perfect and pure unity to which intellectual life aspires is realized. A supreme principle, indefinable yet evident; one might say demanded, called for by the very otherness of intelligence, which could not exist as it does without a unity that sustains it without itself becoming divided. One might say, introducing here a category already belonging to emotional experience, that this unity, this One, is ineffable and therefore undefinable within the categories of conceptual thought.

But can this First be an object of contemplation? Or rather, is it itself contemplation, theoria? It would seem that, just as it lies beyond being — which is contemplation — it must also be found beyond contemplation, in the sphere where all distinction and all limit disappear.

But perhaps we ought to pause over the consideration of certain notions, over the meditation of certain speculative phases, over some of the levels of the ascent along which Plotinus leads us. In doing so we shall refine our glimpses of the content of contemplation, above all as regards the soul's mediation between the sensible and the intelligible, and its metaphysical situation — in which, having descended, it ascends, and in which, at the very moment it transcends and rises above itself, it finds itself, at the luminous end of this ascent.

As we have already seen, in the contemplation of the intelligible — that is, in contemplation properly so called — the soul ascends by way of a purification consisting in its liberation from every sensible propensity, from every disordered passion, and even from every trace of temporality — indeed from every memory. Thus purified within itself, the soul no longer attends to the ephemeral but to the eternal; it no longer sees the passing but the permanent; gathered into itself, it lives in the world of the intelligible, and in contemplating it, contemplates itself, since it is, in its higher and divine part, that very intelligible world. Whereby contemplation is a reintegration of the soul into its own homeland — which, in a profound metaphysical sense, turns out to be nothing other than a higher region of the soul itself. This ascent is a gathering-in, since, by turning back upon itself, the soul rises to what it itself is; so that in what we might call the act of contemplation, or the process of contemplation, two movements occur — or rather two operations within a single indivisible act; two movements that are one and the same: the gathering-in and the ascent.

In sum, contemplation is a form of life in which the soul sees the intelligible, not as something foreign to itself, but as something in which the soul recognizes and possesses itself. In other words, in contemplation the soul is, or becomes, intelligence, or, as Plotinus puts it, condensing his thought: in contemplation the soul does not merely see, but truly is vision.

Plotinus sometimes speaks of the soul as identical with intelligence, at least in the soul's higher region. At other times he calls the soul the image of the intelligible, the word (verbum) of intelligence — whereby the soul is distinguished from intelligence as being a new hypostasis. The soul proceeds from intelligence. It is less eminent, and though divine, is, by its filiation, inferior. For this same reason the soul returns to itself (a gathering-in) and, in a certain way, given its place in the hierarchy of the real, transcends itself in the act of contemplation. Here the soul is itself insofar as it is the contemplating subject, and is intelligence insofar as it is the intelligible object. Thus the distinction between two regions of the soul — higher and lower — which does not destroy its unity, clarifies to some extent the apparent contradiction between the movement we might call self-absorption and the movement of transcendence, or elevation, which together constitute, in their metaphysical and psychological unity, contemplation.

The Enneads contain numerous and extensive psychological passages: on sensation, on passion, on intelligence. But Plotinus does not always separate his considerations of an ontological order from his views on the modes and forms of the soul's inner occurrences. So that when he speaks of contemplation, rather than describing an inner experience with its affective concomitants, he is concerned with defining metaphysically the soul's situation and ontological significance. Through contemplation the soul assumes a certain quality, a certain ontological condition: it ascends even as it gathers itself in, as though the gathering-in were itself an ascent and a return; and in this same act the soul is vision and is intelligible. For all of which one might say that seeing and being are identified in contemplation.

As a general condition inherent in contemplative activity, as the affective ground of contemplation understood not merely as a state but as a permanent, transfiguring form of all spiritual life oriented toward the divine, Plotinus regards happiness — the tranquil joy that fills the soul of the sage, raised above the earthly and transitory to the vision of the eternal. Neither sickness nor death will trouble the serenity of this life, which lives beyond time with its gaze turned toward the heights. All this, however, refers principally to intellectual contemplation, since mystical ecstasy properly so called could not be characterized by the mere serenity proper to intellective vision. Other categories of feeling would apply to ecstasy: joyous stupor, awe, wonder. In sum, the soul's mystical aspiration corresponds, step by step, to the degrees at which the planes of reality are arranged, and the feelings and affections that arise along that ascent are steeped in deep religiosity — so much so that Plotinus compares the domain of contemplation to a sanctuary in whose innermost depths dwells, mysterious, the First, who is at once the divine end of the spiritual journey.

But the religiosity of Plotinus is scarcely distinguishable from his sense of beauty, understood as reflection, gleam, trace of the primordial light — or better, as a ray that is, at the same time, the path by which the soul can fly to the sun. As we have seen, sensible beauty is an invitation to ascend; while intelligible beauty is itself a lofty height, and a step toward the beautiful that has neither name nor form. "The first reality, the First, is without form" (VI. 7,33). "Its beauty is of another kind than beauty; it is a beauty beyond beauty" (VI. 7,32).

Although that supreme beauty communicates itself, in a certain way, with beautiful objects, whether sensible or intelligible, and although the aesthetic ascent of contemplation moves from visible forms to the invisible forms of the intelligible realm, beauty itself is not enclosed within any of those forms. These, limited as they are, cannot contain the first and total reality, but can only reflect it. Forms are beautiful, but they are not beauty; reflections, images, they are inferior to it; they are ascending degrees, though they must be abolished and forgotten in the final, inexpressible rapture of the summit. This formless beauty, principle and origin of all beauty, is the object of the loving desire that inflames the soul and carries it, by the path of absolute purity and detachment, to see the Alone alone.

As a trace of the affective reflection of this rapture we have the expression "joyous stupor," which denotes a not-knowing accompanied by joy. It is a presence that is not the presence of something else; that does not stand before the contemplator but rather identifies and merges with him in an act of love. The lover (intelligence, the soul) loses itself in the beloved, and in this state all is evidence, truth, joy, light, and no word can render it, because in it every distinction is erased and every determination vanishes. "Indeterminacy of the object, indeterminacy of the subject," says Bréhier, characterizing the supreme union whose reality mere thought cannot reach, which no description or explanation can make manifest, because it lies beyond every image and idea. Only the illumined knows this state in its ineffable mysteriousness. It is the mystical state that already transcends the bounds of contemplation properly so called, although in an elevated sense it might perhaps be said to be the very perfection, the completion and the sum of contemplation; George Mehlis speaks of a "seeing by way of a not-seeing," alluding to the indescribable character of this state in which all is light and in which every distinct apprehension disappears.

It has not been the intention of this study to examine the philosophy of Plotinus in its entirety, but to set forth, in summary form, the great mystic's doctrine of contemplation. In carrying out this purpose, it has seemed to us that the ultimate structure of the metaphysical thought of Plotinus — a structure which naturally determines his ideas on contemplation — could be represented by the scheme of two opposed and complementary movements: one, descending, emanating from the pure unity which, without losing itself, gives rise to the diversity of intelligence and soul; and an ascending, involutive movement, by which the rays of the primal light tend, or converge upward, to reintegrate themselves in their original focus. In this dialectic we see how contemplation is comparable to a radiance that turns back toward its source, thereby realizing, through degrees of increasing elevation and perfection, the divine unity. And we see that perhaps the final secret of this metaphysics and of this mysticism would consist in the inexpressible necessity that prescribes the descent of the divine toward matter, and that, at the same time, institutes in that very thing which descends the vocation of reintegration into the heights. Plotinus thus consecrates the soul's faith in its own divinity, and its hope of absolute deification through purification and contemplation.

In this way one might say that contemplation undoes, and in a certain sense annuls, the descending process that goes from the One to intelligence, and from intelligence to the soul. To return is to abolish, to forget the exodus. To ascend is to undo, to eliminate, to abandon all that is corporeality, temporality, and plurality, in order to arrive, by this way of negation, at the Supreme. But then, faced with this imperative of mystical love, which prescribes undoing and separation as the means, and return as the end, this question arises — one perhaps without an answer within the strict terms of the philosophy of Plotinus: What meaning does this procession have, whose steps must be abolished, effaced on the way of return? If the One is the only meaning, what significance does multiplicity have, and to what end is the splendor of the world unfolded?
