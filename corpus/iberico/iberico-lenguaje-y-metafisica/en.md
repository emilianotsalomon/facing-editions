## III

## LENGUAJE Y METAFÍSICA — LANGUAGE AND METAPHYSICS

If there is any sphere of experience in which the metaphysical arcanum of reality becomes visible — in a certain way, without losing its enigmatic character — it is without doubt in the phenomenon of language. In language, indeed, we can isolate three things: a sensible element (audible); a supra-sensible meaning which, by constituting a form of existence in the mind and by opposing itself as a subsistent entity in itself to the sensible appearance, we call the ontological element; and finally, a relation between the two by means of which the ontological ground becomes apprehensible through the word, through the verb. If we now reflect that the metaphysical problem par excellence consists, when all is said and done, in comprehending and intuiting the relation between being as the ground and meaning of reality and appearing as its expression, we must conclude that language is, in truth, a phenomenon representative of the deepest and most mysterious structure of existence.

This character that we would call exemplary or representative of language in relation to the universal phenomenon of appearing may perhaps serve us to infer, beyond the mere schematism of being and appearing, through the psychological experience of the planes of appearance and by means of the study of the link between meaning and expression, something about the passage from being to appearing. The movement from meaning to verbal expression is a centrifugal movement. Expression supposes a centrifugal movement that goes from a certain idea or feeling of the soul to the sensible exteriority of the word. It is a centrifugal and evolutionary movement. Comprehension is the inverse movement — involutive and centripetal — by which, beyond expression, one seeks the originary meaning, the interior ground that we call the ontological foundation of the word. The difficulty in describing these movements lies in the fact that, though contrary, they are complementary and simultaneous. The speaker interprets himself, understands himself, hears himself. The listener, to a certain degree, expresses himself in the speaker's words. And thus the emanation of the meaning from which the word springs and the return to meaning that constitutes comprehension are, by their very nature and by the depth and difficulty of the questions they raise, comparable if not identical to the opposed and inseparable metaphysical steps that Neoplatonic intuition penetrated so profoundly — steps that are none other than the implication and the genetic explanation of the real.

By considering two examples we will set out to discover the general types of link between meanings and signs, and then, together with the basis for an overall conception of expression in language, the possibility of making some legitimate inferences about the marvelous phenomenon of universal appearing.

Let us suppose that we propose to isolate the meaning of the stanza by Jorge Manrique that reads:

Nuestras vidas son los rios Que van a dar a la mar Que es el morir. [Our lives are the rivers / That flow toward the sea / Which is dying.]

If we were to say that the meaning of this stanza is that "our lives end with death," we would be making an error, for if Jorge Manrique had wanted to say only that our lives end in death, he would have expressed it in that way; but he did not. Rather, the poet thought of life and "dying" under the guise of rivers and the sea. He saw our lives as the rivers that flow to the sea, which is dying. Only in this form could his feeling about life and death be expressed, and in such a way that not a single word could be changed or removed without leaving the stanza truncated and, consequently, its meaning unexpressed. From this we conclude that meaning does not adhere externally to the word, nor is it deposited in it like wine in a cup; rather it penetrates and informs the word, communicating itself to the totality of the expression and composing with it, by infusion, a single and indivisible reality. One might say that meaning and expression are two categorial ideas employed, respectively, to indicate the unity in relation to the variety, the invisible and only imaginable in relation to the visible, the permanent in relation to what can change. Meaning would thus be unity, the invisible, the permanent — that which has the qualities ordinarily predicated of being. Expression would be what is subject to change, what is ultimately caught even in the unstoppable flow of life. And so, in the light of this example, we could affirm simultaneously the logical distinction and the inseparability of expression and meaning.

What we have said apropos of Manrique's stanza does not suffice, however, even for the purpose of presenting with clarity both the essential distinction and the link between meaning and expression. For there is a type of expression to which the description we have attempted does not apply. Let us take, for example, the formula F = ma. Here we realize that the meaning could have been expressed in another form, with other expressions. And that the formula, far from emanating directly from a certain movement of the soul, implies an intellectual operation directed at schematizing in terms of the most rigorous simplicity all the possible variety of experience. And in this way these examples lead us to presume that in language there occur two kinds of operations, equivalent to two different intellectual — or more properly, psychic — attitudes: an emanatistic or hypostatic act, corresponding chiefly to poetic expression, in which the primitive unity of meaning diffuses itself into the varied multiplicity of appearance; and an act we shall call demiurgic, which reduces the incoherent, chaotic, and immediate multiplicity of appearances to organization, to schema, to a system of conceptual forms. These operations perhaps correspond to the two kinds of energy that Klages discovers in the activity of the word: the symbolic, indicative in nature and kinetic in character, and the delimiting, conceptual and potential in nature.

Let us first consider what we call the demiurgic operation, reserving for later the study of the hypostatic operation, to which, moreover, we shall grant greater metaphysical importance and significance.

The demiurgic operation — so named in recollection of the Platonic god who turned chaos into cosmos, guided by the vision of the Ideas — performs an abstractive and stabilizing function in the heterogeneous and changing domain of immediate experience. In the qualitative and variable multiplicity that fills both the world of objective images and the interior world of our sensibility, the word establishes certain common forms, certain general designations which, while serving theoretical systematization, are at the same time instruments of action, designative forms of communication and universal intelligibility. The names that apply to a multiplicity of images or internal affective states erase what each of those images or states has that is irreducible and unique, retaining only their common characters; moreover, in the incessant, irreversible flow of inner life, language fixes forever, in the relatively invariable form of the name, certain modes of being of the soul and confers upon them a false solidity. And so Bergson can say, in a sentence unassailable from his point of view: "Thus the word with well-defined outlines, the brutal word, which stores up what is stable, common, and consequently impersonal in the impressions of humanity, overwhelms, or at least covers over, the delicate and fugitive impressions of our individual consciousness. To struggle with equal weapons, these should be expressed by precise words; but these words, as soon as they are formed, would turn against the sensation that gave them birth, invented as they were to certify that the sensation is unstable, they would impose their own stability upon it."

This classificatory and stabilizing function of language obeys, on one hand, a pragmatic need of an eminently social character, and on the other, a theoretical intention. The need for inter-individual communication doubtless imposes a certain depersonalization of the psychic content that is communicated and requires the creation of signs capable of circulating like currency. The theoretical intention pursues, in the first place, an ideal of clarity and distinction, and for this reason introduces into the more or less confused and chaotic material of appearances separations and groupings susceptible to simple designations; and in the second place, the theoretical intention, in seeking grounds or essences, performs a work of reduction that eliminates accidental particularities in order to retain only the substantial, which it designates with a name. There are philosophers for whom, genetically speaking, the pragmatic attitude is prior to the speculative attitude, and according to whom concepts (and consequently the terms that express them) would originally arise more as directions for practical activity than as references to the ontological structure of the real. Be that as it may, the demiurgic attitude, at the same time as it orders, immobilizes; and in any of its directions, pragmatic or speculative, it is always schematic, impersonal, and hostile to the concrete variety and creative mobility of life.

The tendency of language, in this aspect of its activity, is toward the most extreme schematism and the most rigorous economy in expression, all of which leads to the establishment of an absolute conventionalism in the adoption and handling of signs. One need only think of mathematical language to perceive both the tendency and the results of this type of linguistic activity. And for greater illustration, consider some logical formulas — for example, the famous verbal combinations Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio, etc. Even in the discipline of law there is a tendency toward a kind of algebraic figuration of legal relations. The schematism and economy that characterize this type of linguistic activity have given rise to the modern tendency to form words that condense a phrase or a series of names by joining their initials, resulting at times in horrible combinations that generally acquire international currency and thus reveal the cosmopolitan tendency of conceptual language — which we have, with some impropriety but not without justification, called demiurgic. Examples: SCIPA, COSACH, FIFA. It is the paradoxical orgy of schema and conventionalism, or, if one prefers, the tyranny of the sign under whose sway the concrete reality of things and images dissipates and is lost.

This, however, does not diminish the intrinsic importance of conceptual language nor its significance in the domains of epistemology and logic. For language, operating in the conceptual direction, not only delimits, fixes, and — so to speak — mints the transmissible signs of thought; it also, thanks to its morphological laws and those governing the relations between meaning and expression, renders transparent, draws, makes sensible the structure, the formal skeleton of intelligence, and reveals, moreover, the possibilities of intentional reference between thought and objects.

Husserl's profound investigations into signification and the a priori laws that govern the linking of significations when they are organized into a unity of meaning refer to the type of linguistic activity we call demiurgic, which is proper to the merely intellectual, logical, and abstractive aspect of the mind.

Significations in their linkage obey laws derived from the very essence of signifying which, setting aside the signified matter, are grounded in form — that is, in the role, in the grammatical category of signification. From this is derived, according to Husserl, "the great problem equally fundamental for logic and for grammar: to establish the a priori constitution that governs the realm of significations and to investigate, 'in a morphology of significations,' the a priori system of formal structures, that is, of structures that dispense with every material particularity of signification."

In this endeavor Husserl arrives at the conception of a pure logical grammar that would establish something like the "ideal schema" of language, which would be the common essential and non-empirical foundation of all languages. An a priori synthesis, a morphology of forms — not accidental or local, but essential and necessary — of the linking of significations in a unity of meaning.

In the course of his admirable developments, Husserl says the following which, by its extra-logical projection, is of special interest to our study: "The sphere of signification is much wider than that of intuition, that is, than the total sphere of possible fulfillments" — meaning, we clarify, the sphere of the objective, real correlates of significations. Extending this thought to the ontological sphere, we would say it is as if appearance were to detach itself from the being of objects and constitute itself in an empty world, in a world of fallacious inferences whose members no doubt allude to the objects of intuition but which, taken together, form complexes without unitary meaning — something less than absurd, yet occurring within the norms of syntactic legality. To this set of forms devoid of unitary meaning we believe the category of unreality properly belongs — not, as is usually maintained, in contradiction of the deep sense of the Platonic tradition, to the world of ideal objects.

In summary, language, as an instrument of conceptualization, plays the same role in the economy of the psychic world as do the forces of stabilization in the torrent of universal becoming — forces that institute certain constancies, certain uniformities. And as syntax, language is without doubt a particular manifestation of the universal empire of form, that is, of all the possibilities for energy to configure and organize itself according to modes of coherence and intelligibility. For all these reasons, it was perhaps that Plato in the Cratylus attributed to the lawgiver the work of naming objects with names that, at the same time as they expressed (imitated) their essence, would indicate their function in the system of relations that constitute the world and life.

We call hypostatic operation that in which meaning — in itself invisible, supra-sensible — descends to incarnate and configure itself in the word. We call it so by analogy with the metaphysical step that the Neoplatonists called hypostatic emanation, in which the One, without ceasing to be one, transferred itself to intellectual otherness, and universal intelligence communicated and transfused itself into the soul. Thus meaning is realized through the distinct planes of expression and, without ceasing to be what it is, configures itself and appears. In this we perhaps renew a most ancient tradition, since some early cosmologists, to allude to the intelligence that orders, governs, and configures the universe, spoke of cosmic syntax, expressing in a single word both the synthetic structure and the expressive quality of universal activity.

But perhaps this analogy may be developed with profit for understanding the great mystery of language. The series of hypostases or hypostatic emanations that go from the One to intelligence and from the latter to the soul — which in turn configures and informs the material world — is integrated with an inverse movement that returns by way of negation and eminence to being and to the One. Procession and return are the names respectively given to these movements, and they are comparable to the movements of expression and apprehension of meaning in language. But there is something more: because the cosmic procession and return, which are not separate but complementary though opposite movements, give us the metaphysical conception in which the relation of implication and opposition between appearance and meaning is, without doubt, best expressed.

Studying what we call the hypostatic operation, we see that language does not have as its sole function the transmitting or comprehending of orders, or the classifying of objects, or the directing of traffic — like a traffic light — in the midst of the confused mobility of life. It appears, on the contrary, that language has also the function of manifesting, without any ordinative or utilitarian intention, the interior content of the soul. To manifest — that is, to reveal the implicit content of life not only to others but to the very eyes of one's own consciousness — as a flash, a radiance, or a particular reflection of the universal phenomenon of appearing.

Let us now seek to approach the process by which this internal content, which is meaning, configures itself in the word.

We know of few truly profound attempts to describe the passage from meaning to image and from image to word. Perhaps because this description is in every respect extremely difficult and perhaps even impossible, since these categories — meaning, image, word — are less historical moments, separate stages of the expressive procession, than ideal moments, aspects of an indivisible reality. Meaning is at once prior and final. It precedes and presides over the phases of expressive evolution and also crowns them, for it appears as a result, as a supra-sensible sprout of the finished expression. And it is both immanent and transcendent. It is immanent, internal, and even consubstantial with all the elements of the phrase or discourse. But it transcends them, and above them it exists and subsists in itself, even though it cannot be defined. Like being, which neither exhausts nor dissolves itself in the infinite variety of appearances, yet which we cannot apprehend or define in the pure, absolute nakedness of its essence.

To speak in Aristotelian language: there is a final form that precedes, presides over, orients, and at last incarnates itself in the configurative movement of the mind, in the same way that the final form of the flower and the fruit presides over the phases of the biological development of the plant. We must not forget one thing, however: that the configurative movement of language is creative in the sense that expression not only manifests and translates but also evokes and produces. Form and act pre-exist to a certain degree, but something new and unforeseeable emerges later en cours de route, without anything, however, breaking the filiation of the whole with respect to the primitive entity of meaning.

Bergson's subtle and profound observations in "L'Intuition Philosophique" on the simplicity of the primordial intuitions of the great philosophers and the more or less considerable complication of the verbal, configurative, and technical apparatus in which that simple intuition is translated — sometimes losing itself and at other times rediscovering itself in the zigzag of exposition — illuminate the tension between the intentionality of meaning and expression. The Bergsonian doctrine of the dynamic schema, Aristotelian at bottom, instructs us on the evolutionary process of psychic content that tends to exteriorize itself through the word. The passage from meaning to expression appears in these studies as something comparable to a luminous projection in which, from a focal point where the figures of appearing exist as mere virtualities, meaning expands and diffuses itself, incarnating in ever more defined forms until it reaches the final plane where, as on the cinema screen, the figures implied in the original intention acquire reality and appear.

In this first approach we have occupied ourselves with language principally as expression of the representative content of the mind: ideas, images, and their relations. We have not considered language as an expressive sign of emotion — not only of those emotions that are accompanied by somatic signs, such as fear or anger, but of those others more vague, perhaps deeper, more subtle and delicate, that seem resistant to all definition and consequently to all expression. We have not dealt with poetry, which is par excellence the expressive organ of emotion, for in it are manifested the most subtle, the most subjective, the most delicate nuances of affective life — they are manifested and propagated as if the act of configuring were not solely an act destined for the listener's comprehension and apprehension of meaning, but a means of transmission and contagion intended to make the listener not merely understand but participate in a certain wave, a certain current or tide of the life of the soul.

And this is how this property of language — as vehicle and expression of affective life — leads us to formulate some brief considerations on poetic imagination, understood as the activity of the soul directed principally toward configuring, transmitting, and sustaining the emotional current of life.

If an enthusiastic person exclaims "how admirable!" or if a novelist writes "John was afraid," everyone immediately grasps the meaning of these phrases; but it is evident that if one disregards the circumstances in the one case and the context in the other, these phrases contain a merely abstract signification. They indicate that the subjects of both sentences experience a certain emotion whose universal essence is conceptually definable and therefore the same for all human beings. They express neither the concrete admiration of X nor the non-transferable fear of John, and moreover they do not allow us to participate emotionally in these psychic states — neither with the real participation that Scheler would call Einsfühlung, nor with the aesthetic participation that Lipps would call Einfühlung.

What conditions must expression fulfill in order truly to be the expression of a feeling, of an emotion of the soul, and in order to evoke — along with affective participation — the mysterious enchantment of poetry, which is, when all is said and done, nothing other than the enchantment of achieved expression, or more precisely of expression that, through the magic of its color or its music, delivers meaning to us vivid and palpitating?

It seems to us that these conditions are of three kinds: imaginative, syntactic, and phonetic.

It would appear that the first condition of poetry consists in the feeling's being incarnated in images. This does not mean that the image is an indirect sign of the emotion; on the contrary, comparable to the expressive gesture, it is the emotion's visible body, in such a way that the expressed feeling and the expressive image appear as the only possible appearance of the manifested emotion. Images, and in general expressive forms, can express feeling in two ways: either the image is a direct, immediate hypostasis of the feeling, or it is something like the kinetic projection of the emotion. Examples of the first kind are the well-known lines by Verlaine:

Il pleure dans mon coeur Comme il pleut sur la ville. [It weeps in my heart / As it rains over the city.]

Or the lines by Nietzsche:

Tag meines Lebens' Gen Abend geht's... [The day of my life / Moves toward evening...]

To clarify what we have just said when we speak of the kinetic projection of feeling as an expressive form of emotion, let us think of the "no" we utter in response to an unpleasant piece of news. This is a no that does not deny the existence of the unwelcome event but rejects it. Another example: the famous sonnet attributed to Saint Teresa of Ávila expresses love as both the cause and the effect of the movements of the soul aroused by the vision of Christ's sacrifice and death.

Considering now the purely verbal aspect of affective expression, two aspects of language present themselves to our attention: they converge and conspire toward the effectiveness of the communicative manifestation of feeling, namely the syntactic aspect and the phonetic aspect of expression.

As regards the syntactic aspect, grammarians and linguists establish first of all that in affective language the logical order — the strictly grammatical structure of the expression or phrase — is disturbed or disappears. It is the anacolutha, apparent disorder; it is interruption, the kinetic repercussion of internal violence. According to Vendryes, while in the written language — presumed non-affective — the order is one of subordination, in spoken language — affective — it is one of juxtaposition. Juxtaposition in clauses, with emphasis on the dominant movements and attenuation or obscuring of merely conceptual or logical relations. Linguists point to the reduplication or repetition of words as a means of accentuating the rise of the affective wave — repetition, reiteration, in which the great universal and poetic phenomenon of rhythm already announces itself. Finally, (Bally) speaks of the plays, transpositions, and grammatical figures in which the simultaneously intellectual and illogical activity of affective language becomes transparent.

Regarding the phonetic aspect, there is an evident relation of vital continuity between the sound of the word and the emotional ground and the affectively charged image it expresses. A mysterious relation in which is doubtless operative, to some degree, the fact that habit establishes a certain automatic association between a given sound and a more or less precise signification; but in which there is something more: an indefinable magical action of the word upon the soul, an action whose influence arouses in the listener a movement kindred to or identical with the verbal movement that strikes the chords of their sensibility. "Like the sound of a lyre," Plotinus would say, "that makes another lyre vibrate sympathetically." We therefore affirm that there exists an efficacy of verbal sound that is reducible neither to the habitual association between sign and thing, nor to the effect of so-called ideo-phonic associations — that is, the acoustic analogy between word and the physical characteristics of the object represented. It must be borne in mind in this regard that what is called imitative harmony or onomatopoeia reproduces, according to Klages's observation, sounds that are more like noises than sounds properly so called.

The efficacy of sound as a vehicle and evoker of emotion is far more subtle and indefinable. It derives from some unexplored relation between physical vibration and the soul — a relation in which certain sounds correspond to certain attitudes or psychic states, independently of any resemblance between the verbal sound and the natural sound it represents. One might say that there exists a pre-established harmony between the melody of words and the oscillation of interior life.

Drawing from an old book of literary criticism and modifying it in the light of personal observation, we present the following schema of psychic-phonetic correlation among the vowels:

A expresses and evokes feelings of calm, of fullness, of elevation, and also of ecstatic transport before the sublime: casa [house], alma [soul], ala [wing], palabra [word], etc.

O is a vowel with a certain cosmic breath. It is the vowel of the Whole, and in the melody of words it evokes a sensation of abundance, grandeur, immensity: sol [sun], todo [all], globo [globe], colmo [fullness], cosmos, etc.

E is an aesthetic and light vowel, essentially euphonic, say the grammarians. It communicates its sweetness to feminine names in which it abounds: Elena, Ester, etc.

I is a sharp, dynamic vowel; it expresses impulse, excess, with a tendency toward the acute and strident — as in grito [cry], chillido [shriek] — or unlimited possibility, as in infinito [infinite]; and also subtlety, delicate smallness, as in hilo [thread], and a certain cutting precision, as in incidente [incident], incisivo [incisive], etc.

U is a dark and deep vowel. It comes from the mysterious metaphysical abyss. It is ontological and mystical: profundo [deep], tumba [tomb], túmulo [tumulus], duda [doubt]. And being abysmal and deep, it is humble with the profound humility of humus — the vegetable earth, the eternal and maternal possibility of life. If we were to allow ourselves a flight of fancy, we would say that U is Orphic, because in the depth of its sound the polar extremes of life and death seem to gather: the cradle and the tomb. It is also interesting to recall, in connection with U, those German words of such deep resonance: Ur, Grund, and Ursprung.

According to Hermann Wirth, in the cosmos and in the human being the vowel A would be born from wonder at the advent of spring; in summer, hailing the apex of the estival light, E and I would arise passionately; in autumn O; and in winter U, with which the human being and the world reintegrate themselves into the womb of the dark and the deep. And in this light, from these examples, one might perhaps affirm that in mere sound, in simple phonemes as such, there would exist a certain allusion to the metaphysical ground of things and to the splendor of appearing. And so one would say, finally, that the sound of spoken expression constitutes the first zone of confluence or interpenetration between the powers of nature and the interior commotion of the soul.

And here let us pause to allow ourselves an overview of the work of poetic imagination — which we are taking as an exemplary, and one might say methodological, experience for arriving at metaphysical comprehension.

In the work of poetic imagination there are moments we might call the summoning of words. The poet calls for the word that must incarnate a certain formal intention, and the predestined word comes, emerging from the unknown depths of the individual subconscious or from the dark universal storehouse of forms. The poet then recognizes it, as if by an effect of reminiscence, recalling having encountered it in a certain metaphysical past where they contemplated their own work in all its fullness and perfection. And only then — that is, only when the expressive word sounds in the poetic space — does the artist know their true emotion, their true formal intention, as if it were born with the body given to it by the word. The unknown possibilities of interior life might be compared to the souls Plotinus speaks of in the Enneads, which incarnate only in those bodies that, by their form, respond to their most essential aspiration. And so we might say, using the words of Bergson — who employs this Neoplatonic simile to explain the actualization of memories: "That bodies rise toward the souls that would communicate complete life to them, and the soul, looking at the body in which it believes it sees the reflection of itself, lets itself be attracted, inclines, and falls." And in this way, finally, it seems to us that we can approach the comprehension of this phenomenon by saying that there is a certain pre-existence of the final and perfect form in the work — filled at times with vehement anxiety — with which the poet seeks expression.

Thus language, through its power, its capacity for expression, for evocation, for the inner commotion of the soul, illuminates us about the concrete heterogeneity and the organic and creative totality of the real. The word is a sound, a physical vibration; but within it is contained and — what is more — within it appears a meaning, irreducible to the simple materiality of expression yet united to it indissolubly. In this way, to speak in Pascalian terms, we might say that language is a form of extension in which the unity of thought diversifies and expresses itself. And more than that: we would say that both aspire to the order of grace in which both sound and meaning rise above pure matter and mere thought, to emotion, to intuition, to the erotic contemplation of the absolute and divine.

But there is one eminent function, simultaneously of imagination and of language, that is of equal interest to ontology and aesthetics. It is the metaphorical function, which we consider encompassed within what we call hypostatic revelation in the phenomenon of expression.

It is commonly believed that the process of metaphor formation is explained by regarding metaphor as the result of a mere association by resemblance. But this is an insufficient explanation. For resemblance, taken in the abstract, is a formal quality that can relate images or appearances of any kind whatsoever, according to the innumerable points of view from which they may be contemplated or compared. Resemblance as a mere formal quality does not, therefore, constitute a true law — as Bergson has demonstrated conclusively in Matter and Memory. Between the appearances that come together in the metaphor there is no doubt resemblance, but there is something more. Between the proper meaning of the metaphorical expression and its figurative meaning there is a deeper psychic bond, a certain emotional link — the feeling that the metaphor is truly the expression and not the simple mask of a natural or internal content.

We do not intend to offer a complete theory of metaphor — a problem that implicates the difficult questions concerning the origin and significance of myth and art. We will limit ourselves to saying, in synthesis, that the metaphor appears at once as the natural form of expression of feeling and as the zone or point of convergence between the self and the not-self, the spirit and matter, nature and the soul.

Nor will we undertake either the exposition or the detailed critique of the numerous theories of metaphor, most of them derived from the psychoanalytic conception or from related doctrines, which in general regard metaphor as an emanation of the subconscious, as a figure in which, by virtue of the principle of ambivalence, the hidden desire — the instinctive and concealed tendency — is at once discovered, realized, and evaded.

What seems to us valid in all these theories is that they regard metaphor as a corporification of the deep tendencies of the soul which, in the metaphorical image, are at once expressed and in a certain way disguised and concealed. What is debatable is the effort of some schools to establish conclusions that we might call mechanical — nomenclatures of symbols with their corresponding significations — as if metaphorical or symbolizing activity were exercised according to rigid laws, as if it were possible to reduce abstractively to schemas the innumerable and imponderable human and cosmic elements that go into the production of the symbolic image: the metaphor.

Calling affective language that which serves as expression for the psychic contents in which sensibility or emotion predominates over abstractive or logical schematism, one can affirm in general that affective language is metaphorical — that is, that psychic contents of that kind always tend to be configured through images taken from the sensible world, or also that sensible images directly apprehended by the senses are expressed with words that allude to or refer to the feelings of the soul. The metaphor expresses the soul with material forms and matter with the forms of the soul. For this reason the metaphor creates a zone of existence in which the interior and the exterior world are transfigured by something like an effect of endosmosis, or — to use the language of Jaspers — in which the one becomes inflamed in the other.

The metaphor constitutes a zone of identification or interpenetration between nature and the soul. In it the visible and tangible forms of matter appear as the expression of a spiritual meaning, and something more: as something that simultaneously retains and exhales the soul. Materialization of the soul and spiritualization of matter. The double and opposed virtuality of the image that simultaneously conceals and reveals the enigma of man and the world — and in which metaphor, as the universal form of imagination, symbolizes, as H. Pongs has seen with profound intuition, the ambivalence of existence, in which the antinomic structure of the objective world and the antinomic structure of the subjective world of feelings correspond to and integrate with each other.

As an incarnation of the subconscious and expression of affective life, as pre-logical, mythical content of psychic life, metaphor belongs to the world of dream — taking the word "dream" in its most significant generality, as a projection of the life of the soul beyond mere intellectual or practical schematism. And in this sense it is interesting to review, even if very summarily, the explanations and interpretations of dream images, which are, in essence, metaphorical images. The psychoanalytic schools, principally those following the Freudian direction, interpret dreams — or rather the images of dreams — as indirect expressions in which instincts are at once disguised and realized. But there is another attitude, another direction of innate tendencies — an attitude that some psychologists call anagogic, from the Greek ἀναγωγικός — which does not conceal itself like repressed instincts, but rather acts as an activity of orientation, of criticism, and of ideal elevation, opposed to mere instinctive projection.

Stating at the same time the cosmic scope of metaphor and its sense of spiritual elevation, Pongs writes: "It is there, in the imagined sphere of the great cosmic phenomena, that this perspective is justified — the perspective by which every authentic poet finds again in the Universe and in the self one and the same great divine unity. The images that offer themselves to them come from the very depths of things and are capable of expressing the principal features of their conception of the Universe."

The anagogic attitude can collaborate antinomically with the instinctive projection. To the merely instinctive dream of subjective scope and significance, the anagogic dream is opposed; and from their unification results the integral dream of the great poets, whose images express not only the subjective personality of the poet but their cosmic conception and — what is more — their primordial relation to cosmic life. (Hölderlin, Goethe.) In the anagogic dream the images do not flee, do not evade, do not contain reticences; rather they accept and exalt the hidden reality of things. They do not lie; they announce. And so Pongs says of Hölderlin these words applicable to every great poet: "The poet comes to be a mediator whose images reflect the divine, veiling it, just as in religious worship the divine is reflected and communicated in the sacrament. In the imaginative creation of the poet, the revealed truth comes to be the communicable truth."

The significative unity of the poetic metaphor constitutes its trans-reality — which is not unreality but rather the (anagogic) projection of vision and image toward a new dimension of the real.

And here poetry poses and at the same time resolves the great question of understanding why, in the very movement in which emotion is expressed and in which the subjective ground of the soul — musical, as Schiller would say — is exhaled, there seems also to manifest itself, in the sound of the word and in the subtler materiality of the image, the immaterial meaning of nature and of life. It is as if lyricism were to transcend itself and, rising above mere sentimental and ephemeral confidences, assume the character of true language of being — a language that, sprung from the metaphysical ground of things, is destined to propagate in space and time the splendor and the love of meaning.

But we cannot yet conclude this study.

In all the foregoing we have considered language — and more especially the word — now in studying the demiurgic operation, in which language orders the sensible world for its theoretical interpretation or practical use; now in describing what we call hypostatic emanation: in both cases as the manifestation of a pre-existent meaning, as the result of a centrifugal movement, in short as something in which the expressive and communicative wave of the soul comes to expire.

We have indeed alluded to it, but we have not explicitly studied another mode of operation of language, perhaps decisive for the spiritual evolution of mankind. It is the creative, or more precisely the formative and constructive, function of language — an operation that, following the direction impressed by Humboldt on linguistic investigations, has been studied by Ernst Cassirer with singular penetration; its principal aspects we shall now contemplate insofar as they are relevant to the central intention of this work.

According to Cassirer, language is not a simple result, a mere product without proper action in the content or activity of consciousness. Just as objective representation is not a simple copy of external reality but owes itself in large measure to the exercise of the synthetic function of the mind, so words are acts of the mind that at once collect, configure, and create the content of internal and external experience. "Language," writes Cassirer, "does not enter a world of completed objective perceptions, simply to add to individual objects, already given and clearly delimited from one another, 'names' that would be purely external and arbitrary signs; rather it is itself a mediator in the formation of objects; it is, in a sense, the mediator par excellence, the most important and precise instrument for the conquest and construction of a true world of objects."

This thesis is illustrated by the observations of child psychology relative to the formation of language, from which it appears that the name acts in the child's mind not as a mere sign adhered externally to things, but as a decisive factor in the fixing and consolidation of objects — and thereby, transcending the infinite heterogeneity of phenomena, enables a new intellectual synopsis of the manifold.

Language likewise acts as a vitally important factor in the formation of the emotional world and in the organization of the will. Language, with its orientation toward reflection, in a certain sense contains and masters the primitive force of emotion. "The vocal and verbal organization of emotion prevents its premature and purely motor explosion and the limitless and unresisting surrender to its impulse." A truth well known to the Greek moralists when they preached the subordination of the passions to the law and the orders of the Logos, whose intimate and profound meaning is essentially related to language. In this way language opens to us the path toward mastery of the inner world, as it had already done in the outer world through the formation of objects. Language constitutes the internal and external "perspective" in which we can be masters of ourselves and of things. "To the tendency, the appetite, the passion that go directly toward things, language always opposes another direction, bearing a contrary sign. In it there always coexist attraction and repulsion, which remain in a kind of ideal equilibrium. For to the need to draw things immediately to oneself and incorporate them simply into the sphere of the self, there is here opposed another need — that of distancing them from the self, of placing them outside it in order to make them 'representable' and to convert them into objects, by the very act that places them outside the self. The force of attraction is balanced by the force of abstraction." A conjunction of two processes that makes possible the intellectual appropriation of the world in the manner proper to language.

The importance of language in the formation of the world of pure imagination (games of illusion, fable, cosmic humanization) is no less notable. To appreciate this, one should consider that there exists, both in the child and in primitive man, an initial "concrescence" between image and thing, between reality and appearance — a concrescence in which language and mythical activity play a solidary and truly inseparable role. In this regard Cassirer ventures the paradox: "the child does not speak to things because it regards them as animated; on the contrary, it regards them as animated because it speaks with them." Language and universal animation are thus two primitively united functions, whose subsequent separation makes the universe, things, and objects fall into the category of pure mute matter — and consequently inanimate.

In children's play this union — this reciprocity between language and the activity of animation — may be observed with singular relief. To speak, for a child, is to transfer to objects one's own inner animation, and it constitutes a fundamental element of play; fabulation is inseparable from children's play. The word is suggested by the image and the image by the word, in such a way that both live, act, and exist for each other. From which we may conclude that language is a specific means of humanization, of anthropogony.

Finally in poetry language reaches the apogee of its constructive and expressive power. In poetry language transcends what seems like an eternal curse condemning it to obscure the very thing it shows — to weave a veil of Maya between human beings and reality. In the expression of poetry, the word seems to surpass the criticisms that have been raised at all times against language, accusing it of obscuring, deforming, and disfiguring the things it shows us — criticisms perhaps condensed in Schiller's verses:

Warum kann der lebendige Geist dem Geist nicht erscheinen? Spricht die Seele, so spricht, ach schon die Seele nicht mehr. [Why can the living spirit not appear to the spirit? / When the soul speaks, alas, the soul speaks no more.]

In poetry, indeed, the highest synthesis is achieved: what is most individual acquires universal transcendence and what is most universal configures itself in the most irreducible and unique appearance. Poetic creation is unique, new, and yet it resounds as if disclosing something unknown that was only waiting to be summoned by the word into the light, to emerge. Thus the poetic word does not conceal but reveals; and in its eternal novelty it gives us the true eternity, intimacy, and depth of life. It does not spring forth as a simple result, but radiates from within itself, and through it the poet's feeling acquires new and more living meaning.

As can be seen from the foregoing indications, language is not exclusively a demiurgic activity that orders and delimits a given matter, nor is it merely an activity of revealing a pre-existent meaning. Language appears as a creator of meaning, as an activity that not only brings to light the content of the soul but, in presenting it, transfigures it and confers upon it a new virtuality of signification and spiritual efficacy. The soul pronounces the word in which it is expressed, but the word, once pronounced, returns to the soul with a new and wonderful coefficient of meaning and life. It is the inexhaustible, the infinite virtuality of the verb.

⁂

Gathering now the threads of our exposition, we believe we can set forth in the following form the conclusions that derive from a meditation on language considered as a representative and symbolic phenomenon of the universal activity of appearing.

1° Just as in the depths of thought and of the soul there is a meaning — something that pre-exists and dominates, permeates and manifests itself in the multiplicity of words — so in the depths of reality, in the principle of reality, there exists a something, or better, a unity that unfolds, without losing itself, in the multiplicity of appearances. The speculative effort of human beings consists in finding, by way of intelligence, that unity, rising above diversity. The mystical longing aspires to annihilate one's own personal essence in the nameless source from which everything flows, and for this reason it follows the way of negation and eminence recommended by the old and immortal Plotinus. Poetry lives the principle and its diffusion in sensible forms; it is, like the soul, the great intermediary between the invisible universality of the intelligible and the One and the visible particularity of the beings in which they manifest themselves and shine.

2° That meaning of existence — that unitary meaning — issues from itself and, without losing its essence, without ceasing to be what it is — indivisible and one — manifests itself in the infinite variety of the images of the soul and the forms of nature. The world is thus — whether considered in the harmony of its totality, or in the rhythmic and melodic succession of its changes, or in the articulation of its appearances — a language. The old cosmologists spoke of "cosmic syntax" and the German Idealist philosophers, principally Novalis, regarded creation as a poem whose meaning was the ultimate metaphysical meaning of reality. According to them, creation was a "trope of the spirit."

3° Just as language exists so that someone may understand it, appearing is given so that someone may contemplate and comprehend it. The word and its significance are reflected in the ear and the mind. The infinite world of appearing is reflected in the mirror of the soul. Thus the act of reflection is, as it were, the intentional object of appearing. And just as manifesting follows being, reflecting follows appearing; and in this way: Being, appearing, and the specular reflection of appearing are given as the supreme metaphysical categories.

All being issues from itself and tends to be contemplated. And thus this sentence of Plotinus becomes clear in its mystery: "All being is contemplation, everything derives from contemplation." As if in the simple act of existing, as if in the mere positing of being, there were already something that, detaching itself from it, restored it in image, in vision, in a word that is not merely pronounced but also and principally heard.

4° We have distinguished two great directions, two great horizons in the activity of language: what we call the demiurgic function, which consists in language — like the mind of which it is simultaneously instrument and factor — exercising itself by ordering the various ensemble of objects, defining and delimiting them. It is an abstractive function that expresses, designates species, and extracts essences. And it acts with a certain exteriority upon the contents it designates and fixes. And what we call the hypostatic operation, in which language is, properly speaking, expression — the going-out-from-itself of being-in-itself. It is the synthetic language of affectivity and poetry.

Between these two functions or directions of language, inherent in the natural operations of the mind, there is a certain opposition. The one is generalizing, abstract, schematic — it corresponds to the logical function. The other — even in its most universal and significant symbolizations — is concrete, imaginative, image-bearing. It is the expressive, poetic function. The one applies to objects, delimiting and ordering them; the other supposes an emanation from the interior, an emanation that like a fluid diffuses itself in physical and psychic space. Yet between them there is an intimate relation, and the secret of that relation is without doubt the great secret of the deepest structure of the real. Language is an emanation — from the soul, from the spirit, from universal being — but the intentional object of language lies beyond it, as something that the word, at the same time and by the very act of naming, always places, with metaphysical subsistence, outside itself.

5° Language prolongs the internal and creative movement that gives it birth, and thus presents us with the example of a becoming whose phases are linked, without doubt, by a relation of filiation with the primitive unity of meaning, but which at the same time enrich and transfigure that unity by revealing it. Poetry is therefore not a mere imitation of the ideal model but the expression of an energy capable of evoking new appearances — unprecedented forms that, while manifesting it, renew meaning and transpose it to ever more distant and extensive planes.

6° Language, like every form of appearing, at the same time as it comes from being, has a returning projection toward the being that is meaning — which, while it gives itself in language, transcends it. To speak is to enact these two ontological categories, inseparable yet distinct: appearing and being. Something is revealed in the sonority of the word — something that is not the word but its signified object. To understand is to undo the mere apparentiality of the word in order to discover the supra-verbal, ontological meaning of the verb. And thus speaking and understanding deliver to us, in summary, the entire metaphysical polarity of the real.

But there is more: as is well understood, in all language there is a projection toward the thing in itself. To speak and to understand is to sense or intuit that beyond the word there exists something that the word without doubt expresses and manifests, but which is not exhausted in the mere sonority of expression. This something, though it cannot in practice be separated as pure intuition without expression, is a thing in itself. And in this way language illuminates us not only about the categorical quality, as we might say, of the thing in itself, nor only about the inevitability of its presence in every phenomenon of expression, but about the reality of its existence — which, in one way or another, becomes transparent in every phenomenon of significant seeing or hearing.

7° Language is the great metaphysical revelation: the key. Language shows us the multiplicity, the plurality of beings. Alterity is an a priori condition of language. What object, what metaphysical significance would language have — what role would it play in the universal economy — if pure unity were the only real existent? Language, then, teaches us distinction and separation. But it also offers us a teaching of the opposite sign: because it reveals the profound unity, the participation of all beings in a single reality of meaning. How could language exist if it did not presuppose the possibility of comprehension that undoes separation and fuses opposing interlocutors in the community of what is said and expressed? Language is communication, and communication is communion, participation, and unity.

And indeed — is not the cosmos a language that comes from a self, directed toward a self? And is not the comprehension of that language the return of the self to the self through appearing?
