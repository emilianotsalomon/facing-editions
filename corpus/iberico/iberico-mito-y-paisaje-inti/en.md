## INTI

The soul, life itself, is like the ultraviolet of pure vision. That is why the mythic image is not a mere luminous and remote figure, but a presence that radiates light and life together. That is why the sun — resplendent, distant, and fecund — represents in celestial space what myth represents in the space of the soul. And that is why, in short, solar cults offer the student of these mysterious expressions of the essence of man a twofold interest: that awakened by the mythic vision in itself, and that which concerns the sacred and secret relations between the heavenly body and the vital sphere where it shines.

Viewed from this standpoint, the solar cult of the ancient Peruvians achieves a perfect harmony between image and life, between the sun as a physical object of contemplation and the sun as the propitious incarnation of germinal and animic powers. Inti is the sun in the fullness of its twofold nature: he is the star that shines in the sky and the legendary ancestor of the Inca, he who infuses life into the plants and who imposes upon men the inviolable rules of order and labor.

As is well known, the mythic civilizers of mankind, Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo, are children of the sun. In this way, all the benefits of culture are placed under solar patronage, both those deriving from social organization and those concerning the harnessing of natural powers and fruits. And since, moreover, the sun is what provokes vegetal germination in the bosom of the earth, it follows that there is an admirable continuity in the progressive march of solar action which, through the mediation of the Inca, passes from the merely biological configuration of beings to the higher and more specifically human realizations of the spirit.

Inti was a god, but the metaphysical feeling of the Indians never went so far as to identify him with the supreme author of things. That supreme author is Huiracocha, who brings forth from the Lake, out of the darkness, both the sun and men together. Inti is thus, like Apollo, a child of the night. Like Apollo, he is born amid the waves, and there is likewise something in his destiny that resembles the Hellenic god, since Inti, like Apollo, was to radiate, together with the light that calls forth the forms of the landscape, the clarity that illumines the inner realm of life.

The mountain landscape, with its tormented geology and its sky of indescribable purity, provides us with a kind of first foundation for the Peruvian Indians' veneration of the sun. The sun is the light that floods space, drives away the fearsome shadows of night, glitters upon the eternal snows and instills a kind of majestic fullness over the horizon of the desolate high plateaus. The sun is color and form, it is integral splendor, the supreme point, ruler of the firmament.

On the other hand, the sun is the fiery dispenser of heat and fire, an eminent title to adoration in the high regions of the sierra, where the air is glacial and snow reigns for a great part of the year. Landscape and climate thus awakened, in the dweller of the sierra, the adoration of the sun.

But the Indians worshipped chiefly the rising sun. Like the Lycians, they loved the solar nativity and delighted in contemplating the star in the tender half-darkness of its appearance, "moist as a newborn child." Those magnificent and pure dawns of the sierra must have struck the Peruvian Indians deeply — that inexpressible transfiguration of darkness into brightness, that solemn symphonic crescendo which, enfolding in its immense unity all the forms and all the incipient tonalities of color, passes from the somber abyss of night to violet, to pale blue, to azure, and finally to the pure light in which each thing has its own coloring and sounds like a free voice, joyful, unrestrainable, amid the triumphant clamor of the cosmos.

And in that adoration their most secret and intimate longings were, without doubt, made manifest. In that daily birth of the sun the melancholy humility of the race was surely exalted. For the Indian it was the supreme spectacle, the epiphany whose distant splendor dazzled his vision and compensated, with its gratuitous abundance, for the obscure limitation of existence. The birth of the sun was for the Indian a holy hour, a moment of intoxication and fullness in which, through the working of light, he attained ecstatic participation in the inexpressible mystery of the world.

The worshippers of Mithras, the genius of light, whom the Romans called "sol invictus," sacrificed, in their hidden and nocturnal rites, a bull whose blood was, for the initiated, the nourishment of immortal life. That sacrifice was performed in commemoration of a feat of the god, who had slain a fabulous bull and, with its blood, had brought forth earthly life. The rite was, in a sense, a renewal of that divine feat, and it was bound up with a complex astral and metaphysical symbolism. The Peruvian worshippers of the sun sacrificed to the star each dawn a llama, which was immolated with its head turned toward the east.

And here we see how the mere observation of this simple sacrifice seems to deliver to us the revelation of the pathos that characterized the solar religion of the Peruvians. This offering of the llama was, who knows, an offering of salutation to Inti, or perhaps a magical sacrifice meant to provoke or hasten the birth of the sun, or perhaps also a propitiatory act to obtain, in the hours to come, the longed-for benefits of the star. In any case it was a morning sacrifice, the symbol of a primitive and sorrowful yearning that seeks light, aspires to warmth, and pays, through the bloody immolation of the beloved animal, for the daily coming of the sun. Whence it may be seen that while the Mithraic sacrifice is a symbol of dualistic and agonistic inspiration — since the death of the animal constituted the necessary condition of life — the Peruvian rite is a simple act of adoration, a materialized vow, a sorrow offered in humility, which, like the shadows of night, dissolves effortlessly before the clarity of day.

Inti is the cosmic power of universal blessing. That beneficent, prodigal, gratuitous character of solar action the Peruvians felt not only in the splendor of the landscape and in the precious gifts of the earth; nor did they merely believe they recognized it in the wise paternal organization presided over by the Inca — they perceived it, in a singular way, in the very ceremonies of the cult, which symbolized not only the veneration and gratitude of mortals but also, as Garcilaso so well observed, constituted on many occasions sumptuous feasts and abundant banquets with which the sun regaled his children.

Such was, par excellence, the Intip Raimi (the feast of Inti), which was celebrated in Cuzco after the June solstice. Then, at the break of the sun, the Inca would drink a toast in his golden cup with his father. Afterward the emperor himself, no longer as son of the sun but as representative of the divinity, would drink a toast with his nobles and then with the people. So that in the ritual chicha the gift was scattered abroad, the paternal blessing of the star was spread, offering itself thus as a supreme gift in the sacred liquor that engendered enthusiasm, amid the dazzling morning zenith of the light.

The sun of the sierra possesses an admirable power of plastic configuration. It carves and defines the appearances of the landscape. It polishes surfaces and hones the edges of the rocks. It cuts out against the deep sky the profile of the distant cordillera, and fills the abysses with shadow. And most wondrous of all: its light seems to impose silence upon things and to confer upon them a kind of solemn, vibrant immobility.

In this way Inti blazes in the space of nature and in the realm of the soul as the supreme element of stability and perfection. From him spring the rays of configuring, vivifying energy; to him return — like the golden reflections of the idol venerated in the Coricancha — the gazes and prayers of men; through him is maintained, organic and harmonious, the age-old economy of the empire. And thus, as Klages profoundly observes, the sun and the Inca, who is its incarnation upon earth, are the vital center, the head and the heart of the cosmic and human circle that turns about its fascinating majesty.

For all these reasons, the myth of Inti and the ritual ceremonies with which his appearance was greeted reveal to us with vivid clarity something that perhaps constitutes the fundamental principle of mythic consciousness, namely: that it is always a consciousness of the concrete totality of life, a totality that vibrates in the luminous irradiation of its images. The Indians, who fell into ecstasy before the dazzling appearance of Inti, lived their whole life within that vision. The sun shone forth from the depths of its legendary past just as it shone from the depths of the sky, and as it rose over the crests of the Andes it poured forth a light that was not merely light, but soul. The landscape emerged, little by little, from the bosom of night, itself like a newborn child, while the Indians, prostrate and absorbed, breathed in the moist maternal tenderness of the earth.
