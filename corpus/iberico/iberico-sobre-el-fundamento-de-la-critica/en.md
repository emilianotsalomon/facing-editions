The philosophy of criticism oscillates between two extremes: the dogmatism that, postulating the existence of norms, principles, aesthetic laws of an absolute and objective character, holds that the critical task must consist fundamentally in a confrontation between the general principle and the concrete case that constitutes the work of art; and the subjectivism that, not admitting the existence of objective aesthetic principles, assigns to criticism, as its proper function, the description of the critic's personal impressions before the work. Dogmatism, whose more or less avowed or denied origin is the philosophy of Platón, is intellectualist. The aesthetic principles or norms on which it rests are formulas that the intellect comprehends and justifies, and critical valuation is at bottom a process of logical reduction designed to subsume the concrete case under the idea or general norm to which it answers. Subjectivism, by contrast, is affective in nature. What we might call its criterion of value consists in the pleasure or displeasure, in the fineness or strangeness of the impressions aroused in the contemplator's soul by the aesthetic object, and its critical work in something like a lyrical confiding of those inner resonances.

It is true that criticism never realizes in its purity either of these two possibilities. Dogmatism cannot sustain itself in a sphere of pure intellectual and objective confrontation, nor can subjectivism absolutely renounce giving its affective reactions a certain objective significance, or justifying and explaining its lyrical impressions of art through reasons of an intellectual order. Dogmatism and subjectivism are thus two merely theoretical extremes that cannot be reconciled with the psychological and aesthetic reality of the critical phenomenon.

Where, then, does the truth lie? In an intermediate or rather eclectic solution that considers criticism as a product of the collaboration between reason and the heart, between intellect and affectivity? We do not think so, because even granting that in criticism there exists a collaboration between reason and the heart, that eclecticism leaves unsolved these two questions that constitute the nucleus of all aesthetic problematics and which are, to wit:

1st. Does an objective sphere of aesthetic values exist?

2nd. What faculty of the soul decides in the last analysis on the aesthetic value or non-value of a work?

The mere existence of criticism — that is, of an activity that formulates value judgments and, above all, communicates them to others with the aim of persuasion — demonstrates that, whatever school of aesthetic criticism one belongs to, a certain objectivity of the aesthetic phenomenon is admitted, an objectivity capable of being perceived and savored by all; from which it results, in an evident manner, that in every critical manifestation there exists an underlying dogmatism, or, stated otherwise, that every critical activity postulates the existence of a sphere of values above mere intellectual subjectivity.

But if we admit that in every critical activity there exists an underlying dogmatism, we find ourselves in serious difficulty when we attempt to define the nature of that dogmatism. It would seem that every dogmatism must be intellectual in nature — or better, rational: it is reason that defines the absolute principles and that, furthermore, judges facts and appraises them according to the degree to which those facts realize the imperatives implicit in the fundamental principles of knowledge or of practice — which stands in opposition to the teachings of Aesthetic Psychology, according to which the reaction of that name is essentially emotional and affective in nature; and so, if we hold to dogmatism we fall into the intellectualism that distorts the psychological reality of the aesthetic reaction, and if we hold to subjectivism we fall into disorientation with regard to the nature of aesthetic objectivity and above all with regard to the foundation of our approval or disapproval, since, according to an old commonplace that all accept without question, "emotion is blind."

There is therefore a solution to be sought that, without ignoring the emotional nature of the aesthetic phenomenon, is capable at the same time of explaining why criticism aspires to formulate appraisals of objective validity and not mere impressionistic effusions; and it seems to us that this solution might be found in Max Scheler's theory of emotional intentionality.

Eminent thinkers — San Agustín, Pascal — recognized the intuitive, cognitive nature of emotion and differentiated, by essential characteristics, emotional intuition from intellectual knowledge. Let us recall the "reasons of the heart" of which Pascal speaks and which reason does not know. But in general this quality of extra-intellectual intuition belonging to emotion remains unknown to philosophers and psychologists; it is regarded (Descartes) as an inferior, confused form of intelligence, or it is regarded (the nineteenth century) as totally blind. The importance of Max Scheler's theory consists, for that reason, in having clarified this intuitive character of emotion and in having determined with extraordinary depth and fineness the diverse categories and the different degrees of the emotional world; on which account, and bearing in mind that Max Scheler has defined in admirable form the types of emotional apprehensions and has glimpsed at once their richness and their hierarchical unity, he may justly be called the Platón of the emotional world.

Before addressing its application to the problem of criticism, let us state briefly what the doctrine of the intentionality of emotional acts consists in.

The term intentionality, which Scheler employs not in the ordinary sense of an end proposed to moral action but rather in a sense akin to that which the scholastics conferred upon it, is the reference to something heterogeneous to consciousness itself yet which in the act of emotive apprehension is given immediately, without the intermediary of the intellectual function. Thus through emotion we can apprehend something that lies beyond emotion itself, yet which is not an idea, a notion, but something essentially irreducible to the category of intellectual objects, something that only emotion can deliver to us and which is, to wit: value. Thus we apprehend the values of the sumptuous, the noble, the beautiful — which are neither material things nor concepts, yet are not mere caprices of sensibility either, but essences whose sole organ of perception is emotion.

From what has been set forth, the principal characteristics of emotional apprehensions may be inferred: 1st. They are apprehensions of an irrational character, that is, they refer to contents absolutely inaccessible to intellectual intentionality; 2nd. They are not apprehensions of existence but apprehensions of value; 3rd. They are given as the extremes of a polarity between yes and no: the beautiful, the ugly, the noble, the base; 4th. Emotional apprehensions are given to us as apprehensions of more or less on the scale of each particular value and of superiority and inferiority in the total world of values; they have an a priori foundation; 5th. Emotional apprehensions are eternal — in other terms, they partake of the extra-temporal character of values and of the very essence of the emotional act.

In the hierarchy of values, Scheler places the aesthetic in the plane of spiritual values, which stand above those pertaining to the agreeable and the useful and in a position of inferiority with respect to the values of the sacred.

And well, criticism would be the exercise of emotional intuition in the sphere of art. This intuition discovers values — objective values, since they are not given as mere modes of being of emotion but as its intentional objects — whereby both intellectualist dogmatism and impressionist subjectivism are overcome, because criticism is neither a cold academic deduction nor a lyrical transcription of temperamental movements, but rather the expression of what the soul lives in the world of true artistic reality, where the individuality of the work appears as impregnated and suspended in the universal and eternal atmosphere of the values of the beautiful; and thereby, moreover, both the possibility and the difficulty of everyone's participation in the goods of beauty and art are explained: the possibility, because those goods are given as universal and permanent objectives in the world of the spirit; the difficulty, because, like Platonic reminiscence, emotional apprehension of values needs to be awakened in the depths of the soul through a careful education.

In offering these brief indications about the essence of aesthetic criticism, we have presented the critical phenomenon in its most simple, abstract form. We are not unaware that aesthetic valuation presents itself, as a general rule, surrounded by an apparatus of erudition and technique: the psychology of the artist is studied, his life, the influences he received and those he may have exerted; the form and content of the work are analyzed; its difficulties, its deficiencies, and its technical achievements are determined. But it seems to us indispensable to say that, from the standpoint of the theory we have briefly set forth, all that erudite and technical apparatus is useful and even necessary, provided it limits itself to being a device designed to project before the eyes of the spectator a pure image of the work.

And finally we add that this theory joins the effort of contemporary philosophy to vindicate the objectivity of the spirit — that is, the objectivity of those values that, to employ an Aristotelian language, we may call the final and supreme forms of emotional life.
