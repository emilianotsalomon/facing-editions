## Vorwort (1906) — Preface (1906)

This work was already published in November 1903 as a “Programme for the Rectoral Ceremony of the University of Basel,” and has undergone here – apart from the appended essay – only minor additions and improvements. After some hesitation I yielded to suggestions I had received to reprint it, on the thought that the question stirred up here belongs before a broader forum of the public than a university programme can win for itself.

It may not be wholly superfluous to draw attention to the title of this book. It speaks of the origin of the philosophy of nature, and that means: not of its paths and goals or of all the conditions of its development; it speaks of the origin of the philosophy of nature, and that means: not of all natural science, or indeed of all observation of nature; it explains this origin less from mysticism itself than from the spirit of mysticism, from communion with it. In spite of all this it will happen (I already have samples of it) that many will read out of the book: natural science is to become mysticism. They will not be talked out of it that by the “origin of the philosophy of nature from the spirit of mysticism” I really mean the “future of natural science under the spell of mysticism.” And at such a prospect they are indignant, and rightly so. But it is no different than if one understood Darwinism as the doctrine that human beings are to become apes. What good does it do me to declare: knowledge of nature has turned ever further away from mysticism, and it ought to (cf. p. v)? I shall be called a mystagogue all the same, and can one be given a more shameful name today? If the ancients took it as their highest pride to be called descendants of the gods, the moderns will put up, as regards their descent, only with the company of the apes. They believe in nothing that is not explained from the animal. They despise everyone who does not degrade them. And if the ancients excused themselves with: naturalia non sunt turpia [natural things are not shameful], so one must today beg for mercy: theologica [p. ii] non sunt turpia [theological things are not shameful]. It is far from me to disturb the moderns in their cult of the talking apes, the clever horses, the blond beasts and the experimental rabbits; in the last analysis I should only like to show that it too is a cult, that already in the admittedly undeniably lowly origin lies the germ of the higher, that in the natural the divine is already divined, in the physical the spiritual already holds sway, in the first objective the subjective already works as a driving force.

Knowledge is cleavage, articulation, separation, and subsequent connection. Unity must precede the separation, no longer as an indifferent state, but as a conscious, lived, importunate one. The unity of the psychic and the sensuous, of subject and object, is lived in feeling. From the swelling, the overswelling of feeling the cleavage of knowledge breaks open. Whoever eats of the tree of knowledge departs from the paradise of feeling. But the tree grows in paradise. The first knowledge grows up in the mystical, that is, as speculation of feeling. And again and again the mystical primal unity must be lived or re-felt, so as to keep awake, amid all progressive separation, the unity of knowledge, the organic wholeness of the human being, who is not merely knowing. Again and again thinking must plunge into the basic feeling of life, in order to draw the strength for ever more life-alien abstractions, for ever more feeling-alien discoveries about nature. Again and again mystical spirit must rise up out of central depths, in order always to be overcome, consumed in the peripherally separating science. Without unity, no drive to separation. Only from the depth comes the strength for breadth.

Let no one fear that the mystical is here treated mystically, the power of feeling praised feelingly. What is wanted is a sober, strongly philological-historical investigation, in order to uncover the common origin of the spirit of the philosophy of nature, in its three classical ages, from the spirit of mysticism. The main emphasis of the investigation falls on antiquity, partly because in the Presocratic philosophy of nature we are given altogether the first [p. iii] epoch, and so the historical origin of the philosophy of nature, partly because there the mystical keynote or overtone has so far been recognized least of all, and a new conception of this whole philosophical epoch must therefore be established. The genetic bond of mysticism and philosophy of nature was clearer, and for that reason quicker to show, in the Renaissance, and the little-exhausted parallels between the philosophical beginnings in antiquity and in modern times could here be made fruitful for a mutual interpretation.

Most clearly of all does that bond of mysticism and philosophy of nature lie before us in the epoch of Schelling. The brief indication that sufficed here receives a certain supplement through the appended essay on archaic Romanticism, originally independent. In substance, with its demonstration of the deep kinship between the old philosophers of nature and the Romantic philosophers of feeling, it belongs here unmistakably, however much it differs in tone from what precedes. Not that, weary now of the dry tone, I wanted to append a satyr-play to the seriousness of the investigation. My colleagues in the field must not put this tone on trial here; it is not meant for them. We professors of philosophy, often found too light by the scientists and too heavy by the laity, will never strike the right tone for everyone; placed at the gate between science and the spirit of the age, we must speak to both, and differently, in fuller tones outward than inward. Is not the distinction between esoteric and exoteric almost as old as philosophy itself! Every means is permitted when the aim is to warm the spirit of the age to the philosophy most remote from the age and seemingly also most alien to it; every means is permitted, except the one that touches the substance. In matters of substance I demand strict criticism. But whoever judges the weight of the substance by the lightly winged tone is not much better than some schoolgirl who finds a lecture bad because of the speaker’s grey necktie. Our scholarly critics, to be sure, have a different taste; they demand grey neckties.

[p. iv] But it is more serious than that! For it was precisely today, to the reawakened Romanticism, that I wanted to link a new understanding of the old philosophers of nature, and after the manner of those historians who have found little following in the history of philosophy, I wanted to regenerate something of the living affect of the old thinkers, to reawaken a gleam of the splendour that we do not see, because we see white statues with extinguished eyes in cold museums and not the coloured originals in the open air and sunshine of the South. The old masters would hardly recognize themselves in the shadow galleries of our handbooks, and do not the Romantics, even more so, refuse every lifeless conception?

This book, then, is no handbook. I say so because our modern scholarliness is oriented almost solely towards writing, understanding and judging handbooks or portions of them, that is, monographs. I give here by no means a course of lectures on Presocratic philosophy of nature or Renaissance philosophy, or even on the history of the natural sciences; I intend neither lexical nor didactic nor specialist completeness, and if someone tells me that Parmenides or Cusanus or Paracelsus can be presented otherwise, I shall smile in agreement; for I did not want to “present” at all. With all respect for those who feel called to reproduce, for the hundredth time and more or less clearly and skilfully, the long-familiar material: what mattered to me here was not the material but the line, a new illumination that brings to light overlooked fundamental features. There is, then, in this book a conscious one-sidedness; what is otherwise known of the material is not meant to be overlooked here, but presupposed. One-sided this attempt must indeed appear, since it essentially seeks to uncover the speculative fountainhead of knowledge of nature. Methodical induction, the mechanistic interpretation of the world, in short the tendencies that have grown up around the speculative germ, covering and overcoming it more and more to the point of invisibility, are not to be, and cannot be, misjudged on that account. Their right [p. v] is so undisputed, their power so unconquerable, their otherness so clear, that I preferred not to speak of them at all. The fact and the necessity of the growing estrangement of knowledge of nature from mysticism, precisely on the ground of other tendencies, is conceded; the explanation of it is a special chapter, and I should like to write it myself some day, or at least to try to characterize a non-mystical motive of unfolding knowledge of nature that has long hovered before me. Here, however, I speak of the germ and not of the unfolding.

A net of heavy problems is already drawn up with this, problems that for the sharper observer are suprahistorical. The history of knowledge becomes the knowledge of history, of the deepest history, the history of the soul, of the connection between the soul and its object and between knowledge and other psychic functions. Psychology and epistemology thus reap from history, and – who knows? – perhaps the springs of metaphysics too will gush forth again, if one digs them open in mysticism and philosophy of nature, whose true, final reconciliation metaphysics is. Or can it not happen, as in the nineteenth century, after Hegel, history inherited the legacy of philosophy, that now history in turn, flooded with material, transforms itself back into philosophy?

Basel Karl Joël

## Vorwort zur zweiten Auflage — Preface to the Second Edition

The two earlier editions having long been out of print, this book appears here as an unaltered new edition. However much the author might, after two decades, improve and supplement in matters of detail, the structure of the whole, to which he holds fast, was not to forfeit by patchwork the unity of the first draft. I could indeed welcome, from the literature that has appeared in the meantime, quite a number of very diverse special studies (by Gruppe, W. Nestle, O. Kern, W. Capelle, Frz. Strunz, Rob. Eisler and others) as a welcome enrichment from differing approaches, especially since they do not contradict the basic line of this book and in part expressly agree with it; but its task was less to round off and conclude on all sides than merely to stimulate, to push forward, to break through in a direction neglected by the traditional view. It therefore presents itself as “consciously one-sided,” as the earlier preface (p. iv) already avows. The supplement envisaged there as necessary I have sought to provide, for the Greek philosophy of nature that stands here in the foreground, in my “Geschichte der antiken Philosophie” I, 1921, to which I must urgently refer if the total picture of the thinkers is to be fully clarified, especially of those already so strongly estranged from mysticism, such as Anaxagoras and Democritus. In this later book, along with the adoption and extension of the results of the earlier one, ample non-mystical (and non-lyrical) formative factors have also been drawn in for Greek philosophy, for whose development there the high road from mysticism to plastic art, from Romanticism (in the sense delimited here, p. 128ff.) to classicism, is indicated. Mysticism nonetheless remains the chief source of speculation, and if the demonstration offered here, along with manifold assent, has also met with contradiction in spite of all the evidence, this is a matter of course with intellectualists and positivists; for they can speak of mysticism only as the deaf speak of music. Yet [p. vii] these tendencies themselves find, no doubt, that it is high time for them gradually to die out. All the same there are also modern learned literati who, in their whole irresponsibility, without any survey of the history of philosophy, but also without a breath of religious experiencing and without any sense for the drive and distress of speculation, accept the latter essentially as literature and its mysticism merely as a linguistic turn of phrase. With the cinema-like haste with which modern critics often read, they do not grasp the sharp delimitation of the concept of mysticism given here, as a totalization of feeling into the infinite, the Absolute, up to the experience of unity of God, soul and nature, but by leaving out individual moments they blur mysticism into that mist which alone they grasp of it. They do not suspect that the thinking human being must first look at nature with a believing, shining, wide eye before he learns to examine it with a sharp eye; but they themselves, the blasé a-mystics, should learn from Plato that the beginning of philosophy is wonder and that the “uninitiated” have to keep silence in it.

For all that, it must be stated that the general mood of the age in the most recent decades has become more favourable to the perspective presented in this book. Mysticism and Romanticism have opened up more deeply to the experience and contemplation of this age, and a classical Orphic scholar too has arisen from it (who, by the way, mentioned this book with friendly citation). Large-scale comparison of religions, furthermore, has now broken through from outside the all too anxiously guarded barriers of zones, times and domains, and the young generation has also grasped inwardly that philosophy is not there merely to turn its back on all faith and all intuitive imagination. In short, it is at last coming to consciousness in the fermenting spirit of becoming of the age that a new worldview can be won only with the totality of the powers of the soul, only as a philosophy of life, and precisely as a philosophy of life this book seeks to [p. viii] present the becoming world-speculation of antiquity, like that of modern times and of the age of Schelling. As a becoming speculation, it is true, which certainly needs again and again long intellectual clarification; but after all clarification, down to the point of emptying out into specialism and rigidifying into formalism, speculation must again and again renew its content in immediate experience of wholeness from the fountain of youth of mysticism. With such a result from the comparison of the three ages of becoming of world-speculation, this book thus reaches beyond the historical into the philosophy of history, and into the very becoming of a modern worldview.

Basel, June 1925 Karl Joël

## Der kosmische Anfang der Philosophie und seine Erklärungen — The Cosmic Beginning of Philosophy and Its Explanations

Our textbooks are accustomed to delimit the first main period of Greek thinking as Presocratic philosophy of nature; they are right to simplify the facts didactically in this way; for in so doing they also follow the well-known tradition of the ancients: that the first λόγος of Greek philosophy was the physical one, that Socrates no longer philosophized about nature, that he called philosophy down from heaven to earth and introduced it into the cities and houses, and so on. Every disposition is crude, and research, free of didactic considerations, may knot the severed threads together again at the points of division. Yet what is at stake here is not only the transitions but the beginning; the question at issue, important for the history of the sciences and that means for the self-criticism of the sciences, is: how could Greek philosophy, and in it European science, begin essentially as knowledge of nature, and precisely as knowledge of the widest, remotest and strangest, in order to advance slowly enough to the knowledge of what is humanly nearest and most our own? How could it take the road from the stars to life instead of the reverse? Precisely in the oldest thinkers there is most cosmology, astronomy; among the younger philosophers of nature it becomes strongly biological, among the Sophists anthropology, and in the Socratic school philosophy of life, philosophy of culture or of spirit. How could knowledge of the object, asks the epistemologist, advance at all, and so far, ahead of that of the subject? Aristotle, who surely knew what knowledge meant in antiquity, sets the πρότερον πρὸς ἡμᾶς [prior for us] and the πρότερον φύσει [prior by nature] in opposition – and yet was what is first in nature also first in knowledge? However the epistemologist may come to terms with this, we wish to approach the question essentially historically – to the benefit of epistemology as well.

[p. 2] Obvious, to begin with, would be the answer, or rather the way out: Greece did not produce those natural sciences but received them in a mature state from the more highly developed Orient – and it is astonishing to me that the orientalizing derivations of Greek philosophy do not appeal to the difficulty of any other explanation of its cosmic beginning. Greek philosophy, after all, arose just when Egypt had opened itself to the Greeks and the Orient came far to meet them in the kingdom of Lydia, and it arose precisely there at the gates of the Orient in Asia Minor, in Miletus, the harbour of Sardis; in short, it arose through stimulation from the Orient: this will be doubted least of all by one who sees in Greek philosophy a mighty emancipation from the Orient, the most genuine achievement of Hellas. The Orient cultivated the astronomical, meteorological and mathematical sciences because the administration of the great empires, and for Egypt also the annual floods, demanded measurements of time and land: so the ancients themselves already knew, in part. This does not hold for Hellas; yet another practical need, navigation, could suggest astronomy to the Greeks as well, and the greatest of the harbour and trading towns of which the Hellas of Asia Minor consisted, the mother-city of eighty colonies, Miletus, became indeed the mother-city of philosophy. And the very ἀρχηγός [founder] of philosophy, the Milesian Thales, is said to have shown seafarers the Little Bear as a heavenly compass, as a guiding star for the north (after Callimachus, Diog. Laert. I 23), to have recommended a geometrical theorem he had found for its significance in estimating maritime distances (Procl. in Eucl. 352), even to have written a “Nautical Astronomy” (Simpl. phys. 23, 28. D. L. 23. Plut. Pyth. orac. 18). And even if the case on the spuriousness of all the writings of Thales is closed (perhaps somewhat too quickly and too summarily), it remains significant that precisely this ναυτικὴ ἀστρολογία [nautical astronomy] attached itself earliest to his name, and so was most readily credited to him. He is said, further, to have enabled the army of Croesus, by a diverting [p. 3] canal, to cross the Halys (Herodotus I 75), and his journey to the land of the learned priests on the Nile, whose floods he, in turn, is said to have explained, is indeed not secured even by the multiplicity of testimonies, but is probable, especially in the age of Milesian trading-stations and mercenary quarters in Egypt. And now, of course, this strong nautical and hydrotechnical interest pointed him to making water the principle of the world? So an eighteenth-century rationalist would perhaps conclude. We, however, shall surely have doubts whether one makes something into a principle because one masters it. Are the seafarers and hydraulic engineers adherents of Thales? Have Phoenicians and Egyptians set up or acknowledged the philosophy of the water principle? No, the instructing Orient and the practical purpose may explain the mathematician, meteorologist and technician Thales, but not the cosmologist, the philosopher, who lays down in germ a worldview, a total science. There remains a weighty fact: the technical, practical need and understanding was far stronger among Phoenicians, Egyptians, Babylonians and Chinese than among the Greeks, and yet precisely this least inventive of the cultured peoples founded real knowledge of nature, probably just because the practical purpose receded with it. Greek natural science kept a strikingly untechnical trait, to its benefit and to its harm. Strong in typifying apprehension, it remains short-sighted for heterogeneous causality and function<sup>1</sup>.

<sup>1</sup> H. Nohl, in his astute work “Sokrates und die Ethik,” is dissatisfied with these statements, which no one can dispute and which he too evidently wishes only to restrict a little, when he objects: “these physicians as well as Socrates take their bearings, in their opposition to mysticism, precisely from technique [Technik], and so there arises in them the knowledge of cause and function; for that reason both appear to us so modern” – so this tendency is after all unancient, as I maintain. I myself presented Socrates as the heir and champion of τέχνη (“Sokrates” I 194f.); but Nohl spoils everything when he takes the primordially Greek τέχνη to be one with modern “technology.” Ancient τέχνη is precisely not very technological; in his characterization, somewhat partisan, I admit, of the untechnological Hellenic culture, Du Bois-Reymond compares it to those coins on which the Greeks stamped a splendid divine countenance but which they did not know how to make round. Ancient τέχνη is precisely typifying, it carries types forward, and in just this sense Socrates seeks to establish types, to chisel out concepts, and asks constantly after the “What?” of things: τί ἐστί [what is it?]; Nohl knows this, and yet he ascribes to him precisely the other, the causal-functional way of thinking? – The physicians, however, give no pure picture here, not only because the Greeks in medicine learned a great deal from the Orient and it would first have to be proved that they surpassed their teachers precisely in practical-technical sense, but also because the Greek physicians “in their opposition to mysticism” are by no means agreed. Nohl himself even finds it a gap (though one not at all laid down in the theme) in my work that “the medicine founded on mysticism is not taken into account, especially since the historical connection of the Renaissance with this literature is the clearest.” I confess that to me it is the least clear, but all the better if I can refer to Nohl for such a supplement to my evidence.

The Orient had to teach not only technical mastery and calculation of nature; it also possessed imaginative apprehension of nature in its mythology; but with that too no philosophy of nature was given. Even the developed mythological world-system of the Babylonians, which, especially since the flowering of the kingdom of Lydia, could find paths enough to Greek ears, lacks the essential thing, the philosophical. Only the Indians do not lack it. Yet apart from the doubtfulness and only very indirect possibility [p. 4] of Indian influence, the more generally human ground of the philosophy of nature, to be developed later, would seem to have the advantage that it lies closer to the Greeks than India does, and moreover could explain the Indian parallel itself. But whoever simply explains that essential thing from the speculative genius of the Greek race may lay down his pen, calm and proud, and hold everything further superfluous: the future will smile at this self-satisfied worship of a word, which takes itself for science and is half sloth, half superstition.

Mythology, or more generally put soon, the power of religion as such, cannot explain the philosophy of nature, otherwise the Orient would have been far more capable of producing it than Hellas. Or did Greek religion and mythology perhaps offer a specifically speculative element? It is astonishing enough: one can call it far rather anti-speculative, more perhaps than any religion: the more colourful and plastic, that is, the more Hellenic, this polytheism became, the farther it stood from the abstract unity of thought; and in fact the Greek philosophy of nature, already in its beginnings, plainly presses towards overcoming it. Xenophanes and Heraclitus fight passionately against the plastic cult, against the fables of the ancients, against Homer and Hesiod, who, according to Herodotus, gave the Greeks their gods. No one, certainly, to whom one gives only Homer to read of the Greeks, would sniff out in him in advance the Ionian philosophy of nature.

But have not the ancients themselves already seen in him the forerunner of Thales, since he extols Oceanus (Il. XIV 201), together with the mother Tethys, as the origin of the gods and (ib. 246) as the origin of all things? Yet this is unmistakably a tendentious construction, already smiled at by Plato in the Theaetetus, from the time when one began to adorn a philosopher with as archaic witnesses as possible and to interpret Homer allegorically. No modern will so press these innocent poetic turns of phrase, issuing from the imagination of a seafaring people who see the ocean embrace everything, and all land rise out of this [p. 5] boundless, that the water principle of Thales springs out. It is a long way from the personally conceived and locally determined Oceanus to all water whatsoever, and a still longer one from all water to all being. The Homeric words perhaps lead still to the subsidiary thesis of Thales, that the earth rests on water, but not to his main doctrine: Everything is, in its essence, water. One may take the Homeric words geographically or theogonically; philosophical they are not. The philosophical lies only in what Thales has in common with the other philosophers of nature, and that is precisely not the emphasis on water, and so the Homeric parallel falls away all the more.

But is it not after all the theogonic poetry, which the Homeric here already touches, that is the forerunner of philosophy, and so the philosophy of nature, after all, a product at least of the further developed Greek religion? Aristotle, however, already clearly separates the oldest philosophers as φυσικοί [physicists] from the θεολόγοι [theologians], and the moderns will probably in the main agree with v. Wilamowitz, who finds as “the power which finally stepped into the way of theological development with superior enmity,” “the Ionian natural science,” which was only “worldly,” “appealed only to the understanding; the heart remained cold” (Hom. Unters. 214. 218). Such an opposition would now cut off entirely the religious explanation of speculation about nature, and so we ask again: how could it arise?

From the sight of nature – so childishly hardly anyone will answer any longer today. It is, to be sure, the simplest explanation, but also the most impossible. It is no different from explaining Newton’s discovery by that apple which he saw fall. Billions of apples had fallen before billions of eyes without the law of gravity being found. Countless peoples saw nature and did not become philosophers of nature, the “nature-peoples” least of all. How many pious souls may have seen church lamps swing, until Galileo derived from it the law of the pendulum and became a heretic. “Let no one believe,” he says [p. 6] later, “that the reading of the loftiest thoughts, which stand shining on the open pages of the book of heaven, is finished once one merely gapes at the splendour of the sun and the stars at their rising and setting, which the animals, in the end, can do too.” And Galileo called himself a philosopher, as did Kepler, who says: “My discoveries have not flowed down into my soul from heaven, but they lay at rest in its depths, and my eyes saw the stars, and the stars awakened those ideas in me only insofar as they stirred me to tireless thirst for knowledge about their nature.” Plato and Aristotle prove right once more: θαυμάζειν [wonder] makes the beginning of philosophy, but for θαυμάζειν the external occasions, the θαυμαστά [wonders], do not suffice, for of these too the unphilosophical Orient had more than Hellas.

Not from outside, then, did speculation about nature come, not from the mere sight of nature, not from the teachings of the Orient or from practical-technical need, but the mythologizing religion too seemed to fail as an explanatory ground. So neither the sight of nature, nor the calculation and mastery of nature, nor the mythology of nature create the philosophy of nature. For all that the unphilosophical Orient had as well. Not the beholding senses, not the calculating understanding, not the practical will and not the extravagant imagination produce speculation about nature, so it does not stem, in the first place, from perceiving, thinking, willing, representing – what then remains of the human spirit? Feeling. But Greek philosophy of nature a product of feeling? That seems more than baroque. The knowledge of the object precisely from the most subjective function? The knowledge of the external world precisely from the innermost of the soul, the knowledge of strange things, and precisely of the remotest first, from the most personal part of the human being? That seems impossible, because contradictory. This philosophy of nature, in which Hellenic clarity and keenness of spirit triumphantly found European science, is supposed to stem from the darkest interior, from the eternally [p. 7] indeterminate, vague, dull feeling? This knowledge, which is supposed to leave the heart cold, being only “worldly,” only “of the understanding,” precisely from the heart, from the never cold, never sober feeling? The philosophy of feeling is called mysticism, but Greek philosophy of nature a child of mysticism? Where then remains the opposition of the φυσικοί to the θεολόγοι? And the religious origin seemed after all impossible?

## Die Naturmystik der Renaissance — The Nature-Mysticism of the Renaissance

Whether the philosophy of nature can be reconciled with mysticism, indeed whether it can originate from it, is a question on which one should first consult history, which can never strictly prove what is impossible, but certainly what is possible. History knows three classical ages of the philosophy of nature: the age of the Presocratics, the Renaissance, and the first decades of the nineteenth century. And in the two later ages the philosophy of nature shows in any case its closest kinship with mysticism in its striking coincidence with inward religious uplifts, partly in the same period, partly even in the same persons.

First of all, it is an oft-stressed but for that reason no less important and astonishing fact that Columbus and Copernicus belong to the age of the Reformation, that is, that the greatest expansions of the outer world went together with the strongest stirring and deepening of the inner world. Piety and aesthetic feeling were powerful driving forces in these great wideners of the horizon. The lack of symmetry in the Ptolemaic system of the world had disturbed and goaded him, Copernicus confesses in the dedication of his work – to the Pope. And Columbus feels his undertaking to be a divine mission, foretold in Holy Scripture, and in his accounts of his voyages he becomes a poet and a painter. On the other hand Luther feels how his new awakening of faith opens the eye to nature. It is the well-known passage of the Table Talk: “We are now at the dawn of the life to come, for we are beginning once more to attain the knowledge of the creatures which we lost through Adam’s fall. Now we look upon the creature quite rightly, more than formerly under the papacy. But Erasmus asks nothing about it and troubles himself little over how the fruit is formed, prepared and made in the mother’s womb; nor does he regard the estate of marriage, how glorious it is. We, however, by God’s grace begin to recognize his glorious works and wonders even in the little flowers, when we consider how almighty and kind [p. 9] God is; therefore we praise and extol him and thank him. In his creatures we recognize the might of his Word, how mighty it is. Even in a peach stone: although its shell is very hard, yet at its time it must open up through the very soft kernel that is inside. This Erasmus passes over lightly and does not heed it, and looks upon the creatures as a cow looks upon a new gate.” Can a φυσικός [physicist] not yet grow out of such a θεολόγος [theologian]?

And in turn the philosophers of nature feel with the Reformer. They are almost his contemporaries: Theophrastus Paracelsus, the new creator of chemistry, who at the same time liked to have himself called the Luther of medicine, and Agrippa of Nettesheim, who praises the “irrefutable heretic” against the monks, just as Bruno later exalts “the Hercules” Luther to the skies. But it is not merely a standing together against the same enemies; it is an inner community, a common origin in mysticism, which together with the seed of the Reformation bore within it also the seed of the philosophy of nature, in its being pervaded by the immanence of God. God is in all creatures, preached Tauler, whose theology Luther praised as the soundest, and still more glowingly the mysticism of the lyric poet of God, Suso, raves of the springtime splendour of nature and the divinity of all beings. Before knowledge of nature comes the transfiguration of nature, which stems from mysticism, and that means from feeling. The mysticism of that age of transition retained a living feeling for nature, from that spring which Suso greeted with a spiritual May-song, to the summer day when Jacob Boehme is drawn out through the gate by his God-filled heart and believes he can see into the innermost nature of all creatures, as indeed he is said often to have divined a plant’s innermost properties from its form and colour. Boehme is the consummator of that mysticism which in Sebastian Franck already declared nature to be something divine. God himself, he held, is in nature; to follow nature is therefore to follow God, and whoever remained in nature would remain in God. Deepest, most pious mysticism here presses toward a valuation of nature which even the materialist cannot [p. 10] surpass. One discovered nature by seeking God in it. Things first lit up and grew young in the wondrous radiance of the creature. It may be said of that age: all mystics pressed more or less toward the philosophy of nature, and especially among the systematizers, from Cusanus and Pico della Mirandola to Boehme, Patritius and Campanella, one can only waver whether to call them rather theosophical mystics or rather philosophers of nature. A separation of the two tendencies or interests would be purely arbitrary for that age. For just as the mystics were at the same time philosophers of nature, so in those days investigators of nature and physicians were at the same time mystics. Not only Cardanus and Caesalpinus, but Kepler too is one to such a degree that Laplace was appalled, and so are the great chemists Helmont, of whom the elder, seized by Thomas à Kempis, comes to medicine and to magical knowledge of nature, filled with the doctrine that God is in all things. In the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries alchemy and astrology are indeed only in full bloom, and knowledge of nature renews itself and takes a new upswing, reaching its summit as magic, which Agrippa of Nettesheim proclaims as the consummation of all the sciences, although he, like the magician Pico della Mirandola before him, disputes astrology. Paracelsus too, the enemy of the alchemists, wants as a true Magus to interpret the signature of the heavenly in the earthly, feels himself called by God, believes in his divine illumination, which reveals to him the secrets of nature, and teaches like the older mystics: only he who unites himself with God knows things in their primal source.

Let us ask now how in this way mysticism can issue in a philosophy of nature, how precisely the subjective deepening of the religious can stimulate and shape a knowledge of the objective world. What is intelligible first of all is how mysticism, which draws God into the heart of man and gives the highest to the individual as his own, thereby strengthens the feeling of individuality, the spiritual self-confidence, and so opens the way to an independent, new contemplation of the world. Admittedly it is an individualism of feeling, but feeling [p. 11] is not merely a counterpart of knowledge; it is precisely also what stimulates and drives knowledge, which presupposes that its objects attract, interest, and so in some way appeal to feeling.

But feeling (which at first is still fused with sensation, with perception) is more than an impulse toward knowledge; it is already nascent knowledge itself. Feeling is undifferentiated knowledge, as knowledge is differentiated feeling. Feeling can after all contain the same thing as knowledge, the same object, only in undifferentiated form. The progress of knowledge certainly lies in differentiation. But the differentiation of objects must be preceded by that of object and subject, which in feeling are one. The knower has separated his object from himself and has it as an alien counterpart, as object; the one who feels has it in unity with himself, that is, subjectively. To feel something means nothing other than to grasp something in unity with oneself. And now we ask: how does man come to speculate about the alien things outside him, about the distant objects, and to want to understand them? He must take an interest in them, but that presupposes that he somehow grasps them as belonging to himself, in unity with himself, that he feels them. Feeling builds the first bridge between subject and object. He must first warm himself to things, relate them to himself, draw them into his life-process, thus appropriate them inwardly, fuse them spiritually with himself, before his consciousness will set them apart from itself in cool objectivity and determine them.

Feeling, then, as it precedes knowledge, grasps the object in unity with the subject. Now the mystical feeling is of course only a particular feeling, and so contains the unity of the subject only with a particular object, the unity of man, of the soul, with God, and it is not yet foreseeable how this mystical feeling of God is supposed to precede and promote precisely knowledge of the world, and specifically of nature. But there must nevertheless be a reason why mysticism teaches, alongside the immanence of God in the soul, also his immanence in the world; for God is to the [p. 12] mystic the source of the world, the universal ground of life. The mystical feeling is precisely not an arbitrary feeling, but so to speak the superlative of feeling, the total feeling, the highest, ecstatic, infinite feeling, the feeling of the infinite. All mysticism stems from a heightened feeling of life, and every expanded feeling of life leads in the end to mysticism. Every feeling draws its object into the life-process of the one who feels, every feeling feels life, and to the mystic in his expanded feeling of life God is the highest, infinite object of feeling as such, and so God is life itself. For the mystic in his great mystical moment everything is transformed into feeling, and that means everything into God. Faust’s proof of God from all the great felt facts of nature, from the sentence “Feeling is everything,” this proof is mystical. With the mystical God-filledness of man the God-filledness of nature is in fact and necessarily given; for the one filled with God feels everything immersed in God, everything divine, everything one in God. And precisely when the world becomes God for the mystic, God in the end turns back again into the world. Mysticism as the philosophy of feeling grasps everything as unity and life. God, world and soul become one for it in the life-process, in felt-ness. God and world, the highest and the widest object, are drawn by it into the consciousness of life in the subject, are felt, are made conscious in their unity with the soul. Therefore mysticism teaches the ensoulment of the world, the worldliness of God, the divinity of the soul.

The philosophy of the Renaissance is wholly pervaded by these three doctrines – Cusanus and Bruno proclaim them just as do Paracelsus and Reuchlin, Kepler and Jacob Boehme, Patritius, Caesalpinus and Cardanus. What speaks in them is the expanded and deepened feeling of life and self-consciousness of the man of the Renaissance and the Reformation; for God, world and soul become one in the universal life-process which the subject feels in himself – the philosophers of that age are all fanatical vitalists. It is really a fourfold unity that they all teach in the [p. 13] felt unity of life: the unity of man, of the soul, with God, the unity of God with the world (whether as immanence or as pantheism), the unity of the world as such, and the unity of man with the world. The first doctrine of unity is the most original, that of the old mysticism, and it remains alive throughout the whole philosophy of the Renaissance. The second is attached to it, but the divinity of the world now becomes fully conscious for the first time, since the deepened feeling expands at the same time, the feeling of God in the Renaissance lets the variegation of the world be drawn into it at the same time, and the infinite feeling opens itself as feeling of the infinite.

With the divinity of the world its unity is given, its unity precisely in God. The divinity of the world means, after all, the feelability, the vitality, of the world. And feeling melts everything into unity, and only what forms a unity has life. The whole philosophy of the Renaissance is monistic; and most loudly in its typical systematizers, in Cusanus and in Bruno, who rails against Aristotelian dualism. Even the passionately sought renewal of Plato did not at that time lead to dualism; they do not find the genuine Plato, they grasp only the Neoplatonic doctrine of the radiation of the world from the divine One.

From unity the Renaissance sees the variegation of the world radiate, and therefore it stresses the world as unity in multiplicity, as harmony. However differently they teach, Bruno, Kepler and Jacob Boehme alike stress the world as harmony. That the ellipse presents a harmony, multiplicity in unity, gives it in Kepler’s eyes the preference over the uniform circular orbit as the path of a star. As Copernicus took offence at the confusion of the Ptolemaic picture of the world, so Galileo finds the Copernican doctrine convincing because it is the simplest. The unity, order and concord of nature, he held, correspond to the wisdom of the Creator, and the true is at the same time the beautiful. The aesthetic-religious tendency, feeling itself with its drive toward unity, is here productive of truth. From mysticism stems the doctrine, to be won from no induction, of [p. 14] the unity of nature, the sense for nature as a whole, and with it all philosophy of nature and at the same time the foundation of all real knowledge of nature.

But precisely because it stems from the mystical feeling, it is at the same time productive of error. Alchemy and astrology are nothing other than the false, because too hasty, consequences of that felt truth, the erroneous exaggerations of that drive toward unity. They are not superstition grown old, but precocious knowledge; they belong not to the dying Middle Ages but to the awakening modern age. Alchemy believes in the unity of substances by exaggerating their transformation, astrology believes in the unity of forces by exaggerating their connection. They believe and exaggerate only what modern science too believes and seeks. They exaggerate because the presaging feeling outruns differentiating knowledge in the unification of nature, which they think they can grasp far too directly and simply in the philosopher’s stone and by means of the horoscope. Magic too, which even the despisers of alchemy and astrology retain as their art, rests wholly and entirely on the most penetrating feeling for the unity of nature; indeed, the magician is only the effective embodier of this unity of nature. True magic, says Pico della Mirandola, investigates the connection or the sympathies of all beings. As the husbandman joins the vine to the elm, so the true magician weds the things of the earth to the powers of the heavenly bodies. And similarly Pomponatius, Agrippa and Paracelsus<sup>2</sup> and still Helmont explain magic, the force that acts at a distance, from the interconnection of the universe, and one can see how in all those doctrines the unity of nature goes together with, indeed derives from, the divinity and ensoulment of nature, from the mystical unity of God, soul and world in the felt life-process. Thus, e.g., Joh. Baptist Porta teaches in his book on natural magic: a universal world-spirit or breath of life binds all things together, also begets our souls and enables them for magic. [p. 15] Nature, he says, is like an animal in which one member shares the joy and suffering of the other; thus precisely is it conceived and treated by magic. The soul of the world, Agrippa teaches similarly, is one single life that fills and binds all things. As in the human body one member is moved by the movement of another through sympathy, so all others are touched with one part of the world, and since words belong to the world, a Magus can through them, by virtue of this reciprocal love, work up into heaven. The universe is like a taut string which, touched at one end, resounds at once everywhere. Everything is in everything and acts upon everything. All things attract or repel one another, are friendly or hostile to one another.

Naturwiss.), finds the designation of Paracelsus as a magician antiquated and my characterization of him here somewhat one-sided. But I do not intend to supply a complete characterization of Paracelsus here, only to draw out certain features that are not popular or recognized in modern times and to illuminate them through parallels. And he himself calls himself Magus and interprets true magic. One must only not think, in the case of the “magician,” of the conjurer of the fairground booths and other deceived deceivers. I draw a fairly thick line between the nature-mysticism of the Renaissance and the old popular superstition, which probably interests Strunz more. It is a gratifying sign of the times to me that in his beautiful book on ancient contemplation and knowledge of nature, which appeared at the same time, he repeatedly touches my tendencies in emphasizing the subjective antecedents of the knowledge of nature. Cf. also his essay: [p. 152] “Die Natur als psychische Lebensmacht im antiken Phantasie- und Geistesleben” (Zeitschr. f. Naturwiss. Bd. 76). Yet in it he deals far less with classical antiquity, least of all with the ancient Greek philosophers of nature, and far more with that primordial mysticism of popular fantasy which is far removed from philosophical mysticism (cf. p. 96). On the other hand, another difference is only apparent. Str. has me “derive the critical vision and the typical character of the examination of facts and causes outright from primary mystical motives” and “displace induction from the history of the natural sciences by the moment of feeling.” Where in all the world do I say or do that? It is that misapprehension characterized in the Preface: because for me the origin of the philosophy of nature lies in unity with mysticism, the whole history of the natural sciences is supposed to be mysticism for me. But if I dispute its omnipotence and primordiality, I still do not fail to recognize the mighty historical significance of induction for the natural sciences.

These comparisons of nature with the living being plainly attest how the unity of nature derives from universal animation. In the general doctrine of the Renaissance of the sympathy of all things there lies unmistakably a humanization of nature. But it is ridiculous to condemn anthropomorphism; it is necessary, and it signified progress at that time, for it brought an organic conception of nature.

And what mattered was more than an analogy between man and nature, it was a unity of the two – precisely that fourth unity –, but a unity not merely because man belongs to nature as a part of nature, but because he is the whole of nature in nuce, the quintessence of nature. Man the bond of the world in miniature, says Cusanus, the centre of the world, says Reuchlin, the model of the world, says Leonardo, the perfected image of the universe, the bond and symbol of all things, says Agrippa; man the microcosm – so the whole philosophy of the Renaissance stresses it for the most part expressly, indeed it is its central idea, for it is in the literal sense humanism. And precisely this central exaltation of man stems from mysticism, which wants to “deify” man. Eckhart, after all, places men above the angels and says: were man not, all things would not be. And Reuchlin, Melanchthon, Taurellus and many others agree: all things exist for the sake of [p. 16] man. And it was bound truly to intervene in the knowledge of the world when Luther extolled the “honour and eminence of a Christian man,” who “through his kingdom is lord of all things.” Knowledge of the world is self-knowledge, teaches the mystic Weigel, and man is the active ground of knowing, which in all growth only comes to itself. Heaven and earth with all beings, and God himself besides, lie in man, says Jacob Boehme.

The philosophy of the Renaissance understands man from nature because it understands nature from man. From man, according to Paracelsus, the secrets of nature are to be read out. “So that the philosopher finds nothing else in heaven and in earth than what he also finds in man, and that the physician finds nothing in man than what heaven and earth also have.” Man eats and drinks from the elements for the maintenance of his blood; his senses, his spirit draw on the power of the stars, but one should not say that he takes after Mars, but rather Mars after man, for man is more than Mars and all the planets. All creatures are, as it were, only letters that point to man as a whole. So taught Paracelsus, and Agrippa too finds in man the signs and characters of all things, and Cusanus compares the human spirit to a brightly polished diamond in which all things are mirrored, and which, if it were alive, would need only to contemplate itself in order to find in itself all the likenesses of things. All knowledge, says Paracelsus, lies in man himself, and all teaching is an admonition to search within ourselves. All men carry all the sciences innate within them, teaches Taurellus, who likewise turned from a θεολόγος [theologian] into a physicist and physician. Knowing, finds Kepler too, is like an awakening from sleep; as the number of stamens in plants, so are the ideas and harmonies innate in men. The eternal measure of things passes over to man as the image of God and is not received through external perception. The lawfulness of the sensible world awakens the innate [p. 17] lawfulness in our spirit, in accordance with which we then explain the former.

It is therefore perverse to make the philosophers of the Renaissance, even the scientific ones, into pure positivists and empiricists; they are rather the opposite. But do they not precisely demand and stress experience, the observation of nature, sense perception? Yes, but over against scholastic authority. Man should trust himself, his own senses, as against tradition. They stress, then, not so much the objects of sense as the subject of sense. Their empiricism is strongly sensualism, and is so even, and most of all, in Telesius, the founder of natural science, who makes sensation and self-impulse the cosmic and ethical principle. Their stress on experience is to be understood from the Renaissance’s drive for independence and for life. So the contradiction in Paracelsus is explained, who wants to understand everything on the one hand from man, on the other hand everything from nature. The investigator of nature too could at that time assent to the mystic Weigel: knowledge must well forth from within into the object. Not the object is what is effective, but our eye, our understanding. It was not the eye that then made man see, but man who made the eye see.

Those men wanted to experience, to enrich themselves inwardly, to feel; therefore they opened their eyes and often went far afield on their wanderings. In truth they went precisely beyond the senses; Columbus and Copernicus emancipate themselves precisely from the evidence of the eyes, rise precisely far above the data of the senses, Galileo stresses the subjectivity of most qualities of perception, and all the others seek beneath the given sensible reality the hidden secret. A strong urge toward mysteries runs through the whole age. It frequently forms secret societies for secret arts, teaches secret sciences, practises all manner of symbolism – from Roger Bacon’s epistola de secretis operibus and Petrarch’s secretum suum through Reuchlin’s ars cabbalistica and Agrippa’s occulta philosophia to Vanini’s dialogues on the wondrous [p. 18] secrets of nature, Jacob Boehme’s Mysterium magnum and Kepler’s Mysterium cosmographicum. In this procession of mysteries the wisdom of that age avows its descent from mysticism. The reason is that behind everything outward, sensible, material it scents an inward, psychic thing, a divine spirit of life, which is supposed to connect all things by an inward, psychic bond among themselves and above all with the soul of the magician, of the God-filled man generally. But this bond is invisible; the consciousness of this inner community that pervades the world must be felt, is a subjective experience, and so not everyone’s affair but only that of the illumined, hence mystery. Thus on the mystical unity of the human soul with God and nature rests the mystery-character of the philosophy of the Renaissance. To bridge the difference between God, soul and nature it employs a far-driven analogistics which seeks to establish artificial parallels, above all that so striking, far-driven number-symbolism which was meant to create, formally and quantitatively, that unity between the spiritual and the physical which was not given in fact. The same monistic tendency is served also by the high esteem of the word (not grounded only theologically), in which the spiritual indeed reveals itself sensibly.

The unification of the psychic and the physical makes the philosophy of Jacob Boehme in particular so hard to understand, indeed incomprehensible to the unmystical mind. There is in it a continual interplay of divine, psychic and physical processes, a shimmering of expression between all three meanings. Nothing is more mistaken than to see in this a mystifying artifice. Boehme really experiences in himself the unity of God, soul and nature. The expression of the one in images of the other is therefore natural to him. He lets it well up and ferment in God as in soul and nature, he feels in his soul the birth of thoughts out of chaos, he feels their passing by “like a downpour,” he feels their growth, “as when a grain is sown in the earth” and “sprouts forth in every storm, with a hundredfold fruit,” he feels within himself “the growth of the new man [p. 19] spring up,” he experiences the saltily contracting desire, the quicksilver mobility, the sulphur-like anguish and all his other shapes of nature, he experiences in the soul nature and in nature the soul.

If one asks how this is possible, the middle term is precisely experience. Nature and the soul both have their transformations, and the philosopher of the Renaissance understands the one from the other; he understands God himself as transformation, as an everlasting shaping, as the eternal bond of life, as Boehme celebrates him; the world, the living image of God, says Campanella. How the world foams in Cusanus, in Bruno and Vanini, in Paracelsus and Boehme, in constant flowing transformation and unfolding of life, in infinite begetting! Had nature changed so much that it now appeared so alive? No, the soul had become different, and first in mysticism and through it. Luther too finds: “Life never rests.” “Life,” says Dilthey of him, “is for him the first thing” and the source of religious knowing. And Franck’s mysticism wants to let God live in itself, and faith consists for him in inward transformation. As in the human soul there is a perpetual welling of the mind and begetting of thoughts, so in the divine being and in nature there is an eternal birth – this comparison of Boehme’s says enough. The philosopher of the Renaissance has so intense a feeling for the transformations of nature because he himself in his soul is so capable of transformation and so eager for it, so full of life. He understands nature as the image and echo of his many-moved soul – that is what makes him a philosopher – and he demands this great resonance of nature because his soul overflows with the urge of life. He wants to experience himself in things, in ever new forms of expression, in infinitely many objects. The I without an object is empty. It is precisely the heightened self-feeling that demands more objects; the subject deepens itself precisely in the fullness of objects. Weigel speaks of the “counter-throw” of knowledge. Thus mysticism has grasped the concept of the object, and with it the goal of knowledge, precisely because it emphasizes the subject so vividly. The [p. 20] feeling of life of the man of the Renaissance is stronger than his person; it swells over onto things. He wants, as I said, to experience, to live through, the process of life in as many varied shapes as possible. So he lives through nature. It is an empathy with nature, to use this modern term, which perhaps still says too little, for it is just as much a feeling of the process of nature, an experiencing of nature in oneself as an experiencing of oneself in nature.

Today people want to explain art through the principle of empathy – one would do quite right to explain the philosophy of the Renaissance too in strongly artistic terms. It is no accident that it has the greatest artists as contemporaries, in Italy as in Germany; it shows in Leonardo the most wonderful union of artistic seeing and anticipatory knowledge of nature; it writes poetry itself, from Petrarch to Campanella; it writes poetry itself as German mysticism, from Suso and Tauler to Angelus Silesius; and in its classical consummator Bruno it experiences, and also demands in principle, the full unity of thinker and poet. Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio are customarily put at the head of Italian Renaissance philosophy as its inspirers, just as the mystics of the same fourteenth century are put before German philosophy – the awakeners of aesthetic and religious feeling are precisely the forerunners of knowledge. And that feeling retained its driving, often only too dominating force in the philosophy of the Renaissance is shown already by the fact that frequently the most passionate spirits of that passionate age were its most stimulating thinkers: the history of philosophy hardly knows hotter natures than those Laurentius Valla and Petrus Ramus, those Agrippa and Paracelsus, Bruno, Vanini and Cardanus. In these combative, adventurous, restless, wandering men there is precisely a breakthrough of the highly swollen feeling of life into thinking that had stiffened into formulas, even into logic, which they seek to enliven and to simplify by relating it to rhetoric; for life and feeling press toward simplification as against distinguishing thought. The whole anti-Aristotelianism of the philosophy of the Renaissance is [p. 21] an overcoming of forms through experience. It is the reference to one’s own feeling of life that at that time brought about an age of reformation for knowledge as well.

Hegel speaks of the drive for originality in Cardanus, who is typical in this: “His positive merit consists rather in the stimulation he imparted, to draw from oneself.” Goethe characterizes it more clearly: “Cardanus regards the sciences everywhere in connection with himself, his personality, his course of life, and so there speaks from his works a naturalness and vitality that attracts us, stirs us, refreshes us and sets us into activity. It is not the doctor in the long gown who instructs us from the lectern; it is the man who walks about, takes note, is astonished, is seized by pain and joy, and presses upon us a passionate communication of it. If one names him pre-eminently among the renewers of the sciences, then this character of his, which has been indicated, has helped him to this place of honour as much as his efforts.” This character is precisely his hotly living, strong nature of feeling, which again has the wonder with which philosophy is supposed to begin, and “is seized by pain and joy” and other passionate stirrings, indeed wants to be seized. He cut and burned himself, like the flagellants, dervishes and fakirs, he bit his arm and lips, merely in order to feel; he walked now quite slowly, now furiously fast, now in silk, now in rags, now as a Spaniard, now as a Turk. One sees again: it is precisely the liveliest feeling of the I that presses toward changing shapes; it wants to experience, and it can experience itself only in others.

It is precisely in the most subjective spirit of the emerging modern age that the feeling for nature awakens, in Petrarch, who called himself Silvanus and Solivagus. We know the scene in which he laboriously climbed Mont Ventoux, an undertaking unheard of in that time because not understood. A detailed description of the view, says Jacob Burckhardt, one now indeed expects in vain – but not because the poet would be insensible to it, but on the contrary because the impression acts on him all too powerfully. For before his soul [p. 22] there rises his whole past life with all its follies – he opens a little book that was then his companion, the Confessions of St. Augustine, but behold, his eye falls on the passage in the tenth book: “and there go men to marvel at the heights of mountains and the mighty waves of the sea and the broad flow of rivers and the compass of the ocean and the courses of the stars, and forget themselves in the doing.” His brother, to whom he reads these words, cannot grasp why he thereupon closes the book and falls silent. He had done what the great mystic, who was then “his companion,” demands: in admiring the high mountains he had not forgotten himself; he had found himself precisely in nature and enjoyed it most highly in this, that he let “his whole past life with all its follies” pass before him.

The sense of nature, then, awakens in the heightened feeling of life; the heightened feeling of life as such already demands and contains a fusion of one’s own existence with another’s, a going out beyond oneself, a drawing of other elements into one’s own process of life. To experience already means to participate, to enjoy along with, to feel what is alien, to transform oneself in what is alien. The I as mere existence is precisely empty; one feels oneself only in another; the heightened feeling presses toward another, toward transformation, toward self-surrender.

From this essence of the feeling of life is explained the apparent contradiction in the philosophy of the Renaissance, and already in mysticism, between the colossally heightened self-consciousness of man and the demanded surrender to God and nature. God needs man, God cannot live without me, teaches the mystic, and at the same time he teaches that man should die to himself and give up his I. What matters to the mystic is precisely not so much the two existences, God and man, as the unity of both in the feeling of God in man, in the experience of the soul. Both are to be absorbed in it; God is to exist only as what is experienced – hence he needs the one who experiences, man; man is to be only the one who experiences, hence the self-surrender.

[p. 23] God is for the mystic experience as such, the infinite object of experience. The mystic experiences only God, God in all things. But if God is all, then all is God: the deification of the world is also a worldification of God. In that the mystic makes God inward, draws him into the soul, he has in fact at the same time externalized him, naturalized him. For in relation to the soul, to the subject, God and nature become one as the infinite object, as the total experience, that which fills the soul entirely. The immanence of God in the soul leads, as said, to his immanence in nature. In the soul God and nature find each other – that is the core of the philosophy of the Renaissance, which with this emphasis on the human soul is rooted in mysticism. Only for the self-feeling of the experiencing soul does nature, so manifold, stand there as a unity, as a total object – otherwise the grasping of nature as a whole, and with it speculation on nature and knowledge of it, would be the greatest riddle. One sees only particulars and has no occasion to grasp the world as world, as a whole – even the addition of what is seen does not yet give the All. It is attained only in the feeling of the All, in the soul’s urge toward infinity. And this urge toward infinity first expressed itself as mysticism. In the mystical feeling the soul first widened its experience to the infinite, summed up all its experience into a unity and valued it as an object demanding surrender. Mysticism has thus widened the horizon of the soul, has raised man beyond his limited bodily sphere, beyond appearance to the spiritual grasping of what is not visible, has urged him to surrender to the object, has given him trust in his own experience, hence in independent knowledge and experience; in sum, then, mysticism has educated him to science.

In God as world-soul, as mysticism understood him, the world as such was first brought near to the human soul. In God, the infinite primal unity, as mysticism stresses him, the human spirit first experienced the world as unity and infinity. From the infinity of God Bruno proves the infinity of the world – only the deeply mystical [p. 24] Cusanus preceded him in this; the investigators of nature, even Copernicus and Kepler, still want to know nothing of the infinity of the world, and yet they too sought and found the invisible order of the world, precisely out of a genuinely mystical feeling for the divinity of the world, for its harmonious unity corresponding to God, and out of an equally mystical trust in the human soul that bears God’s secrets within itself and knows them.

The infinity as well as the unity of the world, these yields of the philosophy of the Renaissance, were not won from inductive inferences. It is feeling that bears within itself the tendency toward unity and infinity. Feeling is at once fusing and boundlessly extravagant, in contrast to thinking, which divides and limits. The mere heightening of feeling leads in the end to the feeling of the All, which fills the soul as one and without limit. The urge, then, to fill the soul entirely, this mystical urge, leads to the grasping of unity and infinity. In that the philosophy of the Renaissance, rising out of mysticism, presses toward filling the soul entirely, toward grasping the All-One and the infinite, it presses toward the cosmos. As a pull toward the infinite, mysticism presses toward the widening of the horizon beyond what is given to the senses; as a pull toward unity, it presses toward the harmonious order of this widened All. Thus the feeling of the All in mysticism presses toward cosmology and astronomy.

So now at last is solved the problem why young knowledge begins precisely with the widest horizon, with the remotest object, and why the new soul of the age of the Reformation sees at the same time a new world, why deepening is at the same time widening. Mysticism gave the human soul the urge and the courage to grasp totality. God is for the mystic first of all that which fills the soul entirely, but the more he is a mystic, the more he lets his self die away, his soul be absorbed in God, the more God becomes for him that which fills everything, the more he experiences in God the whole content not merely of the soul but of the world. The more the mystic fills his soul, the more he forgets his soul. The utmost subjectivity thus turns [p. 25] into the utmost objectivity, since in the mystical feeling the unity of subject and object, and thus their passing over into each other, is given. The urge toward the highest inwardness becomes the urge toward the remotest. In feeling, man first conquers what is remotest. For feeling, because it is extravagant, the pull toward the far is the most natural, the most immediate, the least enigmatic; to the feeling soul the remotest stands as near as the nearest; it experiences both within itself. “In the youth,” says Schopenhauer, “all perception first produces sensation and mood, indeed mingles with it; as Byron expresses very beautifully:

I live not in myself, but I become

Portion of that around me; and to me,

High mountains are a feeling.”

He draws the high mountains, as it were, into his soul, but at the same time he draws his soul to the high mountains. It is as with his forerunner Petrarch, whom it draws to the high mountains, where he – knows himself. It is precisely the lyric soul that longs for the distance and soars in rapture to the stars. The music of its soul it wants to hear again in the harmony of the spheres, as Kepler too expressly recognized in the course of the stars only the harmony inborn in his soul.

In this bringing of the stars near to the human soul astrology had its root. Man has a share in the sidereal sphere, teach Agrippa, Paracelsus, Weigel and all the other nature-mystics; he bears heaven and earth in his soul, says Boehme; he eats in bread heaven and earth and all the stars, says Paracelsus, and the physician must at the same time be an astronomer, just as in Campanella the head of the state is at the same time an astrologer. The sidereal sphere, they teach, stands closer to God than the earthly, and so in fact for the mystic the cosmic, astronomical interest lies nearer than the special, earthly one. The feeling of God in mysticism is a feeling of the All, and so the mystic grasps the All sooner than the particular, the remotest, the infinite sooner than the near and finite; indeed he grasps the finite through the infinite, the particular through the whole, and thereby he founds [p. 26] scientific knowledge. To mysticism, and to it alone, the infinite is not the last but the first, in it alone the pull toward the All, the germ of the cosmic conception, is inborn; it experiences the All, the infinite.

The God of mysticism already bears totality, the character of the All, within himself. And as I said, the more mysticism obeys itself, the more the mystical soul sinks selflessly into its content, the more it feels in its feeling of the All the All, the more the subject of the feeling vanishes in the object. Paradoxical as it sounds, the deeper mysticism goes, the more it grazes the border of naturalism. The philosophy of the Renaissance did indeed turn into naturalism, in the wild radicalism of a Laurentius Valla and Vanini, who in the end declare nature to be God. But Cusanus too, for whom the world becomes the explicated God, and Boehme, for whom even evil falls within God, do not stand so far from the pantheism that Bruno reaches, and from which again it is only a step to naturalism. To be sure – all these are transitions, possible paths, not necessary consequences. Some unity between God and nature, even with all the superiority of God in value, lies in the essence of mysticism; God and nature become one as the totality experienced in the feeling soul. Eckhart already teaches: God is all and all is God, and as God is all things, so the soul too is all things, the noblest of all things. The soul, whose elevation and filling is quite properly the core of mysticism, unites for it God and nature. The divinity of nature means the ensoulment of nature. In God as All-soul nature becomes one for the mystic with the human soul, is subjectivized. In God, the All-object of the mystical soul and at the same time the All-subject of nature, the sought-for bridge between subject and object is given. Nature can be in the soul because it is in God.

In the God grasped mystically (that is, psychically, as the fulfilment of the soul), then, the totality of the world is first laid on man’s heart, the remoteness of nature brought near. The All of nature is felt, that is, experienced by the soul, grasped as life. The contact between soul and nature means the [p. 27] vitality of nature, and God is for the mystic precisely the ground of this contact, the ground of life, life itself. The contact between soul and nature, since they are different, can be grasped not in being but only in transformation. The soul transforms itself in and with nature. Nature transforms itself with the soul, that is, precisely, it lives, it unfolds. The ensoulment of nature is the vitality, the unfolding, of nature. The mystical soul, that is, the soul filled with the All, thus presses toward attention to the transformations of nature, in which it finds itself again. God, soul, nature one unfolding, one life and thereby one unity of essence – in this thought lies the unity of mysticism and philosophy of nature.

It is surely clear – a slight hint may suffice – that this whole conception is also the dominant one in the third classical age of the philosophy of nature, at the beginning of the nineteenth century. We have this unity of mysticism and philosophy of nature in all the renewals of Boehme and also of Bruno, who have expressed the interpenetration of God, soul and nature in the unfolding of life perhaps most ardently and richly, above all in Schelling and the great investigators of nature of his school, the Okens, Caruses, Oersteds and so on. Then in Baader, the mystic and geologist, also in Schopenhauer, who not for nothing esteems the “German Theology” and other mystics, only putting the anti-God in place of God, but otherwise grasping nature as the unfolding of life of the All-drive, to be understood from the human soul. He calls the world the macranthropos, as Novalis at that time calls man the source of analogies for the universe, as both also wish to renew in magic the practical nature-mysticism of the Renaissance. And Oken grasps the whole animal kingdom as man laid out asunder into his component parts, and Steffens is filled with the deep-lying connection of man with the whole of nature. Mysticism contains, once and for all, a strong impulse to illuminate nature in its secrets and to grasp it in its infinity. It may seem accidental that in France the great biologists at that time appeared simultaneously with the theological school of philosophers [p. 28] appear. More evident still is how precisely the infinity of their subjectivism leads the Romantics to the philosophy of nature. The companion of these Romantic philosophers of nature is Schleiermacher, who grounds religion precisely on feeling, draws it most deeply into the soul, subjectivizes it most purely – certainly a theologian estranged from nature; but let us not forget that the very feeling in which he places religion, the feeling for the universe, is the feeling of dependence upon the infinite. The Deity is for him the unity of the world-whole, and every part of the world stands for him in reciprocal interaction with the others. Once again it is shown that the expansive, cosmic drive, the idea of the unity of infinite nature, is innate to genuine mysticism.

## Mystische Subjektivität bei den antiken Naturphilosophen — Mystical Subjectivity in the Ancient Philosophers of Nature

### Der lyrische Zeitgeist — The Lyrical Spirit of the Age

Now at last the real main problem begins: can this interpretation of the philosophy of nature out of mysticism, or at least out of unity with mysticism, which we have found, be applied to antiquity, in particular to the Presocratic, that is, to the first classical period of the philosophy of nature, and so to its actual origin? Does the parallel with the two other principal epochs of the philosophy of nature hold good for it? To begin with, the one, the Renaissance, expressly presents itself as a renewal of antiquity. People have here thrust Plato and Aristotle too dominantly into the foreground. I believe the age was least of all akin to these. Dilthey, in his significant essays on the conception and analysis of man in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries (Archiv f. Gesch. d. Philos. IVf.), pointed to deep-lying Stoic (and, alongside them, Epicurean) influences. One ought now also to examine the renewals of the Presocratics (to whom Stoa and Epicurus themselves point back). How many, all by themselves, wished at that time to follow Pythagoras and his symbolic wisdom of number: Gemistos Plethon and Marsilius Ficinus, Reuchlin and Agrippa, Patritius and Campanella, who wrote a didactic poem on Pythagorean philosophy, and many others look back to him. More important still is that Copernicus expressly finds his world-system to be a renewal of the Pythagorean, which as such was also condemned by the Church, and that, on the other hand, Bruno and Kepler extol Pythagorean astronomy. The Italian philosophy of nature rises in Magna Graecia like a resurrection of the Presocratics buried there. Telesius may be called by Bacon the restorer of Parmenides, whom, besides him, Plethon had already taken as a model and Bruno still did. Bruno in general proudly feels himself a kindred spirit of his Eleatic countrymen and of Empedocles, and his friend Naudé [p. 30] praises him for having been born in the fatherland of Parmenides, Philolaus and Zeno, and for having resembled them in character as well. And finally it has now been observed how far Galileo touches Democritus, whom he places above Aristotle.

These indications may suffice, but they suffice only to prove a conscious relation, not so far-reaching a parallel that one might unscrupulously draw further inferences. Undoubtedly the renewal of antiquity proves some community of soul with it. Undoubtedly, on the other hand, there are deep-lying differences which make every comparison between antiquity and modern times dangerous; but to renounce comparison on that account is to renounce knowledge; we do not understand antiquity by quoting it, but by translating it. Let the differences be brought out as a warning wherever they matter. What is lacking, as I said, in the culture and especially in the science of the Greeks is the strong practical, technical trait of modern times. Yet this trait, just because it does not lead to the unity and infinity of nature, nor to the cosmic view, did not create the philosophy of nature, not even that of modern times; it only afterwards sharpened the mechanistic conception, whereas the philosophy of nature in its awakening is vitalistic. The practical England stands far behind artistic Italy and religiously contemplative Germany in the philosophy of nature of the Renaissance, and yet centuries before the great Bacon founded knowledge of nature mechanistically on the practical principle of invention, his countryman Roger Bacon had dreamed of great ships steered and swiftly moved by one man, of flying instruments and automobiles. Here too, then, the wish is the father of the thought, dream and impulse the forerunner of experience and knowledge.

Not the practical-technical, but other deficiencies of Hellenic spiritual culture threaten to forbid that parallel, because they are precisely deficiencies which seem to exclude the mystical explanation of the philosophy of nature. Mysticism is religious subjectivism. But in religious power classical antiquity stands far behind, as behind the [p. 31] Orient so behind the ages of Boehme and Schelling, and subjective consciousness appears in antiquity so weakly developed that philosophical subjectivism is pretty much the only modern tendency that has no ancient parallel, just as the Greeks also lack a real naming and valuation of feeling (as against the objective and lower αἰσθήσεις and πάθη [sensations and affections]). And now it is precisely a combination of what antiquity more or less lacks that is supposed to enable it to create the philosophy of nature? Through the two factors of mysticism, the subjective and the religious, that is, from the simultaneous emphasis on soul and God, the consciousness of nature ascends, – so it showed itself, and all other explanations failed. It only remains to examine once more the relation of the Greek spirit to these two factors of mysticism.

Subjective consciousness, to begin with, is certainly not the strength of the Orient (at least of the Orient within the Greek horizon) – but are not the Greeks, by contrast, the people of independence and self-knowledge, of many personalities, of heightened individualism? But individual and subjective do not coincide; independent, original does not yet mean emotional. Greek individualism was indeed not exactly favourable to the development of many feelings, such as the social ones. And yet! Swelling subjectivity awakens individuality and presupposes a disposition to individuality. There must have been an epoch in Greece in which both were at one, in which individuality awoke subjectively, in which the urge to independence had not yet turned critical against the feelings, in which it expressed itself subjectively, emotionally – and such an epoch, in short a lyrical epoch of the Greek spirit, there indeed has been, and precisely in this its subjective epoch it created the philosophy of nature.

It seems a strange coincidence in time, the flowering of lyric poetry and the awakening of the philosophy of nature, – we now know from the parallels that it is not accidental but deeply grounded, and we see a confirmation in [p. 32] this coincidence. Who, then, are the awakeners of the sense of nature in modern times? The most lyrical spirit of the Renaissance, Petrarch, mystical lyric poets such as Suso and other lyric poets of nature, then in the eighteenth century Haller, the lyric poet and philosopher of nature, and his Swiss countryman Rousseau, the philosopher of the “heart,” and finally the arch-lyric poet Goethe becomes a student of nature on the grand scale. Epic poets, dramatists, didactic poets live in historical, courtly and urban, in short social worlds; the lyric poet feels himself alone with nature; it is nearest to him as material, image, echo. The poetic celebration of nature is in itself already lyrical, and the spring song is probably as old and as original as poetry itself. The lyrical spirit is a brightly seeing spirit, not merely a feeling one. For it feels itself in another, to which it gives itself in seeing. Our crude school aesthetics sees only the subjective side. But the lyric poet is no egoist, and an egoist is no lyric poet. Fr. Th. Vischer knew better; he says in his Aesthetics: lyric poetry is “an ever-accomplished, ever-withdrawing passage over onto other ground, a hovering between the pure, unconscious perceiving of oneself and the conscious perceiving of things, a mist with bright glimpses through.”

Lyric poetry has existed always and everywhere, but perhaps there was never a people that had so pronounced a lyrical epoch as Hellas in the seventh and sixth centuries. As this people shows all historical forms more purely, so too the sequence of the epic, the lyrical and the dramatic age. At that time the feelings swelled, pressed for utterance, and thereby – so one may say – the Greek soul awoke, there awoke the profusion of individualities that is peculiarly Greek. Lyric poetry became a conscious art form which, being far more mobile and rich in forms than the epic, drove personalities forward and upward in their particularity and their ability in a thousand genuinely Greek contests, and stamped them ever more sharply and consciously.

The sharper and more conscious they became, the more critical. In lyric poetry self-consciousness first expresses itself, [p. 33] and in it the world, life, is first felt, valued, grasped as the great Other to the I. So long as the I rejoices in life, is at one with the world, feeling remains in its natural unity and in its purely lyrical utterance. But the feeling of dissonance drives beyond itself to reflection. If the I suffers from the world, then it sets the world over against itself, feeling decomposes, becomes critical representation. Plaintive lyric poetry is philosophy in the making, pessimism is decomposed lyric poetry: as lyric poetry is the poetry of youth, so pessimism is the philosophy of the years of youth, also of those of the Greek people<sup>3</sup>. Greek lyric poetry, which develops so significantly as elegy, shows early on, in a strikingly pessimistic trait, the urge to reflection. The well-known, ever-recurring loud laments of the Greek lyric poets over the transience of the bloom of life have comparisons with nature close enough at hand<sup>4</sup>, and there speaks in them that intense feeling of life and that awakened sense of the power of transformation which proved so fundamental for the philosophy of nature of the Renaissance. The feeling for nature that precedes knowledge of nature comes to conscious, loud expression first precisely in lyric poetry. Cf. what is well known on this in Bergk, Griech. Literaturgeschichte II, 109f.: “That the Greeks did not lack sense and receptivity for the beauty of nature is proved precisely by the surviving remains of the lyric poets. To be sure, the depiction of nature is never an end in itself, and elaborate descriptions of the surrounding landscape are not exactly frequent; one contents oneself with a few but telling touches, and the image of nature is mostly presented as a reflection of the mood of the heart.” Thus similarly to Petrarch. Here among the Greeks we grasp precisely that feeling of a unity of soul and nature which emerged as the main motif of the philosophy of the Renaissance. “The depiction of nature is the point from which lyrical feeling spreads further.” – Bergk refers to the Alcaeus fragments 34, 39, 45 as examples – “The poet is impelled to make known the intimate feeling for nature which he feels.”

<sup>3</sup> Cf. on the poetic and youthful character of the pessimists my “Philosophenwege,” p. 202f.

<sup>4</sup> Cf. as a further example that offers itself the nature-parallel in the likewise pessimistic Bacchylides III 87.

[p. 34] And in the midst of this age of lyric poetry swollen with feeling for nature, the philosophy of nature now arises<sup>5</sup>. And as lyric poetry leads toward the contemplation of nature, so, on the other hand, the philosophy of nature bears strong lyrical traits and traces. To begin with, this philosophy of nature appears in part in poetic form, and if we judge its origin and character neither by its late offshoot Democritus nor by the thinkers known only indirectly or from meagre fragments, then it presents itself in wide measure as poetry. No one will take the verse-writing of Xenophanes, Parmenides, Empedocles, of whom, besides Heraclitus, we have precisely the most fragments, for accidental, for an empty form. Xenophanes, on whom the other two are dependent, is a rhapsode, and it is surely not a matter of indifference that this first Greek philosopher who speaks to us in longer fragments, the far-reaching father of a main tendency of the “philosophy of nature,” practises poetry by profession and brings forward his wisdom in the consciousness of competition with other poets. Let no one say that the poetizing philosophers of nature are, after all, epic poets in form. Apart from the fact that Xenophanes in particular also offers lyric poetry (ἐλεγείας καὶ ἰάμβους [elegies and iambs] Diog. Laert. IX 18), the hexameter does not make the poetic. The Ionian philosophers of nature do not write in verse, and yet the prose of Heraclitus is more poetic still than the poetry of Parmenides. Whence this indestructibility of the poetic even in verseless language? The epic, the dramatic, the didactic can be wholly dissolved into the prose of narrating, presenting, teaching, the lyrical cannot. There is an independent material, an objective content, which in its poetic form can be detached from the poet himself; here in lyric poetry, however, the essential thing is precisely the relation of the poet to his material, the relation of subject to object, of the I to the world. But precisely this holds also for the philosopher of nature. What impression the world makes on him, how it appears to him, and what it means to him, that is what the lyric poet and the philosopher of nature say; for philosophy of nature is more than description of nature, it is interpretation of nature, through [p. 35] which it is brought nearer to the spirit and its need.

<sup>5</sup> How even today the feeling for nature can stimulate knowledge of nature in poetic intuition, in sympathetic harmony of nature and soul, Helmholtz has attested as a classical witness in those golden words which he spoke in praise of Heidelberg: “Is it a coincidence that from these green hills the spiritual gaze of man first penetrated the immeasurable spaces of the universe with the insight of how the chemical nature of the heavenly bodies was to be deciphered, an undertaking which immediately beforehand must still have appeared the most adventurous of impossibilities? I believe the opposite. Something of the poet’s vision must also be borne within the investigator. Admittedly the latter requires laborious and patient work in order to sift the material and make it ready. But work alone cannot compel the ideas that give light. These spring forth like Minerva from the head of Jupiter, unexpected, unforeseen; we do not know whence they come. Only this is certain: they do not come to one who has known life only among books and papers, nor to one who is vexed by monotonous labour. The sensation of fullness of life and strength must be there, as is given above all by wandering in the pure air of the heights. And when the quiet peace of the forest separates the wanderer from the unrest of the world, when at his feet he embraces in one glance the rich, luxuriant plain with its fields and villages, and the sinking sun spins golden threads over the distant mountains, then there stir sympathetically, in the dark background of his soul, the seeds of new ideas, fit to make light and order shine forth in the inner world of representations, where before there was chaos and darkness.”

In a threefold way ancient philosophy of nature attests that here an I is seeking its relation to the world, wishes to grasp its relation to the world; in a threefold way, then, it attests its strongly subjective character, and in this its community with lyric poetry. All three moments recur in the philosophy of the Renaissance, aim more or less at the mystical unity of the soul with nature, and belong altogether to the essence of mysticism.

### Selbstgefühl — Self-Feeling

The first is the tremendous self-feeling and the wholly personal tone of these philosophers of nature. They do not hide behind the object like the specialist researcher, like the epic poet and the dramatist as well; they say “I,” and say it as loudly as only the lyric poet does. They say: I think, I know, I speak. I will proclaim from what all things arose, I will begin anew, returning, deriving speech from speech, I implore you, Muse, hear my prayer, now that I make known a good doctrine about the blessed gods, but you, hear first the fourfold root of all things, hear the unerring course of the proof, and so on: thus personally speaks, e.g., Empedocles (fr. 4. 6. 8. 17. 35. 38. 131, cf. further fr. 1. 9. 21. 23. 24. 25. 62 – I cite the fragments of the Presocratics according to the edition of Diels), and, be it well noted, it is precisely his main doctrines that he brings forward with so strongly subjective an accent, as personal dogmas. The same holds of Parmenides, who declares in fr. 11 that he will begin to tell how earth and sun and moon and the common ether and the heavenly milk and farthest Olympus and the hot power of the stars pressed toward birth. He has himself promised by his wise goddess that he shall learn everything (fr. 1), that he is to be warned against the one path of thought, that to him courage alone remains for that other, that this discourse is not to be allowed to him, that he must take to heart that proclamation, and the like (fr. 4. 6. 7. 8). [p. 36] “Herewith I end the trustworthy speaking and thinking about the truth; from here on learn the opinions of mortals, hearing the deceptive order of my verses,” “I disclose to you this whole apparent arrangement of the world, so that no human doctrine may overtake you” (fr. 8). With sovereign gesture these poets direct their material, their stream of thought and speech. “Now I shall pass on to another discourse and show the path,” sings Xenophanes.

Let no one say that Xenophanes, Parmenides, Empedocles arrive at the personal tone through the poetic form. It is rather the reverse. It breaks through the epic form as well, and Heraclitus speaks still more personally without verse: “Men do not understand words and works as I (ἐγώ) treat them, examining and explaining each according to its nature, how it stands” (fr. 1). “One is to me ten thousand” (fr. 49). “Everything that is visible, audible, learnable, that I prefer (ἐγώ)” (fr. 55). “I have searched myself” (fr. 101, cf. fr. 50. 108). But even the soberer, younger philosophers of nature do not forget themselves over the subjects of their teaching. With what self-feeling even Anaxagoras concludes a main part of his system: This, then, is my explanation of the separating-out (fr. 4). In everything everything is contained, as I set forth above (fr. 12). Καλῶς μοι δοκοῦντι [it seems to me well] – so Archytas begins his Harmonics, and similarly Diogenes of Apollonia his writing on nature: λόγου παντὸς ἀρχόμενον δοκεῖ μοι [it seems to me, in beginning any discourse]. And this δοκεῖ μοι [it seems to me], in which precisely the subjectively dogmatic consciousness expresses itself, so appealed to the calm thinker and researcher that it appears five times in his few fragments (fr. 1. 2. 5. 8), and precisely at the opening of the doctrines and where he talks big about God and the All. Even Democritus has this δοκεῖ μοι [it seems to me] in his so personal pronouncements on the possession of children (fr. 276. 277).

Altogether this most mature, most scientific of the philosophers of nature speaks so much of “I” and “we” that his usage for the first person has been noted [p. 37] (Apoll. de pron. p. 65, 15. 92, 20 Schn.); is it not tragicomic that, of the two fragments of his astronomy in particular, only the word “mine” can be noted (Diels fr. 13)? And as the philosophical poet Ion of Chios begins his “Triagmos”: ἀρχὴ δέ μοι τοῦ λόγου [and the beginning of my discourse], so Democritus probably placed at the beginning of his main work on the philosophy of nature the words: What follows I say about the All (fr. 163). He composed this work, so he reports autobiographically, 730 years after the capture of Troy, and further, he was young when Anaxagoras was old (Diog. IX 41), and he laments in astonishment: “I came to Athens, and no one knew me” (ib. 36). One may therefore, with Zeller, Gomperz and others, credit him with the words transmitted in Clem. Strom. I 15, 69 p. 356 P: “I (ἐγὼ), however, of my contemporaries have roamed most over the earth in the most extensive researches, and have seen the most zones and lands and heard the most scholars, and in the composition of lines with proof no one has yet surpassed me, not even the Egyptian surveyors.”

It is entirely the tone in which the philosophers of the Renaissance speak of themselves, as when Cardanus calls himself the seventh great physician since the time of creation, such as comes only once every thousand years, or as when Lorenzo Valla claims to have written in his Elegantiae, for the first time in centuries, real Latin again and to have taught 2000 things previously unknown, or as Kepler concludes: I cast the die and write the book, whether the present generation will read it or a future one, that is all one to me, it can await its readers, or as Bruno introduces himself as “a philosopher known at the first academies of Europe, tried and everywhere well received, a stranger to no one but the barbarians and the ignoble,” or as the quiet scholar Reuchlin defies: “Before me there has been none who ventured to bring the rules of the Hebrew language into a book, and though envy should break its heart, yet I am the first. Exegi monumentum ære perennius [I have built a monument more lasting than bronze].” Or as [p. 38] Paracelsus boasts: “Thus I have experienced the high schools for many years among the Germans, among the Italians, among the French, and sought the ground of medicine, not wishing to give myself over only to their doctrines and writings and books, but have travelled further to Granada, to Lisbon – through Spain, through England, through the Mark, through Prussia, through Lithuania, through Poland, Hungary, Wallachia, Transylvania, Croatia, and also other lands not necessary to relate, and in all those ends and places have diligently and assiduously inquired and had investigation of certain and experienced true arts,” etc.

The Greek philosophers of nature are eager wanderers, just like those of the Renaissance – again a sign that knowledge of nature springs from the individual urge of life, which drives the wanderer and thereby leads to broadened intuition. “Sixty-seven years there are already that have driven my spirit up and down the Hellenic land,” sings Xenophanes himself; “but I threw myself from city to city.” The inclination toward the autobiographical that comes forward in all these utterances recurs indeed in the times of Cardanus, but it is, if one does not count spurious letters, as foreign to later antiquity, to a Plato and an Aristotle, as to a Spinoza and a Kant. Descartes’ Meditations are the last piece of Renaissance. Yet let us not forget Augustine! The Confiteor is the natural form of mysticism. For it everything becomes personal experience.

The self-consciousness of the ancient philosophers of nature is in part a generic one – “we” they say very often (e.g., Anaximenes fr. 2 and 3, Xenophanes fr. 33, Heraclitus 21. 77, Melissus 8, Anaxagoras 4. 19. 21, Democritus 7. 9. 10. 117, Archytas 1) and mean by it themselves as men and above all as subject of knowledge; but far more lively is their individual self-consciousness. They know and say that their own “way leads away from the highway of men” (Parmenides fr. 1, Empedocles fr. 2), and they do not cease to contend against “the delusion of mortals,” against the opinions and errors of the multitude (Xenophanes fr. 14, Heraclitus fr. 1. 2. 5. 17. 57. 72 etc., Parmenides fr. 1. 6. 8. [p. 39] 50ff., Empedocles fr. 2. 11. 39, Anaxagoras fr. 17, Democritus fr. 117 etc.). And with what passion they contend! “Simpletons,” Empedocles scolds men (fr. 11), “Wretched, utterly wretched” (fr. 141)! Against whom all Xenophanes inveighs in his few fragments: against the poets of myth (fr. 1), against the venerators of athletes (2), against his ostentatious fellow citizens (3), against Homer and Hesiod (11f.), against the anthropomorphic conceptions of the gods (14ff.), against Simonides “the skinflint” (21)! Even Democritus is quite hostile to Anaxagoras (fr. 5) and to the sophistic “quarrellers and rope-twisters” (fr. 150). Parmenides, in a rage, warns (fr. 6) against Heracliteanism, against those “knowing-nothing mortals who stagger about, the double-headed, whose perplexity steers the wavering mind in their breast, who drift along, mute and blind at once, staring, an undiscerning crew.” Heraclitus himself, however, is the wildest of these hot polemicists. How he lashes out on all sides, so that hardly anyone stands before him: against men who all fail to understand his word, do not know what they do when awake (fr. 1), do not understand what they have heard, are present absent like the deaf (fr. 34), let themselves be deceived in their worldview (56), against those who trust ballad-singers and take the great crowd as teacher (104), against idol-worshippers and Bacchants (5. 14f.), against his fellow citizens, who ought all to hang themselves (121), against the learned folly of Hesiod and Pythagoras, Xenophanes, Hecataeus (40), against Homer and Archilochus, who ought to be whipped out with rods (42). Heraclitus teems with comparisons to sweepings, dung, filth, to oxen, dogs, asses, etc. (fr. 4. 5. 9. 13. 29. 96. 97. 124 etc.). But also, e.g., in Xenophanes crude animal comparisons form a main argument in his struggle against the anthropomorphism of the gods (fr. 15), and even in Democritus there seems to be no lack of them (cf. fr. 147). It is once again the strong feeling of self and of life that speaks from this vigorous naturalism and so leads from flight from men to the contemplation of nature.

[p. 40] All this wild lashing out, this contempt for men, this passion and image-rich naturalistic coarseness of polemic against formalists of belief and learned authorities can again be illustrated by parallels from the Renaissance<sup>6</sup>. Listen to Lorenzo Valla, the hurler of invectives, who attacked pretty much all the authorities of Scholasticism and compared the cloister to the asylum of Romulus, because the dregs of humanity gather in it, or Petrus Ramus, who publicly maintains the thesis: quæcunque ab Aristotele dicta essent, commentitia esse [whatever Aristotle said is fabrication], or Cardanus, who divides mankind, apart from a few wise men, into mere dupes and duped deceivers, or Paracelsus, who calls the Galenists painted physicians who cover their folly with red hats, and who publicly burns the highest medical authority, the Canon of Avicenna, or Bruno, who tells how, in the disputation with him, the “tamer of haughty, back-barking stupidity,” “that little chicken in the way,” “that swine” which the Oxford academy, “the widow of true science,” set against him, got stuck 15 times, or indeed the wild declamatio of Agrippa of Nettesheim (de incertitudine et vanitate omnium scientiarum et artium), in which he wants to bite like a dog, sting like a serpent, wound like a dragon, calls the court a school of vices, wants to recognize the nobles by their coats of arms as beasts of prey, the monks by the rope which they wear and deserve, the soldiers robbers, the jurists twisters of the law, the theologians seducers of the people, etc. etc. These are times full of struggle and coarse mockery in struggle, the times of the Letters of Obscure Men and of the Praise of Folly!

<sup>6</sup> Compare with Heraclitus’ coarse, comparative contempt for men, for example, Lionardo, who describes men as a mere passage for food, multipliers of filth, since nothing of them remains but full latrines. In that naturalistic symbolism he also seriously elaborates those animal parallels, as, characteristically, the so-called bestiaries, the allegorical natural histories, were popular in the Renaissance.

And a similar polemical-satirical spirit pervades precisely the epoch of Greek philosophy of nature and again prefigures itself in lyric poetry. It is not only the passion of war and party that sings in Tyrtaeus and Alcaeus, Theognis and Solon, there are all the laments and accusations of the elegists and above all the bitter mockery of an Archilochus, Simonides the Elder and Hipponax. And note that Ionia, the home, so to speak, of critical lyric poetry in contrast to the [p. 41] festive melos, the home of elegy and iambography – immediately before it became the home of the philosophy of nature; indeed, the birthplaces coincide: Miletus, Samos, Colophon, Ephesus, the cities from which Callinus, Simonides, Phocylides, Mimnermus, Hipponax come, are the home cities of all the great philosophers of the sixth century. Everywhere the lamenting and mocking poet precedes the thinker. Xenophanes, the philosopher of Colophon, where in Hermesianax and Phoenix elegy and iambus still bloom late, himself writes elegies and parodies, and in Epicharmus’ poetry we grasp quite directly the unity of the comedian and the philosopher. Hipponax, whom people called the inventor of parody, the older countryman of Heraclitus and later of Anaxagoras, shows a wild sarcasm and a coarseness of depiction which could well have had after-effects. The satirical poets, above all Archilochus and Simonides, preceded the philosophers of nature in drawing the animal world into parallels with man. The mocker is probably the first realist and naturalist; he points with fingers at the characteristic and sharpens the intuition of the real. He does not create it alone, he is already reaction. Conscious reality settles between two exaggerations of feeling, between ideal and caricature, between longing and mockery, between elegy and iambus.

In mockery and lament critical consciousness is sharpened, self-feeling is raised – this the philosophers of nature received from the lyric poets. “No victor in the chariot race with all his prizes is more worthy of the prize than I (ὥσπερ ἐγώ [as I]). For higher than the strength of men and horses stands our wisdom.” So sings Xenophanes (fr. 2). And who is the man, “whose fame penetrates all Hellas and will never fade, so long as Hellenes sing songs” (fr. 6)? And often this or that philosopher of nature assures us that the doctrines which he proclaims are eternal and bear fruit, unknown and uncomprehended by mortals and at the same time unsurpassed, that the truth about everything is revealed to him, that against it all treasures of men and even the Persian [p. 42] throne are worthless (Heraclitus fr. 1, Parmenides 1. 8, 60f. 10, Empedocles 4. 5. 17, 25f. 110. 114, Democritus 35. 118. 299). It is a self-feeling so hostile to authority, so sovereign and superhuman, as probably recurs only in the Renaissance. Listen only to Paracelsus: “I tell you, the hair on my neck knows more than you and all your scribes, and my shoe-laces are more learned than your Galen and Avicenna, and my beard has experienced more than all your high schools. Follow me, you, follow me, Avicenna, Galen etc., follow me and I not you, you of Paris, you of Montpellier, you of Swabia etc., you islands in the sea, you Italy, you Athens etc., follow me and I not you, none of you will remain in the remotest corner, where the dogs smell, I shall be Monarch and mine shall be the Monarchy, and I shall lead the Monarchy and gird your loins.” Paracelsus feels himself a magician, as the lands are indeed full of his miracle cures, and people prayed for healing even at his grave. Through him one may understand Empedocles (fr. 112): “I (ἐγώ), however, walk now before you as an immortal god, no longer as a mortal, honoured by all, as is fitting, my head wound with fillets and blooming garlands. As soon as I enter the flourishing cities, I am revered, and countless follow me, seeking the path to salvation. Some ask for prophecies, others inquire about manifold diseases etc.” That wonder-worker whom Empedocles (fr. 111) has addressed in self-promise, to whom alone it is given to know all poisons and to ward off illness and age, to banish and to call forth winds, rain, drought and to raise the dead, that is wholly the magician of whom the philosophers of the Renaissance, above all Agrippa, dream, who, in possession of higher forces of nature, works miracles, foretells fates from the stars, heals all infirmities and precisely also raises the dead. So tremendously does Empedocles’ self-feeling swell that he (fr. 113) does not want to make much ado about his deeds, as if they were great deeds for him: “for I stand high above the sorrow-laden mortals.” He knows himself to be of higher rank and [p. 43] feels degraded by commerce with earthly beings, as he confesses in fr. 119.

One must beware of taking this lyrical swelling of self-feeling, up to the fantastic, as a mere poetic addition that can easily be cut away. I maintain that the old philosophy of nature was impossible without that self-feeling, as lyric poetry first brought it heightened to expression, indeed impossible without its growth into the monstrous. For this whole philosophy of nature is a mighty paradox, and more, a chain of mighty paradoxes, each of which demands prophetic courage, an unceasing contradiction of all appearance and of all prevailing opinion. This whole philosophy of nature rests on the denial of all coming-to-be and passing-away and thereby places itself everywhere in pronounced opposition to the “opinion of the Hellenes,” to the “delusion of mortals.” What a gigantically strong subjective consciousness, what a rock-high trust in one’s own spiritual need against objective experience must have dwelt even in the student of nature Democritus, when he has the audacity to build his doctrine on the thesis that the Nothing exists no less than the Something! The flux and contradiction of all things Heraclitus could find comparable only from a sovereign strength of his own feeling of identity. Conversely, in the old Ionian and Eleatic demand for the unity of primal matter or respectively of being, the unity of self-consciousness is reflected. One should not fail to recognize the heroic obstinacy with which Parmenides, Zeno and Melissus, in constant consciousness of the monstrous paradox, deny all multiplicity and all becoming, i.e., indeed make themselves blind to the visible world. Parmenides says it outright that “thinking and being are the same” (fr. 5), that only a being is thinkable (fr. 6). He feels being, then, at the same time as the content of his thinking consciousness, and indeed, the world contracts for the Eleatic into unity precisely in his consciousness. Timon (Sext. Pyrrh. I 224) already has Xenophanes say: “Wherever I let my spirit roam, everything dissolved into one closed unity [p. 44] .” But expressly Parmenides confesses it: “See how what is absent is so surely present to the spirit (to consciousness); for you cannot cut off what is from the connection of what is” (fr. 2). “A connection offers itself to me, from wherever I begin; for thither I shall also return” (that is, in the circle of consciousness) (fr. 3). From the strength of their consciousness all these philosophers of nature are sharp critics of the senses (cf. Heraclitus fr. 107, Parmenides 1. 35, Empedocles 2. 4, Anaxagoras 7. 21, Democritus 7-11. 125), and above all Heraclitus practises and demands self-knowledge (fr. 101. 116).

The conventional, external conception of those philosophers of nature as researchers of nature so self-forgetfully directed at the object gives quite a false picture. There is in them a very conscious wrestling between subject and object, a burning desire to make intuition in thinking do justice to the spirit, indeed, at bottom they want and give nothing but a critique of intuition from the need for consistency, i.e., from the unity of consciousness. And they feel knowledge not as passive experience but as an act of their self, as a striding of their spirit. Xenophanes shows “the way” (fr. 7), Parmenides does not cease to describe the “ways” of inquiry (fr. 4. 6. 7. 8), and Empedocles too speaks much of “way,” “summit” and “goals” of the doctrine, of the “path” of the songs, of the “course” of the proof, etc. (fr. 17. 24. 35. 62). Consider that these expressions, familiar to us to the point of emptiness, once had living colour. The doctrine of Parmenides is expressly, in its whole disposition, laid out precisely upon the image of the two ways. He begins (fr. 1) by describing his journey to the light, how the mares bore him, as far as his heart desired, onto the renowned way that leads the knowing man to everything. He does not forget the grinding of the axle of the wagon, nor the veil of the guiding Heliad maidens; he spares himself not the smallest detail in the description of the gate which he must pass through to the gracious reception at the [p. 45] house of truth. What is the point of this broadly executed image? At the very least it shows that knowledge means to a Parmenides not a merely intellectual process, but at the same time a mighty experience of feeling, that he feels his whole self drawn along and exalted in it, and since the experiences of the soul can only be expressed in images, he paints with a thick brush the heavy journey to the light and the gate of truth that is hard to unbolt.

To give shape in fantasy-images to the experiences of one’s own soul – that is the truest lyric poetry, and when those experiences become cosmic, the truest mysticism. Empedocles here outdoes even the Eleatic, for he paints the destinies of his divinely felt I as transmigrations of the soul: “I wept and wailed when I beheld the unaccustomed place” (fr. 118). “From what rank of honour and what height of bliss have I sunk down into the company of mortals?” One must ask how this seeming visionary could become, of all things, the founder of the doctrine of the elements. Zeller finds no bridge between the philosophy of nature and the doctrine of the soul in Empedocles. The doctrine of transmigration springs precisely from a tremendously swollen self-feeling, which bursts the barriers of the bodily individual and lets the subject extend its existence beyond the objectively given limits. But just because it feels itself so intensely as subject, as person, it sets off the objects as objects all the more sharply, as changing materials, shells, shapes of the subject. The more the individual feels itself as force, the more consciously the substances become substances for it. The most intense, most unified, firmest feeling of one’s own self grasps precisely the extensity, the multiplicity and the change of the elements. Nature transforms everything, Empedocles teaches in fr. 126, by clothing the souls in a strange garment of body. The sinful souls must enter earthly life. “The might of the air chases them to the sea, the sea spits them out onto the ground of the earth, the earth brings them to the rays of the shining sun, which hurls them again into the whirlings of the air. – One receives them from another, but all abhor them. To these (souls) I too now belong” (fr. 115). “I was [p. 46] once already a youth and a maiden, a bush, a bird and a fish risen from the sea” (fr. 117), that is, he has already lived according to the difference of the sexes in greater or lesser warmth (Aristot. part. anim. II 2. 648a 25), already as a plant of the earth, as a dweller of the air and of the water, hence in the play of all the elements. From the mighty, abiding consciousness of the I he grasps the interplay of the elements. Thus in Empedocles the doctrine of transmigration, so apparently alien, belongs with deep inward necessity to the doctrine of the elements.

Pythagoras too, after Heraclides Ponticus (Diog. VIII 4), is said to have spoken already of the wanderings of his own soul, and the doctrine of transmigration is, as is well known, the earliest and most widely attested doctrine, the only certain one that can be ascribed to him himself. A primal, elemental faith in themselves filled the prophets Pythagoras and Empedocles; they feel themselves infinite, they simply cannot believe in their short, narrow, once-only existence; their self-consciousness testifies against death. The superhuman feeling of life and of self compels the dogma of rebirth; we can understand that today from the example of Nietzsche, if we understand it! Individualism in particular, as Lessing and Lichtenberg show, inclines to these dogmas, which even the godless Hume knows how to defend.

The heightened, living self-feeling drives the Renaissance too to the question of personal immortality. It is this that at that time kindled the great quarrel of the Averroists and the Alexandrists and finally led beyond Aristotle, who fails here. The reflection of the age pressed mightily, indeed, for an emphasis on inwardness, on personality, which triumphed in the Reformation; it pressed for the exaltation of the soul and for the problem of life and death. The heightened, sacrifice-ready concern for eternal blessedness, the painted dances of death, the declamations of Petrarch, Poggio and many others against the fear of death and their laments over the fleetingness of life, bear witness to it. And here we again lay hold of a kinship with that ancient Renaissance in which the philosophy of nature arose, and again it is lyric [p. 47] poetry that led the way in such a valuation of life and of the soul. But the elevation of soul and life beyond transience, the swelling of self-feeling up to immortality, is more than lyrical, it is mystical. The mystical feeling is the lyrical feeling raised to the power of the eternal, the infinite, the Absolute. That overpowering self-feeling which breaks through all earthly barriers comes, after all, from mysticism. It makes Eckhart say: Were I not, God would not be; and Angelus Silesius: I myself am eternity.

### Wertung von Seele und Leben — Valuation of Soul and Life

There is here something more than the lyrical-mystical style of the I, which continues in the philosophers of nature. It is something different, to speak of oneself and to speak of the soul and of the value of life. Many apparent contradictions that have been found in the philosophers of nature, Empedocles above all, would resolve themselves if one had not simply identified I and soul. The soul is a form of existence of the I, and so the same I can without contradiction migrate into manifold souls, animal ones too, and continue to exist, even if the individual psychic constitution is bound to the bodily combination of matter. The I can set over against itself its soul and all the forms, forces and parts of its soul. What first echoes in the Homeric τέτλαθι δὴ κραδίη [endure, my heart], the address to one’s own heart, has been since Archilochus a favourite turn of the old Greek lyric poetry, which has a great deal to tell of the life of the soul and already begins, if never to set its various forms and forces systematically over against one another, yet to bring them out one by one<sup>7</sup>. Greek lyric poetry first let the inner life step forth with power. Inwardness lies in the essence of lyric poetry, but its stepping forth was of particular significance among this people, so strongly directed to the plastically visible.

<sup>7</sup> See the details in W. Schrader, die Seelenlehre der Griechen in der älteren Lyrik, Philos. Abh. für Haym p. 1ff.

In the philosophers of nature this inward tendency is not lacking but rather heightened. Their wisdom, which they esteem so much [p. 48] more highly than bodily strength (Xenophanes fr. 2), should be, Empedocles demands, “stored within in the hollow of the breast” (fr. 3) and deeply impressed, that it is “ever present”, but not only for the intellect; for the teachings “grow of themselves into the character, where the core of each one’s being rests”, and whatever else counts among men sinks away beside them (fr. 110). Still deeper does Heraclitus look into the struggling, growing and shining of the soul, and he values it as highly as scarcely any of the later thinkers. “To the soul belongs the word that nourishes itself” (Heraclitus fr. 115). “Eyes and ears are liars for men who have barbarian souls” (107). He called the remedies those that act upon the soul (68). “It is hard to fight with the heart. For whatever it wishes, it buys at the price of its soul” (85). Ἦθος ἀνθρώπῳ δαίμων [character is for man his daimon] (119, a saying that is diminished by the best translation). “The limits of the soul you could not discover, even if you travelled every road; so deep a ground has it” (45). The soul infinite and unfathomable, a mystery – so Jacob Boehme must have felt when he looked into himself. Corpses, one should throw away rather than dung – Heraclitus thunders on (fr. 96) in fanatical cult of the soul. That the soul is the essence, the body only the husk, and the goods of the soul to be esteemed far higher than those of the body, Democritus too preaches often enough in his ethical fragments, which, incidentally, even if half of them should be spurious, would still be far more numerous than his writings on the philosophy of nature. By his writings he is just as much a philologist and an aesthetician.

Altogether it is indeed impossible – to run through, before him, the series of the great philosophers of nature: the practical man Thales, the leader of colonists Anaximander, the ethical-religious reformer Pythagoras and the so strongly political order of the Pythagoreans with their wordy ethics, the people-hating prophet Heraclitus, the rhapsode Xenophanes, the lawgiver Parmenides and the tyrant-hater Zeno, both of whom in their direction of life are praised as ἄνδρες Πυθαγόρειοι [Pythagorean men] (Strabo VI 1, 1), the admiral Melissus, the great leader of the democrats and [p. 49] prophetic magician Empedocles, in whom Aristotle sees the creator of rhetoric – it is impossible to take all these as pure students of nature, and even Anaxagoras, the only exception, will not have talked with his friend Pericles merely about the stars. Those men stand in life, they live in an epoch of transition rich in destiny, a stormy epoch, political and social, when in the Orient mighty empires break like children’s toys, others rise up and drag the very home cities of philosophy into danger and servitude, when these cities moreover resound with wild party strife, when everywhere in Hellas the middle class presses up over the aristocracy, and everywhere tyrants’ thrones are raised and overthrown. And these great philosophers of nature heighten still further the transformation of life, as leaders, wanderers, emigrants; they further life, they enjoy it, and they suffer from it. They value life, they judge it and seek a new one. Antiquity speaks of the Πυθαγόρειος καὶ Παρμενίδειος βίος [the Pythagorean and Parmenidean way of life] (Cebes tab. 2), but also the “weeping” Heraclitus, and Democritus, who did not indeed always laugh but yet wrote “on cheerfulness”, and the vegetarian apostle of love Empedocles represent a mood of life, champion an ideal of life. It was the time when the Greek felt life, when the Greek soul was born, for which life becomes too narrow, which seeks a better, wider life and an eternal one. The improvers of life appear, moralists, lawgivers and reformers, and to them belong above all the Italian “philosophers of nature”. But Heraclitus and Democritus preach no less than Pythagoras, Xenophanes and Empedocles.

The word, which Heraclitus ranks so high, is their weapon, which they brandish over life. In lyric poetry, the forerunner of rhetoric too, the word first seizes the whole man through feeling. Once the laws were composed in rhythm and delivered in a sing-song tone, and in turn the Greek called the first hymns “laws” (νόμους); the connection that Aristotle notes, probl. 19, 28, lies probably deeper than Bergk, Litgsch. II 163, is willing to admit. Then [p. 50] when in Solon the lyric poet becomes lawgiver, when Tyrtaeus’ lyre won battles, when the verse-wisdom of proverbs, rising everywhere, became the educator of the people, then the word sought and found in the awakened Greek soul a mightier resonance. It is a significant fact: the flowering of lyric poetry and, in its train, the awakening of the philosophy of nature come with and soon after the wider diffusion of writing in the seventh century. Miletus, the birthplace of the philosophy of nature, gives the alphabet that became generally Greek, and gives us the oldest inscriptions just around 600, when Thales began to be active. Again the philosophy of the Renaissance offers a parallel, for it follows the invention of printing. One will no longer say post hoc ergo propter hoc here with Du Bois-Reymond. Only the soul that has something to say seeks and uses new ways of communication. To be sure, the eased, broader way of communication then in turn entices, especially in rivalry, to say more and more. Writing at first has an individualizing effect on the spirit and then a sobering one. By relieving the memory, it draws out one’s own judgment. Poetry, stripped by writing of its mnemonic advantage, becomes art, and communication becomes prose, impersonal, abstract; the realm of the intellectual, the objective, becomes independent through writing. But the transition to prosaic science is made by individualized poetry, lyric poetry, which first enjoys the advantage of writing, freed from memory, from tradition, the epic too, from authority, and first lets one’s own judgment be spoken. Each paints there his picture of world and life, and the philosophers are here the continuers of the lyric poets.

The soul rises in judgment above life, not because it was then so poor, but because it was so rich that the awakened drive of life swelled out beyond life. Elegy does not cease to lament longingly the transience and frailty of life, and thereby it touches half unconsciously the questions of eternal life. But the melic lyric too, with its songs of praise and of victory, [p. 51] which raise the hero to heaven, strives beyond this life. Just as in the Renaissance, the passionate agonistic individualism of that time drove toward a panegyrical cult of heroes. Never, probably, did fame stand higher in price than in the times of Pindar and of Petrarch, who admired “the tremendous men” of the past and was himself so admired that “the Apennine mountains glowed with longing to be touched by his holy feet”. How, too, does Empedocles, e.g. fr. 129, portray Pythagoras as a superman, who beholds everything in the world over ten and twenty generations of men. The ardent drive for personality demands for its heroes a survival after death, and the immortality of fame touches on the immortality of the soul: this is best shown by Pindar, who is full of thoughts of the beyond. The great lyric poet has been placed in the circle of the Pythagoreans, and among these at any rate the doctrines of transmigration are connected, as with the prophetic self-feeling of the master, so with the heroic veneration which he enjoyed among his pupils. Of Parmenides too it is handed down (Diog. IX 21) that he erected a heroon to his deceased Pythagorean teacher, which was nothing rare at that time in southern Italy (cf. Diels, Hermes 35 p. 198). “Fallen heroes are honoured by gods and men”, says Heraclitus fr. 24, and with inimitably solemn harmony he proclaims fr. 25: μόροι γὰρ μέζονες μέζονας μοίρας λαγχάνουσι [for greater deaths win greater destinies]. And Heraclitus lets the dead man become alive, “kindle his light in the night” (fr. 26), and calls out to men that “after death there awaits” them “what they neither hope nor imagine” (27, cf. fr. 98). Empedocles, however, says in fr. 15, in Diels’ beautiful translation: “No wise man will let such things dream in his mind, that as long as we live, what men call living, only so long are we there and misfortune and good befall us; whereas before we mortals were put together from the elements and after we have parted asunder, we are nothing at all.” Even the materialist Democritus composes a work “on the things in Hades”. And one wanted to understand Greek [p. 52] philosophy of nature without the mystical background and did not see that the physical is only a view of the foreground!

Unconsciously, the Greek philosophers of nature were perhaps interested in only one problem, the same that drives the philosophy of the Renaissance too: the problem of life. Astonishingly numerous are the fragments that deal with the riddles of birth and death. It is again as if the lyric poetry of wedding songs and funeral laments were continuing itself reflectively in the philosophers of nature. They dig in the riddles of the limits of life, first lit up and marvelled at by lyric feeling; they seek an answer to the questions of transience that tormented the lyric poets. They find it by clearing birth and death out of the world. They deny all arising and passing away: that is the basic dogma of their whole explanation of the world. By removing the limits of life they fulfil the tremendous drive of life that is their essence, as it is that of the philosophers of the Renaissance and of all true mystics. It is the same drive for the perpetuation of existence that makes the Eleatics and all the younger philosophers of nature deny becoming and perishing, and thereby creates the foundation of their physics; that, further, makes Heraclitus proclaim that eternal becoming in which life passes into death only because death is at the same time, in another respect, life (fr. 26. 36. 62. 77); the same living drive for eternity that finally brings forth the doctrines of transmigration of the Pythagoreans and of Empedocles. Let us at last break with the habit, contradicting all testimonies, as unjustified as it is convenient, of treating these chief doctrines as trifles, as foreign addition and concession and superstitious appendage. Our historians of philosophy carry their rationalism into antiquity, and nothing is more intolerant than rationalism. The question of the soul, and that means the question of life, is the chief question for the old philosophers of nature, and yet it does not properly concern the physicist at all. But there is hardly a single one of these so-called φυσικοί [physicists] who, even in the meagre fragments from which we know him, failed [p. 53] to speak of the soul – that should long since have been noticed for the overall judgment. Even the old Ionians make no exception; Thales already says that the magnet too has a soul (Aristot. de an. I 2. 405a 19), and of the three short fragments of Anaximenes, one speaks of our soul, another of the incorporeal. The Pythagorean doctrine of the soul indeed reaches beyond metempsychosis as well (palingenesis, soul as harmony, soul as sun-mote, ethics of the soul). And all the others, Heraclitus as well as Parmenides, Empedocles as well as Anaxagoras, who, according to Aristotle, did not distinguish the soul from the νοῦς [mind] so essential to him (Arist. de an. I 2. 404a 25b 1. 405a 13, cf. Anaxagoras fr. 12), Diogenes of Apollonia as already Anaximenes, and finally Democritus – they all explain the soul and its powers in connection with their physics, for which the particular data are too well known to be enumerated here (cf. Siebeck, Gesch. der Psychol. I 1, and Rohde’s Psyche). This psychological element is usually represented by modern scholars as the concluding point of those systems of the philosophy of nature; but it is just to be asked whether, appearing everywhere so indispensably, it is not equally well their starting point, whether, that is, the soul is only explained from the physical world and not the world from the soul, from life, from man in general.

### Anthropomorphismus — Anthropomorphism

Therewith we touch the third, most important point in the mystical and, as will be shown, also in the lyrical disposition of the philosophy of nature: the unity of the soul with the world, the elevation of man to a symbol of the world, to the key of the world. Such a conception of the philosophers of nature seems an impossible paradox, an inversion of the facts, as indeed it signifies an inversion of the prevailing conception hitherto. Let us take the old Ionians. Thales explains the world from water, Anaximander from the infinite primal matter, Anaximenes from air, Heraclitus from fire – how in all the world can one, in view of [p. 54] these purely physical first principles, assert that they interpret the world according to the soul? Do not rather Anaximenes, Heraclitus, Diogenes and others declare precisely the soul to be air or fire respectively, hence according to its physical principle? And yet appearance deceives and has deceived long enough. One need only ask why they chose precisely these principles of the world.

Anaximenes gives the clearest answer for himself: “As our soul, being breath, holds us together, so breath and air encompass the whole universe” (fr. 2). Thereby he avows the anthropomorphic origin of his world-principle. The world is explained by analogy with our life, whose criterion is breath. The world is for Anaximenes a living being, which exists as such by breathing. He seeks as ground of the world the ground of life and finds it in breath, without which there is no life. As the soul, so the world, he says – we have here, then, already in him the doctrine of Renaissance mysticism of the unity of world and soul in life, of the ensoulment of the world. With his principle he seeks in truth the soul of the world; he finds it by fitting the human soul as far as possible to the world and the world as far as possible to the soul. He materializes the soul and he dematerializes the world as far as possible. The soul in its most material mark, as the breath of life, and the world in its most immaterial stuff, as air, meet. Therefore he chooses air as world-principle – he says expressly in fr. 3: “Air stands close to the incorporeal”, and I see no right to declare this citation spurious because it agrees less with the hitherto one-sided conception of Anaximenes. He can just as well call the thin, intangible air ἐγγὺς τοῦ ἀσωμάτου [close to the incorporeal], as Heraclitus calls his fire-principle ἀσωματώτατον [most incorporeal] (Arist. de an. I 2. 425a 25). He also declares, Hippolyt. Refut. I 7, air as such to be invisible and perceptible only in the sensations of cold and warmth, moisture and motion, and from here, that is, from human sensation, he establishes subjectively as properties and developments of the air its mobility, its becoming warm, its becoming cold and moist, and the two purely sensible fundamental processes of the air, [p. 55] becoming cold and becoming warm, he equates with condensation and rarefaction of the air, hence with stronger or weaker materialization. And even for this he appeals to an experience of man which he transfers to the world: the air expelled with pressed lips is cold, that breathed out with open mouth is warm.

He emphasizes the unceasing motion and changeability of the air – no doubt that this too, as Simplicius already says, determined him in the choice of the principle. But precisely in this emphasized mobility the air showed itself as symbol of vitality, showed its capacity to be a principle of life, its analogy and communion with the ever-mobile soul. Our breath of life comes from the breath of the world. “Because we arise through its outflow (so again man as ground of knowledge of the world!), it must be infinite and rich, since it never runs out” (fr. 3). Whoever sees the at once personal-symbolic conception of the world-breath will not, with Zeller, take offence at the predicate “rich”. Anaximenes in fr. 2 calls his world-principle also πνεῦμα [breath, spirit] – not in the later purely spiritual sense, but in the sense of spirit of life. His conception of the world is just as little purely “pneumatic” as it is purely physical. It is both at once, monistic (hylozoistic says too little); in finding the unity of soul and world in the breath of life it is vitalistic – just like the nature-mysticism of the Renaissance.

But Anaximenes does not decide the origin of the philosophy of nature, and if his principle of air, as the breath of life, establishes a bond between soul and world, does not the same hold, then, for the principle of the founder of the philosophy of nature? A soul of water seems an absurdity, and so Thales seems to have chosen his principle purely physically. If only there were not a small report, which Aristotle already gives (de an. I 2. 405a 19): Thales is said to have ascribed a soul to the stone, because it moves iron. Whoever finds even the stone ensouled (as Paracelsus likewise declares) finds everything, finds the world ensouled, and indeed other testimonies too say that, appealing to the [p. 56] magnet, he ascribed souls also to unensouled things, and naturally to plants as well. Thus in him the philosophy of nature begins with the mystical doctrine of universal ensoulment – and strange! he seizes upon precisely the magnet, which for the nature-mysticism of even Schelling and Baader is quite the type of the explanation of the world, and for the mysticism of the Renaissance too, above all for Agrippa of Nettesheim, a favourite example, as also at that time Gilbert explained the earth and Kepler the sun as magnets. There is, incidentally, something of the magician in what is told of Thales, in his deeper knowledge of nature, his technical marvels and his predictions (not merely of the eclipse of the sun, but also of a good wine year).

But how does this mystical element, everything is ensouled, agree with the chief doctrine: everything is water? It must surely be possible: even the chemical mysticism of Helmont, the pupil of Paracelsus, combines panpsychism with the doctrine of water as the primal element of things. Hippon already, the ancient renewer of the water-principle, expressly explained the soul too from it, and the ground that Aristotle merely conjectures for Thales will in any case have been stated by him (cf. Zeller 255, 5): that the seed and nourishment of all living beings is moist. Of all living beings, just as Schleiden too (to whom Ueberweg-Heinze rightly appeal) explains the proposition of Thales thus: “The sea is the mother and cradle of all that lives.” If this interpretation is correct for Thales (and Anaximander, who stands nearest to him, teaches expressly the origin of all organisms from the sea, and the omne vivum ex aqua makes the water-principle still today the most plausible), then the world is explained by Thales after the living being, and the origin of life is for him the origin of the world.

At this vitalism too of Thales I shall not doubt; but perhaps his water-principle has besides a more general meaning, a more inward ground. Yes, there must be such a ground, which unites Thales with his successors, which drives the Ionian basic view that comes in the highest sharpness to [p. 57] expression in Heraclitus. It is said that the Ionians seek a material principle – that unites them. But that explains nothing yet and itself wants first to be explained. The consideration of the chosen material principles leads further: water, air, fire – nothing but mobilities; why did the Ionians not also reach for solid substances, for resting poles as principles? Because they seek no principle of materiality at all, but a principle of mobility, no purely material one but at the same time a dynamic principle, a substance that makes visible the most intense motion, the greatest capacity for transformation. Yes, makes visible, as the plastic sense of the Greek demands. He seeks the substance essentially as material for intuition, he seeks the picture of the world, he seeks it in a substance which he finds typical of his impression of the world, which expresses most clearly his world-mood, his contact with the world. And the spiritually mobile Ionian saw the world infinitely mobile. Wanderer, founder of colonies, mercenary, seafarer, trader, he was outwardly already in constant motion and change of life. Never and nowhere, perhaps, has an average man experienced more transformations than an Ionian of the sixth century, who lived through Nebuchadnezzar and Psammetichus, Croesus and Cyrus and felt the effects of their deeds and destinies, who saw great empires and small tyrants come and go, who had the Lydian and the Persian at his neck and whose native soil was hot with unceasing coups d’état, party revolutions and neighbourly feuds. And the Milesian above all, who in his central harbour and market had before his eyes most of all the contact of Orient and Occident, the fluctuation of human relations and values, the mobility of all possessions and the transformation of material products, whose life the destruction of Nineveh shook as it did that of Sybaris. Everything changes, life and history preach to the Ionian. Nature remains the same and does not preach. But because he found the drive to motion in his soul, the universal transformation in his life, therefore he found them again and noticed them in nature (as precisely the eternally restless Bruno, driven by passion, [p. 58] finds nature comprehended in constant flowing, so that no body today is what it was yesterday). The highly heightened drive of life of his soul, the fullness of transformation in his life, sought and found wider expression, universal resonance in nature.

Universal transformation it was that made nature interesting to the Ionian, that he first saw in it, indeed more: he saw actually not nature but universal transformation. It is, after all, a fundamentally important fact that nature, φύσις – linguistically considered according to the origin of the concept – means not the totality of existing things, the world of being, bodily reality, but generation, living coming-into-being. Nature thus originally signifies not what it signifies for us (say, in contrast to spirit), universal material being, but a universal process, and not a purely material, physical, chemical one, but the biogenetic, living becoming. So the starting point from life lies in the origin of the concept of nature; the first knowledge of nature is knowledge of life. The process of life, heightened in the Greek Renaissance, presses toward extension beyond man, presses toward universal expression, and it discovers nature as the most universal, co-vibrating circle of the process of life that it feels in its own breast. To the over-living one everything lives, and nature lights up for him first as the greatest mirroring of his feeling of life.

Everything lives – conceived subjectively, that means: everything has soul; objectively, according to visible appearance, that means: everything is in motion, is unceasing becoming, infinite alternation of arising and passing away. Both are the basic views of the Ionian philosophy of nature: they teach universal ensoulment, and they teach the infinite process of becoming. Heraclitus brings it to its most conscious expression in his πάντα ῥεῖ [all things flow]. And now let one understand from Heraclitus his forerunner Thales. Everything surges, everything a wave-motion of arising and passing away, everything in the stream of becoming, so that one cannot step twice into the same river, says [p. 59] Heraclitus – is not that the most naive expression for this πάντα ῥεῖ: everything is water? One may say, then the principle of Thales would be merely symbolic. I say, it is also symbolic (as even more the flowing in Heraclitus). The representations of the first philosophers of nature are still, in unconscious unity, real and symbolic. They seize upon a reality at once as picture and as type of the world. The stream of life that Thales felt in himself he found again in the infinite play of waves of the sea, out of which the native shore rose. And finds of sea shells in the mountains showed, probably<sup>8</sup>, that the life of the sea once reached further, and the magnet showed that even the stones are not dead, that even the firmest is permeated by living force. Everything lives – only in this do the two theses of Thales unite: everything is ensouled and everything is fluid. A quickening of the world lies in his liquefaction of the world.

<sup>8</sup> Cf. Thales in Ueberweg-Heinze’s Grundriß. In the Renaissance too, e.g., Lionardo shows a strong interest in fossil shells and in the conclusions that follow from their explanation.

The mood of the sea, the image of the change of all things, is tragic. It is the mood of all the lyric poets who lament the transience of life. Greek philosophy of nature and Greek elegy are born of the same mood, have the same basic theme: the change of life. Not only Schopenhauer understood Heraclitus’ doctrine of the flux of all things as a lament; the ancients too thought: Heraclitus appears as accuser of birth, since he says: Once born, they strive to live and to fall prey to death, or rather to rest, and they leave children behind, that they too may fall prey to death (fr. 20). But already in Anaximander the Ionian philosophy avows that everything that arises is worth being destroyed, that life is a guilt, and that the cradle of beings is also their coffin: “Whence things arise, thither must they also perish, according to necessity; for they must pay one another penalty and retribution for the injustice according to the ordering of time.” But the philosophers of nature are indeed the greater continuers of the elegists. They lament with them the transient lot of individual life, yet they console with the all-life, in which one individual dies that another may arise. Mark well, they do not console with [p. 60] the All of the world, they console with the universal process of life, which is greater than the world, which in it is itself only something individual and transient. They all teach, Anaximander and Anaximenes, Heraclitus and Diogenes, an infinite alternation of the arising and passing away of worlds. This shows again: not nature, not the existing bodily world is their basic interest, but the infinite process of life, for which our existing world is only a point of passage. When Anaximander makes the infinite the principle of the world, we are told expressly as the reason for it, not that the world seems to him unlimited, but that he finds in the infinite alone the cause of all arising and passing away, because only the infinite is not exhausted in the unceasing birth. The ἄπειρον [the boundless] of Anaximander is thus properly the inexhaustible, the inexhaustible source of the process of life, whence he also calls it “immortal” and “imperishable”. Whence had he the concept of the infinite of the process of life? Neither the concept of the infinite nor that of life can be gained by purely external experience, only one’s own overflowing, infinite feeling of life leads to it.

From the infinite Anaximander lets the world arise through the separation of the warm and the cold – thereby he posits as basic substances of the world contrasts of sensation, and so this first specialization of nature in this first systematist is subjectively gained. I leave aside that his doctrine of the suspended state of the earth in equilibrium is probably to be understood at that time only through its conception as a living being, with which Aristotle’s explanatory comparison (de coelo II 13. 295b) agrees, and recall only the ethical humanization of the process of the world which Anaximander’s cited chief fragment pronounces as his basic view: all things must pay one another “penalty” and “retribution” for their “injustice”. Does a pure φυσικός [physicist] speak so? And “order” and “necessity” there are in this process of the world – whoever is not wholly blinded by the mechanistic spirit of the age sees that such concepts, however much they may today have faded into the bloodlessly abstract [p. 61] exist originally only for willing and feeling beings, and that the knowledge of nature therefore begins by transferring what is human to the world. More correctly: man begins to experience nature along with himself and so to understand it out of himself. The basic concepts of natural science are, after all, faded anthropomorphisms; they must be, because all knowing is only an understanding of the unknown from the known.

This humanization of the world in the early Ionian philosophy of nature is completed in Heraclitus. One might claim that none of his concepts of nature is without a human, psychic meaning or secondary meaning. No one will believe that his philosophy began with a physical or chemical induction that led him to fire as the world-principle. Zeller already stressed clearly that the doctrine of fire cannot have been the primary one, but only that of the flux of things, of the restless alternation of appearances, of the absolute aliveness of nature, to illustrate which fire then presented itself to him as the world-principle. Aristotle and Simplicius expressly confirm this ground of his principle. He chose fire, as they say, on account of its incorporeality and eternal mobility, on account of its life-creating (ζωογόνον) and formative power, just as he himself introduces it as “ever-living” (ἀείζωον) (fr. 30). Like the other Ionians, then, he is so little in search of a primal matter, as people have always claimed of them, but rather of life-force, for which he chooses a primal matter merely as image and type.

But has he not, after all, explained the psychic only physically, not the reverse, and does the soul not seem to be appended merely at the end of his physics, through an artificial transference of bodily predicates, when he celebrates the dry soul as the best and, at the same time pointing to the drunkard, designates it as the death of the soul to become water? In truth the soul stands already at the beginning of his physics. He chose fire as the world-principle precisely because it is the most living, the most incorporeal (Arist. de an. I 2. 405a 25. Themist. de an. II 24 Sp.), and therefore the most soul-like. And he did not [p. 62] merely, at the last, explain the soul too as fire, but primarily explained fire too as soul, so that for him the soul is one with the world-principle (cf. Themist. op. cit. Aet. IV 3, 12. 7, 2); or else, as Aristotle says (op. cit.), he explained the world-principle outright as soul, with the ἀναθυμίασις [exhalation] serving him as the middle term for the equation πῦρ = ψυχή [fire = soul], or perhaps also the ethereal (cf. Zeller p. 648), in which conception soul and fire meet. Quite directly and expressly he draws the soul, instead of fire, into the world-process: “For souls it is death to become water, for water it is death to become earth; from earth water comes to be again, from water soul” (fr. 36). That is indeed the Heraclitean cycle of the coming-to-be of the world, and so “the way down” is for him in fact a materialization of the world, “the way up” a dematerialization into soul. It is truly a process not of the world, but between soul and world; soul becomes world, world becomes soul. It is as much a moral process as a physical one, and Heraclitus describes the ever-recurring dissolution of the world into fire (similarly to Anaximander before him) as a judgment of the world: “Fire, in its coming, will judge and seize upon all things” (fr. 66). And the moral is more important to him than the physical: one should quench wantonness sooner than a conflagration (fr. 43). On the whole, if only for the sake of the Stoa, one would do well to take Heraclitus strongly in a moral sense: the most ethical school of antiquity must surely have known why it reached back precisely to Heraclitean physics.

One understands Heraclitus’ soul of fire materialistically, but one should explain it, like everything in him, symbolically at the same time. Think of the light of life as an image of the soul (cf. fr. 26), of the “little spark” in us of which German mysticism speaks, of the divine flash of fire which in Boehme, psychically and physically at once, transforms the realm of wrath and darkness into the realm of love and brightness. And Heraclitus has the flash of fire “steer the world,” and the soul strike through the covering of the body “like lightning through the clouds.” Boehme speaks of fire in general in Heraclitean turns of phrase and says outright: “All things [p. 63] have their origin in fire.” If one still wants to find Heraclitism in the Renaissance, one may listen, for example, to Leonardo: “The flame first prepares the matter which it is to feed upon, and then feeds upon it” – the Heraclitean cycle! Or: “Where the flame does not live, no creature that breathes lives.” “Where there is life, there is warmth.” “Fire destroys falsehood, etc.; fire is the symbol of truth, etc.”<sup>9</sup>

<sup>9</sup> See Leonardo da Vinci, transl. by Marie Herzfeld, Eugen Diederichs, Leipzig 1925, pp. 27, 31, 130.

Above all, though, does one not grasp that the most passionate of the ancient thinkers had to speak of the soul of fire? He bore blazing fire within himself and felt it as his soul, and his whole life was a struggle against the souls of the multitude, which dilute everything and sink into dullness. Nietzsche too, the thinker of passion, compares his own being to fire. The more primitive thinker cannot separate image and reality entirely here. He feels his soul grow great in passion, and he experiences this bodily as a rising warm current, as a heating of the blood; warmth and heat he knows as the effect of fire (which must therefore be at work in the soul), and so for him the most fiery soul is the best, and the essence of the soul is fire, because it is passion. The essence of the soul – and the essence of the world, which for him are one.

He quite obviously interprets the world according to the soul, not only in his conception of its unity, in that he makes fire, as the most living, most incorporeal, most soul-like thing, as vivid passion, into the principle, but also in the incipient specialization and explanation of the coming-to-be of the world. Whence the cycle of becoming, the journey “downward” and “upward”? Why do the souls materialize and dematerialize again? Because they “need alternation and grow weary of persisting in the same condition” (Stob. ecl. I 896. 906). He therefore explains the world-process psychologically, from the life-drive of his soul, which demands constant alternation. Let no one think that he speaks only of individual souls. Fr. 65 says expressly: “He says that fire is the cause of the whole ordering of the world; but he calls it want and satiety. Want is, according to him, [p. 64] the formation of the world, the conflagration of the world satiety.” Between want and satiety the passionate soul swings unceasingly. The law of this soul, the law of passion, is therefore for Heraclitus the law of the world, the ground of all becoming.

But the Heraclitean world consists not only in the alternation of want and satiety; it consists altogether in the alternation of opposites. In his doctrine of the necessity of opposites Heraclitus here merely brings to its sharpest expression what others too had thought and taught. We are touching here the innermost core of the older philosophy of nature. All its systems are built on one schema: 1. The world is a unity. 2. It unfolds itself in opposites<sup>10</sup>. Neither of the two, however, is demonstrable, either empirically or logically. Whence, then, do these conceptions originate? There is only one common source for both: feeling. It lies in the nature of feeling that it seeks to fuse everything into unity, and at the same time that it constantly moves in opposites. Psychologists agree on this, whether they distinguish simply only pleasure and displeasure as forms of feeling, or assume, as pleasures and displeasures, a plurality of positive and negative qualities. Wundt in particular, who goes beyond that opposition, has sharply brought out antithetic as a law of feeling; besides the feelings of pleasure and displeasure he names as basic forms also those of excitement and calming, of tension and release – and all the individual and complex forms, hatred and love, joy and sorrow, admiration and contempt, freshness and weariness, etc., confirm it: feelings move in opposites, whereas perceptions move in differences. To be sure, the more subjective they are (that is, in particular with the lower senses), and so the closer to feeling, the more do sense-sensations too move in opposites (warm and cold, sweet and bitter, etc.). Alcmaeon already points consciously to the subjective origin of this antithetic when he says that the multitude of human experiences moves in opposites, such as white and black, sweet and bitter, good and bad, small and great (Arist. met. I 5. 986a). The older philosophical systems of nature, Anaximander and [p. 65] Anaximenes, Heraclitus and Parmenides, now have the formation of the world begin precisely with these opposites of sensation: warm and cold, light and dark, which replace the elements for them, just as Empedocles too, incidentally, traces his elements back to such antithesis (Arist. met. I 4. 985a 31, gen. et corr. II 30. 330b 19), and may have derived his fourfold number of elements from the crossing of the opposites of perception, warm and cold, dry and moist, in just the same way as Aristotle, who follows him here. Still more, though, Empedocles belongs here, in so far as he explains the whole course of the world from the opposition of the two human feelings: hatred and love.

<sup>10</sup> Cf. on monism and antithetics as the basic structure of the Ionian and Pythagorean systems my essay in Ztschr. f. Philos. Bd. 97, p. 161ff.

In the Renaissance this cosmic antithetic is renewed. Telesius, the restorer of Parmenides, explains the formation of the world from the basic principles of the warm and the cold, that is, from opposites of sensation. Paracelsus argues: all primal beginnings are enclosed in the great mystery, in chaos, but there is need of opposition for them to come forth; for who can know the good without the evil? How naive it is here to derive the objective necessity of the world-opposition from the subjective necessity of the knower! Most important here is Boehme’s world-opposition of light and dark. But his whole doctrine has altogether the same basic theme as the Heraclitean: the necessity of opposites. Both grasped it in its principial depth. Boehme also grounds it subjectively, as it arose in him: that only opposition makes opposition “perceptible.” Heraclitus, according to Theophrastus (de sensu I 1), even taught, like Anaxagoras later, that our senses perceive only what is opposite to themselves. And further he teaches in fr. 111: sickness makes health pleasant (a determination of feeling, then!), evil the good, hunger satiety, weariness rest. Thus life demands opposition, and the feeling of life is mightily heightened in Heraclitus as in Boehme. Both want life, and for that reason praise the stinging opposition, the strife that brings life forth. Not only Heraclitus calls strife the father of things; Boehme too says: in strife [p. 66] all things have their origin; without strife all things would be “a nothing and would stand still without movement.” Everything consists in Yes and No and needs “a counter-will, which drives it to the stirring.” He teaches the infinite movement, the eternal change and transition, the flowing-into-one-another of all forms of nature, as loudly as Heraclitus.

There are, then, three doctrines that make up the common core of the systems of Heraclitus and Boehme: 1. the necessity of opposites, up to the high value placed on strife; 2. the flowing-into-one-another of opposites; 3. the unceasingness of this world-flux in opposites. There is only one thing in which all three principles are given immediately: feeling. Feeling lives, as has been said, in constant opposites (the Heraclitean law of the world, of “weariness” in persisting and “recovery” in alternation, is a law of feeling). Feeling, further, fuses all opposites, lets them “blur”; its essence is to let everything become one, but precisely in the stream of feeling; its essence is not to separate and not to persist, but to flow always and without limit; its essence is transformation as such: therein lies the necessity of opposites, between which the transformation takes place; therein lies the transformation itself as the passing-over of opposites into one another; therein lies the constancy, the unboundedness of the transformation. Ionian philosophy, Heraclitus most decidedly, and again the philosophy of the Renaissance, Boehme most decidedly, which conceive the world as an infinite flux, as constant transformation between opposites, conceive it in accordance with feeling. The intuition of the changes in the world, the appeal to the data of experience, does not suffice; for the world presents itself just as much as persisting as flowing. That they read out precisely flux as the essential thing is due to the fact that feeling is so alive in them, that they read the book of the world with feeling, that is, with the flowing soul. The rhythm of the soul becomes for them the rhythm of the world. The whole of Ionian philosophy is an absolute doctrine of the transformation of the world, and in this a conception of the world according to feeling. All the determinations of this conception of the world: unity, [p. 67] opposition, transition, flux, unboundedness, as absolute determinations, are given not in outer experience but only in inner, as properties of feeling.

One more word on antithetic! Consider that opposition as such is perhaps originally only an experience of feeling, and in any case is given most immediately in it: in felt dissonance, in hatred; hence Heraclitus, in his world-opposition as strife and war, less pictures than makes plain, grasps it most originally, traces it back to immediate consciousness. Antithetic is not only given first; it is also at all times given most loudly and most insistently in feeling, which swings in the highest waves between pleasure and displeasure, joy and sorrow, love and hatred. Feeling as such therefore produces consciousness of oppositeness. One now grasps that lyric poetry in turn, which lives precisely on those opposites of feeling and expresses them in heightened form, thereby sharpens the sense and the eye for the feeling-stirring dissonances of the world. Antithetic, this basic schema of the old systems of nature, comes to expression first and so decisively in lyric poetry that the genres of lyric poetry are distinguished according to opposites of mood. The ancients wanted to let this opposition live on in the philosophy of nature, by setting the laughing Democritus over against the elegiac Heraclitus, who incidentally expressly makes the opposition of pleasure and displeasure the practical basic principle (fr. 4).

But not only the opposites, still more their reversal, their rapid transition, was realized in those lyric heroes of feeling, in those Sappho- and Archilochus-natures who, now exultant to the heavens, now grieved unto death, lived life as that wave-motion, as that infinite flux which became the image of the world for their countrymen Thales and Heraclitus. That tremendous oscillation of the feeling of life, which the lyric centuries of Greece displayed perhaps more strongly than any other age or nation, longed to come to rest, and, out of the eternal dissatisfaction of changing one-sided feelings, pressed naturally toward an [p. 68] enduring, eternal feeling that wholly satisfies the soul and fills it, as the goal of life. This all-feeling leading beyond the opposites was given by mysticism. Thus the lyric antithetic of feeling leads, through its own heightening, to mysticism. Mysticism is the overcoming of the antithetic of feeling in total feeling; mysticism is thus the summit of feeling, but for that very reason the beginning of calming, of the lowering of feeling, and thereby the beginning of knowledge. The mystic, delving deep in the feelings, knows their oppositeness and its meaning. “Who can speak of joys, who has felt no sorrow?” So asks Jacob Boehme. But the mystic wants precisely to overcome this opposition of the individual feelings. “To whom sorrow is as joy and joy as sorrow, let him thank God for such equality” – so already Tauler writes. The mystical overcoming of the one-sided, opposed feelings takes place through the all-feeling. God the absolute, total object of feeling, hence the overcoming of the antithetic of feeling: therefore mysticism teaches, in its great systematists, God as the unity of opposites, God as the unity of all Yes and No – so the Cusanus proclaims in his deepest teaching, in the theologia mystica. God the unity of opposites, so Bruno too teaches, so the mystic Weigel; but above all Boehme struggled through to the conception of God as the unity of light and dark, of wrath and love, of good and evil. And now hear Heraclitus: “God is day and night, winter and summer, war and peace, satiety and hunger,” in short all opposites at once (fr. 67). Day and night, good and bad are one (fr. 57. 58), and just as the Cusanus’ “mystical theology” affirms and denies, at one and the same time, designations of the primal One, the Absolute, so does Heraclitus similarly (fr. 32). In God every dissonance is resolved for him; only to men do ethical opposites appear (fr. 102). “The same is Hades and Dionysus,” it says in fr. 15; one is therefore the god of death and the god of foaming life; tragedy and exultation are one in God. – Does one now grasp that mysticism, in so far as it drew all individual feelings together in God, thereby relieved the individual things of the world of feeling, made them [p. 69] accessible to objective contemplation, and that thus mysticism became the beginning of knowledge?

From the summit of feeling, from the All-One, there proceeds a calming, a moderation, an ordering, an intellectualization; the All-One acts upon everything individual in a ruling, determining, delimiting way. Thus the mystical, crowning conclusion of feeling becomes the beginning of the system. The world-One becomes for Heraclitus the rational order of the world, becomes intellectual, the ἓν σοφόν [the one wise] (fr. 32. 41. 64). Total feeling becomes thinking. The Absolute, the goal of mysticism, becomes the beginning of speculation. That totality toward which mysticism always pressed, Heraclitus designates as his principle. He emphasizes the universal character of thinking (fr. 113); he sees in all human laws only emanations of the one divine law (114). He insists unceasingly on universal reason and universal order, on the universal word which all individuals are to recognize and obey (fr. 1. 2. 30. 41. 50. 64. 72). All individuals stand in constant contact with this universal (72); those who are awake (the conscious) have a single common world (89), and even the sleepers co-operate in the life of the universe (75). One sees that he does not speak simply of the universe, but that what matters to him chiefly is the contact of the individual with the universe, the connection of man with nature. He does not forget the individual, man above nature, he proceeds from him, and he admonishes him “to act according to nature” (fr. 112), just as the mystic Sebastian Franck teaches: “To follow nature means thus to follow God. The ancients, who gave ear to nature, have become wise and learned in God.” Heraclitus comes to nature not as a mere object of observation, but as an ethical-spiritual ideal, as the universal extension of man and thereby of his enlightenment and elevation. Nature is therefore for him the “common” (ξυνόν, κοινόν) [the common], the universal for man; therein lies already the unity of man with nature, therein lies the right and occasion to interpret nature after man. Nature is there, as it were, the All-man, governed by the All-soul.

[p. 70] In fact the Heraclitean world-principle rules the world as a master rules men, as reason rules the passions. It is the all-steerer, the all-reason (fr. 41. 64. 72. 114); it rules this world of strife through and as δίκη [justice] (fr. 94, cf. 23. 28.) and νόμος [law] (114), μέτρον [measure] (30. 31. 94) and λόγος [word, reason] (1. 2. 4a. 31. 50). Are these not mere anthropomorphisms? Steerer, reason, strife, right, law, measure, word – these are the concepts and terms by which Heraclitus explains and determines the world. They are chosen from a political-moral conception of the world. In the times of the wildest, most quarrelsome individualism, this great preacher of warning points to the universal order of nature as an ideal. The course of the world has its eternal measures in becoming and passing away (fr. 30), the sea has its measures (31), the sun will not overstep its own; otherwise the Erinyes, the handmaids of Dike, would catch it out (94) – the presumptuous sun! One often does not know whether Heraclitus is speaking of the cosmic or of human life, e.g. where he praises Δίκη [Justice] (fr. 23. 28). In the principle of “war” he leaps, in fr. 53, quite plainly from the one to the other. And he can do so from the unity of the two, which was certain to him, in virtue of which he interprets nature after man. And now consider that the concept of the law of nature is an anthropomorphism. As such it appears here for the first time in Heraclitus. Human laws are for him an emanation of the divine law, that is, of the cosmic (114) – again, therefore, the unity of man and All! But in the genesis of knowledge it was naturally the reverse. The human law gave image and name, and thereby the concept of the cosmic. Heraclitus is first of all a venerator of law in the proper, the political sense (fr. 44, cf. 33). It was then that the age of the great lawgivers came to a close, and Heraclitus’ one friend, to whom he enthusiastically professes himself politically attached (fr. 121), is held by many to have been the adviser of the Roman Twelve Tables legislation. It is once again similar, as at the end of the Renaissance the great laws of nature were found, when the veneration of law had been heightened by political mystics and the human law of nature was [p. 71] discovered. Galileo and Kepler came at the same time as Hugo Grotius, and came when Bodinus had placed the law above the king, had determined the state as a community determined according to reason or the law of nature, and when Mariana (like Heraclitus) had extolled law as unmoved reason and as an emanation of the divine spirit. And they demand monarchy in unity with the exemplary laws of nature; the universe has a steerer, and the king is the breathing image of almighty God. Heraclitus too teaches world-monarchy, and fr. 33 asserts meaningfully: it is law also to follow the will of one.

The analogistic, not only that of the political with the cosmic, is in Heraclitus deep-going, principial, quite simply a method of grasping the world. Fire, water, air, earth “live” and “die” (fr. 76), and the world goes the “ways upward and downward.” And he reaches still more boldly into life for comparisons for the course of the world. At one time it appears to him as trade in wares (fr. 90), at another as a stirred mixed drink (125), at another as a heap of rubbish (124), at another as a boy’s game (52) – it is the mood in which Franck’s mysticism compares the world to a pigsty or a carnival play, where God lets figures “rise up high” and “brings them to a mocking end,” “all gone as it came” (said in Heraclitean terms: the way up and the way down the same). “Whoever looks at this matter in earnest, it would be no wonder if his heart broke with weeping. If one looks at it, like Democritus, scornfully, one might burst with laughter. So the world juggles.” Heraclitus cannot merely weep; he can also go with the mystic to the other side and sits at the head of the bench of the scoffers. He also calls, incidentally, human thoughts children’s toys (fr. 70) – like the course of the world.

Man and world, life and nature, the spiritual and the physical go together because they are one in life: that is the deep ground and sense of his whole analogistic. He preaches morality through comparisons of the psychic and the bodily (cf. e.g. fr. 43. 46. 68). When Carriere [p. 72] already compared Heraclitus, with good reason, with Jacob Boehme in the difficulty of being understood, this difficulty has the same ground in both: that constant reciprocal imaging of nature and the psychic-human, which rests on genuine fellow-feeling, on an experience of nature in the human soul and of the human soul in nature. This shimmering-into-one-another of the two forces symbolic expression; the one is interpreted as a sign through the other. Heraclitus knows this and therefore defends himself by appeal to the oracle, which neither says nor conceals, but gives a sign (fr. 93). Nature loves to hide itself (fr. 123). Thus he openly confesses that symbolic treatment, that conception of nature as mystery, which was so characteristic of the mystical spirit of Renaissance philosophy (cf. p. 17).

This symbolic-mystical trait leads to the cult of the word (cf. p. 18). The word is a symbol, a spiritual formulation of the sensible, a sensible expression of the spiritual, the mediator of soul and world, the seal of their unity. And Reuchlin wrote de verbo mirifico [On the Wonder-Working Word]; and Agrippa taught: since words belong to the world, the magician can, by virtue of the connection of the world, work through them even into heaven. God must “speak his word,” says Eckhart, that is, God begets the Son. The proclamation of the Gospel of John, of the Logos as Son of God, as beginning of the world, creator of the world, receives renewed deeper attention and leads in mystical philosophy to the parallel, indeed the unity, of the development of God and of the world. The “Word” is drawn into the human soul and into nature. Only the word and faith, says Luther, rule in the soul, and in creatures we recognize the power of the word. Thomas Münzer: if the word becomes alive in man, he is made divine; Reuchlin: God is spirit, the word is breath, man breathes; God is the word. Above all, though, Jacob Boehme celebrates the word as the beginning of all things, as an emanation of the divine will and of divine knowledge. Precisely the intellectual value of the word comes to [p. 73] the highest recognition. Suarez finds in the word the goal, the result of the act of knowing; Laurentius Valla, Agricola, Nizolius, Vives, Ramus and others had already earlier demanded the close connection of logic with rhetoric, “as heart and tongue hang together.” The full heart of the age pressed toward the word.

And it was similar when Greek lyric poetry overflowed into nature-mysticism, and most of all Heraclitus’ words have a vibrating sound, as if a world were pressing out of them. And he already emphasized the word so mystically that his philosophy becomes a doctrine of the Logos. He too begins with the “Word,” which is eternal and according to which everything happens (fr. 1), which all are to follow, as it is common to all (fr. 2). The “Word” determines the seasons of the stars (4a) and the measure of the sea (31). Men are to hear the “Word,” in which lies that all is one (50); they are not to quarrel with the “Word,” with which they are in most constant connection, for it steers the All (72). And at the same time the soul possesses the “Word,” which increases itself (114). In the word, then, the unity and order of nature and its contact with man and soul are given to him. In this position of the word the mysticism in Heraclitus comes, it seems, to its strongest expression. Nature is humanized, it “speaks,” and it is understood by the soul. The first understanding of nature will always be mystical.

The word had acquired a new power. One had sought it, and now it demanded attention. The old philosophy of nature began after the spread of writing (cf. p. 50), the philosophy of the Renaissance after the invention of printing, and it does not now cease to speak of the “book” of nature. Galileo still finds the book of nature written in mathematical letters, sees the pages of the book of heaven, and teaches, in Heraclitean phrase, that inexorable nature will not overstep the wording of its laws. The world is God’s codex autographus [autograph manuscript], says Campanella, and Paracelsus already reads the codex naturae [book of nature] by wandering. “The Scripture is [p. 74] investigated through its letters, nature however through land after land. As often a land, as often a page.” All creatures are letters to him, which point to man as a whole. Should such symbolism have been lacking in the old mysticism of nature? We have, after all, a weighty testimony in the name of the element. Nature is composed of elements, στοιχεῖα [elements, letters], letters. And if in Aristotle the differences of Democritus’ atoms are made clear by letters, this may already be tradition. The great atomist was also a philologist and even wrote about letters. About well- and ill-sounding letters he wrote, as about rhythms and harmony, about linguistic correctness and obscure words. In those days, after all, words were taken in more with the ear than with the reading eye; the physicist too an orator, as precisely the Democritean Nausiphanes explains (fr. 1).

Heraclitus loves word-play, assonances, as all mysticism loves them (even Baader!); it hears in the assonance precisely that unity which it seeks; it hears in the sensible word the spiritual meaning; symbolism leads it, seduces it, to etymologizing, which not by chance sprang precisely from Heraclitism (see Cratylus!). That mysticism which interprets σῶμα [body] as σῆμα [tomb], Demeter as Ge-meter [Earth-mother], Zeus as the god of ζῆν [to live], is not unrelated to Jacob Boehme, who clarifies his doctrine of unity by saying that the dark hell and the shining brightness sound out of one ground, and that welling and willing are one.

The sound and effect of the word were raised up by lyric poetry. It is the poetry which was bound with music, and so most of all with the impression on the ear, to feeling; it is the poetry of the heart, which presses to the word, it speaks out of the soul through the world to the soul, it is most of all the poetry of imagery; it must symbolize first; for the psychic can be communicated only metaphorically, as according to the mystic Hamann all passion speaks in images; it humanizes the world, grasps it according to the soul; it brings to expression first the opposed, the dissonances of the world, but it seeks [p. 75] for that very reason artistic reconciliation and, already as musical poetry, harmony. However little Heraclitus may have intended it, it is perhaps not wholly by chance that the basic concepts of the Heraclitean order of the world – word, metron, nomos and harmony – play a role in lyric poetry. This may sound strange, but it was after all in lyric poetry that a process of life, an impression of the world, was brought into measure and rule of expression; bound manifoldness, the limitation of the unlimited, unity in contrast, harmony, became formally conscious, emphasized, demanded, and thus a schema of order not only of words but soon also of ideas, a type of knowledge.

Such consideration leads us to Pythagoreanism, whose mystical subjectivity one might summarize briefly by saying that it is a conception of the world out of the spirit of music. One knows what music meant to the Pythagoreans, and indeed already to the older ones; I believe it is the root of their whole doctrine. Music is the most subjective, the pure art of feeling and always the favourite art of mysticism. Luther felt its significance for the heart; Suso already finds himself moved by nature as by sweet sound, hears inner heavenly music, and Boehme still believes he hears it as he dies. As the pure art of the sense of time, of transformation, it mediates inner world and outer world, which have in common only the sense of time, find themselves one only in transformation. Feeling is unlimited transformation – and it is precisely now the achievement of music (just as expression of feeling) that it limits this transformation through the determinateness and order of tones. The expression of feeling becomes a calming, a taming of feeling, and in this sense lyric poetry already signifies a clarification of feeling, the transition into the intellectual that begins through the casting of form. We hear that the Pythagoreans cultivated music in its moral effect as a soothing, a taming of the passions. Passion the unbounded – and precisely the opposition of the bounded and the unbounded, the opposition of bound and of pure, unbound [p. 76] feeling becomes the Pythagorean basic opposition. The indeterminate feeling seeks and finds in tones determinatenesses of expression; they are the first determinatenesses to which dawning knowledge in Pythagoreanism attends; and because they are quantitative determinatenesses, the Pythagorean says: all is number, which means, more originally: all is tone, all sounds, all is determinate expression of feeling, and so we come again upon the mystical all-feeling as the germ of Pythagoreanism.

Let no one think that tone and feeling are here read into an original Pythagorean mathematics and cosmology. The Pythagorean says not only: all is number, he says from the very beginning: all is harmony, and so he grasps everything musically. The Pythagoreans, it is said, called philosophy music, interpreted the essence of all things through music, explained the whole heaven, the universe, as harmony or according to harmony (Arist. met. I 5, Strabo X 3, 10, Athen. XIII 632b). All is harmony, means: all sounds – how could it be otherwise, since they make the remotest, the most imperceptible things, the stars, sound? The Pythagorean harmony of the spheres is the full expression of the fact that all is music, all speaks to feeling. To the mystical feeling, to the all-feeling, the All sounds; therefore the Pythagorean speaks precisely of the harmony of the spheres. Thus the solution of the riddle is revealed once more: the Pythagorean begins with the cosmos because he begins with feeling, which alone brings even the remotest near to man; he begins with the cosmos because he is a mystic and the universe is nearer to the all-feeling than the individual thing. He begins in truth subjectively, with feeling, not with cosmology but with music, for he interprets the former according to the latter, not the reverse. He does not begin with mathematics either; for music contains mathematics, but mathematics does not contain music, and with mere mathematics no one can begin, because the meaning of numbers must first be brought near through phenomena, such as precisely tones. Practical-technical interests, which suggested mathematics to the Orientals (cf. p. 2), are not visible in Pythagoras. Tones [p. 77] alone do not yet give numbers either, but the measurement of tones does, to which the cultivation of music, the striving for harmony, leads. The measurability of tones becomes visible in the instrument, most visible precisely in the old monochord, in which a movable bridge divided the string differently and so pointed to the correspondence of pitch and length of string. This “discovery” is expressly ascribed even to the old Pythagoras, and I see no occasion to distrust the report<sup>11</sup>. With this discovery the meaning of number was given. Not the comparison of tones alone, but that of tones and strings yielded it. The meaning of quantity comes to light only in the qualitatively different. Number could detach itself only from different series of phenomena which were, however, parallel in number. It thus became clear as the principle of parallelism, of the comparability of things, as the bond of things. Thus there lies in number the unity of all things through their parallelism, the principle of analogy.

<sup>11</sup> Cf. also Th. Gomperz, Griech. Denker I 83.

[p. 77] The attention to number arises only from attention to analogy, and so already at the root of Pythagoreanism lies that analogistic, that symbolizing monism which makes Pythagoreanism so mystical – just as, on the other hand, mysticism as such presses toward number, because in number the symbolic bond is given in which feeling finds its striving for unity satisfied. Renaissance philosophy, from Plethon and Cusanus onward, bristles with number-mysticism, often in conscious renewal of Pythagoreanism, to which it feels akin. In the nineteenth century the very consummator of Schelling’s nature-mysticism, Oken, proclaimed the Pythagorean dogma word for word: everything real is absolutely nothing other than a number. The pious Romantic Novalis exalted mathematics to heaven, the mystic Pascal invented the calculating machine, Spinoza’s mathematically constructed system issues in mysticism, and Comte, who wanted to transform life and science into mathematics, was bound to become a mystic.

Comte, the pupil of Saint-Simon, the new Pythagoras, like the old one a founder of an order and a social reformer on [p. 78] the basis of a hierarchy of the knowing! What is told of the grades of the order and the secret doctrines of the Pythagoreans contains, in spite of our historical rationalism, a true kernel; for it accords with the idea of the order, with ordering mysticism. And here again there are parallels in the Renaissance, in the secret doctrines and secret societies in which, e.g., Agrippa is absorbed, in mystical ideal lawgivers from Plethon onward, who takes Pythagoras, Lycurgus and Numa as models, down to Campanella, who calls for the astrologer for his hierarchical City of the Sun, as Bodin does for his political theory of number symbolism. He speaks, like the Pythagoreans, of proportions of justice. For the Pythagoreans, as mathematicians, are at the same time zealous moralists, as they are musicians. But they do not love moral order and musical harmony because they love number; they love number out of the feeling for order and harmony. The value of number must first be experienced and felt in something else before it detaches itself abstractly. Then, in turn, feeling could expand by way of the analogy of number. How can one love order in one domain only and not in the other? So asks, in recommending virtue, the mathematician Sophie Germain. That it is precisely in mathematics that women have so far achieved the most also testifies that it is precisely feeling that makes one receptive to numbers. Four women mathematicians crowned by the Paris Academy proved at the same time to be artistic and passionate natures<sup>12</sup>. In the Renaissance, when the education and esteem of woman were also rising, the magician Agrippa wrote “On the Nobility and Preeminence of the Female Sex.” And did not the mystics demand that the soul of man should resemble woman in receptive devotion? Of all the philosophical schools of antiquity, it is in the Pythagorean that women’s names are mentioned most often and with the greatest weight. The un-Greek Pythagorean feminism meets again with the cult of woman of the number-mystic Comte. Add to this woman’s receptivity for music, the “secret mathematics”! The [p. 79]Romantic Bettina writes with Pythagorean symbolism: “As every thought, every soul is melody, so the human spirit, through its embracing of the All, shall become harmony.”

<sup>12</sup> Cf. “Philosophenwege” p. 98ff.

Through its embracing of the All; for feeling is satisfied only when the All resounds in and with the soul. Everything is music means: everything is feelable, everything in unity, in contact with the soul. The all-feeling of mysticism demands the harmony of the world, and so Jacob Boehme proclaims it with particular fervor: all instruments are like the barking of dogs against the divine sound, or the Musica, which arises through it from eternity to eternity. Practical mysticism, magic, rested on the musical conception of the world. Agrippa compares the universe to a stretched string which, touched at one end, at once resounds everywhere. Or again: as a struck string makes other strings sound with it, so through one part of the world all the others are touched, and by virtue of this reciprocal love the magician could work up even into heaven, and his deeds would be no more contrary to nature or supernatural than the enchantment which a musician produces through the power of tones in the souls of his listeners. Thus a bond of feeling unites all parts of the world and the world with the feeling soul. Musical harmony, he says, soothes, heals, and uplifts the soul, and it corresponds to the harmony of the spheres, the interval of the tones to the distance of the planets – so Agrippa teaches, in agreement with the Pythagoreans. Still more important, however, is that Kepler not only renews with enthusiasm the thought of the harmony of the world, of cosmic music (the sun as Apollo eternally listening to the song of the planetary Muses!), and is led by the Pythagorean harmony of the spheres to the relation between the distance and the period of revolution of the planets, but also emphasizes precisely the harmonic contact between soul and the world of bodies: the contemplation of the harmonious outer world drives us to become inwardly harmonious. When we are seized by the concord of tones, this is not merely a passive reception but an awakening of the tone-ideas innate in the soul and of the corresponding movements of the mind, and since will and [p. 80] vital force are most intimately bound together, not merely is the soul seized by music, but the movement of the body too is ordered according to its measure. As harmonious tones stir man inwardly, so, Kepler teaches, the play of the harmony of the world acts unconsciously on the earth and on men. At certain positions of the planets the earth exhales violent vapours, men grow more restless, more passionate, the orator carries them away more easily, and warlike courage swells –

From Kepler’s mysticism we understand the Pythagoreans. Mysticism lies in the deeply subjectively accented, felt unity of the world, as of the world with the soul. The harmonizing of the world is a humanizing of the world, its adaptation to the soul. It was from such a drive toward harmonization, that is, from needs of feeling, that Copernicus and Kepler, and already the Pythagoreans, corrected the picture of the world. The musical All is the felt All, the All brought close to the soul. Cosmic interest and study are thereby made not only more intense but also freer. The more inward the picture of the world becomes, the freer it grows from the pressure of the earth and of appearance. The horizon of the soul is wider than that of the senses; to feeling the earth can lighten itself into a star, and in accordance with an inner need the constructive imagination can boldly reshape the world. The soul grows with the world as the world with the soul.

The Pythagoreans dethrone the earth; in the central fire they seek the invisible “hearth” of the world, the cosy centre, as it were, the heart of the world; they speak of the right and left side of the world, of the breathing of the world; and, carrying this enlivening of the world, the unity of the psychic and the physical, down to the smallest things, they explain motes of sunlight as souls.

According to Philolaus (fr. 11) number is the cohesion of the world, but also of life and of knowledge; it makes everything harmonious to the soul, knowable to sensation, and corresponding to one another (κατὰν ψυχὰν ἁρμόζων αἰσθήσει πάντα γνωστὰ καὶ ποτάγορα ἀλλάλοις). So it is precisely that bond, also emphasized by Kepler, both of the world [p. 81] with itself and of the soul with the world, of subject with object. When Pythagoras measures the tones that stir the mind on visible strings, the contact between inner world and outer world is thereby discovered in number. He now believes he can build up both worlds on numbers. For Pythagoreanism number becomes the principle of analogy at once for the psychic – the virtues, the concepts are numbers! – and for the physical – the bodies are numbers! It is only analogy that makes bodies appear to him as consisting of geometrical figures, and these of numbers. No doubt, the world of figures and bodies is not his primary interest but (what the stuff is to the Ionian) at first a visible analogy, an illustration, a pictorial rendering of the numbers. But even the value of number is given to him only by harmony; and the value of harmony is given only by the feeling that enjoys it.

The feeling for harmony, however, is not a simple one but a developed, complicated one – therein lies its generative power for knowledge. Feeling as such is and demands unity. But it is unity in transformation between opposites (cf. p. 64ff.); hence it demands contrasts; but since it at the same time demands unity, it demands precisely unity of opposites, that is, harmony. Pythagoreanism manifests its origin in feeling, satisfies the needs of feeling, by teaching (like Heraclitus): 1. the world is unity, 2. the world is opposition, 3. the world is harmony – all of them propositions obtained neither logically nor empirically! Precisely the opposition of the limited and the unlimited, which Zeller can explain only as a later abstraction, but which he nevertheless finds at the head of the system in Philolaus and in the table of opposites, and which belongs to Pythagoras himself just as the concept of the boundless and its separations belongs to his contemporary Anaximander – this basic Pythagorean opposition is given, indeed, as the primal opposition of feeling in measure and measurelessness. Everything, the Pythagoreans teach, consists of opposites, and everything is harmony. Yet only feeling impels us to sharpen the manifoldness of the world so absolutely into opposites to the point of [p. 82] fanaticism, and only feeling in turn impels us to reconcile them in harmony. One must thank it; for thereby the eye was opened for the manifoldness of the world and the connection of the world, for the world as an orderable plurality. Antithetics and harmonistics stem from feeling, but they open up the activity of thinking, which is indeed a separating and combining. In harmony something that is to be separated is felt as a connection, a manifoldness as a unity, immediately experienced. Harmony, unity in manifoldness, is the prototype of the system; the feeling for harmony is the germ of knowledge, incipient differentiation, which in harmony justifies itself once more before feeling, but soon was sought ever more scientifically beyond feeling. I recall how Kepler frees the picture of the world from the constraint of the all too uniform circular orbit and hits upon the ellipse as a more varied and yet harmonious figure. The sense of the variegation of the world grew through the feeling for the unity of the world. The unity was the more beautiful and valuable, the greater the variegation. Greek lyric poetry had shown change within unity as a formal pattern. The philosophy of nature shows the world as unity of opposites, as harmony. Knowledge begins precisely as differentiated feeling, as specialized mysticism.

Eleatism seems here to be a step backward; it knows only unity, it proscribes manifoldness, change, plurality. In truth it has thereby raised manifoldness to a problem and brought to full consciousness the sense of unity necessary for a system. But it is feeling that by its nature presses toward unity, and it is feeling heightened to the highest and last, absolute feeling, that is, mystical feeling, that leads to the One-and-Only. It is no inference, it is quite simply a tautological proposition, that mystical feeling contains the ἓν καὶ πᾶν [one and all]. Absolute feeling is the feeling of the Absolute, that is, of the One-and-Only. Mystical feeling is the all-feeling in which everything particular sinks, everything becomes one. For the Eleatic the world sinks before the One-and-Only; what a power of inwardness [p. 83] belongs to his denial of the motley, changing world! In Eleatism mysticism lies most clearly in the open; its doctrine is a doctrine of the One-and-Only – what pure mysticism of all peoples and times taught, and most polyphonically again the mysticism of the Renaissance. Not only in Cusanus, in Bruno and other fanatical monists: even Zwingli, as Dilthey remarks, can appropriate the formula of the Eleatics, that all is One, as he also praises the ancient doctrine that nature is God.

Yes, Eleatism upholds this mystical doctrine so dogmatically and harshly that mysticism turns over into rationalism and criticism. It takes mysticism at its word and defends the One-and-Only to the point of nonsense, until it bursts, and the successors of the Eleatic monists establish pluralism, the multiplicity of world-principles. In Eleatism mysticism becomes rigid dogma and thereby ripe for overcoming. The Eleatic drove the unitary impulse of feeling to its extreme, but precisely thereby made unity conscious, consolidated it from an experience of feeling into a thought. Unity as feeling is life-giving; unity as thought, pure unity, becomes deadly. But as a transition it was necessary. The changing individual feelings of the lyric age had first to come to rest in a highest feeling. With this culmination of feeling, with this rest of the goal, thinking begins; its doing is consolidating; where the flowing feeling has skimmed off its foam, thinking posits a principle, drives a nail in, as it were, finds a being, whereas feeling knows only becoming. From this first foothold, being as such, it could proceed to further fixed points, to elements of being. To be sure, Eleatism stopped where feeling had still carried it; it consolidates only the end-point of feeling as the sole principle; it still lived too much on mysticism.

Yet it already signifies a spiritual clarification as against the Ionians. The Ionian unity of the world is unity in the change of opposites, that is, it is still too much a unity of feeling; for to feeling, as was shown, opposition and change are essential. The highest longing, the final goal of [p. 84] feeling is to be removed from this change of opposites, to die away in an enduring, uniform state of unity without needs – Eleatism made principled earnest of this longed-for final state; it teaches an enduring being, a resting, eternal unity without any change and opposition, “lacking nothing” (Parmenides fr. 8), “painless,” “free of suffering,” “sound” (Melissus fr. 7) – does one not still sense the human, the ideal of feeling, in these predicates of the Absolute?

But as with the Ionians and Pythagoreans, what has been spiritually experienced and gained is at the same time rendered sensuously vivid, the inward at the same time made distinct physically, plastically. The One-and-Only, says Parmenides, is like a well-rounded sphere. He too, then, practises analogistic, and like all nature-mysticism he sees in the sphere a symbol of perfection, which is just what he seeks, of uniform, closed totality lacking nothing. This symbolism probably first helped the earth and the stars to their right bodily form, as indeed Parmenides in his world of appearance already grants them the spherical shape. It is an important result for our problem: the philosophers of nature arrive at nature less through direct intuition than more or less through symbolism, through pictorial rendering, plastic embodiment of their feeling of life, through a finding of contact, a drawing of parallels, between the psychic and the physical. The ideal of feeling becomes idea, the idea image; thus perfection becomes a sphere.

All knowledge rests on a certain symbolism, insofar as it demands some correspondence of subject and object; all knowledge therefore springs, in its deepest ground, from mysticism. But the mystical unity of subject and object, which is given immediately in feeling and loosened in knowledge, is once more grasped in principle by Parmenides when he proclaims the unity of thinking and being (fr. 5, 6, 8), and further when he lets like be perceived only by like. This absolute agreement, indeed identity, of thinking and being, of subject and object – is it not a grandiose anthropomorphism, the greatest that is philosophically possible?

[p. 85] In keeping with limiting thought, Parmenides conceives the one being as limited, whereas the less strict Ionian Eleatic Melissus conceives it, more in the manner of feeling, as infinite. For a Parmenides thinking becomes lawgiving for being; logical compulsion embodies itself for him in the punishing Δίκη [Justice], who holds the keys to the gate of truth and did not release becoming and passing away from their bonds, in the mighty Ανάγκη [Necessity] or Μοῖρα [Fate], who holds being within fixed limits and fetters (fr. 1, 8). Thus thinking forced the world, in truth the feeling of the world, to a consistency that was needed. At first, to be sure, the world was lost in the consistency, or at least sank to a world of appearance in contrast to pure being, and that world of appearance the man of the “two ways” articulates in turn according to the opposition of light and dark – it was discussed (p. 64–68) how this whole antithetics rests on a genuine ground of feeling.

As a moment of feeling that anthropomorphizes the contemplation of the world, I single out only the feminine or sexual trait that stands out conspicuously in the few fragments of Parmenides. He travels the way of the goddess who leads to everything; maidens, leaving the house of Night, steer and speed the journey, coax Dike for him to hand over the keys; then the goddess receives him graciously and promises him all wisdom (fr. 1). She goes on speaking as his Pythia. But the goddess is not only the guide of knowledge; now as Μοῖρα, now as Δίκη, now as ᾿Ανάγκη, always feminine, the government of the world appears to him (fr. 8), and in the middle of the world stands the goddess, steering all things. She leads all things to birth and copulation, mates woman to man and man to woman (fr. 12). So the goddess of love governs the world, and indeed she created, first of all the gods (as the first and only male being that Parmenides names for us), Eros (13). Then fr. 17 and 18 still deal with the problem of copulation and birth, and fr. 20 leads to the grove of much-revered Aphrodite, which awakens longing. In the Renaissance and the Reformation, too, speculation strongly emphasizes the problem of love<sup>13</sup> and birth. I recall once more [p. 86] Luther’s words: “Now we look at the creature quite properly, more than under the papacy. But Erasmus asks nothing about it, troubles himself little how the fruit in the mother’s womb is formed, prepared and made; nor does he heed the married state, how glorious it is.” It is a vitalistic-mystical trait that makes the embryological interest appear so strikingly strong among the Presocratics in the few fragments; the problem of birth becomes for them symbolic of the problem of the origin of the world. And need I say that the love-lyric, so loud at that time, suggested the riddle of the erotic? It is again a lyrical trait that the old philosophy of nature seizes upon it with both hands. The lyric poet Ibycus renews the Hesiodic thought of the origin of Eros already out of Chaos. The feminine trait in mysticism was noted on p. 78. Not only in Baader and Saint-Martin does the deepest mysticism come to the erotic.

<sup>13</sup> The mystics of the Renaissance offer a certain parallel here in their exaltation of Mary, to which my colleague Heman drew my attention. Jacob Boehme protests against those who conceive Mary not as an earthly being but as the eternal Virgin; he himself, however, speaks of the heavenly Virgin as eternal Wisdom.

And now there appears, as the consummator of the Parmenidean mysticism of love, as the real philosopher of love, Empedocles. And to give it foil and effectiveness, he adds hatred or strife. We know that feeling needs opposites; antithetics is a form of feeling, and Empedocles cultivates it like the other philosophers of nature (cf. p. 64, 81). I point further to fr. 122, 123, where Discord and Harmony, Lady Beautiful and Lady Ugly, down to the demons of Silence and Speech, ten pairs of opposites in all (female, of course) appear. Above all, however, love and hatred form the basic opposition that governs the world in Empedocles and explains the whole world-process. Consider: feelings govern the world, the whole world-process a process of feeling, a wrestling of love and hatred. Here the subjective emerges so strongly and nakedly that the objective, the material, had to detach itself from it. One emphasizes in Empedocles the doctrine of the elements, but for him it is only a by-product. He did not primarily seek the elements at all; there was nothing to seek in his elements – earth, water, air and the fire of the stars lay open before every child’s eye. No, Empedocles sought the forces of the world, and [p. 87] he grasps them so subjectively, so psychically inwardly, that matter could no longer follow, that there was no longer, as with the Ionians, a matter offering itself for pictorial rendering, as a type of force. As a dynamist Empedocles becomes the destroyer of the unity of force and matter, and as such the discoverer of the elements. He does not seek the elements: they fall away from the forces for him. He discovers only their That, not their What. For what he names as elements needed no discovery. He discovered only their independence, the negative of their detachment from force. Because he builds the unity of the world only on the process of force, he leaves the material with the visible plurality, and he needs the material plurality because the explanatory process of force is for him an alternation of uniting love and separating hatred. Love and hatred demand the plurality of the materials, not the reverse. In the forces love and hatred lies the original thought of Empedocles, not in the long-known elements. The elements are only the material of the forces; he compares them anthropomorphistically to the materials, the pigments of painters (fr. 23). From the forces the world-system of Empedocles is to be interpreted; his basic doctrine is the doctrine of love and strife.

The world a battle-play of love and strife – Boehme teaches nothing else in his Mysterium Magnum. It is an obvious interpretation of the world according to the life of feeling, according to the human soul. But again let no one scold anthropomorphism. The explanation of the world from love and hatred is indeed its first, unfaded explanation from attraction and repulsion. In Empedocles the forces of the world still show warm human blood. He does not stand impartially over against them, but seeks to realize in them his ideal of feeling. The realm of love stands higher for him, as for Boehme, than that of strife. The physicist Empedocles is to be understood from a deeply moral experience of the soul, the author of the treatise on nature from the author of the Purifications. Full of remorse he beats his own breast, feels himself sunk into the play of the elements because he trusted in raging strife (fr. 115). He laments the human beings unspeakably, that they are born of strife (124). He shudders before murder and [p. 88] rancor (121), feels murder as a glaring dissonance, and inveighs against men who tear one another to pieces (136). He depicts the golden age, when neither Ares nor the din of war counted as divine, neither Zeus was king, nor Cronus or Poseidon, but only Aphrodite was queen (fr. 128), when no animals bled on the altar, when all creatures approached man tamely and the warmest cordiality prevailed (128, 130). One sees, the ideal of love in his tender heart passes over to all men and from men to all animals. They are his brothers, of one essence with us, indeed ourselves in a transformed state. And as the animals, so also the plants. Love, community expands for him; hence he proclaims the transmigration of the souls of men into animals, also into plants (127), hence the fanatically demanded abstention from meat, indeed in part from plants (139ff.). And love passes at last to the elements, and the ideal state is reached when the “mild-minded divine impulse of blameless love” (35) comes to victory, in the blessed unity of the elements. The Sphairos embodies the mystical ideal.

For Empedocles the chief task of his physics is to show the world-force of love, which he presents also as friendship, longing, harmony, but above all, clearly enough, as Aphrodite (fr. 16–22, 26f., 30, 35f., 59, 64, 66, 71, 73, 75, 86f., 95f., 98, 151). Aphrodite joins the elements together there with nails of love, moistens the earth, breathes warmth upon it, until the earth drops anchor in Aphrodite’s blessed harbour; Aphrodite forms eyes, forms animals, etc. And water loves, and the stars long, and the earth is accommodating (19, 21, 96, etc.) – in short, we have here, only still more strong in feeling, more primordial, the magical conception of nature of the Renaissance, that of universal sympathy. At its base lies the mystical all-feeling, the mystical unity and inner community of the soul with the world, of man with nature. Empedocles says it openly: she it is who awakens in men the impulse of love and friendship, and whom they call Joy and Aphrodite; but that she also vibrates in the elements, no mortal man knows [p. 89] (fr. 17). So he teaches a mystery, which consists in the extension of the humanly known love to a world-principle. And he too occupies himself in detail with the problem of birth (fr. 63–69, 153a), which has for him precisely a world-symbolic significance. It is also plainly said in fr. 109: with our love we behold the love of the world, with our hatred its hatred<sup>14</sup>, as with the earthy in us we know the earth, with water water, etc.; in short, from man we know the world, because he is one with the world, has a share in all parts of the cosmos, as microcosm.

<sup>14</sup> For like acts upon like – so he teaches, as, e.g., also Agrippa of Nettesheim.

Because he understands the world from the soul and from man, he understands it as life. He is a moral and physical vitalist like all mystics. Life is for him the highest and most universal – he venerates “life-giving” Aphrodite (151), he rejects animal sacrifices as an abomination because they rob life, he is more than a vegetarian, wants to spare not only the animals but also plants. He is a fanatical biologist even as a physicist, and the origin of organisms interests him most. But in his eyes everything is organic. He speaks of the “limbs” of the Sphairos, the united mass of the world (30f., 35), he speaks indeed not of elements but of “roots” of all things, as Agrippa too in his Occult Philosophy designates the four elements as roots of all things. He speaks also of the roots of the air, which plunge into the earth (54), of the sea as the sweat of the earth (55), of the fearless countenance of the sun (44), of the hatred of the elements (115). He makes Strife swear (30), Tyche will (103), Ananke speak (115), Charis hate (116), he sees the sharp-shooting Helios and the friendly Selene (40), the lonely, blind-eyed Night (49) and the throngs of the Fates (121) – one will say that this is only poetical speech; but I ask why he speaks so poetically. He takes everything as living and personal because he feels it so; he lives with all beings, with the elements, and lets them live with him. He feels his thinking at the same time as the streaming of the blood (105), he lets the understanding grow parallel to the body (106), he thinks, rejoices [p. 90] and grieves with the elements (107). In short, the soul becomes one with the world for him, as the mystic teaches. Everything is ensouled, everything conscious, everything has a share in thinking (103, 110).

Not everything is ensouled, teaches Anaxagoras – thereby he continues precisely the turning away from mysticism, which takes place in progressive differentiation altogether. Yet it is no simple cutting off; deep-lying threads remain as a bond. Mysticism does not simply teach the unity of soul and body, of force and matter – otherwise the purest mysticism would be materialism (which, to be sure, with this community of force and matter on an equal footing already half plays the mystic). Were this the thesis of the old philosophers of nature, I should never have addressed them as mystics. But this absolute uniform unity not a single one of them has taught. Not only Empedocles separates force and matter, the doctrine of the old Ionians already consists precisely in this, that they do not let all materials subsist at once as elementary forces, but choose one as the matter of force and transformation, which determines the other materials. They all seek force and seek it only in the material most suited for it, but not in all materials as such. And finally, Diogenes of Apollonia teaches air as this matter of force and transformation, matter of life, because air gives life to men and animals and animal seed is air-like – one sees again the anthropomorphic or vitalistic origin of the principle, which becomes a world-principle because, as in man or animal, so in the world too it can be a life-creating force, a moving world-soul, world-reason, since air penetrates everything. It is therefore not the absolute, uniform unity of identity of force or soul and matter, but their unity of immanence, that those philosophers of nature teach, and just this unity of immanence mysticism too teaches. To be sure, immanence becomes identity for both, but it only becomes so. The being of spirit and force becomes world, nature, becomes all materials, but is not in itself all materials, one with each material. Here lies the dividing line between mysticism and materialism.

[p. 91] Anaxagoras strikes out becoming, transformation, but he does not strike out immanence as such. Or rather, he teaches both doctrines of nature-mysticism, universal transformation or mixture and immanence, but separately. Nature-mysticism has the one penetrating, guiding, forming primal being transform itself into everything, so that everything passes over into everything else, everything is in everything. Anaxagoras teaches the one penetrating, guiding, forming primal being, which, evidently precisely in order to make clear its capacity for immanence, he nevertheless at the same time designates materially as the thinnest, finest of all things (λεπτότατον πάντων χρημάτων [the finest of all things]). On the other hand he teaches the infinite passing over, the infinite mixture of all things: everything is in everything, which doctrine indeed recurs word for word in the mysticism of the Renaissance (in Cusanus, in Agrippa, Weigel, and others) and is pointed up by Paracelsus to the effect that in a piece of bread one eats heaven and earth and all the stars. Only the primal being, the νοῦς [mind], does Anaxagoras exclude from the universal mixture, and yet he lets the νοῦς dwell more or less in beings, and if not in all, then at least guide all, and precisely for that reason he withdraws it from the universal mixture, so that it may guide all the more unhindered (fr. 12). In both doctrines the basic dogma of nature-mysticism, of the living unity of nature, comes to expression. The νοῦς as moving, ordering force explains the unity of the unfolding of nature, the world-system<sup>15</sup>, nature as an organism, so to speak. But the unity of the unfolding of nature lies also in the doctrine of matter of Anaxagoras: everything is in everything. Only thereby, he expressly teaches, is it explicable that things act upon one another and pass over into one another. He teaches, then, the universal infinite mixture because, like mysticism, he wants universal action and infinite transformation. “All things have a share in everything,” he teaches – on this rests universal sympathy according to the magical conception of nature of the Renaissance.

<sup>15</sup> Cf. Dilthey, Einl. in die Geisteswissenschaften I 205f.

The primal materials are, he says, “not hacked apart as with an axe” – he too seeks, after all, an organic conception of nature, and his vitalistic way of thinking betrays itself [p. 92] already in the fact that he calls the primal materials “seeds” (σπέρματα). Indeed, whether or not he also used the name homoeomeries, which in Aristotle is only biological, as examples of primal materials he names in any case organic ones, hair, flesh, bones (cf. fr. 10 beside Aristotle), whereas he explains precisely the inorganic elements only as mixtures. So Anaxagoras in fact explains the inorganic from the organic – more mystically than the mystics. He also, at bottom, professes the doctrine of universal ensoulment, if for him the air is a mixture of “seeds” by which the earth was fertilized for the formation of plants and animals. With this is connected the fact that he lets all animals breathe, even the fishes, whose gill-breathing he is said to have discovered. According to one report he also let plants breathe; at any rate he ascribed to them, like Empedocles and Democritus, life and sensation – these so-called mechanists and materialists are at once mystics and vitalists, like Fechner. Anaxagoras has plants feel pleasure when they grow, mourn when their leaves fall – the nature-mysticism of a Telesius and Campanella taught likewise. Plants and animals, however, naturally stand psychically behind man, that is, according to Anaxagoras: the νοῦς has communicated itself to them in lesser quantity. So he teaches the world-spirit like nature-mysticism. The preeminence of man he symbolizes again at the same time materially through the possession of the hand, which recurs in Cardanus. Fr. 4 and 20 show how man plays into his cosmic contemplation. But is it not clear enough why he makes the νοῦς the principle and lifts it out of the materials? Because he emphasizes man and man’s own highest force, the intellect (whereas Empedocles emphasizes the drives, in which man more easily feels himself one with the other beings of nature). And because nature as order shows him an intellectual countenance, and that means a human one, or at least one understood from man.

To append here briefly the most important point on Democritus, whom one believes wrested from the spell of mysticism: he states word for word the favourite dogma of the nature-[p. 93]mysticism of the Renaissance by calling man a microcosm (fr. 34). But still more finely he designates man as the point of departure of knowledge, by defining him as “that which is known to all” (164). He is a many-sided anthropologist and praises the ancients for having assigned the parts of the wonderful human body to gods. His admiration strengthens his anatomical zeal; he finds, with his teacher Leucippus, that nature does everything from ground and necessity. He grasps it, for he teaches its unity and rationality; he teaches, indeed, universal ensoulment, world-reason, the all-penetrating soul-matter from which we draw life and soul, and which does not let even the corpse wholly lack sensation and vital warmth. Is this materialist not at the same time a mystic, such as even the Renaissance could not have wished for better?

## Mystische Religiosität bei den antiken Naturphilosophen — Mystical Religiosity among the Ancient Philosophers of Nature

### Das verkannte religiöse Element — The Misjudged Religious Element

The last consideration yielded, for the old philosophers of nature, a lyrical and finally mystical swelling of self-feeling, then a genuinely mystical elevation of everything psychic and living in general, and finally an elevation of man, the most highly ensouled being, to world-significance, to the type of knowledge of nature, to the microcosm – in short, a humanization and thereby an ensoulment of nature. In sum, then, the unity of soul and nature resulted. But mysticism contains and demands more: the unity of both with God. Four unities showed themselves given therein (cf. p. 12f.): the unity of the soul with God, the unity of God with nature, the unity of nature with the soul, and finally, as a consequence of these, the unity of nature itself. Nature, the world, presents itself at first as a manifold that bears within itself no demand to be grasped as a whole, as a unity. The unity of the world results only when the world is conceived like the soul, which is in itself unitary, when God is conceived as world-soul, as the bond of world and soul; thus the unity of the world results only from the unity of God, soul, and world, and that means from mysticism. One cannot grasp the concept of nature, one cannot think nature, without having grasped it as a whole, as a unity to be comprehended precisely in the concept. The grasping of nature presupposes the grasping of its unity; the grasping of its unity stems from mysticism: hence the beginning of the knowledge of nature stems from mysticism.

It is clear enough: nature, with its motley variety, cannot be grasped as a unity out of itself; it can be so only when something else is grasped alongside it. Nature presents itself as a whole only when seen from an Other as standpoint; nature as such presents itself only as vis-à-vis the soul, as the great Other, the object over against the [p. 95] subject. Thus the subject, the soul, must grasp itself in order to grasp the object, nature. Hence the beginning of the philosophy of nature in the age of the lyrical I-feeling, of the mystical emphasis on the soul, of awakened human consciousness. But the mere alien confrontation of the psychic subject and the natural object does not suffice; it leaves nature uncomprehended as that which is foreign to the soul, and it unifies nature only negatively, over against the subject; for objects as such can be many. Only the subject, the soul, is unity in itself. Understanding, grasping nature as a unity, therefore demands that it be understood like the soul, and thereby demands a tertium in which soul and nature are united. The world can be understood after the pattern of the soul only if there is a world-soul. In God as world-soul, therefore, the first mystical grasp of nature is concentrated.

The grasping of the divine as the presupposition of the grasping of nature – this seems here merely a logical construction. Do the facts want to agree with it? In the Renaissance, certainly, but the Renaissance has behind it more than a millennium of the deepest religious education, which classical antiquity lacks. Should the Greeks have created philosophy precisely out of religious power, almost the only respect in which they trail the Orient, the very philosophy through which they became the people of Enlightenment and scepticism? Should the θεολόγοι [theologians] and φυσικοί [students of nature] still come together there? It is hazardous to regard a people of rich history like the Greeks, and a general spiritual phenomenon like religion, as fixed, uniform determinacies. Certainly the Greeks are the people of individualism, which turns critical. But there was for individualism a young period when it was still essentially positive, essentially subjectivism, when it was still more an intensification of feeling than of intellect, and there is for religion a form in which it takes individualism fully into itself, namely mysticism. In that strongly subjective, lyrical epoch of which we speak, the Greeks became religious and, so to speak, religion became Greek. The individual rose in feeling and religion sank into the individual, and [p. 96] precisely out of this contact of heaven and earth, of God and soul, mysticism arose. Awakening individualism deepens religion; it draws it into the soul. And so Hellas too, with the awakening of its individualities, with its Renaissance, had at the same time its Reformation. Certainly not comparable in depth, power, and effect to the Christian one, yet a movement that made souls swell and shudder with pious stirrings, deepened, spiritualized, and moralized the view of life of the sense-loving people, and let well up an idealism of feeling that needed only to be formed and consolidated intellectually and artistically – in short, a movement without which Hellas is unthinkable, because it first gave Hellas depth.

Hellenic mysticism was formerly too little noticed; more recently it has been drawn forth, with a certain predilection, as a broad, mighty undercurrent that connects the irreligious, intellectual Hellas with the faith and superstition of all ancient peoples. But what matters is not so much what is general and primordial in it as precisely what is Hellenic, late, and developed. The mysticism of which I speak is not that rank, all-human world of belief out of which Hellas worked its way intellectually, but a conscious re-attachment to it in a new, individualized, spiritual form. It is a return, but for that very reason something quite different from the primordial, because it is a deed of its own, spiritual and moral, a conscious turning back, a turning away from the given world, free restoration, Reformation. Resolve and deed of renewal show a different human being and are more important than what is renewed. The “mysticism” of primitive peoples differs from the later, conscious renewal as the pictorial language of primitive peoples differs from the individual art-poetry of the age of writing. As the latter is the first real art, so the former is the first real mysticism. As art is a free restoring of the original unity of feeling of the soul with an object, of the subject with the single object, so real mysticism is a free restoring of the original unity of the subject with the totality of objects in the [p. 97] feeling of the All. The freedom of the subject belongs to it, and therefore this mysticism comes only at a higher stage of development, therefore it comes precisely among the people that classically developed the freedom of personality, therefore precisely at the time when in Hellas the souls became free and the individualities awoke. The rich, clear world of representations of the Homeric poems, which had already differentiated itself so highly out of the dull feelings of primal mysticism, had to sink back again into the unity of feeling of the subject, so that conscious mysticism could arise. In the sixth century, when the Greek Middle Ages had died away, the new religious urge bursts forth with an almost sudden force, so that all for whom history is rectilinear progress toward “Enlightenment” stand baffled before an insoluble riddle. Mystery cult, hero cult, catharsis of every kind, Orphic and theogonic poetry fill the Greek world – and precisely in this epoch, simultaneously with this highest religious upsurge of Hellas, the philosophy of nature blossoms.

It would run counter to all probability, indeed possibility, counter to all historical spirit, to see no connection here. Now religious moments are indeed transmitted to us in the systems of the philosophy of nature; but these are still treated today essentially as weeds, as foreign intruders into the philosophical systems. Religion and philosophy are set over against each other as alien powers and the question is posed: has religion exercised influence on philosophy (which is, after all, the younger)? And now one finds chiefly a negative influence and, with Zeller (Ph. d. Gr. I5 48f.), thanks Greek religion for leaving Greek philosophy so free and independent. As positive influence one finds essentially the doctrine of the transmigration of souls, which came from the priests to the philosophers. “However, it is a question whether one should rate its philosophical significance highly in the earlier period. It is found indeed in Pythagoras and his school, whom Empedocles joins in this; Heraclitus also speaks of a higher life after death. [p. 98] But none of these philosophers has brought that doctrine into such a connection with his scientific assumptions that it would become an essential component of his philosophical system, but with all of them it runs alongside the scientific theory as an article of faith standing by itself, and no one would find a gap in the latter if it were missing” (ib. p. 65). And so Zeller finds in sum that “we can scarcely attribute to the mystery religion greater importance for the origin of Greek philosophy than to the public one” (op. cit.). But was there not perhaps in the old philosophers of nature themselves a religious impulse that led them to set up theological doctrines of their own? Whencesoever they came, it is a weighty fact that hardly in a single one of these philosophers of nature, even in the meagre fragments, are religious moments lacking. How has our scholarship dealt with this? Since Zeller co-determined in part the standpoint that has prevailed hitherto, but in any case gave it its most mature and clearest expression, it is fitting to survey his treatment of this old philosophy of religion.

The theology of the old Ionians is disposed of by finding the traditions that have them call their world-principle divinity more or less incredible (for Thales op. cit. p. 190ff., for Anaximander p. 218, for Anaximenes p. 243). Thales in particular is, in his religious belief, a Greek polytheist, in his philosophical view a pantheistic hylozoist (p. 192, 1)! – Greater difficulty was offered by the religious element in Pythagoreanism; but it is simply left outside as an unphilosophical element, however importantly it appears. “No other of the Pythagorean doctrines is better known, and none can be traced back with greater certainty to the founder of the school than the doctrine of the transmigration of souls and of survival after death” (p. 449). “But however important this belief undoubtedly was to the Pythagoreans, they seem to have connected it so little with their philosophical assumptions” (p. 454). Is this not strange? “Our dogma thus appears not at all as a component of the [p. 99] Pythagorean philosophy, but as a tradition of the Pythagorean mysteries, which, probably sprung from older, Orphic traditions, stands in no scientific connection with the philosophical principle of the Pythagoreans” (p. 455). Even in the souls of sun-motes, which are surely no worse than the Ionian souls of air and fire, there is to be sought not a philosopheme but only “simply a piece of Pythagorean superstition” (p. 453). “Strictly speaking, even their theological conceptions cannot be counted as belonging to the Pythagorean philosophy” (p. 458), since the Pythagoreans “in all probability likewise brought them into no scientific connection with their philosophical principle. That the idea of God nonetheless had the greatest importance for them as a religious idea cannot be doubted” (p. 456f.)! – And now the Eleatics! Xenophanes appears essentially as a critical opponent of the popular religion, and probably in a pantheistic sense (p. 536). Parmenides, to be sure, assigns to the world-governing divinity, the begetter of all gods and things, her seat in the middle of the world-whole. “But for his astronomical system this mythical figure has as little serious significance as the Eros produced by her, borrowed from the old cosmogonies” (p. 578). That Parmenides taught transmigration of souls or pre-existence is (despite his Pythagorean influences and despite Simpl. Phys. 39, 19!) improbable (p. 581). “One could more readily accept the report that Melissus declined every utterance about the gods. However, the witness is insufficient, and if Melissus really did say this, he wanted thereby – – to evade the awkward declaration about the relation of his view to popular belief” (618). – Heraclitus taught the most outspoken pantheism (672). With his physical views he brought “the mythical conceptions of life after death into a connection which was, to be sure, not demanded by his philosophical presuppositions” (709). Here he seems in part “to have retained the usual conceptions of Hades [p. 100] ” (713). It turns out that the assumption of immortality was “not grown out of his system, but carried into it from outside” (714). In some doctrines he connects with the Orphics (729). He attacks vigorously some conceptions and practices of the popular religion (727ff.), “but yet it does not seem that Heraclitus wanted to touch the popular religion in its existence as a whole” (727. 732). – Still more vacillating appears Empedocles. “However important to him this belief (in transmigration) and these precepts were for his person, they cohere with his philosophical system only partially inwardly, while from another side they unmistakably contradict it” (p. 809). He has “neglected to establish a scientific connection between his religious and physical doctrines, or even merely to demonstrate their compatibility” (810). “There remains therefore only the assumption that he took over the doctrine of the transmigration of souls, and what is connected with it, from the Orphic-Pythagorean tradition, without connecting these articles of faith scientifically with his philosophical convictions” (811). In his theological doctrines he connects in part with popular belief (813ff.), in part seeks to purify it (816). “But this purer belief in gods too lacks a scientific connection with his philosophical views” (816). “The more spiritual idea of God of our philosopher therefore stands beside his scientific views just as unmediated as the popular belief to which it first attaches, and we shall therefore be able to derive it not directly from those, but only from other grounds” (817). – Anaxagoras “has hardly gone into religion more closely,” and as to the relation of his astronomical assumptions to popular belief, he has “probably hardly expressed himself explicitly” (1018f.). Later witnesses assert “probably wrongly” that he designated the Nous as divinity (996). – Finally Democritus. “That he could not share the belief in gods of his people is evident” (936). “The divine in the [p. 101] proper sense is for him only nature.” “It is merely a matter of expression when for this, in popular language, the gods are put.” “In the gods of mythology, on the other hand, he could see only creations of fantasy” (which he explains in part from the impression of extraordinary natural phenomena). “However freely he stands over against popular belief, he nonetheless cannot resolve to declare everything that was told of appearances of higher beings and of their influence on men simply to be deception” (936f.). “In substance these (the idols) are nothing other than the demons of popular belief, and Democritus can in this respect be regarded as the first who, to mediate between philosophy and popular religion, took the path so common in later times of reducing the gods to demons.” “By no means did he think himself entitled to put himself in contradiction with the existing religion” (939). In some other assumptions too Democritus “first likewise follows popular belief more than his natural-scientific system, even though he subsequently seeks to reconcile them with it as well” (940).

To sum up, then, the conception of the religious element in the old philosophers of nature that has prevailed until now and has become classical in Zeller: this element is in part not present or insufficiently attested; where it is certainly present and attested, it stands more or less unmediated, indeed in part contradictory, beside the philosophical, as a mere dogma of faith, deriving from foreign sources, from attachment to Orphism or to popular belief; where it deviates from popular belief, it is simply a contesting of popular belief, in part left unspoken out of consideration, or its correction and purification, or a mediation between philosophy and popular belief. In sum, then, the religious element is in the philosophers of nature, if present at all, present only as negation or as concession, as contestation, correction, adaptation, mediation, in any case as something foreign, neither grown in the philosophies themselves nor organically bound up with them. [p. 102] Philosophy comports itself toward the religious as arch-indifferent or hostile or coolly hospitable, making room for it in a corner after some purification. At bottom the religious appears there as belonging to the gentlemen of the other faculty, with whom the philosophers actually want to have nothing to do, but whom they mostly “cannot bring themselves” to contradict altogether.

Over against this, the question now is whether the religious element in those philosophers of nature is not a necessary and original one, indeed perhaps even the original one, so that, conversely to hitherto, one must ask: not how they came from the philosophical to the religious, but how they came from the religious to the philosophical. Not only that the former is in any case earlier and that the latter is what has to be explained from something else in its origin: the high importance, emphasized by Zeller himself too, which the religious has for so many of these philosophers, simply cannot be reconciled with its being only a foreign appendage. Are the very first philosophers supposed to have been already so two-headedly complicated that faith and knowledge were separated in them? The “two truths” come as a late resignation after long struggle. Moreover, it points to a fundamental character that the religious, even in our meagre tradition of the philosophers of nature, not only appears at times very important, but also, as said, is nowhere wholly lacking.

### Das Absolute — The Absolute

Were the religious to be lacking in the last of them, this would certainly not have much to say about the origin and character of this philosophy of nature; but it would, were it lacking in the first. Yet now an Aristotelian testimony, recognized as reliable, reports of Thales that he taught: all things are full of gods. How does this dogma agree with the picture of Thales as a pure φυσικός [student of nature]? Th. Gomperz (Griech. Denker I2, p. 423) sees in it a remnant of fetishistic ways of intuition, a piece of most primitive nature-religion, and adduces Indian and other primitive peoples for [p. 103] comparison. I see in it no remnant but a beginning, the beginning of conscious nature-mysticism, which, to be sure, perhaps unconsciously re-attaches to primal mysticism, but by that very fact is already something else. What distinguishes it from the spirit-belief of primitive peoples is also the stronger feeling of totality. It is, after all, something else to scent spirits here and there and further everywhere, than to pronounce it and to render the general judgment: all is full of gods. This lies as far apart as the application and the pronouncement of the principle of identity. And that judgment stems just as little from induction as the principle of identity. Did Thales collect the gods in nature by counting them? A soul full of world-feeling belongs to this judgment, one that has directed the unity of its consciousness upon the whole (i.e., not upon the composite, the collected) of the world, a soul that has the All over against itself, full of courage to grasp it and to grasp it precisely after itself. For universal deification is bound up with universal ensoulment, as Aristotle intimates (de an. I 5. 411a 7), and as is also handed down together as a thesis of Thales: τὸν κόσμον ἔμψυχον καὶ δαιμόνων πλήρη [the cosmos ensouled and full of daimones] (Diog. L. I 27, cf. Diels Doxogr. 301). Since according to Aristotle he also declared the stone to be ensouled, he taught universal ensoulment, and he is in this as little a fetishist as Paracelsus or Fechner. The former in particular, who lets undines and sylphs, salamanders and gnomes weave their round dance through the whole of nature, plainly teaches the πάντα δαιμόνων πλήρη [all things full of daimones].

There is a clear coherence, indeed really only one sense, in the doctrines of Thales: all is full of gods or daimones, all is ensouled, living, all is in streaming motion, i.e., all is water, as the sea is indeed the cradle of the living (cf. p. 56). Universal ensoulment, universal animation explains both the pandaimonism and the water-principle and alone explains the compatibility of both, which otherwise stand side by side quite incomprehensibly. So long as one does not unite both doctrines, the water-principle remains uncomprehended. Only a soul full of feeling of life finds: all lives, and that means both: all is full of daimonic forces, and: all is [p. 104] flood, streams from an infinite source. Thales stresses the universal forces and sources of life. Daimonic, streaming life he feels as world-life out of his soul. Three propositions only does Thales utter: all is water, all is ensouled, all is full of the divine; but in these three propositions we have the basic doctrines of mysticism: the unity of nature, universal ensoulment, divine immanence. If we take the three propositions of Thales together, they yield the four mystical doctrines of unity: the unity of nature (all from one stuff and of psychic-daimonic force), the unity of soul and nature (universal ensoulment), the unity of soul and God (all ensouled = all full of the divine), the unity of God and nature (general divine immanence, not pantheistic-materialistic identity, is expressed by the πλήρη [full]). The first philosopher of nature is thus in all principal respects at once a fundamental mystic. Not only does Jacob Boehme speak of the clear water-spirit of divine love, the seed of all things; Helmont too, the pupil of Paracelsus and of Thomas a Kempis, unites the three propositions of Thales: water the primal element of things, the whole of nature ensouled, and God contained in all things.

The saying of Thales, all is full of the divine, then played a great role in late antiquity. Mysticism then independently savored this its basic thought in all its depth, yet Agrippa of Nettesheim, e.g., also expressly cites from the pagans the dictum: all is full of God. Let it not be said that the more mystical singular formulation belongs only to late antiquity, that Thales professes only a primitive belief in daimones. If this is only fetishistic superstition, then it sits in any case very deep in the old philosophy of nature; for Pythagoras too, Diog. L. VIII, 32, is credited with a very similar, and Heraclitus ib. IX, 7 with precisely the same dictum: all is full of souls and daimones. But the nature-mysticism of the Renaissance also has, as said, such pandaimonism, and it hindered Paracelsus just as little from speaking of the one, all-filling divine being as it did Heraclitus. We press from our [p. 105] present-day standpoint the opposition of monotheism and polytheism to a degree that for classical antiquity, and really for all mysticism (as opposed to supranaturalism), has no sense. The older philosophers of nature in particular stress the one divine all-being, and yet they, and likewise all later ones, speak at the same time also of the gods, without being aware of a contradiction. Does the All-force exclude the individual forces? They speak of gods and daimones as well as Paracelsus speaks of his nature-spirits or Boehme of his source-figures or Mothers, and yet both are especially deeply penetrated by the unity of the divine all-being. But precisely because it is the One in the sense of the total, all-embracing, absolute, not in the sense of the sole, the exclusive, it can bear relatively divine individual figures, indeed even demands them. The mystical All-divinity makes everything into gods. It is precisely the essence of mysticism that it deifies everything individual; every man too becomes to it a “little god,” and all things have religion, as Campanella says. Whoever is in justice, says Eckhart, is in God, indeed is himself God. God is for mysticism not only the One, nor only the whole of the world, but God is also all things, as each thing also is God, not merely a part of God. And therefore mysticism precisely, since for it God is not only one but also all, can also multiply God and could say both: all is full of gods or daimones, and: all is full of God.

Late antiquity too has made no distinction here for Thales; it has also had him speak of the one divinity as the world-reason that penetrates and forms everything (Plac. I 7, 11, Philop. de an. C 7, Athenag. suppl. c. 23, Cyrill. c. Jul. II p. 28 c, cf. Diels, Doxogr. p. 128. 301). One tends to distrust this tradition and to regard that doctrine of Thales as a late fiction, since it sounds all too Stoic. I myself, in “Sokrates” II 874ff., joined in this general distrust, yet already set forth two things in doing so: 1. that this doctrine stands in firm, closed connection with others attributed to Thales, [p. 106] 2. that this whole sequence of doctrines was brought forward as Thales’s doctrine not by the Stoa but already earlier, by its Cynic forerunner, as indeed Aristotle too already cites presentations of Thales (de an. 405a 19). Now I go one step further and assume that the Cynic too did not essentially invent these doctrines of Thales but received them as transmitted. Why does one want at all costs to see late invention in them? Because they are Stoic? But for that reason they can still be old-Ionian, as indeed the Stoa expressly follows the related doctrine of Heraclitus. Did we not have all too many fragments of him, I fear one would today declare the whole Heraclitus too a Stoic invention.

But that doctrine of Thales is supposed to be fiction, because for the thought of a world-soul, of a divine world-reason, there belongs a separation of the moving force from matter and its analogization with the human spirit, which were first brought up by Anaxagoras (Zeller p. 190. 192)? But did not Heraclitus already have, without such separation, the world-soul and God as world-reason? Only Cicero’s unfortunate expression (nat. deor. I 10, 25) could mislead one to understand the doctrine dualistically and therefore to deny it to Thales. And the analogy with the human spirit is not a late discovery, but lies, as has been shown, at the ground of the whole Greek philosophy of nature. Thales already pronounces this analogy as the ensoulment of all things. Anaximenes says it plainly: “As our soul, being air, holds us together, so breath and air encompass the whole universe” (fr. 2). Thus our soul is air, and what our soul is for us, air is universally for the world: one cannot teach the world-soul more plainly. He sets forth his air-principle as ground and primal force of the world, as infinite, all-moving, encompassing, soul-like holding everything together and of one essence with the soul – and he is supposed not to have called this his Absolute divinity?

I refer again to the nature-mysticism of the Renaissance. Joh. Baptist Porta, in his book on natural [p. 107] Magic, says: a universal world-spirit or breath of life binds all things, unites them and generates our souls too. And air is, according to Agrippa too, the all-penetrating, moving, binding, filling breath of life. Just as air fills everything, teaches Seb. Franck’s deep mysticism, and is nowhere absent or leaves anything empty and yet can be enclosed in no place, so is God – and everything enclosed in him. Agrippa (renewing Empedocles’ “four roots of all things”) praises also in Heraclitean fashion (like Boehme) of fire that it prevails everywhere and animates everything, and again that nothing could live and grow without water. As the waters of the river, says Reuchlin, spring from the source and as the sea receives them in its bosom, so God embraces the All. The Renaissance thinks, to be sure, in a purer theistic manner; yet one can understand the old philosophy of nature best from its nature-mysticism, and one has, as said (p. 59. 62f. 84), every reason to grasp the elemental principles of the old Ionians as at once real and symbolic (cf. further Heraclitus fr. 67 θεὸς ὅκωσπερ πῦρ [God is like fire]). It is the deep-rooted fundamental error of the previous conception that the Ionians seek the world-stuff. They seek the world-force, which they typically picture and make intuitable in a stuff, and which they at the same time conceive, after the force known to them in their own soul, as world-soul and so as divinity. That Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes speak of an indeterminately large number of world-gods speaks not against but rather for the assumption of an All-divinity that unfolds itself creatively in the individual forces, as Empedocles too calls both the Sphairos God and its members, the elements, and also designates the forces Love and Hate as gods. The simultaneous unity and multiplication of the divine lies, as said, in the essence of mysticism.

With what right, then, does one doubt that the older Ionians spoke of the one world-divinity as the all-principle? The ancients report it not only of Thales, but also of Anaximander (Aristot. Phys. 3,4 p. 203b 13) and of Anaximenes (Doxogr. 302b 5. 531a 17. b 1f.). They give their principle all the properties that belong only to divinity.

[p. 108] For Anaximander too declares his principle not only as the all-embracing infinite and the beginning of all things, but also as “all-steering” primal force and as eternal and “immortal” – and E. Rohde is right: “Immortal and divine are interchangeable concepts; the essential predicate of the god and only of the god is precisely immortality.” The single fragment of Anaximander teaches, in the most literal sense, a moral world-order – without a world-divinity? Xenophanes’ whole doctrine is only the proclamation of the one world-divinity, Heraclitus teaches the All-divinity as world-reason, Pythagoreanism teaches us through the mouth of Philolaus: All is enclosed by the divinity as in a custody – and only the old Ionians are not to be allowed to teach the world-divinity, in spite of the parallels, in spite of the testimonies, and in spite of the sense of their doctrines, which lay upon the Absolute the divine predicates and functions? The sense of the whole older philosophy of nature is not hylozoism, nor pantheism, if one understands by this that they conceive the stuff, the world, as animated and divine, but their doctrine is that of mysticism, panentheism. God or the world-principle is to them more than the world. For Pythagoreanism the quotation just given attests literally panentheism: the world enclosed by God! The founder of Eleaticism teaches so zealously the one world-divinity that over it the motley world sank away and was denied by his successors. And the Ionians (Heraclitus included) teach indeed the periodic passing away of the world, the taking back of the world into the divine world-principle.

Yes, the world is “mortal” (cf. Empedocles fr. 22. 119), but the longing for the immortal Absolute dominates especially the older philosophy of nature. It celebrates this divine Absolute hardly less exuberantly than the mysticism of the Renaissance. The eternally welling divine Chaos of Boehme, his divine Un-ground and primal ground, opens itself already in Anaximander’s inexhaustibly generating (and taking back) divine Indeterminate, also a unity of opposites, as which indeed Heraclitus (fr. 67), just like Cusanus, Weigel and Boehme, explains God, as also [p. 109] Paracelsus teaches: God is the ground of all things, all primal beginnings are enclosed in the great mystery, in Chaos, but there is need of opposition that they may step forth. God, teaches Heraclitus, is day and night, hunger and satiety, and the ethical opposites that men see disappear in him, for whom everything is beautiful, good, and just (fr. 67. 102). Names fail Heraclitus, as they do Cusanus, for this divine primal being, which, at once Zeus and not Zeus, at once Hades and Dionysus, can “at will” be named with all possible names (fr. 15. 32. 67), as in the Renaissance Mutianus Rufus taught: there is one God and one Goddess, but many names: Jupiter, Sol, Apollo, Moses, Christ, Luna, Ceres, Proserpina, Tellus, Mary – yet this must be shrouded in silence like the mysteries. Precisely the mystical conception is able to name God also in the feminine, as Parmenides makes Aphrodite the world-divinity (fr. 12f.) and similarly Empedocles (fr. 17, 24), exalting Love as world-force. Similarly Heraclitus too praises the world-order as Δίκη [Justice] or, as the Pythagoreans, Apollo, as force of the world-harmony. One may, according to Heraclitus, name God with all possible names, because he appears different, transforms himself (fr. 67) – like God in Boehme and other mystics. One may from the mystical standpoint, as the old philosophers of nature do, now unify God, now multiply him, now call him the one world-force, now all elements, all stars, indeed everything individual God or divine; the divine totality remains preserved; one may, like them, cite all the gods of the people, for they are potencies of life; one may only not do one thing from the mystical standpoint: restrict God, determine God, banish God into any shape and singularity – this goes against the living feeling of the All of mysticism.

One misunderstands Xenophanes if one sees in his protests against the plastic humanization of God only a protest against anthropomorphism or only a pure spiritualization of God. The Xenophanean God, who is also “all eye” and “all ear” and “abides in the same place,” who above all according to Aristotle and Theophrastus [p. 110] is one with the world-whole, is by no means purely spiritual, and the human figure of God is made ridiculous precisely by the fact that the animal figure is found just as justified and plausible for him (fr. 15). Not the bodily and not the human as such is opposed to God, but the restriction to mortal form is contrary to the nature of the Absolute. The initiate, full of God, can tear God to pieces in every image, because none suffices him. Every image is dead to him compared with the God whom he feels overflowingly alive in soul and All. Understand here Xenophanes, Heraclitus and Empedocles from the iconoclasts of the Reformation age. Agrippa too rails against the godlessness that lies in setting dead, mute images on the altars, lighting them with wax candles and adoring them. “They pray to these images of the gods,” says Heraclitus fr. 5, “as if one were to talk with the walls. They do not know at all the true nature of the gods and heroes.” One cannot, says Empedocles fr. 133f., bring the Godhead near to oneself in visibility and tangibility, as men otherwise convince themselves of something, for the Godhead has not head, arms, legs, it is an all-penetrating world-spirit. He also speaks of the members of the world-God, of the Sphairos. The sphere is the bodily form of the Absolute also for the Eleatics.

There lies in the Absolute a twofold: the One All-embracing and the highest, perfect, thus the Absolute in being and the Absolute in value. The exclusive emphasis on the first meaning would yield pantheism, on the second theism; panentheism unites both, and just this is given in the old philosophers of nature. God is for them not only the one principle of being, the All-One, but at the same time the highest. Naively it comes out in Xenophanes: One single God there is, greatest among gods and men, neither in shape nor in thought comparable to mortals (fr. 23). The single God and the greatest – an apparent contradiction, but mystical panentheism, which conceives God on the one hand ontologically as total, on the other superlatively, resolves it. Xenophanes did not deny [p. 111] the many gods of whom he also speaks elsewhere (Freudenthal’s demonstration holds good here); he has denied them next to God as the World-One as little as he has denied men and things next to the World-One. The Absolute has for him not only total but also gradual significance, not only significance of being but also of value, i.e., significance for feeling. It is not only the One of the world but also the ideal of the world. Indeed, the second meaning is probably more important to him. From the perfection of God, who could have no power outside himself, and for whom it “is not fitting to wander hither and thither,” he grasps the unity and unchangeability of the world. Xenophanes is, after all, more a θεολόγος [theologian] than a φυσικός [student of nature], and the whole of Eleaticism is explicable only from this theological origin. The monstrous paradox of the denial of all multiplicity and changeability, the bare, empty unity of being, would be unbearable if there did not accrue to it, as in Spinoza, content of feeling, religious ideal predicates, which it bears itself even in Parmenides and Melissus (see p. 84 and cf. Gomperz op. cit. p. 150). Eleaticism stems from the deepest mysticism, which sacrifices the world for God.

Almost exactly the Eleatic predicates are attributed to God also by the Philolaus fragment 20. The Pythagorean, ib. fr. 11, says that number is effective in divine and daimonic things, and fr. 20 has the Pythagorean “sacred word,” and Philolaus in the Bacchae, teach theology through mathematics, which Cusanus, Reuchlin and all the other number-mystics of the Renaissance repeat. Precisely the quantitative conception of the world naturally becomes a graded one; for the Pythagoreans numbers are values. But Heraclitus too builds steps and praises God as the highest: the most beautiful man, he says, is to God as the most beautiful ape is to man (fr. 82f.), and man stands as a minor under God as the child under the man (79). With God all is beautiful, good and just (102). Because for them God is the absolute ideal, they all contend – “Pythagoras” (Diog. L. VIII 21) as well as Xenophanes (fr. 1. 11f.), Heraclitus (fr. 5. 14f. 42. 69 etc.) as well as Empedocles (fr. 128. 137) – so [p. 112] passionately against immoral cult and immoral myths, which Homer and Hesiod in particular have spread about the gods.

The two meanings of the Absolute, the ontological and the normative, the meaning for the world and the meaning for value, are united in the Godhead as immanent order of the world, as a governing, rational, all-pervading force of the world. The Ionians profess it: Anaximander’s fragment proclaims the moral order of the world, Anaximenes proclaims the all-directing, all-embracing air as the world-divinity, Heraclitus speaks most loudly and most beautifully of the divine law, of the fiery God as world-reason, which pervades, directs and will judge all things. “For everything that creeps is driven to pasture with God’s scourge” – so Diels translates fr. 11. None remains hidden, indeed, from the Imperishable, fr. 16 admonishes, morally enough. The mind of man plans not far, but the divine mind does (fr. 78). Follow God, runs the oft-repeated maxim of the Pythagoreans, who at the same time declare God to be harmony. But more: they posit the central fire as the fundamental force and support of the world, as “mother of the gods,” as “citadel of Zeus” or “altar,” around which the stars dance in solemn order, singing: the cosmos, then, a sanctuary, a well-ordered divine service! Besides this, the God of Xenophanes “sets the All a-swing effortlessly with the force of the mind’s reason” (fr. 25), and in the midst of the parmenidean world of the senses stands the goddess who knows all, directs all and joins all in pairs (fr. 1. 12), and the Godhead of Empedocles speeds with swift thoughts through the whole universe (fr. 134), in which the law of the world is spread out broadly (135), and which in the play of its elements fulfils a divine order of penance (115). Finally, Diogenes of Apollonia and Anaxagoras teach the world-moving, immanent, all-knowing force of reason, appearing as material in both, only in Anaxagoras unmixed with other substances. The later authorities report that he designated this Nus as Godhead; Zeller doubts it, but only because earlier mentions, which incidentally characterize it as a deus ex machina, content themselves with the more characteristic, more specific [p. 113] name Nus; even Zeller admits that “the Nus in point of substance at any rate corresponds to the Godhead.” In sum, then, the material (more correctly: at the same time material) conception of the divine essence of the world does just as little to abolish the strongly religious character of the older systems of nature as it does in the case of the Stoa or of Tertullian.

### Inspiration — Inspiration

The panentheism that mysticism proclaims – God embracing all things, all things from God and God in all things – is thus given in the older philosophy of nature, but indeed already in Orphism: Ζεὺς κεφαλή (or ἀρχή), Ζεὺς μέσσα, Διὸς δ᾽ ἐκ πάντα τέτυκται [Zeus the head (or beginning), Zeus the middle, from Zeus all things have been made] (Orph. fragm. 46 ed. Abel). It seems to me the beginning of a breakthrough to a correct conception of the old philosophy of nature that people have lately been searching so eagerly for Orphic influences upon it. They found them, leaving aside the all too taciturn Thales, already at the beginnings of philosophy: already in Anaximander’s fragment on the penance of the world (cf. Diels, ein orph. Demeterhymnus, Festschrift für Gomperz, p. 1). Pythagoras, according to Rohde, already experienced the influence of the Orphic communities he found in southern Italy, and Pythagoreanism is in any case inseparably entangled with Orphism and unthinkable without it. Diels and Otto Kern in particular have opened our eyes to what is Orphic in Heraclitus, in Parmenides, in Empedocles. An ancient tradition that made Orpheus the oldest philosopher even had Anaxagoras take his doctrine of the original mixture of all substances from mystical poetry (Diog. Laert. proöm. 4f.). One will not be surprised that the mysticism of the Renaissance also returns to this love, as e.g. Marsilius Ficinus wants to follow Orpheus, and Pico of Mirandola ascribes to the hymns of Orpheus a greater effect than to any bodily force. They had before them, to be sure, an “Orpheus” overlaid with additions of late antiquity, but just as recent finds have dated many a “late” thing astonishingly far back, and as the rhapsodic Theogony [p. 114] too has probably been rightly (cf. especially Dümmler, Kl. Schr. II pp. 155ff.) transferred from Hellenistic-Roman times to the century of the first philosophers of nature, so, in general – this is a chief result of this whole investigation – the early Greek age was spiritually far more similar to religious late antiquity than our historical rationalism ever dreamed.

Yet I will resist the temptation to pursue the Orphic “influences” in the old philosophy of nature any further, since I cannot altogether follow these newer endeavours, not because I do not see the strong points of contact between Orphism and the philosophy of nature, but because I see them only too well to stress “influences” as though the two had been strangers from the beginning. To put it briefly, the older philosophers of nature seem to me in a certain sense Orphics themselves, in any case far too much of one spirit with the Orphics to need their influences, which were certainly often present, but are surely reciprocal and are more often still explained rather as natural agreements of kindred spirits. It is a fact of fundamental importance: Orphism and the philosophy of nature flower at the same time, in the sixth century, are children of one spirit of the age! Because they are of Orphic spirit, Xenophanes, Parmenides and Empedocles write their philosophy as poetry. A theological rhapsode founds one of the three old schools of the philosophy of nature, the Eleatic. The second, Pythagoreanism, is precisely in its unity with Orphism, in its whole substance, one single protest against the separation of the θεολόγοι and φυσικοί [theologians and physicists]. Philolaus appeals to “the old theologians and seers” (fr. 14). What is told of Pythagoras, the “son of Apollo,” as later of Empedocles, in the way of tales of wonder (presence in several places at once, raising of the dead, unnatural death and the like) has its parallels in the “magicians” Agrippa and Paracelsus. Only the oldest, the Ionian school, is supposed to lack the hieratic element? But their home, Miletus (like the native city of Xenophanes), surely not for nothing had within its territory a famous oracle of Apollo and [p. 115] in the Branchidae a powerful priestly family. And Ephesus above all was a religious centre, and Heraclitus belonged there to a family rich in tradition, in which the supervisory office over the Eleusinian mysteries was inherited. Here one should also look for his spiritual origin.

It will be objected that he rebukes the magicians, Bacchants, mystae, the whole swarm of enthusiasts and expiatory cult (fr. 5. 14f.) and the mystical syncretism of Pythagoras. But Pico della Mirandola, Agrippa, Kepler also rebuke astrological superstition, Agrippa also the kabbalistic game of allegories, and yet they profess magic, or a mystical conception of the world. Heraclitus rebukes the mystical cult because it is not mystical enough for him, because they “do not know the true nature of the gods and heroes” (fr. 5), because the mystical consecrations take place “in an unholy manner” (14). And he threatens the mystae – with mysticism, with the punishment after death, with the fiery judgment (ib.)<sup>16</sup>. The orgies would be shameful if they were not celebrated for Dionysus, who (for the mystic cf. p. 68 and below) is one with the god of death (fr. 15), and one may read in Rohde how it is deeply rooted in the cult of Dionysus that he is also “Lord of the souls” in the underworld. Heraclitus wants to be mystical and professes himself a prophet. The lord of the Delphic oracle, who speaks mysteriously (fr. 93), is his model, and the Sibyl, who with raving mouth utters things unlaughed-at and unrouged and unperfumed, and whose voice reaches through a thousand years by virtue of the god (92). Thus he professes the μανία [madness] of the inspired, the God-filledness.

And you, much-celebrated, white-armed maiden Muse, I implore, send from Piety the pliant chariot” etc. (fr. 4). “Know how the revelations of our Muse command” (5). “Know this clearly, since you have heard the discourse (through me) from God” (23). “If you, immortal Muse, have turned your mind to our striving, then stand by me again now as I entreat, since I want to proclaim a good discourse about the blessed gods” (131). What does Jacob Boehme say? “There my spirit broke through the gates of hell, into the innermost birth of the Godhead, and was there embraced with love as a bridegroom embraces his bride.”

We are not yet done with inspiration and vision among the Presocratics. Even the last great one of the philosophers of nature, the materialist Democritus, teaches both; he stresses the necessity of divine inspiration for the poet, he teaches the prophetic, visionary significance of dreams, he teaches the epiphany of the gods as real [p. 117] beings who reveal themselves visionarily to men, acting upon them in prophecy, in benefaction and in ruin, so that he wished to receive happy visions. Zeller says: Democritus could not “bring himself” to declare all tales of apparitions and effects of higher beings simply to be delusion. But Goethe says: “How especially the ancients must have been endowed with these idols can be inferred from Democritus’ doctrine of idols; he can have arrived at it only from his own living experience.” And again we find the same doctrine of the images of real beings, which as aerial mirage exercise prophetic and sympathetic effects at a distance, in the mysticism of the Renaissance, in Agrippa.

But to bring just one great parallel from that age for the treatment of cosmology as an inspired hymn to God, I cite Kepler, who, like the Pythagoreans, not only lets the sun as Apollo listen eternally to the planet-Muses, but also proclaims: ὁ θεὸς ἀεὶ γεωμετρεῖ [God always geometrizes], and with the discovery of the harmony of the world feels himself a priest of the living God, an organ of His revelation. He says he is raising a holy discourse and a true hymn for God, to whom it is the sweetest sacrificial incense when a man investigates His omnipotence, wisdom and goodness and proclaims them to others. “I may in holy rapture exult before mortals with the confession that I have taken the golden vessels of the Egyptians, to build from them, far from the borders of the Egyptians, an altar to my God.” “But when we adapt our voice to the ideal melody and begin a song not heard before, then we imitate God, who is harmony itself (as with the Pythagoreans) and everywhere presents an image of His essence. The measure of things in the divine spirit from eternity gives the pattern of the order of the world and passes with the likeness of God to man. But because God lives in all things and so everything is a symbol for Him, therefore Plato and Pythagoras taught us much that is wonderful about the nature of the immortal essence in the image of numbers and lines.” And at the close of the work he prays: “O You, [p. 118] who by the light of nature arouse in us the longing for the light of grace, in order to raise us into the light of glory, to You I give thanks, Creator and Lord, that You let me exult over Your works. – Praise the Lord, you heavenly harmonies, and you who recognize the harmonies discovered! Praise also, my soul, your God, as long as I live! For from Him, through Him and in Him is all, the sensible as well as the spiritual, what we know and what we do not yet know; for there is still much to do.”

The gods have not revealed everything to mortals from the beginning, but let them discover it gradually, says Xenophanes (fr. 18), and he finds human wisdom limited especially concerning the gods (34. 36). Alcmaeon too is penetrated by this (fr. 1), and Heraclitus and Empedocles also complain that the truth about the divine is so hard to convey to men, because it is a matter of faith and trust (Heraclitus fr. 5. 18. 28. 86, Emped. 114. 132. 133), because it is unfathomable and inaccessible (H. 18) and unutterable (E. 134). For the old philosophers of nature the divine is mystery; it is reached not with the easily teaching understanding but with visionary feeling, and thereby the philosopher became one with the religious poet, with the Orphic.

In Orphism we have lyric poetry widened from the individual feeling to the feeling of the All, thus mystically widened and thereby cosmic. In Pindar lyric poetry shows its deep inclination to Orphism, professes also the All-divinity (cf. e.g. fr. 140), for which it had been receptive much earlier and probably first of all precisely in its originally so strongly religious character (cf. Terpander fr. 4). Precisely religious subjectivity conceives itself over against the All-divinity, just as precisely the soft, wild feeling also conceives itself over against strict objective necessity. Only he who knows the opposite too grasps necessity and law. And for that reason lyric poetry first proclaimed so loudly universal necessity, the ᾿Ανάγκη [Necessity] (see also Bacchylides X 46. 72. XX 1), and Pindar sang of the divine Νόμος [Law]. Even before him Orphism [p. 119] raised Δίκη and Νόμος [Justice and Law] to world-significance. So again the philosophy of nature, which stresses both especially in Heraclitus, Parmenides and Empedocles, proceeds, precisely in setting up a cosmos ordered by law and necessity, in the Orphic-lyrical spirit, just as indeed even the name Cosmos probably derives from the Orphic language of images.

Mysticism loves images and puns; in Pherecydes it makes Ζεύς into Ζάς [Zeus into Zas], in order to be able to interpret him, as Heraclitus too does, as the highest principle of life (cf. Gomperz p. 70). Genuinely mystical vitalism is displayed also by the Orphic rhapsodic Theogony, in that it lets the All arise from the cosmic egg. The world as an order felt to be alive is the world as harmony, and here the spirit of Orphism was decisive, since everything that attaches to Orpheus is rooted in music. And the old intimate connection of Greek music with religious cult is well known, and in ancient Miletus precisely it was cultivated earnestly and with dignity (Athen. XIV 625 B). The Pythagorean cosmos is song and dance of the stars, and music and dance are important especially in the Dionysian cult at its old night festivals in free mountain nature. The night, whose beauty early lyric poetry describes (Alcman fr. 60), the night, the divine first principle of Orphic cosmogonies, has always been the friend of mysticism. It is night in which the stars shine. Is not in this too the hour of birth of mysticism also that of astronomy?

### Eschatologie — Eschatology

The mystical cults are those of Demeter (and Kore) and of Dionysus, the cult of the two goddesses and the cult to which, from of old, precisely female ecstasy, Maenadism, belongs. A strong character of feeling is not only proper to it but is its essence. The mystical divinities are the divinities of the natural impulse, of fertility; but they are more. Heraclitus reconciles himself in fr. 15 with the Dionysian orgiasm, with the phallic song, because Dionysus is at the same time Hades. The mystical divinities [p. 120] are at the same time divinities of death. The bond of birth and death is sanctioned in their cult. The constant parallelizing of wedding and death in Greek poetry and art is the immediate expression of daily religious experience, says v. Wilamowitz<sup>18</sup>.

<sup>18</sup> Herakles II 147, cf. also Diels, Sibyllin. Blätter p. 48, 2.

The mystical cults above all bring to the Greeks the spirit of transformation to consciousness; they present an action as mystery – Demeter lamenting and finding again, Persephone vanishing and reappearing, Dionysus torn to pieces and born again! It is a transformation of joy and sorrow, of arising and passing away, a transformation therefore in the soul as well as in nature – and yet at the same time a fate of the gods felt religiously. Erwin Rohde is right: the action of the Eleusinian mysteries was felt by the believers at first not as a symbolic but as a holy action, taking place upon the Godhead – and yet it concerned not merely the Godhead but drew in the souls of the men who took part in the mystery and lived it with it, promising them in the end, in connection with Persephone’s fate, a blessed lot after death. Rohde may also be right in this: Dionysus (the specifically mystical god) is originally soul-god rather than nature-god, the god of the ecstatic uplift of the soul – but the general excitation of the soul comes with the great transformation of nature, expresses itself in bacchantic revelling through nature, gives its god satyrs and similar nature-spirits for company, and comes with the ripening wine, through the enjoyment of which it is heightened and which it gives to the god as symbol. Whether they were so originally or not, Demeter and Dionysus in any case become divinities of nature and precisely of that nature with which man comes into traffic, which dispenses fruits to him for his labour and thereby regulates his life. And at the same time they are divinities of the soul, promising blessedness and “deification” in ecstatic uplift. God, soul, nature are simultaneously involved in the mystical cult. Epiphany and aphany of the Godhead, exultant uplift and lamenting impotence of the soul, flowering and [p. 121] withering of nature together form an ever-recurring transformation. God, soul, nature one in the rhythm of transformation – that is indeed the fundamental doctrine of mysticism, and it became clear to the early mystical thinkers thus already from the mystical cults.

The unity of life of God, soul, nature is now, however, painfully disturbed by the fact that the soul cannot keep pace with the infinite life of the two total powers, God and nature. The Godhead as such is immortal. Nature presents itself as constant recurrence; but the ensouled man shows, in visibility, a single, brief existence; yet the essence of mysticism is the idea of the unity of life of God, soul, nature, and so it demands here the adaptation of the soul to the two infinite powers and thus gives to the soul what the Godhead has and what nature has, immortality and eternal recurrence. The old philosophy of nature teaches both. It is said that the eschatological doctrines are foreign additions, inappropriate concessions of the philosophers to theology. Such consideration for popular belief could be understood, but also for Orphic private cults? And besides, the primary and central position of these doctrines, especially in some systems, would be incomprehensible. One could far sooner assert the reverse: the mystical longing for eternal life has created this whole philosophy. In any case I recognize in the doctrine of the transmigration of souls a quite necessary foundation of the old philosophy of nature; for transmigration is the form in which man first lives himself wholly into nature, finds himself wholly again in it, recognizes alien beings as akin to himself, as possible forms of his own existence. Only thereby is nature brought wholly close to man’s interest, only thereby has man himself also entered into nature, in that he participates in the cycle of its transformation.

Nature is alive like the human soul, so those ancients saw it; yet they found the life of nature to be eternal recurrence, a cycle. But, says Alcmaeon (fr. 2), “men perish because they cannot join the beginning [p. 122] to the end.” Yet, says Heraclitus (fr. 103), “on the circumference of a circle beginning and end are common.” The circle is the mystical ideal figure, which that old astronomy therefore finds again everywhere. The old philosophers of nature feel nature very intensely as an infinite cycle of becoming, indeed, they frequently heighten this rhythm of transformation to the eternal alternation of the coming-to-be and passing-away of worlds. The “way up” and the “way down” in Heraclitus intertwine, like the processes of the union and the sundering of the world in Empedocles (cf. further, specially for this cycle of becoming, Emp. fr. 17, v. 13). Life and death pass unceasingly into one another; from the living comes the dead, but also from the dead the living (cf. especially Heraclitus fr. 26. 76, Empedocles fr. 9. 15. 125).

In truth, however, for the old philosophers of nature there is no death, but only transformation. The death of one form is only the reviving of another. “Fire lives the death of air, and air lives the death of fire; water lives the death of earth, and earth that of water” (Heraclitus fr. 76). For souls it is pleasure or death to become wet. The pleasure, however, consists in their entry into life. Elsewhere, however, he says: “We live their death, the souls’, and they live our death” (fr. 77). “For souls it is death to become water, for water death to become earth.” “From earth comes water, from water soul” (fr. 36). So the soul is clearly drawn into the cycle of nature. Nothing dies and nothing arises, everything is transformed. The doctrine of transmigration of the old philosophers of nature is most closely connected with their fundamental denial of all arising and passing away; with this basic dogma they teach eternal life. Souls vanish only to be born again in another shape; rebirth, palingenesis, is, as Rohde shows, the oldest and commonest name for transmigration. And here too the nature-mysticism of the Renaissance agrees, especially Agrippa and Paracelsus teach: there is no death, all dying is rebirth, and the tales of the transmigration of souls have [p. 123] good sense. The moriens renasci [dying, to be born again] Kepler proclaims in a poem as longing and sure hope.

But the cycle recurring manifoldly in nature obtains absolute character only, indeed is fully understood as a cycle at all only, by being brought into relation to the absolute principle itself, to the Godhead. Thales is already said to have said: God is the oldest, for He is unbecome, i.e. He is the beginning of all becoming. One is accustomed to doubt the authenticity of this surely very old saying, but precisely the earliest philosophy seeks the ἀρχή [first principle], the oldest, the beginning of all things. Yet it seeks at the same time the end of all things, positing beginning and end as one in the Absolute. And so the immediate successor of Thales, Anaximander, already explicitly teaches that that from which things arise and that into which they pass away are one. Only in the Absolute is the world completed as a cycle, and Anaximander already called his Absolute Godhead, as Orphism too had already called Zeus the beginning and centre of all things. And again we must bring in the mysticism of the Renaissance: Reuchlin calls God the First and the Last; Suso, Boehme and other mystics call God a circle. “Therefore all goings-out are for the sake of the returnings-in, therefore the course of heaven is the noblest and most perfect, because it most properly begins again in its origin from which it springs. So too the course of man is the noblest and most perfect, for it goes most properly into its origin.” So speaks the mysticism of Tauler, and thus in its own way draws the human soul into the divine cycle of nature.

The perpetuation of the human soul means its becoming God just as much as its becoming nature. To be God and to be immortal are, as said, interchangeable concepts. Thales is said to have been the first to teach immortality, a doctrine which the moderns indeed again dispute, but which agrees well enough with pandaimonism and the universal ensoulment<sup>19</sup>. Heraclitus, further, speaks often enough of life after death. Pythagoreanism is full of doctrines of immortality and transmigration. Xenophanes (fr. 7) and [p. 124] Parmenides (Simpl. phys. 39, 19) mention them, and Empedocles makes them his central dogma.

<sup>19</sup> Incidentally, the water principle too could be justified mystically: first, water is an excellent image of the cycle and eternal rhythm; second, mystical enthusiasm is bacchantically heightened by wine, which Empedocles too interprets as water (fr. 81, 91); third, water, as Agrippa in particular praises it, is the mystical means of purification, of expiation (cf. Rohde, Psyche p. 362¹), hence also of a restoration of the original; yet I will [p. 154] concede that such mystical associations can at most have hovered before Thales only incidentally.

But it is now important that the doctrines lead not merely to a parallel but to a unity: the human soul becomes immortal and eternal not only like God and nature, but as divine being and as natural being. In transmigration men do not always become men, but also various animals (whereby lyric poetry, in its depiction of the characters of animals, may again have stimulated speculation). Man becomes plant too in Empedocles; the soul becomes water in Heraclitus and then further elements. But all this is descent of the soul; the ascent leads it to the gods, and here the old philosophy of nature shows itself most genuinely as mysticism: in the deification of man, even if this deification is conceived differently, more outwardly, than in Christian mysticism. The mystical cult-doctrine of the Greeks already raised the souls of the pure to be table-companions of the gods. Pythagoreanism above all declares not only men to be “images of the Godhead,” but also makes expressly the ethical demand upon man “to follow God and to become as like Him as possible.” Pythagoras is already said to have said: “we become best when we go to the gods.” Heraclitus sees the bridge between heaven and earth, the transition between man and God, still more closely. War makes for him “some gods, others men” (fr. 53). “Immortals mortal, mortals immortal: they live each other’s death and die each other’s life” (fr. 62). Parmenides rises in his great opening vision to the heights of the immortals. Above all, however, Empedocles paints how guilty daimones sink down from the divine height into the mortality of men and lower beings, until, purified in long transmigration and ascending again, they finally become, from higher men, immortal gods (see fr. 112. 115. 146. 147).

The world-process is there everywhere, in Anaximander as in Heraclitus, in the Pythagoreans as in Empedocles, a [p. 125] moral one. It is, more or less distinctly, a sinking down from the divine first principle into the material, mortal particular, and a return of the finite fullness of the world into the eternal, divine primal One, at bottom a self-estrangement, self-unfolding, self-development, transformation of God, such as mysticism also taught in the times of Boehme and Schelling. In Boehme as in Schelling the theogonic process is of world-significance. Theogonic poetry is the forerunner and companion of the old Greek philosophy of nature, and it agrees with it in the basic mystical thought of the unfolding of the world from the divine, of the divine succession of becoming. Theogonic poetry has something deeply stimulating for knowledge of nature: it has sanctified transformation and succession, it has indeed even several times made “Chronos” a first principle and thus anticipated the significance of the concept of time, and it has awakened the sense of causality, if in the most naively vitalistic sense: the development of the world as a succession of births – that is, as the name says, the original “genetic” conception.

So, in sum, a wealth of concepts has flowed to knowledge of nature from mysticism, or at least from community with it: the world as unity and as infinity, the world as unity of manifoldness, as harmonious system, the world as order, lawfulness and necessity, the world as unfolding of force and life and, in opposition to this, as matter and elements, in sum as object (“Gegenwurf,” cf. pp. 19. 45. 87) over against the subject, the world as transformation and cycle, as causal succession, as development, as descendence and ascendence, in short, it is probably the supreme basic concepts, the whole basic schema of all knowledge of nature, that was given in mysticism. Knowledge of nature has developed ever further out of mysticism; it has become ever more differentiated, more specialist, more mechanistic – but mysticism demands unity and life. Knowledge of nature<sup>20</sup> has turned ever further away from mysticism, and it should, and yet it has twice already returned to the mysticism from which it sprang as old Greek philosophy of nature, [p. 126] and precisely in the times of the run-up to the highest ascent: in the Renaissance and at the beginning of the nineteenth century. Should there not lie a law in this? Knowledge of nature must in its development remove itself ever further from the central mysticism, become, as it were, ever more peripheral, and yet it must, and precisely in its highest progress, which always comes as rebirth, preserve most deeply and most consciously the connection with its mystical roots, with those subjective and anthropomorphic, vitalistic and panentheistic, in short idealistic sources from which it draws ever new strength when it threatens to become externalized in the mechanical, impoverished in the specialized.

<sup>20</sup> Against the conclusion of the investigation that now follows, Wilhelm Ostwald, in his “Annalen der Naturphilosophie,” has raised “contradiction from point to point.” This protest did not come to me unexpectedly. It is necessary and instructive to examine the ground and force of the objections of one who today perhaps most loudly unfolds the philosophy of nature as a programme and has most energetically compelled its renewed attention. “The assertion that knowledge of nature ‘sprang’ from the ancient Greek philosophy of nature is obviously quite untenable.” I do not say so either, but I say, by way of restriction: knowledge of nature, as ancient Greek philosophy of nature, sprang from mysticism, or rather from communion with it. But, Ostwald continues, “long before, e.g. among the Egyptians, there was a developed knowledge of nature.” Is this supposed to have been unknown to me? If Ostwald had heeded the first pages of my work as well as the last lines, he would have seen that I start precisely from the cultivation of the natural sciences in the Orient and from its stimuli for the emergence of the Greek philosophy of nature. But the philosophy of nature itself is, after all, a Greek creation, and Ostwald in particular should be the last to deny the significance of the philosophy of nature for the natural sciences. The Greeks did, after all, raise the grasp of nature to a higher level, and for millennia they became the teachers of the sciences, because out of technical specialisms they created universal, systematic knowledge, out of natural sciences natural science, “knowledge of nature” proper, i.e. knowledge of nature as a whole. They could do so because in them the natural sciences precisely took their passage through philosophy, through the totality of science, because in their first thinkers philosophy and natural science were one. In that sense I may and must say that “real knowledge of nature” (p. 3) must at the same time arise philosophically and that it arose precisely as Greek philosophy of nature. But the main point: can Ostwald dispute the origin of Greek philosophy of nature from communion with mysticism? He does not do so with a single word. He even concedes “the mystical currents in the Renaissance and at the beginning of the nineteenth century,” but “not as beginnings of scientific development, but as accompanying phenomena of one already under way.” He also concedes “periodic relapses” into mysticism and is even just enough to admit “that mysticism at times seems to have performed pioneer service, in that it anticipated certain thoughts whose importance later came to light.” I ask no more; with that the factual matter I wanted to show is conceded. But the natural scientist Ostwald cannot possibly regard these “pioneer services,” this “periodic” return of mysticism and this constant “accompanying phenomenon” at the upswing of the development of knowledge of nature as “accidental” [p. 155] or can he? Mysticism is an accompanying phenomenon for me too, in so far as I derive the philosophy of nature more from communion with it than from itself, and mysticism for me is, after all, not simply absorbed in it and does not dissolve in the philosophy of nature. But to let mysticism, like Ostwald, merely “run alongside,” as an accompanying phenomenon of a development of natural science already under way, simply explicable as a fantastic exaggeration of the “progress just realized” – such a view already contradicts the facts; for the upswing of mysticism has historically always preceded the upswing of knowledge of nature, and in that view there lies at the same time a truly grandiose misjudgment of mysticism, which is allowed only to eke out a negative existence, to flourish as weeds on the fruitful field of the natural sciences and to let itself be rooted out. As if it did not also have a soil of its own to nourish it! Ostwald does not see the grave problem at all: how does it come about that at certain times theology and philosophy of nature seem to join hands? And precisely at the times of an upswing of the philosophy of nature? How does an overall conception of nature arise at all, which after all confronts man only in individual phenomena? How does it come about that the philosophy of nature apparently begins with the remotest, the cosmic, instead of with the nearest, man and his surroundings? How does it come about that the conception of nature becomes entirely new at certain times? Nature remains the same. So it lies with men, who have become other. And in those times, in ancient Hellas, in the Renaissance, in the Classicism of a hundred years ago, a renewal of the whole man did take place. The eye remained the same, and the laws of logic remain the same. But man saw and thought differently, because he was different; because he grew subjectively, his objective view grew. How could Ostwald explain the temporary new upswing of knowledge of nature? A few gifted heads of natural scientists happened to fall from heaven. But that is the renunciation of explanation, or belief in miracles. “Mysticism can flourish only in fog,” says Ostwald. But what then is fog? There is nothing merely negative. Out of fog worlds can come to be. I see in mysticism, because in it for the first time awakened subjectivity shows the tendency toward thought that embraces the world, the germ of the universal conception of nature. The germ! For I myself say: “Knowledge of nature has always developed further out of mysticism – always turned further away from mysticism, and it should do so.” Ostwald surely cannot demand more, or does he wish to “raise contradiction” against this too? Since he otherwise, as said, concedes the facts, the difference lies probably more in the placing of the accent, and here Ostwald shows himself to be a partisan with an interest; for he avows: “The reporter finds himself prompted to a protest against every attempt at a rehabilitation or habilitation of mysticism in science precisely because he is aware of the danger connected with the reintroduction of the [p. 156] name philosophy of nature for certain philosophical endeavours of modern times.” Here we see the real ground of his protest against our results: it is a fear. He has an understandable interest in removing his philosophy of nature as far as possible from mysticism, and nothing can be more inconvenient to him than the exhibition of a bond between the two, even if only a genetic one. But whether the fear is now well founded or not, whether it is fear of certain consequences or perhaps only of certain prejudices, – the fear too surely belongs among the feelings he proscribes, which “are attainable only by giving up the will to objectivity.” “It has been shown elsewhere how the spiritual development of mankind undoubtedly proceeds in the direction of an increasing intellectualization.” The increase of intellectualization is undoubted; but if it is to mean that development goes one-sidedly in this direction, that thinking increasingly nourishes itself at the expense of feeling and willing and finally absorbs both, then the Scholastics and the Chinese have gone further than we. One-sided intellectualization leads not to progress but to rigidity. The mere specialized training of a psychic organ lets the whole man wither and finally the organ itself suffer. And for that very reason a certain balance in the psyche must again and again take place, and life must stream in from the whole personality even into the most advanced, most capable-of-development organ. Ostwald calls this “periodic relapses” “as a consequence of self-regulation” “according to the general law of oscillation,” whereby it is only remarkable that these “relapses” always coincided precisely with the upswing in the direction desired by Ostwald, and so were at the same time and in fact advances. How does it come about, then, that the advances of mysticism coincided precisely with the great renewals of knowledge of nature, indeed preceded them? Ostwald has no explanation for this; for his explanation would suffice at most for a subsequent mysticism, precisely as an obvious exaggeration of progress. But mysticism is precisely not a satellite, but a forerunner and independent companion of the new philosophy of nature. And that unhistorical explanation would moreover yield an equally unhistorical consequence: the more progress, the more mysticism! But Ostwald surely does not mean that, since he continues: “hereby the role of mysticism is correspondingly restricted, and its domain is incessantly shifted in the direction of the not yet investigated.” Here it appears again that mysticism is for Ostwald only something negative, i.e. actually has no role, that it is at bottom only the empty space which advancing research fills more and more, conquers. But mere not-yet-having-been-investigated, mere darkness does not make mysticism; otherwise the greatest blockhead would be the deepest mystic. That mysticism is dark, darker than science, and that it must again and again be overcome, I do not dispute [p. 157] at all. But it is precisely not only this darkness, the negation of light, but at the same time something positive, the drive toward light. And it is after all basically self-evident that the drive toward light comes out of the dark; and so what I assert is clear in itself, but Ostwald disputes that the germ of knowledge is mystical. Mysticism must always be overcome by knowledge as the germ by the blossom. But before new unfoldings there must be new germs. And Ostwald could have heard it already from Wundt: “Feeling – the pioneer of knowledge!” I do not wish to say more, but only to elaborate precisely this: mysticism, i.e. the universal philosophy of feeling, as pioneer of the knowledge of the world! “Finally the reporter would like to say a word against the ridiculous and unmanly worship of the Greeks which also plies its trade in this treatise. It rests on the fiction that every human and superhuman perfection belonged to that people infected with lies and envy, and that one therefore need only prove of a thing that it is good or clever or beautiful in order thereby to have proved that the Greeks possessed it. Has one still not grasped that this dangerous superstition has, among other things, held back the development of the fine arts and of drama in Germany for centuries? In all areas where Greek ‘models’ have been handed down, European development has stagnated; in all where, fortunately, such were not present (natural sciences, algebra, partly painting) a magnificent development has taken place.” I would not weaken the monstrousness of these sentences in its effect by a refutation, which is indeed already given in the fact that the greatest European movement of progress was precisely the Renaissance, i.e. the rebirth of Greekdom, and that it brought precisely no stagnation but the greatest upswing of the fine arts there has ever been, had Shakespeare in its train, and let the great innovators of the investigation of nature such as Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo appeal to Pythagoreans, Democritus, and others. Yet one could fill books with all that testifies against those sentences; I learn from them only two things. First, that the reporter cannot have read my treatise seriously; for it contains no more “worship of the Greeks” than classical philology, archaeology, or any other occupation with antiquity at all, which perhaps the reporter too in his heart may proscribe as “ridiculous and unmanly”; it points out one-sidednesses of Greek culture and (e.g. pp. 3, 30) of Greek natural science in particular; it rests precisely not “on the fiction” that “one need only prove of a thing [here, then, mysticism] that it is good or clever or beautiful in order thereby to have proved that the Greeks possessed it.” It is rather, conversely, precisely written first of all with a complete gathering and working-up of the material for conducting the proof that the Greeks possessed mysticism in their speculation; so it is not inferred from the [p. 158] mysticism to the Greeks, but from the Greeks to the mysticism. Has Ostwald refuted with a single word this demonstration of the mystical in Greek philosophy of nature? No; he merely reviles the Greeks and reviles mysticism and reviles the author for “worshipping” the despicable Greeks by assigning to them the despicable mysticism. – And the second thing that I learn from those sentences is that one can think quite unhistorically, indeed barbarically, and yet be a great natural scientist.

## Anhang: Archaische Romantik — Appendix: Archaic Romanticism

[p. 128] I should like to speak of those eldest ones with whom things grow bright and spiritual in history, of the first who thought and wrote, of the fathers of science, whom one may call the patriarchs of Europe. For we must see the ancients as young again; then the young will strike us as old, and we shall progress. But what are the oldest Greek sages to us, the thinkers before Socrates? Grey torsos, and from their rigid archaic mouths there wells up something quite strange: “all is water,” or “all is air,” or “fire,” and the like. To us, however, everything is stone, and we are concerned only that at the examination “water” does not turn into “air” for us, or that we do not give to Anaximenes what belonged to Anaximander. And we were told that they explained everything from water on account of a few mussel shells which they found on the land – we were not told that they found pearls in the shells. Or did not the venerable master Thales already find stones “ensouled” and everything “full of the divine”? We were told, to be sure, that those ancients were good physicists – and I should know a very fine chapter on Lavoisier and Laplace, Robert Mayer and Darwin two and a half millennia ago –, but we were forgotten to be told that those ancients were also human beings, human beings with warmly flooded hearts, yes, perhaps the first physicists precisely because they were such full human beings that they had to embrace heaven and earth, and could express and find again the urge of their soul only in the great language of nature.

I am told, of course, that this would be to understand the ancients far too romantically, and people refuse to believe it; for we learned at school that antiquity is classical and the classical the opposite of the Romantic; we are convinced that the Greeks came into the world as marble, and are proud, by contrast, of our fever-surging hearts, of our now reawakened Romanticism – but I should like to show that our youngest movements of the spirit are at the same time the oldest, that through our newest friends, the Romantics, we might understand precisely those so alien primal fathers of wisdom as well. I do not say that those ancients were [p. 129] Romantics – such exaggeration I leave to my critics. In those beginners dwell the seeds of the classical as of the Romantic; but the Romantic is more visible, because the classical in its purity emerges precisely as ripeness, as the bloom of manhood, whereas the Romantic emerges as budding longing, as the ferment of youth, and therefore as the mood of the early age.

Or what other marks of the Romantic does one wish to name? The visionary trait in the thinker’s countenance? The thinker on Pegasus, indeed grown together with him into a centaur? That is, the unity of poet and philosopher, as Romanticism so loudly proclaims it? I know of no period of philosophy in which that unity was more fully realized than precisely the early age of Greek thinking. If they did not already write in verse, like Xenophanes the old minstrel, and Parmenides and Empedocles, then they wrote, like Heraclitus, in images that are bolder still in their poetry.

But, it is said, they were epic poets of nature, and Romanticism is lyrical at its root, sounds subjective, and revels in the depths of the soul, in the cult of the personal and the ingenious. “A philosopher must talk about himself, as much as a lyric poet,” says Fr. Schlegel. Yet the oldest thinkers in Hellas are not for nothing the contemporaries of precisely the lyric poets; they speak astonishingly much of themselves, practise a quite un-Hellenic hero-worship of the spirit, and rummage in their souls. “I have searched myself.” “For man his mind is his god.” “The soul has a word of its own, which increases itself.” “The boundaries of the soul you could not find out, even if you walked every road; so deep is its ground.” Beside these magic spells of Heraclitus the words of the Romantics sound almost stale: “We do not know the depths of our spirit.” “The greatest mystery is man to himself.”

But, it is objected, Romanticism “demands and sees mystery everywhere.” Romantics are symbolists and mystics through and through; they want to “relate the semblance of the finite to the truth of the infinite and dissolve it into that truth through allegory, through symbols”; for “all [p. 130] meaning is symbolic”; they want to write only for the initiated, in the style of the mysteries: “Nature is incomprehensible per se.” “The highest can be said only allegorically, precisely because it is unutterable.” “Every concept of God is empty prattle.” Yet is all this so far from the mystery wisdom of the Pythagoreans, the companions of the Orphics, and from all the sayings of a Xenophanes and Alcmaeon, Heraclitus and Empedocles about the “unfathomable,” “unutterable” Godhead, which is a matter of faith alone? “The fullness of the divine escapes the knowledge of the unbeliever.” “Nature loves to hide,” cries Heraclitus the Obscure, who loves to hide himself, like nature and like the god whom he proclaims: “The lord whose oracle is at Delphi neither speaks nor conceals, but gives a sign.” When Fr. Schlegel has to justify himself on account of his obscurity and positively demands the “mysticism of expression,” he laments: “But Apollo, who neither conceals nor speaks, but gives a sign, is no longer revered” – so the words of Heraclitus echo back like sunken bells after millennia. And Heraclitus knew it: “The Sibyl, who with raving mouth utters things unlaughed-at, unadorned, and unperfumed, reaches with her voice across a thousand years; for the god drives her.” Yes, the god drove him, to whom Fr. Schlegel’s saying applies most purely: “the poetizing philosopher, the philosophizing poet is a prophet.” And he writes in Pythian oracles, in aphorisms, in fragments – like the Romantics.

It would be petty to believe that the common style of prophet and poet is merely something external. It comes from the inner unity of thinker, poet, and prophet, which among the ancient Hellenes, when the vocations of the spirit and the powers of the soul still lay undivided within one another, was nature, but among the Romantics, because it was after all no longer entirely recoverable, was art and longing, tendency and programme. And here already one can guess why I see in those ancients so much that is Romantic and yet no Romantics. They have a similar content of spirit as the Romantics, but they [p. 131] have it, to speak in Schiller’s manner, “naively,” while the Romantics want to have it “sentimentally,” striving. Those have it without willing it. So they also contend against poets and hierophants, precisely because they still feel themselves one with them and want to separate from them, with whom the Romantics are striving to come together again. But when poet, thinker, and prophet, in particular lyric poet, physicist, and theosophist, are fused in their functions, then the materials too which they live through are fused, and so from the unity of vocation and function there results a dogmatic unity: the unity of soul, nature, and God. It is the fundamental doctrine of mysticism, in which the ancient Hellenes and the Romantics meet. And the psychic, the sensuous, the divine, whose lived unity they proclaim, they must interpret through one another, and for that reason they symbolize, obscurely for all who do not experience the same. For Fr. Schlegel “the infinity of the human spirit, the divinity of all natural things, and the humanity of the gods is the eternal great theme.” It is also the eternal great theme for those oldest Greek philosophers. They teach the divinity of the soul, the ensoulment of the world, and the worldliness of God.

They come from the neighbourhood of the Orient and from mythology and the mysteries, toward which precisely the Romantics are striving again. “In the Orient we must seek the highest Romantic.” “The core, the centre of poetry is to be found in mythology and in the mysteries of the ancients.” These fantasts! cry the moderns, and from the height of their empirical science they are horrified at such words of Novalis as: “physics is nothing other than the doctrine of fantasy,” and “visible objects are the expression of feelings.” Should we not rather take note that for them the feelings go to the visible, and that the doctrine of fantasy is precisely physics? “Spirit is philosophy of nature,” cries Fr. Schlegel, and for Novalis the “poet is the prophet of nature in images,” and “metaphysics and astronomy are one science.” This fits best, it fits positively as an apology for the speculative, cosmological [p. 132] poets, for the poets of nature, with whom Greek philosophy begins. They make their inner life worldly to the point of materialism, and they make the world inward to the point of anthropomorphism. The world becomes for them a living being that breathes, that has a right and a left side, that wanders, that alternates between hunger and satiety. The world becomes the “macranthropos,” man the “microcosm” – among the ancient philosophers of nature as among the Romantics. “A spiritualized, moral universe,” to which Novalis wants to return, was already beheld by the primeval Anaximander in his world as an order of penance, and by the ancient Heraclitus in his world-judgment according to the laws of nature, and by the ancient Pythagoreans in their dance of the stars as temple service. And as those physicists speak also idealistically, so the Romantics speak also materialistically. “Thinking – also galvanism,” “Thinking is a muscular movement.” You suppose that it is Karl Vogt or Haeckel speaking, but it is Novalis, the Romantic.

The modern bureaucrats of schools and tendencies will never grasp how precisely the naturalist turns over into Romanticism, precisely the Romantic into naturalism. He naturalizes the soul by ensouling nature. The same Novalis wishes for man “the sweet passion for the weaving of nature, the eye for her enchanting mysteries. Would that he but once learned to feel! – Then the stars would rise within him, he would learn to feel the whole world” – Yes, they learned to feel the world, and the stars began to dance divinely for them. So it went with the ancient speculators on nature – for one must learn to feel nature before one learns to think her; she must first address man, bloom before him in gleaming colours –, but do not the colours come from man? In that century in which the Greek soul swelled highest in the tyrant’s sense of power, in the lyric poet’s feeling of love, in the mystical urge of the Orphic and the Bacchant, Greek thinking too lifted itself in Dionysian surge to experience of the world, to a grasping of the All, to a gleaming vision of nature. It was a thinking of feeling – that was its greatness, for it was precisely by the [p. 133] swelling feeling that thinking was borne up to the breadth of speculation –, and that was its limit, which was overcome in the critical, that is, the separating, feeling-detaching progress of science. Yet again and again, when science wants to rejuvenate itself, it must experience the world, grasp it in unity with feeling, so that it may become new to it. That is the temporary right of Romanticism, which wants to renew, to rejuvenate. It proffers a cup that is to quicken us from time to time, but not to make us drunk in constant indulgence. And the very essence and will of Romanticism is neither thinking nor feeling, but the blissful and often so unblissful unity of both. If, therefore, we want to understand not only the Romantics but those oldest thinkers, in whom the unity of thinking and feeling is given so much more originally, we must become psychologists and ask ourselves what feeling means and how it must colour the thinking into which it streams.

Naturally artistic-religious; for art and religion are just as much a developed feeling as science is a developed thinking. And so in the thinking of feeling there lies already that ancient unity of thinker, poet, and prophet, and also the unity of their materials: world, soul, God. And the ancient Hellenes do it as the Romantics do, only more originally, more powerfully: they make everything worldly, embody everything, they ensoul everything, they deify everything. So they proceed from Thales onward, of whom only three propositions stand firm: all is water, all is full of the divine, and in the magnet even the stone shows itself ensouled. But how can nature, God, soul become one? Only in the experience of feeling. And now: what do we say when we say “feel”? How does the world become for the one who feels?

### Unendlicher Strom — Infinite Stream

If you think, you cut sharply and set boundaries or bind fast; but when you feel, it goes through your soul like a stream, a boundlessly flooding one, so that you know of no beginning and end. A gentle stream it is [p. 134] or even a wild, tearing one. Tieck’s Sternbald sees into his agitated mind “as into an unfathomable whirlpool, where wave presses on wave and foams, an endless fury of the enraged element!” Foaming, flooding souls are the Romantics, restlessly moved; eternal wanderers are they, like their heroes. Fr. Schlegel advises “young friends of truth, instead of school, rather a journey round the world.” Precisely the oldest “friends of truth” followed this. Solon wanders through the world “philosophizing” – in such a connection the word appears, after all, for the first time in Herodotus. Legend exaggerates history only a little when it sends Thales and Anaximander soon on far journeys, and above all the All-everywhere Pythagoras. Are not many of the ancient heads of schools emigrants, and does not Empedocles himself tell of his wanderings, and Xenophanes that he roamed the lands for 67 years, and even Democritus that he made the farthest journeys of his time?

As they wander through the world, so the world transforms itself for them. In changing images the world appears to them, until it becomes the mirror of their wander-loving urge for life. They experience the world, that is, they feel it, that is, they experience it as an infinite stream. And so the Ionians proclaim the world as infinite transformation, until the last draws the conclusion: all flows. Crudely Thales says it: all is water. Say it more sharply, cries Anaximander: the infinite it must be, for only the infinite is inexhaustible in transformation. Say it more sharply, cries Anaximenes: air it must be, the mobile transforming being of the world. Say it more sharply, cries Heraclitus: it is fire, the eternally flickering. It is an intensification of the foaming feeling of the world and at the same time of the power of transformation up to the strongest, the hottest, up to passion: the water of Thales swells to the infinite of Anaximander, billows up steaming in the air of Anaximenes, until it completes the heating in the pure fire of Heraclitus. Our historians naturally call that a playful construction. But what does history ask about our historians? And above all the history of speculation about the enemies of [p. 135] speculation? Ideas have their own causality; the idea of unlimited transformation had to intensify and did intensify from Thales to Heraclitus – it is meant to say no more than that.

The Romantics feel it after them, the infinite world-stream. Novalis sees “as an immeasurable spectacle the history of the generation of nature” out of the “infinite fluidity.” But above all that urge which tears open the Ionian horizon to the doctrine of infinite worlds, which drives Anaximander to raise the boundless itself to the world-principle, this urge toward the infinite it is, after all, that thrills the Romantics through and through as well, indeed that becomes the most visible fundamental trait of their nature. And they know that with their drive toward the infinite they touch the early antiquity, the primordial, poetic feelings. Fr. Schlegel praises the striving for the infinite in the Greek lyric poets, the contemporaries and countrymen of the philosophers of nature. “Saturate the feeling of life with the idea of the infinite, and you will understand the ancients and poetry.” “Nothing is more attainable by the spirit than the infinite.” Yet they grasp the infinite precisely in Anaximander’s sense as the inexhaustible, as the fullness of becoming of nature, as the divine “chaos” from which “infinite worlds” arise. “The infinite conceived in that fullness is the Godhead – the eternal life – the one infinite full,” “the ‘infinite’ holy fullness of life of formative nature,” the “eternal agility of the infinitely full chaos,” the “chaos struggling for new and wondrous births, which hides beneath the ordered creation, indeed in its very womb.” Such words of the Romantics one must read if one wishes to look into the heart of the old Anaximander, for whom infinite world-births rise from the infinite womb.

“Whose heart does not stir in leaping joy when the innermost life of nature in all its fullness comes into his mind, when that mighty feeling, for which language has no other names than love and voluptuousness, expands within him like a mighty, all-dissolving vapour, and he sinks, trembling in sweet anguish, into the dark, [p. 136] enticing womb of nature, – nothing remains but a focal point of the immeasurable power of generation, an engulfing whirlpool in the great ocean! What is the flame that appears everywhere?” Here we have in Novalis, in one breath, all the world-forms of the old Ionians hinted at as in a dream, and still others of the later ones, the world-ocean of Thales, the dark womb of nature of Anaximander, the all-powerful vapour of Anaximenes, and the all-flame of Heraclitus, and one sees how they all rise as images from a Dionysian swollen soul that feels itself one with nature – and was not for the Ionians too the pure essence of the world materially one with the essence of the soul?

Whoever tells me now that I have read the spirit of the old Ionians artificially into that quotation from Novalis, let him read only, in the same place, in “The Novices at Sais,” the following lines: “Not wrongly did ancient sages seek the origin of things in water, and truly they spoke of a higher water than sea-water and spring-water. In that the primal fluid reveals itself only, as it comes to light in liquid metal, and therefore men may always revere it as divine. How few have yet immersed themselves in the mysteries of the fluid, and many a one has surely never had this presentiment of the highest enjoyment and life arise in his intoxicated soul. In thirst this world-soul reveals itself, this mighty longing for dissolving. The intoxicated feel only too well this supraterrestrial bliss of the fluid, and in the end all pleasant sensations in us are manifold dissolvings, stirrings of those primal waters in us. – How many men stand by the intoxicating rivers and do not hear the cradle-song of these maternal waters, and do not enjoy the enchanting play of their infinite waves!” Heraclitus heard it and enjoyed it, he who compared the world to an infinitely flowing river into which one cannot step down twice, and to the play of a boy on the seashore, and also to a mixed drink that must be shaken. For he too was an “intoxicated one” and full of the Dionysian spirit, like Novalis here.

[p. 137] There is in the Ionians one idea, intensifying itself, of the eternally changing fullness of life, from the water of Thales to the fire of Heraclitus, and the Romantics naturally renew it most in the highest, most extreme form, that of world-fire and soul-fire, as unconscious Heracliteans. “Worship the fire,” demands Fr. Schlegel expressly, and confesses: “I revere fire,” and extols “fiery reason” as against the “watery,” exactly as Heraclitus the fiery dry soul as against the moist. “In the centre lies the eternal fire veiled,” begins Tieck’s sonnet to Fr. Schlegel, and Goethe speaks of fire-air from Fr. Schlegel’s laboratory. Formerly there were fantasts who made Heraclitus the fire-worshipper a follower of Zoroaster; since then there have been only purists who keep him far from every Parsee influence. He was perhaps not much more a Parsee than the new Zarathustra, but how do our purists imagine it, that Heraclitus should have heard nothing of the religion of the people under whose rule he lived? – “The sun like a god – is the life of the planets anything other than sun-worship? Here too you come to meet us, primeval, childlike religion of the Parsees, and we find in you the religion of the universe.” So speaks the pious Novalis, who seeks the “magus”; yet he is also drawn to inward parallels. “The sun is in astronomy what God is in metaphysics; freedom and immortality are like light and warmth.” “We are occupied with nothing but the preservation of a holy and mysterious flame.”

But what do they want to say, Heraclitus like the Romantics, with that fiery element which they let blaze divinely ruling through world and soul? The flame is for them the image and the force of the life which they feel, which unites soul and world; it is the infinite power of transformation which, driving and consuming, rises and falls in the eternal alternation of coming-to-be and passing away. And the unity of the two ways, the unity of the up and down in the eternally reversing process of becoming, of which Heraclitus speaks, returns with the image of the flame in Novalis: “The act of leaping over oneself is everywhere the highest, the primal point, the [p. 138] genesis of life. So the flame is nothing but such a point; all life is an exuberant process of renewal, which only from one side has the appearance of a process of annihilation. The precipitate of life is something living, capable of life. As warmth is to flame, so spirit is to life.”

Emphasizers of life, vitalists, are the old Ionians like the Romantics; and the former have been misunderstood as emphasizers of matter, as materialists, as the Romantics have been as despisers of nature! But life unites in both nature, soul, God. The Romantics too seek truth in nature: “There is only one real system – the great hidden, the eternal nature or truth.” But nature is for them “the countenance of a Godhead.” “God we do not behold, but everywhere we behold the divine; yet first and most properly in the midst of a man of sense.” The old Ionians too are pantheists and find the divine most purely in the soul. God is for them the world-soul, the all-being, the universal of nature. “All is driven to pasture with God’s scourge,” teaches Heraclitus, and does not cease to admonish us to follow the universal, the divine nature. And it echoes almost literally in Novalis: “One will never miss the way if one heeds the universal in us and around us,” and the individual soul should become in accord with the world-soul. He speaks of the “world-reason,” like the old Ephesian. Before God all is beautiful, good, just, only not for men, says Heraclitus. Novalis puts it pointedly: For God there is no devil at all – but for us he is, alas, a very effective chimera.

God, soul, nature – all one fire, all one life, one infinite transformation! All flows, there is no being, there is only becoming, proclaims the fire-spirit Heraclitus. Eternally becoming ones are the Romantics; they know it and want to be so. “One can only become a philosopher, not be one,” asserts Fr. Schlegel, and extols the “divine unrest” of Lessing, and teaches outright: “the Romantic kind of poetry is still in becoming, indeed that is its very essence, that it can [p. 139] eternally only become, never be completed.” “In the universe of poetry itself, however, nothing rests, all becomes and transforms itself.” The Romantics say it of the universe of poetry and Heraclitus of the universe of nature. Do you wish to insist on the difference? The Romantics, after all, declare nature itself to be a “poem” and teach: “if you want to penetrate into the interior of physics, let yourself be initiated into the mysteries of poetry.” “When one reads and hears genuine poems, one feels an inner understanding of nature stirring – Investigators of nature and poets have always shown themselves, through one language, as one people. – When the latter pursued with light mind the fluid and the fleeting – –” “all the holy games of art only distant imitations of the infinite game of the world.” And so Novalis sings of the “primal play of every nature” and feels able to understand nature, “the one poem of the Godhead,” the “infinite plaything.” All a game – that is thought in arch-Romantic fashion, and Romanticism appeals to the ancient lyric poetry of that Simonides who counsels taking life like a game. But do you know who said it still more loudly? Heraclitus compared human thoughts to children’s games and the whole course of the world to the game of a boy who moves stones to and fro on the board, who heaps up sand hills on the shore which collapse again and again.

All a game, thought and nature, soul and world, so he teaches, and all at the same time a struggle! All an up and down, a change and reversal, an alternation of opposites – ebb and flow, play of waves went for him through soul and world. All is opposition, all turns over into one another, all becomes one in the reversal of the opposites: day becomes night and night day, sleeping and waking, death and life are one, all is at once cold and warm, young and old, good and bad. So Heraclitus proclaims it, and so the contrasts chase one another and collapse together as relative also in the flooding soul of Romanticism. “Between good and evil, between joy and sorrow” Tieck finds “only a second,” and for Novalis “the man is in a way also woman, as the woman is man,” and “fortune and [p. 140] misfortune are in constant balance.” Fr. Schlegel lives “in an alternation of melancholy and exuberance,” in “waking dreams,” and Novalis wishes for a mixture of the tragic and the comic and would like always to sleep and wake at once. “Life is the beginning of death,” “death and life are really one,” “death is life.” And as the Romantics do here, so Heraclitus takes pains, in astonishingly many sayings, to work out the opposition and, again, the unity precisely of sleep and waking, of life and death. “The living touches the dead, the waking the sleeping.” And yet for him the unphilosophical are sleepers, just as for the Romantic philosophizing is “only a threefold or twofold being awake.” “Even sleep is nothing but the flood of that invisible world-ocean, and waking the onset of the ebb.” So says Novalis. Is he interpreting sayings of Heraclitus? “The dry soul the wisest”; “the sleepers fellow-workers in the happening of the world –”

Sleep and waking, death and life give them indeed the great wave-beat of the infinite process of life; for death too belongs to life. “Death a means to life”; “through death life is strengthened.” “Only in the midst of death does the lightning of eternal life ignite.” “Death is transformation.” Death and life are really one, so the Romantics proclaim. Death as transformation, immortality, reviving out of death is proclaimed also by Heraclitus. For Hades and Dionysus are one, the god of death and the god of life. “This god of nature,” Novalis continues here, as it were, “eats us, bears us – lets himself be eaten by us, begotten and borne by us.”

The world is eternal alternation, a struggling up and down, and it must be stirred, like the barley drink which otherwise decomposes – so speaks the philosophical Bacchant and mystic Heraclitus, as the ancient Orphics too gave to the mixing-bowl of Dionysus a world-significance. The world is fermentation, foaming mixture of opposites, struggle, and struggle fills the soul too. “To me, however, polemic is the seal of the most living activity of the divine in [p. 141] man,” says Fr. Schlegel, and Romanticism strikes out against the “rabble” with “holy wrath,” and as untiringly as Heraclitus, the wrathful fighter, the “reviler of the rabble,” for whom war is the father and king of things. The world a struggle and a game, a river and a fire, an eternal transformation, an infinite life, full of passion –

### Rhythmus und Harmonie — Rhythm and Harmony

Here music sounds; calm harmonies soothe passion, regulate the infinite stream; firm rhythms strike, taming, into the stormy allegro of the Ionians. The ancient Greek spirit speaks its second word in the Pythagoreans. Yet one must not take this crudely as a sequence. The Pythagorean round dance already winds into the Ionian one, as in a canon the deeper voice enters upon the higher; and the Ionians already let sound as overtone what the Pythagoreans bring out as melody: already Anaximander hears behind the rushing infinity of becoming the dull beat of a world-clock, “necessity” as the “order of time,” and precisely the foaming Heraclitus speaks of the firm measures of becoming and of the world as a harmony of opposites. The world as harmony of opposites, as order of the boundless – so the Pythagoreans too take it; but they let the order and harmony ring out more, and the Ionians more the boundless and the struggle of the opposites.

Our old textbooks set a chasm here between the Greeks of the East and the Greeks of the West. They do not see that Pythagoras came from the waters of Ionia to the West, that the Ionian and the Pythagorean principle, complementing and conditioning each other, positing each other, strike into one another like matter and form, like running and rein, time and measure, song and beat. Only from the sense of limit does one grasp the boundless, only from the limitless the limit, only from the firm does one appraise the stream, only from the stream the firm. One must already [p. 142] feel in a Pythagorean way in order to think in an Ionian way, and one must still feel in an Ionian way in order to think in a Pythagorean way.

Let us only understand it again from lyrical thinking, from the essence of feeling. Can one, then, feel poetically without forming? Let us take a poem: it is passion tamed by metre and rhythm. The Ionian stream itself presses toward the Pythagorean measure. The philosophers of nature – the contemporaries of the lyric poets! Feeling – that means first an unbounded living stream. Whither does its fullness stream? To harmony. Feeling – that means also the urge toward harmony. Music – the purest art of feeling! The Pythagoreans are fanatics of music – like the Romantics. The Pythagoreans, it is said, began the day with music, soothed the passions by music, called philosophy music, interpreted the essence of all things through music, explained the whole universe “according to harmony,” taught the “dance” of the stars and their eternal song according to the “harmony of the spheres.” All is harmony, they said. The Romantic would like to “go under in the great harmony of the All.” Novalis wishes “that man might understand the inner music of nature and have a sense for outer harmony,” and he teaches “how the stars had united in a melodious round.” “One would like to say, nature dances.” “Nature is an Aeolian harp, a musical instrument.” “The musical relations seem to me quite properly to be the fundamental relations of nature.” “Is it not true that stones and forests obey music?” So Novalis asks, and he knows the “deep meaning of the old Orphic legend of the wonders of the art of tone as the mysterious doctrine of music as the former and soother of the universe.” He demands the “philosopher as Orpheus.” And full of Orphic spirit are the old philosophers of nature and precisely the Pythagoreans.

Pythagoreanism and Orphism have been called the masculine and the feminine side of the same fundamental tendency; closely bound they appear, as the Pythagoreans are bound with the women, whose names in this [p. 143] school of philosophers come forward as loudly as in no other ancient one, almost as loudly as the women among the Romantics. Women bow to holy order. The Romantic longing “to become like God” and to make the whole of life “divine service,” “priestly service,” was already the conscious striving of the ancient Pythagoreans. Human life is to become an order, as the universe is harmonious order, a cosmos – so the Pythagoreans first called it. Cosmos means ornament – if one wishes to understand it, let one hear Novalis demand the devout contemplation of nature which seeks “to grasp the world in its fullness and its interconnection,” not forgetting, “over the isolation, the flashing thread which links the members in rows and forms the holy chandelier,” and “finding itself blissful in the contemplation of this living ornament hovering above nocturnal depths.”

The world is an order, life ought to be one. The world is harmony of opposites, so the Pythagoreans find; the equilibrium in human life, so Fr. Schlegel finds with Wilhelm Meister, can be maintained only through opposites. Human life consists in opposites, so Alcmaeon, who is ranked with the old Pythagoreans, already found, and to maintain it in equilibrium he felt called as physician. Now we understand Novalis: “Every illness is a musical problem, the cure a musical resolution. The shorter and yet the more complete the resolution, the greater the musical talent of the physician. Should one not be able to cure illnesses by illnesses?” “All arts and sciences rest on partial harmonies.”

All is music and therefore all is number, so the Pythagoreans teach already from their old master onward, who measured the tone on the string. They are as zealous mathematicians as they are musicians. They are it, the Romantics in their turn want to be it. They extol mathematics, and precisely as an interpretation of the world, quite in Pythagorean fashion, indeed, over-Pythagorean. They demand not only “mathematical enthusiasm,” Novalis himself cries enthusiastically: “the mathematicians are the only [p. 144] fortunate ones.” “The life of the gods is mathematics.” “The mathematician knows everything.” “All historical science strives to become mathematical, the mathematical power is the ordering power. Every mathematical science strives again to become philosophical, to be animated or rationalized; then poetic, finally moral, last of all religious.” It is a return; one sees the transitions in the religious-moral-artistic-philosophical-mathematical Pythagoreans. And one sees it in the Romantics, how feeling, music seeks to precipitate itself into the measure and form of numbers. “Music has much similarity with algebra,” says Novalis, but that says too little still. He hears everything as music, and he hears in all music the number, he hears everything as rhythm, and he hears in rhythm the order by which one knows everything. “Seasons, times of day, life and fates are all, remarkably enough, thoroughly rhythmical, metrical, in time. In all crafts and arts, all machines, the organic bodies, our daily occupations – everywhere rhythm, metre, beat, melody – rhythm is found everywhere –” “All method is rhythm: if one has the rhythm in one’s power, one has the world in one’s power. Every man has his individual rhythm. Algebra is poetry. Rhythmic sense is genius.” In rhythm the Pythagorean Romantic overhears the meaning of the world. He wants to make a verse out of the world; he wants, as those ancients did, to grasp the world by forming it, harmonizing it. Therefore verses sound to him out of nature, and again out of verses the order of nature already sounds to him. Rhythm vibrates for him as world-law. “When one reads and hears genuine poems, one feels an inner understanding of nature stirring – – Investigators of nature and poets have one language.” And when in a “head the great rhythm of the hexameter in periods, this inner poetic mechanism, has become native, – there appears, as the highest thoughts of themselves join these singular vibrations and into the richest, most manifold orders [p. 145] come together, the deep meaning – of the mysterious doctrine of music as the former and soother of the universe.”

### Einheit und Liebe — Unity and Love

Strange! The Pythagoreans listen to the tones on the strings, hear music from the whole world, attune everything to harmony, but they do not sing. The harmony is there, but text and melody are first brought by the Eleatics and their heir Empedocles. They bring them with their epics of nature; they truly let, as the Romantic says, in poems “the inner understanding of nature move” and “the highest thoughts join the great rhythm of the hexameter”, thoughts of the universe. And what do they sing? They sing of the One of nature. It sounds, to be sure, different from what Fr. Schlegel sings:

“Through all the tones that sound in earth’s many-coloured dream, one soft tone is drawn, for him who listens secretly.”

Yet the old Eleatics, even then, overheard that One, and in return let the motley variety of the earth fade into a dream, into mere semblance. Wherever I let my spirit roam, everything dissolves for me into a unity; thus it is put into the mouth of the leader of the Eleatic chorus, Xenophanes. And this world-One is for him the Godhead, and the Godhead is for him eternal and unchangeable. I think of Fr. Schlegel’s doctrine of the centre: God as “the person of the universe”, as “centre”, and God “eternal, equal to himself and unchangeable”: so it stands in the “Ideen”.

I hear the objection that it is surely a long way from the rigid monism of the Eleatics to the serenely pious enthusiasm of the Romantics. But let us not picture the Eleatics as Indian penitents and grave-diggers! To the clink of the beaker the old singer Xenophanes strikes up his hymn to the world-Godhead, and Parmenides begins with an ascent to heaven. From Xenophanes to Melissus Eleaticism preaches the deification of the world, the unity of the world in God, as God: pantheism. But does not even Novalis [p. 146] speak of the god of nature who bears us and eats us, and whom we eat and bear, and is not Romanticism full of pantheistic urge and of monistic urge? Romanticism wants to unify everything, up to the fusion of all opposites. One says, of course: unifying is not unity. Certainly: unity is for the Eleatic truth and reality, for the Romantic ideal and longing. He seeks what the other found. And just for that reason I again do not call the Eleatics Romantics. They teach naively what Romanticism teaches sentimentally.

But let us not insist too much on this opposition, as though there were no transitions! Romanticism too at times solidifies unity into dogma, and in the old epic poets of nature it at times soars up into the ideal. Does not Xenophanes teach more the unity of God than that of the world? And is not unity for Empedocles only the blessed state that is lost to this world but will one day return, just as at the end of Novalis’ Ofterdingen all kinds and natural opposites “return” to unity? But the greatest Eleatic, the rigid Parmenides! Ah! let us only gaze longer into these rigid features, and beneath them we shall find the soft smile of love! One does not want to believe it? But does one rather believe that Parmenides saw nothing but a rigid One and wished and was able to make himself blind to the apparent plenitude? He has, after all, described the world of appearance of the many at length; he has given it an interpretation and a tendency toward true unity. Does not unity thus become an ideal for him too? And does not the plenitude of the world necessarily press unity back into the ideal, whether the ideal of knowledge or of striving?

Unity as ideal, as longing, as Romanticism, is love; the substitute for unity in the divided world is called love, and from this we understand what our handbooks do not understand, why the fanatic of unity, why Parmenides of all men exalts Eros as the first-created god and expressly sets in the midst of the world of the senses the goddess of love, who incites birth and mating. Fr. Schlegel thinks that a primal love will probably have preceded [p. 147] primal generation, and praises chaos, which “waits only for the touch of love to unfold into a harmonious world”. “All life must spring from love.” “God is original love”, the world “the hieroglyph of the One eternal love”. W. Schlegel too sees the “ensouling spirit of original love” hover over the waters. And Novalis feels “the innermost life of nature” as “that mighty feeling for which language has no other names than love and voluptuousness”, as “the focal point of the immeasurable power of generation”, and he beholds “the history of the generation of nature, and every fixed point that settles in the infinite fluidity becomes for him a new revelation of the genius of love”. And like Romanticism, and like Parmenides before it, Empedocles extols the world-forming power of love; to be sure, he lets the separating power of strife rule as well, but love is for him the higher power, the one that forms organisms, the harmonious, ideal power, and he himself feels himself a penitent because he “trusted in strife”, and he extols the golden age when there was as yet no god of war and indeed no god and king at all, but “only a queen, love”.

Only a queen, love – has not the old sage thereby spoken the motto of Romanticism? “Only the understanding separates women and love”, says the Romantic. From this let us also understand the strange feminism of Parmenides, who lets himself be led up by maidens to the goddess of truth, lets her, as Pythia, proclaim all wisdom to him, and, as “centre of the world”, like Fr. Schlegel, beholds the goddess of love, the far-famed Aphrodite. Who would have credited him with it, the rigid Eleatic, before whose “icy abstractions” even Nietzsche shuddered? The Godhead of nature, who first created Eros, is for him feminine, as for Novalis “the whole of nature is feminine, virgin and mother at once”. And the erotic, feminist trait of Parmenides continues to work in Empedocles. Imploring the Muse, “the white-armed virgin”, he lets the elements be reared as two pairs, and he beholds “the earth-mother and the far-seeing sun-maiden, the bloody [p. 148] strife and the grave-eyed harmony, Lady Beauty and Lady Ugliness, Lady Swift and Lady Slow, loving truthfulness and black-haired confusion”, and all the others too are feminine for him, “the spirits of growth and decay, of sleeping and waking, of movement and rest, of richly crowned splendour and of filth, of silence and speech”. Respiration he compares to a girl’s play with a water-clock, and he himself, in the transmigration of souls, wants to have been a girl once. Now Charis, now Ananke he calls the mistress of the world, but above all love or Aphrodite. She, “who rouses thoughts of love and brings concord to perfection among men, it is she too who whirls in the elements; but that no mortal man knows”.

True, the immortal Empedocles, who interprets the world by the soul, knows that besides love strife dwells in it, equally necessary to the formation of the world, just as Fr. Schlegel in the “Lucinde” believes he feels and sees “the eternal discord through which everything becomes and exists”, the “unending war down to the most hidden depths of existence”. Yet precisely as it says in the same Lucinde: “not hatred forms beings, but love”; so Empedocles paints how love joins the earthly shapes together out of the elements, how everything in love longs for and unites with everything else and combines from afar in a “world-order of love”, how Aphrodite waters the earth and then hands it over to fire for solidification, how “beneath the hands, with the love-nails of the divine Aphrodite” the bodies of animals are fitted together, the eyes formed, and how the earth “has dropped anchor in the perfect harbour of Aphrodite”. Aphrodite, Aphrodite, queen of the world, builder of the world – so it still resounds through many fragments of Empedocles that one thinks of Fr. Schlegel: “whoever does not come to know nature through love will never come to know it.”

At least not philosophically, so the Romantic means it. “For”, says Novalis, “dephilosophize science, and what remains? Earth, air and water.” [p. 149] The isolated elements of Empedocles, which, however, the philosopher Empedocles joins through love, through the love that dwells in his own soul. He confesses it, after all: “only with our love do we grasp the love of the world, and its hatred with our sorrowful hatred”, as we behold the earth “with our earthly matter, with our air the divine air, etc.” For like is known only by like: so he teaches, as Parmenides already did and as Novalis would one day: “the eye sees nothing but eye, thinking sees nothing but thinking”. They teach the attraction of like, which the magician understands, as Empedocles was one, who went as a wonder-worker through the Hellenic lands, the magician whom the Romantic, in turn, would like to become, and whom Novalis longs for as the ideal of man. The “magician” knows the interconnection of nature; he rules nature through love. He knows with Novalis “how the world came into being through wondrous sympathy”; he sees with Empedocles himself “the earth lovingly attuned”, sees also with him “the white bones arise, divinely fair, fitted together by the glue of harmony”. And “all harmony”, says Fr. Schlegel, “is a gift of love”.

Three words were spoken and followed one another at the entrance of Greek philosophy. The Ionian speaks: I hear the world rushing like an infinite stream. The Pythagorean speaks: and I hear music in it and well-measured harmony. The Eleatic speaks: and everything melts into love and fades away into One. From infinity through number and harmony to unity! The doctrines follow one another; yet let no one think that they are therefore so different and separate in origin! The Ionians already teach unity and harmony too, and the Eleatics still teach harmony and infinity. And these very three doctrines return united among the Romantics. Is it so wonderful? The world is for them at once infinite, harmonious, unitary. Theory may separate here, the understanding even form opposites, but in life and in [p. 150] feeling they are united. For how do we experience life in ourselves? As a streaming plenitude, but at the same time as rhythmic order in the circulation of the blood, in breath and pulse-beat, and again at the same time as the wholeness and unity of our being. To experience life means to feel, and in feeling everything dissolves, everything is harmonized, everything fuses. Feeling knows nothing of sharp division and fixed limit. Everything becomes for it at once the infinite and the One, and both pass into one another through harmonious measure. Thus feeling needs measure, in order to equalize the flood of the infinite into the calm of the One. It is a self-equalization of feeling – or is the poet’s heightened feeling to clothe itself in rhyme and meter and rhythm merely out of caprice? Heightened feeling needs measure as the heightened flood needs the beat of the waves.

To grasp the world as infinite flood, as harmony and as unity – that means to live through it, to feel it through. So did the great beginners of Greek wisdom. So it befits the beginner; for first we must seize the world with the living feeling of youth, before, ripe, cold and stern, we take it under the magnifying glass of science. The Romantic youths wanted to be beginners again, and like the ancients they lived through, felt through the world. They wanted to remain youths, and one may scold them for it. But the world runs in periods; youths return, but greybeards too ripen. Certainly, there is immature science, but also overripe science, Alexandrian, scholastic. It can happen that the analyses of science become sterile, its boundary lines rigid, that the organic in it calcifies into the mechanical, the dead, – then it is time to descend to the Mothers and to drink oneself young at the springs of the old, ever young sages.

## Nachwort — Postscript

After the completion of this reprint I received the recently published book by E. Howald, “Die Anfänge der europäischen Philosophie,” which endeavours, in an interesting, peculiarly subjective manner, to reach an inner understanding of the early Greek thinkers and in its introduction also takes a position on our work. It sees in it first of all, in so far as it overcomes the hitherto essentially natural-scientific conception of the Presocratics, a “step forward significant for the sake of its negation,” and further finds “the fruitful element in the new knowledge” in the “growing understanding of the close affinity” of that philosophy of nature with the “tremendous religious movement” of the sixth century, that is, with the mysticism of that time. Nevertheless “in the essence of the philosophy of Thales and his disciples there are no mystical or even religious thoughts,” and the “evidential material of the adherents of the mystical idea could not withstand a strict criticism.” But where is this criticism? I have found only such as, in its rejection, entirely ignored my abundant evidential material and only too plainly showed that it knew nothing of real mysticism, or wished to know nothing, in an unmodern idiosyncrasy which surely proves nothing on the merits. As scientifically “strict criticism” I can recognize only one that had set against my repeatedly defined concept of mysticism, drawn from recognized mystics (above all as a feeling-into-one of soul, world, and God), another concept of it determined at least as “clearly” and “cautiously,” and had checked my material accordingly. But as long as those critics pass heedlessly, without explanation, over the divinization of the world attested already for Thales since Aristotle, and the permeation of the world with soul perceptible even in stones, and over all the similar doctrines of his successors, I may well remain just as [p. 160] deaf to their rejection of Presocratic mysticism, carried out purely dogmatically, without any attempt at a counter-concept of mysticism, without grounds and testimonies, as they remained blind to all my proofs. My latest, subtler critic at least indicates where, to be sure, only the classical philologist takes his idea of mysticism from. It has now been “proved that the mysticism of which one [?] thought in the very first place, the mysticism of Orpheus, gained very little foothold in Ionia.” If this is to be a counter-proof, the first reply is that universal human mysticism is, after all, as little confined to Orphism as Presocratic thought is to Ionia. Of the Pythagoreans, moreover, H. himself concedes that they moved not only later in “number-mysticism” and “mystical forms of expression,” but that already Pythagoras “comes under the influence of Orphism,” indeed that in Pythagoreanism, which “stands much closer to religion,” “we have before us a daughter-society of Orphism.” Does that not suffice for a mystical “origin”? Equally aptly the work of the chief Eleatic, Parmenides, filled with “great faith,” is then characterized: it “ranks entirely among the poems of the Orphics; not only the introduction betrays this, but inner grounds too suggest it.” And in his world-picture of the Doxa he even resorts “to myth” and gives “expression to the need of the mystes.” Even Empedocles, with whose generation alone the “de-divinization of the world” sets in, is “like Pythagoras profoundly indebted” to the Orphic sects and is a mixture of “wonder-worker, magician” and strict scientist. But could H. at least keep the Ionian thinkers free of mysticism? Soon it is said of Anaximander, who after all opens philosophical literature, that he presents “solemnly” and “mysteriously” “the excitement and sublimity of a great and impressive gospel” and proclaims doctrines of “original sin” which occur simultaneously in Orphism and “must of course have arisen in the contemporary religious atmosphere.” The other great Ionian whom H. brings to the fore, Heraclitus, too, is said to have accomplished with exuberant enthusiasm and “great fervor” “an almost impossible feat of faith.” [p. 161] His language is said to be that “of the mysteries in the widest sense; for precisely by taking aim at the ordinary mystai as ‘unholily consecrated’ he proclaims his intention to offer a purer mystery.” I have nothing to add here but thanks to my critic for having set forth so clearly himself the mystical element of the Presocratics which he denies. In mysticism I myself see a more or less religious speculation of feeling, in which the soul, in longing, soars up to unity with God and nature. H. too seeks behind the natural-scientific search for truth of the Presocratics an experience of the soul, which passionately wants to “redeem” itself to harmony in the image of the cosmos, and he recognizes in it a “thoroughly parallel process to the religious revival of this generation,” that is, to the specifically Orphic mysticism. What, then, is supposed to separate those thinkers from all mysticism? That they seek “harmony,” which H. stresses as a discovery? But according to p. 15 he also has Orphic mysticism strive for harmony, and the “divine harmony of the world,” as Kepler or J. Boehme proclaim it quite mystically, remains related to the equally “divine” world-harmony of the Presocratics even if a student of antiquity happens to have no interest in modern parallels. The “parallelism between microcosm and macrocosm,” too, which H. rightly illuminates so strongly as a “bridge between inner and outer,” I found everywhere again among the mystics (see esp. the chapters pp. 13 ff. and 53 ff.), indeed grounded in the essence of mysticism, which precisely places the soul in immediate contact with the whole of the world and the Absolute. But if H. is intent on deepening the oldest speculation above all by interpreting it again and again significantly as “symbol” and “parable” for an “irrationally” experienced “mystery,” then surely no one will call an interpretation with such marks anything but mystical, and indeed it belongs originally to “mysticism” that it offers a mystery which it gladly, indeed necessarily, expresses without justification only in symbol or parable. In such an interpretation I have at most [p. 162] to object that for the ancient Greeks it is – far too mystical. For nowhere do I declare them to be mere mystics; I wished rather only to uncover their hitherto veiled and misjudged mystical source and admixture, their “origin” from the “spirit” of mysticism, but I see them wandering into science coming from mysticism. With so much contact in the more psychic conception of the early thinkers, the objections of my newest critic seem to me to amount rather to a dispute about words, which arises from his taking the concept of mysticism in an unclarified narrowness which is burst by the doctrines of an Eckhart or Cusanus, Boehme or Baader, and other lofty figures of mysticism, in short, by history.
