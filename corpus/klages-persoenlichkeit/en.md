## Persönlichkeit — Personality

*Introduction to Characterology*

*By Ludwig Klages*

Müller & Kiepenheuer Verlag, Potsdam, and Orell Füssli Verlag, Zürich

Since an introduction to characterology cannot unfold characterology in its full breadth — even if the space available did not in any case forbid it — it seems fitting to state beforehand what principles guided the author in selecting the material. In the first place, he has preferred the more thorough treatment of a partial field to a more summary treatment of the whole domain, as is reported more precisely in the text. Next, the emphasis lies on a searching discussion of the presuppositions of characterological inquiry that belong to the science of the soul and to the science of consciousness, partly because without the most exact knowledge of these the doctrine [p. 8] of personality remains unworkable, partly in order to reject that playful treatment of the subject which believes it has achieved something with the invention — as cheap as it is useless — of "types." Finally, particular attention is given to those questions that elsewhere could only be touched upon; hence this small work would like to supplement our principal work, the Grundlagen der Charakterkunde, at least in a few respects. — Detailed elaborations of particulars have been consigned to the "Notes," and what is indispensable concerning the history of the subject will be found in the appendix under "Sources." Our chief aim has been to convince the unprejudiced reader that what is at issue here is not a matter of opinions, but of a science capable of methodical perfection.

August 1927. The Author

## I. Was Bedeutet „Charakterisieren“? — I. What Does "Characterizing" Mean?

[p. 11] One will answer the question posed in the heading by saying that to characterize means to describe a character. But since this requires concepts of character-traits, the further question arises: what are character-traits? Before we decide this, let us ward off the misunderstanding, which keeps recurring from time to time, that traits are parts of the state of affairs that bears them. According to the teachings of so-called phrenology, for instance, there were supposed to be numerous "faculties" — a sense for thievery, a sense for calculation, a sense for music — corresponding to different regions of the brain and therefore legible from the shape of the skull. That, however, could hold true only for parts of the interpreted facts. But a trait is never a part of its substrate; it is a side of it.

[p. 12] The leaves are parts of the tree and therefore detachable from it; the colour of the leaf, by contrast, is a side of the leaf, the shape another side, the veining and surface texture a third side, and everyone recognizes that each of these interpenetrates the two others and is in turn interpenetrated by them. All possible traits whatever, whether of things or of characters, can indeed be distinguished, but not separated from one another. So much to ward off the confusion of the concept of a trait with the concept of a part. This brief preliminary consideration should already have assured us that we must give an account of what character-traits are in relation to the traits of things, and hence, in the first place, what characters themselves are in relation to things. If one speaks of the constitution of the sea, one means a thing; but if one speaks of the character of the sea, one means at least something like a being. Accordingly character would be roughly: the distinguishing particularity of a being<sup>1</sup>. In this widest sense the name fits any state of affairs whatever (compare the character [p. 13] of democracy, of criminal law, of number-symbolism, and so on).

<sup>1</sup> To page 12. — This still betrays itself to some degree in the origin of the word, which, stressed on the last syllable and with an e pronounced like ä, means in ancient Greek "mark, distinguishing sign" and, coming from the verb charassein = to scratch, to incise, to imprint, actually points to written characters. Such signs, however, had in ancient times a symbolic or magical sense, which is why, for instance, a fairy tale says that mysterious "characters" had been engraved on the talisman, i.e. signs that lent it a magical vitality. In fact it is units of life and their expressions to which, in the abstract use of the word, the name was originally applied by preference — as, for instance, when, exactly as still today, one spoke of the character of a people's dialect.

We leave it an open question whether, on some way of looking at things, something truly being-like and alive might really pertain to any state of affairs whatever; for there is no doubt that by a "science of characters" we mean the characters of organic individual beings, which are called, in the borrowed term, "individuals." Individuum comes from in- (un-) and dividuum (divisible), and would thus mean, in literal translation, "the indivisible" (in Greek: atomon). Therein lies a profound insight of language. For if things, one and all, can be divided, it belongs to the nature of the living individual being to be indivisible in at least one respect. I can indeed cut a plant to pieces, or amputate a leg from an animal or a human being; but in doing so I do not likewise cut to pieces the unit of life of the plant, the animal, the human being. Even the single-celled organism, when it doubles itself by division, has thereby transformed itself, just as surely, into two units of life, each of which is in turn capable of doubling itself again. [p. 14] Beings, then, are units of life, and the unit of life is truly indivisible = individual. We translate individuum as individual being, and thereby express that it is the aliveness peculiar to it in respect of which it cannot be divided.

To be sure, every transient change of the living body is accompanied by a transient, and every lasting change by a lasting, change of the soul, and, strictly speaking, the reverse holds too; but whereas, for instance, every mutilation — say, castration — by severing an organ from it, also divides the body, at least in part, the polar change of the soul, and hence of its character, that belongs with it is always of a purely qualitative nature and not in the remotest way comparable to a cutting-to-pieces. Just so crude an example may set drastically before our eyes that the study of the soul, because wholly concerned with the world of qualities, differs in aim and method fundamentally from what have hitherto been called the natural sciences, which were and are bent on imputing to the world at large, disregarding its appearance for the sake of rendering [p. 15] calculable all the particular processes that can tolerably be circumscribed, a "system" in which, in the end, there are only two kinds of states of affairs: displacements and quanta. Unwittingly led on a leash by a tendency we shall shortly come to speak of, people have squandered on this, for some three centuries, more acuteness and genius than on the great cultural deeds of as many millennia before, taken together. But even one who held what has undoubtedly been achieved — namely, mankind's mastery of the earth through the human will — to be a form of knowledge, which in fact it is not, would have to learn to renounce this genus of knowledge, together with its preliminaries, before even the slightest successes could blossom for him on the ground of the study of the soul.

As already indicated, individual beings fall into three great realms: that of plants, that of animals, that of human beings; and the science of characters accordingly falls into the characterology of plants, of animals, and of human beings. Since only the characters of the human being concern us here, we must ask whether the human character is distinguished [p. 16] from the character of plants and especially of animals by a general mark. For historical man, whom we chiefly have in mind, such a mark has long since been recognized: he alone, of all beings, has I-consciousness, and he alone is therefore called a person. Accordingly the human character, or personality, would have to be defined as the individual self, and characterology would have to do with the traits of personal individual beings<sup>2</sup>. With this we have, for the purposes of scientific inquiry, rejected a narrower sense of the word character on the one hand and of the word personality on the other, but we do not therefore wish to let slip the pointers that this narrower sense is able to give. When Goethe writes in Tasso: "A talent forms itself in stillness, / A character in the stream of the world," he has, with the help of the word "talent," distinguished the scope of the spiritual faculties from "character" as the domain of the personal will, and has thus [p. 17] reserved the name character for the stratum of personal mainsprings, with which we shall concern ourselves more closely in what follows. This restriction, well compatible with a usage of language still alive today, is otherwise also maintained by him, though in express opposition to the still further narrowing usage that credits the constitution of the will only with morally high-grade mainsprings, and locates the ground of firmness of conviction and constancy to principle exclusively in the virtues of the will; according to which the bearer of such virtues would have to be called "full of character," and one lacking them "characterless." Against this, Goethe declares in his remarks on Newton that character consists in a personality persisting in its own manner amid constant traits, and letting nothing turn it aside from this. Further: "The chief mark of character is decided willing, without regard to right and wrong, good and evil, truth and error." Both restrictions alike, however, point to the fact that the personal dispositions of will are of paramount importance for [p. 18] the appraisal of character. — With the likewise distinguishing special use of personality, on the other hand, people wished to emphasize the self-sufficiency and independence with which strong and distinctive characters tend to resist conventional custom. This usage, still perceptible today but on the wane, corresponded to the disposition, oriented toward "freedom," of the so-called Sturm und Drang period of the eighteenth century, and has found a late echo in the verses of Goethe's old age, only partly congenial to us today: "Folk and bondsman and conqueror, / They confess at every time: / The highest fortune of earth's children / Is personality alone."

<sup>2</sup> To page 16. — Kant, for example, opens the first part of his "Anthropologie" with the words: "That man can have the I in his representation raises him infinitely above all other beings living on earth. Thereby he is a person, and by virtue of the unity of consciousness through all the changes that may befall him, one and the same person — that is, a being wholly distinct, by rank and dignity, from things, such as the non-rational animals are, with which one may deal and dispose as one pleases." Ever since Romantic psychology, and Carus in particular, pointed out that "person" derives from personare — to sound through — and originally denoted the mask of the ancient actor, then also his role, and only later what one today understands by "person," this has often been repeated, because even without clear knowledge of it one could not shake the feeling that personality is the fateful fusion-product of two elements: one fully living element (= the It, life) and one element living, if not merely apparently so, then at least in a wholly different sense (= the I, spirit). It is worth examining very thoroughly the speaking mask of the ancient stage, and indeed the mask in general among all "natural peoples," for its contributions to psychology. Here we may further refer to our essay "Über den Begriff der Persönlichkeit" in "Mensch und Erde" (Diederichs Verlag, Jena). Finally, let it not go unmentioned that the difference thereby established between man as a rational being (= homo sapiens) and the animal by no means coincides with the event that released original man from animality, i.e. a man who precedes rational man by hundreds of thousands of years. We have expressed ourselves on this at several places, thus in "Wesen des Bewußtseins," in "Ausdrucksbewegung und Gestaltungskraft," in the "Grundlagen der Charakterkunde." We turn once more to the difference between character-traits and the traits of things. Things are divisible insofar as they are lifeless; beings are indivisible insofar as they are alive. Lifelessness means, among other things, that no inner ground [p. 19] of change dwells in the lifeless; aliveness is uninterrupted happening and cannot be thought without the co-assumption of an inner ground of unceasing transformation. I describe a billiard ball sufficiently by stating its shape, size, material, surface quality, weight; I can add the properties of the ivory of which it consists, such as hardness, specific gravity, behaviour under fluctuations of temperature, and so on; I can finally also depict its chemistry, and with all this I have pronounced just as many laws of its behaviour under specifiable conditions. Of a being, by contrast, I never know in advance with certainty how it will behave later on the basis of its present traits, because, in consequence of its aliveness, its later traits can no longer be exactly identical with those correctly ascertained at present. The concept of a trait itself thus changes when it is transferred from things to beings. To the lawfulness of its working under given conditions there is added, in the concept of the character-trait, a principle of [p. 20] transformation — or, as one prefers to say, of development — which can only ever be broken down into laws approximately, and never without remainder.

As is well known, there has been much dispute over whether character is innate or acquired. Some maintain its innateness, others its acquiredness, still others let it consist of traits partly innate, partly acquired. The dispute collapses once one reflects clearly on the content of the word "character-trait." If I investigate the character of a thirty-year-old man, the traits peculiar to him are of very different degrees of originality, and there is not the slightest doubt that I would find partly different traits had their bearer grown up, say from his third year of life on, under environmental influences as opposite as possible<sup>3</sup>. But if I now examine two thirtyyear-old men — twins, say — whose environmental influences, from the first day of their life, were as similar to one another as possible, it is not excluded that I should nonetheless encounter two extraordinarily different characters. [p. 21] That, however, means: both have, among other things, acquired different traits, inasmuch as they already brought with them different traits, in consequence of which each was compelled to answer very similar environmental influences with a "development" peculiar to himself alone. Character is as surely innate as the body of its bearer is, and to character there belongs, just as surely, an again individual principle of changeability, as such a principle belongs to the living body of its bearer.

<sup>3</sup> To page 20. — The, however superficial, yet most striking proof of this is the process of learning. Whenever we have learned something new, we are in some respect changed. For otherwise nothing could now, in principle, ever become present to our consciousness that had not been present to it before.

There is no general consideration that would allow us to grade character-traits categorically by degrees of originality. We may, with good reason, presuppose that a ten-year-old child, transplanted into such-and-such surroundings, will develop in such-and-such a respect differently than under environmental influences as contrary as possible; but we know this only after — the trial has actually taken place. Research efforts aimed at the range of variability of character-traits are in the most primitive beginnings, and may hope for any success only if they rest, without the least prejudice, on [p. 22] experience . It is beyond dispute that the most original traits are also the most persistent. But whoever, for that reason — with Schopenhauer, say — held the so-called moral character to be the unchangeable core of personality would sometimes have to convince himself that, in the course of a human life, the interests can traverse an unexpectedly large scope of development, whereas the far more peripheral "temperament" for the most part shows only a gradual decline of liveliness in advanced age, but otherwise remains the same for everyone. It is further established that the first seven years of life shape character more deeply and more lastingly than the following seventy taken together. And it is finally established that supposed changes of character are, in by far the greater number of cases, no changes at all, but speak only of differences in the forms in which one and the same trait is realized under a thoroughgoing change in the conditions of its unfolding. Thus the excess of the drinker's habit is transposed into fanatical exaggerations of abstinence once the drinker has left [p. 23] a Salvation Army meeting as a "convert" . Tertullian, that fiery scion of African desert-conflagrations, remained by his own account "a man of strife and of flames" even after, at thirty-five, he had transformed himself from a Punic heathen into the fanatical Christian. And many a one who resolved to do it quite differently next time has afterward had to confess that he did exactly the same thing again, merely with a deceptive avoidance of a few externals! But if, for that reason, one did not wish to do an injustice to those who insist on the assumption of a metaphysical core that nothing can change, we must nonetheless confess that no one has hitherto been able to mark it out beyond challenge. —

But even if we disregarded the extraordinary difficulties that the investigation of its course of development brings with it in respect of character, all efforts directed to that end would still be labour lost if undertaken before character itself had been investigated. How am I to give an account of the [p. 24] origin of a character-trait if I do not know whether what I am speaking of is a character-trait at all, and how could I ever come to clarity about this otherwise than by examining the characterological occurrences at the hand of the manifestations they actually present! The inclination, grounded in the character of the age, to comprehend everything existent from the point of view of its having-become and consequently of its having-arisen out of some other existent, is, for characterology, a hopeless wrong path for as long as the existent itself has not been ascertained down to the ground; for indeed almost everything that has hitherto been gathered along the path in question tells only of its discoverers' belief in a "perfectibility" that is called upon to flatter the wishes for self-justification of the human will to power.

## II. Charakterologische Stammbegriffe — II. Fundamental Concepts of Characterology

[p. 26] [p. 27] We cannot reflect on the soul in general, on that of the human being in particular, and hence also not on the differences among human souls, without relying in doing so on the general usage of language. If, for instance, we find thinking, feeling, willing dealt with chapter by chapter in a textbook of psychology, we should never forget that it was not the author of the textbook who discovered thinking, feeling, willing, but that he has at best rediscovered them — where he has not merely assumed them to exist because language names and distinguishes them. And if we attribute to someone more than ordinary ambition, we do well to remember that we speak of ambition only because the name for it stands at our [p. 28] disposal. This holds, in the end, of all the states of affairs we speak of — of green, say, or cat, or space; but whereas we can point to intuitable contents of meaning, it is always necessary, as against the states and properties of the soul that are in and of themselves unintuitable, to reflect back on the content of the names, if we do not wish to run the risk of accepting mere ways of looking at things, unexamined, as facts.

If we now examine the words, numbering in the thousands, that language places at our disposal for inner states, processes, and properties, we soon come upon a very manifold group whose members only seem to speak of character-traits, but in truth voice value-judgments on human behaviour from the standpoint of one's fellow man. "Honesty," for instance, looks like a character-trait, but is in truth nothing of the kind, as the following consideration reveals. If we call someone honest, this at first only states that he is not dishonest — that is, that he does not deliberately deceive other people, [p. 29] whether with words or with works and deeds. But evidently very different character-traits can guarantee such a restriction of the workings of universal human egoism: in one case, say, the fear of possible reprisals (the penal code); in another, the ambition always to appear in the best light; in a third, the already more sublimated sense of honour, most closely bound up with self-esteem; in a fourth, the sense of truthfulness<sup>4</sup>; in a fifth, a far-reaching lack of self-interest, which may again point back to strongly overemphasized objective interests, and so forth. All these persons, and many others, can display honesty to a high degree without their characters, for that reason, needing to resemble one another, as one notices. Honesty is consequently not a character-trait. — With this we already know that moral classifications must never be confused with characterological classifications. Even if the greatest differences of opinion did not exist, according to periods and peoples, as to what is to be called "good" and [p. 30] what "evil," a classification according to good and evil would still be just as unfruitful for the student of the soul as a classification of plants into edible and inedible would be for the botanist. Whoever cannot set aside his moral standards of value, at least provisionally, is lost to the study of character.

<sup>4</sup> To page 29. — What we have here called the sense of truthfulness is called, in everyday speech, the sense of truth. In general one may, even in scholarship, retain this compound. It is advisable at times, however, to remember that someone's "good will" to speak, act, and conduct himself in accordance with truth has not the slightest thing to do with whether or not he — hits upon the truth. I can, despite passionate striving after truth, that is, with the greatest personal truthfulness, be exposed to grave deceptions and errors, and I can possess diplomatic skill, that is, talent for more subtle lying, and at the same time be capable, to any degree whatever, of making discoveries. "So learn, then," Nietzsche rightly admonishes in the "Morgenröte," "that truth is proved otherwise than truthfulness, and that the latter is by no means an argument for the former."

If we now sift out all the designations that serve only to judge human action in relation to the weal and woe of one's fellow man, the remainder fall into several classes whose members indeed have direct relations to one another, but not to the members of the other classes. We single out from among them, as the most important, five: material (matter), texture (structure), nature (quality), architecture (tectonics), dispositions of bearing (aspect).

*I. Material (Matter).* — By material we understand all the capacities of the personality, such as memory, power of apprehension, acuteness, forgetfulness, strength of will, sensitivity, dullness, and so on. Instead of capacities one may, if one likes, [p. 31] also say gifts or talents, but must then always remain aware that there are gifts of feeling and of will just as much as of understanding. The capacities drastically expose the error of the metaphor, coined by the Stoa and taken up again by the English "sensationalists," of the "smooth tablet" (= tabula rasa), by which it was meant that human beings were "by nature" all equal to one another, and that it was only education and environment that first created their differences. To be sure, no one brings "innate ideas" with him, but everyone does bring dispositions, in consequence of which he is compelled to experience the world differently from others, and again dispositions in consequence of which he makes different stores of his experiences mentally his own. If one nonetheless wishes to win something from that metaphor, let one bear in mind that the character of one person would then resemble a wax tablet, that of a second a wooden tablet, that of a third a stone tablet, and so on, since the "traces" of environmental influences will turn out extremely different in strength, form, and durability.

What the gifts more precisely amount to, any example [p. 32] whatever can teach us. — There are two people who have the lively wish to become piano virtuosos, or mathematicians, or athletes. Both practise and learn for the same length of time and with the same zeal; but after a year has elapsed, the one has each time got much further than the other, thanks, namely, to his gift! For this very reason there is also, for each person, a personally characteristic upper limit of his capacity for achievement, which he no longer exceeds by prolonging practice. From our examples it is already clear that gifts are quantitative traits, which can be expressed symbolically by numbers. One has more or less memory, more or less strength of will, more or less sensitivity, and so forth. In their totality they signify, as it were, a natural capital with which one can "work," or which one can "fritter away"; hence the "material" of character. — The decisive word on the relation of this capital to what one does with it is found in Goethe: "Capacities [p. 33] are presupposed; they are to become proficiencies"<sup>5</sup>. II. Texture (Structure). — Among the properties of texture belong such as: full of temperament and lacking temperament, mobile and immobile, excitable and unexcitable. As one sees, there is no direct relation between these and the properties of gift. Whether, for instance, a strong memory goes better with much or with little temperament cannot be gathered from the properties in question. For here we evidently have in view dispositions that determine, above all, the course of inner processes, and it is natural to think of these as depending on, so to speak, the density of the soul-substance (hence the "texture" of character). Unlike the gifts, they can always be determined by the ratio of a magnitude of impulse to a magnitude of resistance, and thus symbolically by quotients. We therefore call them relational properties<sup>6</sup>.

<sup>5</sup> To page 32. — After the explanations of the first chapter we scarcely need repeat that there are no quantity-properties, in the strict and proper sense of the natural-scientific usage of the word, in the science of man. If we ascribe (rightly) a strong memory to one person and a weak one to another, we have unquestionably lent the former a plus of memory relative to the latter. Yet closer examination shows that even this difference in truth denotes a difference of kind; which is why in our "Grundlagen der Charakterkunde" we have characterized all properties of aptitude qualitatively. Both in that work and especially in "Wesen des Bewußtseins" it is set forth why one felt induced to take certain differences of state for differences of degree and, in consequence, even for differences of quantity, and what in general grounds the practice of comparison in domains that admit neither counting nor measuring. The proper sense of our "quantity-properties," however, in relation to the remaining groups, is that all the consequences of these properties accessible to outside observation are of a purely quantitative character.

<sup>6</sup> To page 33. — In our "Grundlagen der Charakterkunde" we have devoted an extensive chapter, the seventh, entitled "Das Gefüge des Charakters," to the properties of structure and especially to the theory of temperament. In that work, however, as scarcely needs saying, the whole of characterology is treated, and in particular also, in detail, the theory of personal aptitudes (= the material of character).

*III. Nature (Quality).* — Two human beings are of different nature insofar as these goals appear desirable to the one, those to the other. If, in considering nature, we fix our eye on the [p. 34] constitution of feeling in which it is grounded that one thing is striven for and another shunned, then the properties belonging here are dispositions of feeling; but if we fix our eye on the accompanying activity of the will, then they are mainsprings, or "interests." — If we further consider what distinguishes, say, ambition from acquisitiveness, and again each of the two from generosity, it is at the very least the direction of the willing. For this reason we call the mainsprings directional properties.

*IV. Architecture (Tectonics).* — To the architecture of character belong all the properties by which the mutual relation of the properties of the aforementioned groups — indeed of all possible groups whatever — expresses itself. Thus we call someone unified or harmonious when his various properties further one another, disunified or even rent when they disturb one another. Unity, firmness, pronouncedness, and their opposites belong among the original dispositions no differently [p. 35] than do all the remaining properties. V. Dispositions of Bearing (Aspect). — When we say that someone is forward, or impudent, or talkative, or shy, or servile, or exuberant, or swaggering, we undoubtedly have properties of his character in mind, but in doing so we think in the first instance of their outward side. It is not traits of being as such that are designated by such words, but traits of being insofar as they furnish the ground for a characteristic manner of behaving. Of such properties of demeanour, or such properties of intercourse, there are, on the testimony of language, countless numbers; their investigation, however, is still in its beginnings. — It is of interest to know that empirical "types" emerge precisely in the domain of the dispositions of bearing, and that the inclination to think typologically has historically taken its point of departure from there (Aristotle, Theophrastus). Thus there is, for instance, an occupational cast of demeanour that one readily perceives in the typical corps-student, officer, pastor, traveller, doorman, and so on. There is, further, a typical bearing of certain social strata, which can characterize particular periods just as much as particular [p. 36] nationalities. One thinks of the "dandy," the "flâneur," and above all of the specifically English gentleman. Despite everything that has been advanced about the lastnamed, from the most various sides and in part with extreme aptness, the psychology of the "gentleman" has not yet been written!

From the purely theoretical standpoint all character-traits are of equal importance; from the standpoint of practice, by contrast, those of the third group have by far the greatest significance! It is they, without doubt, to which Wallenstein's famous utterance in Schiller's drama of the same name refers:

Have I once searched out the core of the man, / I know as well his willing and his deed. In view of the fact that we must impose great restrictions upon ourselves in this introduction, we consider it more fitting to offer at least a somewhat comprehensive conception of the mainsprings and their presuppositions than a mere "sample" of all the groups. We therefore treat exclusively the psychology of the third group.

## III. Von den Stimmungen und Wallungen — III. On Moods and Surges of Emotion

[p. 38] [p. 39] That someone follows a mainspring whenever he acts out of self-interest, or love of strife, or craving for distinction, is clear to us; but that the mainspring in question should at the same time be a disposition of feeling is less readily apparent to us. Conversely, the very name itself seems already to point to a distinctive disposition of feeling when someone omits something out of a "sense of duty," whereas it may perhaps give us pause to call the sense of duty a mainspring. Yet how could there be, for instance, a craving for distinction, if a feeling of satisfaction did not accompany distinctions for its bearer, and, still more, a sensitive displeasure accompany the failure of an expected distinction to appear and the perception of preferred rivals! Consequently there belongs to the [p. 40] craving for distinction a receptivity of feeling for specific fulfilments, which for that very reason become, for the bearer of the property, preferred goals of his wishing and acting. It is, to be sure, fundamentally mistaken intellectualism when the Stoic Chrysippus declares: "Affects are judgments"; but his reasoning — "for avarice consists in the assumption that money is something beautiful" — nonetheless harbours the insight that the property of avarice would be unthinkable had the consciousness of possessing money not, in the avaricious man, the feeling-tone of pleasantness. — On the other hand, the sense of duty and conscientiousness reveal themselves readily as mainsprings, inasmuch as they can prescribe definite modes of action to their possessor. But conscious and willed omissions, too, count among actions; and so the sense of duty, conscience, moral strictness, and further also firmness of conviction, love of justice, constancy to one's convictions, and so on, are real mainsprings, even if predominantly inhibitory mainsprings. We do not wish, however, to content ourselves with examples, but shall assure ourselves, by an [p. 41] analysis of the feelings, that every disposition of feeling is necessarily a basis of impulse.

Present-day psychology distinguishes sense-experiences, which it calls "sensations," from psychic experiences of narrower meaning, which it calls "feelings" — such as hope, expectation, joy, fear, anger, indignation, astonishment, and so on. We leave it an open question here whether quite different sides of the process of lived experience are not also to be distinguished besides these, but we lay weight on the fact that with this, admittedly, two sides of lived experience are circumscribed, according to which no sense-experience is met with without some alteration of the overall feeling, and, conversely, no feeling without a perceptible modification of the state of the body — even though, to be sure, the one need scarcely be noticed together with the other for that reason. We need only recall somewhat extreme cases to become aware of the inseparability of both sides of the life-process. Whoever has a strong preference for the colour blue will perceive a deep blue with decided pleasure, a glaring red with a slight [p. 42] displeasure; and whoever finds himself for a longer time in a state of grave ill-humour and joyless dejection will no longer be able to avoid noting various internal bodily discomforts, which also announce themselves outwardly in increasing pallor and emaciation.

Here, to be sure, the intimacy of the connection between soul and body differs for the various feelings, and one can without difficulty mark off from one another: predominantly sensuous, predominantly psychic, and predominantly spiritual feelings. The researcher's feeling of joy over the finally successful solution of a problem long wooed in vain is of predominantly spiritual nature; the feeling of wistfulness at the thought of the lost paradise of childhood is of predominantly psychic nature; wherefore in both cases it sometimes presents difficulties to ascertain the alterations of the body that cling to such feelings too. The feeling of hunger or of thirst, by contrast, is so very much of a sensuous nature that its mood-character is not seldom drowned out by internal bodily sensations. [p. 43] — Yet such complications do not prevent us from attending to each of the various sides of the life-process on its own, just as we also have to attend to the various sides of character on their own, provided we are minded to investigate character. Here, then, we are concerned exclusively with the feelings.

In any feeling whatever, for example the one just already cited, that of hunger, intensity or strength stands out against quality or nature. We feel more or less hunger, more or less thirst — that is, we perform, on two qualitatively (= innature) different feelings, one and the same grading of their strength, thereby clearly distinguishing this from the feeling- "colours" or feeling-"tones." This may at once instruct us concerning the much-disputed difference between feelings and surges of emotion (also called "affects" or "emotions"). Every feeling becomes a surge of emotion the more its colour recedes behind its strength; every surge of emotion becomes a feeling the more its strength recedes behind the colour, to which we shall henceforth give the name "mood." Where mood predominates in a feeling, we speak simply of feelings; [p. 44] where, however, the strength predominates, we speak simply of surges of emotion. Feelings in the narrower sense, or moods, would accordingly be: for example, low spirits, grief, exhaustion, discontent, cheerfulness; surges of emotion would be: for example, fright, consternation, surprise, an overflow of joy, anger.

From words like surge of emotion or emotion [Gemütsbewegung] we hear, as it were, directly what the intensity of feeling actually amounts to. For inner states are thereby named with the help of their coming to light in movements of the living body. Now since a movement-impulse underlies bodily movement, it is evidently the strength of the impulse that we have in view when we speak of violent surges of emotion. Every feeling is qualitatively distinctive thanks to the peculiarity of its mood, and every feeling can be graded by degrees of intensity thanks to the increasability of an impulse inseparable from it. This impulse, to be sure, we must grasp now as a cause of acceleration, now as a cause of retardation. The dejected man feels differently "disposed" than the elated [p. 45] man; but with the mood of elation there goes at once an increase of impulse and a readiness for movement, with the mood of dejection a diminution of impulse and a disinclination for activity. In relation to strong joy, a stronger joy is an enlarged impulse-force; in relation to strong depression, by contrast, a stronger depression is a diminished impulse-force.

So far we have considered the impulse only with regard to whether it accelerates or retards movements; but every impulse also has, in addition, a direction. In mechanical impulses we determine this by their angle with another direction; in vital impulses, by their goal, to be grasped in general terms. Anger, for instance, aims at annihilation, astonishment at orientation, joy at making happy, inclination at union with the object of inclination, aversion at warding-off, fear at flight, admiration at the elevation (of its object), and so forth; hence, in accordance with the inherent direction of the stirring, we have to call anger the urge to annihilate, astonishment the urge to orient, joy the urge to bestow, inclination [p. 46] the urge to unite, aversion the urge to ward off, fear an urge to flight, admiration an urge to elevate. From this it follows, in the first place, that the impulse-side of feeling too partakes in the mood of feeling; for in the urge to annihilate something different is felt than in the urge to unite. But it was further made clear by this in what way every disposition of feeling is a basis of impulse. If someone is capable of envy to a high degree, he will not only fall into the mood of envy more often and on slighter occasions than the less envious person, but he will also, as often as it occurs, be compelled to experience that stirring which is bent on the diminishment of another's goods of fortune. If, however, he need by no means already behave in accordance with his envy, that is only because stronger stirrings contend with it in him. Everyone believes he sees that the strength of a feeling admits of grading, even though very few have brought it to consciousness that the concept of strength (intensity) could never have arisen without the state of affairs, experienceable by us, of [p. 47] moving "forces." By contrast, it tends to arouse astonishment when one adds that something gradable occurs in mood as well. For just as, at equal luminosity, we can distinguish degrees of saturation in the quality of red, so too, in two mood-colours, degrees of depth. Beside deep feelings there are shallow feelings, and everyone can recall that he experienced now deep, now shallow, feelings, even if he should designate the feelings experienced by one and the same name. Beside deep inclination stands shallow inclination, deep anger beside shallow anger, deep pleasure beside shallow pleasure. Feelings differing wholly in nature, moreover, not seldom differ quite extraordinarily as to their degree of depth. The highly characteristic state of impatience, or of curiosity, or of boredom, will never attain the depth of mood that the state of longing, or of wistfulness, or of renunciation can at least attain. For those still unpractised in systematic self-reflection, we shall clarify this by setting strength and depth side by side.

[p. 48] A deep feeling can at once be violently stirred but also little stirred; a violent surge of emotion at once deeply upheaving, but also extremely shallow. No one will ascribe depth to the violent vexation he had to undergo; no one will call his deep being-moved at the sight of the glowing red evening sun sinking into the sea a violent agitation — whereas the stormy feeling of happiness of two lovers finding each other again after a long separation displays depth and violence together. — Finally, let it be mentioned that, for reasons belonging to the philosophy of language, whose discussion would lead too far here, "depth" occurs in numerous cases as synonymous with "height." "Deep enthusiasm" means essentially the same as "high enthusiasm," "deep joy" as "high joy," "deep veneration" as "high veneration."

## IV. Triebe und Interessen — IV. Drives and Interests

[p. 50] [p. 51] Although we shall in general speak of mainsprings and not of interests, we have nonetheless, in the heading, preferred the borrowed word interest, in order to avoid the resemblance in name to "drive." The failure to distinguish drives from interests is a chief source of numerous inadequacies in contemporary psychology, and especially of the fruitlessness of its attempts at a science of character. That the two words mean different things admits of no doubt. Of the merchant who acts out of acquisitiveness, it is correctly said that he safeguarded his interests; but no one will call the sexual drive of the bull a sexual interest, hunger an interest in eating, thirst an interest in drinking, the migratory drive of birds of [p. 52] passage an interest in locomotion, the drive of swans toward their element an interest in swimming! Granted, then, that we agree to render "interest" in German as "mainspring," drives and mainsprings are different from one another.

Insofar as the human being too belongs among the animals, he has drives as they do; insofar as he is, besides, an I-being, he has, alongside these, mainsprings or interests. To the question of what drives are, the answer, if not exhaustive, is at least sufficient: vital causes of movement (= causes of vital movements). Even had what is essential for us not already been supplied in the previous chapter, it would go beyond the scope of an introduction to set forth at length in what respect vital movement differs from mechanical movement. Moreover, the wholly indispensable minimum that we must present is already something decisive. Mechanical movements occur thanks to an impelling "force"; in the context of the organism, by contrast, such "forces" are always determined by a goal. If a tile falls from a roof, this happens out of the "force [p. 53] of attraction of the earth," or (with regard to the reciprocity of effects) out of general gravitation; and it does not happen because the tile, instead of hanging on the roof, had striven to lie on the ground. But if a thirsting animal sets itself in motion in order to find a watering-place, it is the goal — namely, union with the water through the act of drinking — that has occasioned the movement. And it is always the same in like manner. If, for instance, the stag pursued by the huntsmen flees, he is striving to escape the huntsmen. One must only take care not to confuse the goal-determinateness, which no vital movement lacks, with knowledge of the goal. Have we not seen, in the previous chapter, that every feeling has a stirring proper to it whose direction is to be understood only from the goal! When the storks set out in autumn from East Prussia for Africa, no phantasm of the future settlingplaces hovers before them; rather, they merely follow the awakening migratory drive — which, however, has no less a characteristic goal than thirst has its goal in the drinking of water.

[p. 54] We may also put it this way: the drive is, among other things, felt privation-suffering, and this has the peculiar gift of developing out of itself movements that find their end in the removal of the privation. It follows from this that we already think the word 'cause' differently than physics does. Mechanical causes are (calculable) conditions for the occurrence of an event; vital causes, by contrast, are producing causes. The concept of the producing cause was created by antiquity; the concept of cause as mere condition was created by the modern age, in the wake of Galileo and Newton. If one asks how vital (= producing) causes are possible at all, the remark must suffice here that they point back to connections between the soul of the individual being and the images of the world. Man too, as noted, has drives; but these are so subordinated to his mainsprings that they can, throughout, be realized only with the latter's help. — Let us assume that someone raised here, in anticipation, the following objection: in the [p. 55] previous chapter it was demonstrated to us, concerning human dispositions of feeling, that each of them is necessarily a basis of impulse and thus deserves to be called a mainspring; but how, then, could the mainspring be different from the drive, when, as it seems, the very same thought is required for characterizing the drives? We answer: because in countless cases man, and only man, arrives at the fulfilling goal of his stirring no longer directly but only indirectly, namely by the detour of willing. Almost all the further expositions that follow will serve at once as a more exact elucidation of this sentence, whose grounding we for now only prepare.

The conceptual definition of the mainspring runs: mainsprings are causes of will. But since what matters here is not the magnitude of the exertion of will, not the ease or difficulty of the decision, nor equally that of its execution — all of which would be matters of aptitude — but solely the decisive goal, one says more precisely: every mainspring is the dispositional condition of a direction of will. That is something fundamentally [p. 56] different from a cause of movement. Whenever the attempt has hitherto been made to interpret the will, the analogy of the cause of movement has always hovered before the mind, and the consequence of this was that, down to the present day, no doctrine of the will could arise. The achievement of the will is to set purposes and to hold fast, in the direction of the purpose, a life of movement available to it. Just as it is by no means the sergeant's marching order that sets the soldier's legs in motion, but rather his 'living force', so likewise it is not the inner command (namely, of the will) but an effective driving force of vitality by which action comes about; and just as the marching order does indeed show the marching soldier the direction, so exactly does the inner command bind the impulsive movement to a punctual goal, which stems from it no more than the marching order stems from the soldier's will. Were we to compare the impulse to the wind that blows into the sails, the will would resemble the rudder; and were we to compare the impulse to the movement of the arm performed by a marksman [p. 57] 'aiming' his rifle, the will would resemble the taking of aim, which, as everyone knows, is bent on avoiding even the slightest wavering. It is no accident that the word 'Zweck' [purpose], which originally meant the peg at the centre of the target, has come to designate exclusively the goal of the will! Far from generating movements, the will acts upon an impulsive movement that has been deprived of its natural goal, steering or regulating it by binding it to the direction of the now 'prescribed' purpose. How is this possible?

It is possible only because a new power enters into the nexus of individual life — namely that power through which it becomes an I-being: its name is spirit. All that lives is ensouled; but man alone is, in addition, spirited — or, more precisely, only historical man, including the immediately prehistoric segment of his unhistorical existence. Spirit stands in such opposition to life that it acts, under all circumstances, at least restrictively upon life's unfolding. Let a simile make this [p. 58] vivid too. We compare the drives to a blazing flame that gives light and spreads warmth. If it is now to drive a machine, we must first build the machine and further see to it that the flame's outflow of heat is confined and used as completely as possible to warm the water in the boiler. If we add further (with which, admittedly, the simile is sprung) that the machine too was somehow won from the fire's heat, it would correspond entirely to the life-coupled spirit, whose effect on the drives would consequently consist in expending part of their energy to regulate the other part, for the sake of realizing the conceptual purpose. Insofar as this actually happens, we no longer speak of drives but of mainsprings or interests, and no longer of driven impulses but of purposeful acts of will. — The example of the machine may at once clarify the addition 'purposeful' for us. The flame blazes and shines and warms; but only insofar as it heats the boiler is it capable — at least indirectly, that is, with the machine's help — of performing, [p. 59] countless times, one and the same piece of work. Drives stir, grow, satisfy themselves and die away, whether in response to impressions or out of inner 'stimuli'; but only that part of them which has fallen under the sway of the will, and has thereby been transformed into an interest, works for years and even decades toward the attainment of one and the same purpose. An animal can flee, seek food, store up winter provisions, brood its eggs, traverse thousands of kilometres: its movements and behaviours are nevertheless always directed as immediately as possible toward the goal inherent in its stirring. But what no animal, and only will-endowed man, is capable of, is this: to carry out movements that serve only to make possible other movements, of which the same holds true, and so on through an unsurveyable series of intermediate links all the way to a purpose lying far in the future.

A young man enters a business, penniless, as an apprentice, with the burning wish himself to become an owner of the business one day. A decade may pass, or more, before fulfilment [p. 60] comes to him. Meanwhile he has carried out a thousand upon a thousand movements which, standing in no connection with his (vital) drives, would have been senseless had they not had the significance of just as many intermediate links in the service of realizing a resolve that stood fixed already at the beginning of all those years! Only such movements rightly bear the name of action. But that is not all: man can pursue purposes, and does so countless times, whose realization lies far beyond the bounds of his own life. When a nature-loving landowner, pained by the destruction of the primeval forests, establishes an easement providing that the trees he plants on a tract of five hundred acres may never be felled, he certainly knows that he himself will never get to see the trunks of at least fifty metres' height whose realization he is striving for, but at most his great-grandchildren will. And when a statesman who plans to double the population of the country pushes through, at the age of sixty, a law that fixes a [p. 61] high extra tax for bachelors but a bonus for all families with more than five children, he likewise knows that only coming generations will experience the consequences of his measure. Over what stretches of time, finally, do great founders of religions consciously span the bow of their will, even though their "community", during their own lifetime, forms a vanishing minority, and that too mostly within a contemporary society that usually looks down with scorn and contempt on the innovator and his band of disciples<sup>7</sup> Should one, in view of all this, consider it possible that the cause of action, that is, the mainspring, could ever have been confused with the cause of the impulsive movement, that is, the drive!

<sup>7</sup> To page 61. — This must of course not be confused with the fact that the drives of animals, e.g. the mating drive, likewise work forward into the future by virtue of their consequences; for such consequences are, after all, not known! When the female mosquito lays her eggs in the water in order to leave them there to their fate, surely no one is bold enough to imagine that she has reflected that the brood thrives only in the moist (although she herself must avoid the moist!), and that she is entertaining interesting thoughts generally about the future of mosquito-kind! To willing, however, belongs the conscious positing of the goal, belongs intention; where this is lacking, willing is lacking. — We say the same thing, only from another point of view, by pointing out that all drives bear the stamp of the species, all volitions the stamp of the person.

## V. Zur Metaphysik des Willens — V. On the Metaphysics of the Will

[p. 64] [p. 65] Goethe's Faust, engaged in translating the 'Holy Scripture', falls into doubt over the meaning of even its first sentence, until, after several attempts that do not quite satisfy him, he resolves to write down: In the beginning was the deed. In keeping with this, the Mosaic creation myth has the world called forth out of nothing by the command of a God. Never has a man experienced, and never will a man experience, that a command, without the assistance of otherwise available powers, could move a stone, let alone create it; and at the "beginning" it was certainly not the deed, because a living and even a spirited body must be present before a deed [p. 66] can be accomplished. All the same, both these data — the Mosaic creation myth and the beginning of Faust's translation — have a profound meaning, one first disclosed by those medieval interpreters of church dogma who, in the sharpest opposition to the whole mode of thought of antiquity, defined spirit as "pure act" and accordingly supposed its essence to be found not in the intellect but in the will<sup>8</sup>. Since then a psychology of 'acts' has developed ever more decisively, even though only a single thinker so far has been able to add to its inherent tendency the fulfilling discovery<sup>9</sup>. This is not the place to offer an investigation of the nature of acts ; but even<sup>10</sup> without it, what we need from this for characterology should become intelligible. — There are two genuine acts, no more and no fewer: the act of apprehension and the act of will. Now at least one act of apprehension must have taken place for an act of will to take place; but it is only from this act — and here those Scholastics were right — that we ascertain the nature of the spiritual deed as such. Let us consider first of all [p. 67] the most original form of the act of apprehension, the act of perception, and let us suppose the case that the object of perception forces itself upon us; then we are undoubtedly unable to establish in ourselves anything resembling an activity. Whoever, out for a walk, stumbles over a jutting pavingstone and is consequently forced, willy-nilly, to notice it, has his perception so thoroughly extorted from him that he would certainly dispute it, were one to try to demonstrate to him that such noticing had required a deed of his spirit. And whoever — following an unmethod that is happily on the wane today — insisted on admitting, of psychic and spiritual states of affairs, only what he could seize by means of so-called selfobservation or introspection, would probably never be convinced of the act-nature of his processes of apprehension. Before we show that there is nevertheless a means of preparing his change of mind, let us consider a consequence of the act of apprehension that is, if not necessary, at least always possible.

<sup>8</sup> To page 66. — The two most significant representatives of this direction of thought are, characteristically, two Englishmen, namely Duns Scotus (ca. 1270 to 1308) and his pupil William of Occam (d. 1347). Within Scholasticism they are preparers of the Renaissance and pathbreakers of the specifically modern form of imperialism.

<sup>9</sup> To page 66. — Namely Melchior Palágyi, whose principal work "Naturphilosophische Vorlesungen über die Grundprobleme des Bewußtseins und des Lebens" (II, Barth, Leipzig) is indispensable not only for every neo-vitalist but also for every characterologist who has the epistemological preconditions of his science at heart.

<sup>10</sup> To page 66. — One finds it, besides in the "Grundlagen der Charakterkunde," above all in "Wesen des Bewußtseins."

[p. 68] Whenever I apprehend something, I can stamp what has been apprehended, by passing a judgment on it. If I perceive something bright, I can at least judge: there is something bright; if I perceive more precisely a light, then, say: there is a light; if I perceive still more precisely, then, say: there is a standing lamp. Judgments are inner actions that can discharge themselves in outer actions, whether through verbal utterance or through some other giving of signs; but actions presuppose volitions, and volition, on the testimony of immediate self-reflection, is an activity of the I. We are not concerned here with the relation of the act of judging to the act of apprehension; but we consider that its very possibility already teaches us that something is being accomplished by us in the moment of grasping. It need not trouble us here that our walker, when he exclaims in annoyance, "This wretched pavement badly needs repair!", already has the concept of the pavement, the concept of stones, the concepts of numerous properties of stones; just as little need the manifold processes of sensation [p. 69] trouble us, to which one is accustomed to have recourse for the sake of explaining the contents of perception, and we lay weight on this one point alone: that one infallibly attributes existence, in the judgment made about it, to the pavingstone against which one has struck oneself; for this one point alone holds without exception of any object of perception whatsoever, however it may otherwise be constituted, and this very point never lies, not ever, in the mere happening-to-one of the stone's appearance. Supposing this to be so, then something in us must already, in the moment of grasping, have added existence to the appearance, and must therefore have been active, even though the bearer of the apprehension noticed nothing of it whatsoever. Now, this active something is the selfsame something whose being-active becomes conscious to us as often as we form resolves, make decisions, resist, exert and strain ourselves spontaneously — in short, as often as we will.

Perhaps one will object — and the greatest thinkers of the past, though not all, have joined in this — that in the appearance [p. 70] of the stone the existence of the stone is also already contained; but then one would have confused existence with actuality! The appearance of the stone is experienced as something actual, and this means, more precisely, as something foreign to the actuality of the one experiencing it. We ask whether it is, for that reason and that reason alone, already something existing. — We think nothing actual without thinking it as temporally actual. If we now try to sink ourselves a little into what "temporality" means, it will not escape us that what first presses itself upon us here — time divisible, stretch-wise, into seconds — was, if a bolder turn of phrase is allowed, invented by ourselves for the sake of the spiritual appropriation of the otherwise ungraspable state of affairs of a never-pausing flight of world-appearance. Time as such has, after all, no incisions and no positions. But now let one consider: only by our bringing something appearing into connection with positions in time does it become something existing — and that is to say, that wholly new kind of actual thing to which we can, thinkingly, have recourse at any time whatever, as to something (forever) identical with itself. Whether we have in mind something of the sort that, like the pavingstone, [p. 71] can also be found again bodily, or something as extraordinarily fleeting in its appearance as lightning, changes nothing in the state of affairs here ascertained. For if I judge "it has lightninged" (say, five minutes ago, or last evening), I assert the presence of the lightning-flash at a position of time that has, admittedly, now elapsed; and likewise with the judgment that such-and-such will take place then and there — analogously, by reference to a position of time still to be expected. Far from being consciousness of actuality, existence-consciousness is the reverse side of a time-consciousness that has laid the arbitrarily extendable stretch beneath the unstoppable flight of world-appearance, onto whose (fictive) positions we compulsorily refer the appearance. But if our faculty of apprehension has, in this way, already accomplished what our judgment about it merely brings to consciousness for us, then it has, in the moment of apprehension, accomplished a deed of which the bearer of the apprehension nonetheless noticed nothing, because he — for reasons that would lead too far afield here — was held captive by the result of the deed and was unable, at the same time, to attend to the doing.

[p. 72] The animal lives, as we do, in an actuality — now chiefly of olfactory appearances, now more of auditory and visual appearances, but fundamentally always of tactile appearances, and thus above all in an actuality of bodies; but no animal lives, as we do, in an actuality of existences or things; for no animal has consciousness of time<sup>11</sup>. But with this we have already found the essence of the new power called spirit, which, in man, separating him from animality, has been added to vitality. — It sets him alongside time, or rather it tears him out of time, in that it makes the temporality of everything actual "objective" to him, that is, into something standing over against him, and thereby transforms the actual itself into that singular something which, with respect to time, "exists". But it can do this only because it has taken up residence within him himself, henceforth forming the ruling centre of his being, in relation to which the already existing centre of his vitality becomes more or less eccentric — a name we characteristically tend to attach to the unreasonable, the [p. 73] uncomprehending, the inexpedient in the metaphysical sense. If we call the spirit thus coupled with vitality the personal I, then we have indicated the state of affairs that alone truly exists, in an actuality of unceasing passing-away, and we can put it to the test by immediate reflection back upon ourselves.

<sup>11</sup> To page 72. — Many a person not trained in philosophy will be inclined to hold "body" to be more or less the same as "thing." But usage first of all argues against this. "Body" in every case denotes something spatially extended. Now let one try, in the following well-known idioms, to replace the word "thing" with the word "body," and one will convince oneself that an entirely different sense would result. "It is a great thing about an important man" or "it is a great thing about the truth"; "faith is not everyone's thing" (Luther); "that is another thing"; "that is not going with proper things." To the objection that here "thing" is used in a so-called figurative sense, one still would not have explained to us why "body" would not equally suit these supposed transferences! Further: if we see something in a mirror, we commonly call it a mirror-image, and could at most speak of mirror-things, whereas we would not speak of mirror-bodies. Finally: as so often, the supposed transferences are no transferences at all, but turns of phrase carrying the original meaning of "thing," which at the outset stands extremely close to "matter" and "fact" and only late acquires the secondary sense of a material substance. The concept of body is conceived in relation to space, the concept of thing in relation to time. "Thing" must as little denote something corporeal as "matter" does; but a thing always denotes something identical with itself over a — however short — stretch of time.

For if someone asks himself what he actually means when he speaks of himself as an I, he finds nothing but a sheer existent to which the concept of alteration, let alone of transformation, finds no application. Looking back over a span of some thirty years, he becomes aware of extraordinary changes in his body, and also of the most manifold changes in his character; but in calling each of the two, body as well as character, his own, he has tied them both to an I unchangeably identical with itself, which he distinguishes, as one and always the same, from every I that ever was, is, or is becoming. It was 'I' [p. 74] who, at five years old, fell into the rain-barrel, and it is 'I' — that is, the very same one — who, at fifty-four, remembers it, notwithstanding that my body of today certainly did not fall into the rain-barrel, and notwithstanding that it would be ridiculous to want to equate my present being with the being of a five-year-old child! But at once we also know what such Ihood must present itself as, if we grasp it by analogy with a drive: as the will to the assertion of existence.

We indicated above that a sober analysis of the object of apprehension could at least prepare the insight that apprehension is, among other things, an achievement of the one apprehending; and we believe we have meanwhile found that the spirit inherent in the one apprehending exchanges, case by case, the mere happening-to-one of world-appearance for existences that are extra-temporal though time-related. Yet we would hardly have hit upon taking this achievement for what it is — the result of deeds or acts of spirit — if what can be apprehended were for us never anything other than an [p. 75] existent object. But now it turns out that this world of existing objects, while indeed first found by us, is then also brought forth by us through the activity of our will. If I will at all, then I also will something (and my purpose might consist solely in proving to myself and to others that I am — capable of willing); and if I already will something, then I posit in thought an object that is not, indeed, existent now, but ought to be, in relation to which the existent objects sink, so to speak, to the level of resistances that must be overcome. Whereas my I seemed, in apprehending, merely to receive the existent object, it evidently, in willing, brings forth the object that ought to be, and thus, in acting, transforms this into — the existent object. The purpose of the will and the really existing object are woven from the same stuff; and if the former undeniably owes its being to a spiritual deed, then it is the very same deed of spirit that called the latter into being. "In the beginning was the deed": that is the worst delusion, if it is meant to signify what the arrogance of the will would like [p. 76] to make man believe — that the deed of spirit brings forth actualities; and it is an irrefutable truth, if it means: the deed of spirit, and it alone, transforms what is actual into (extra-temporal) existences.

But should this derivation strike anyone as a piece of dialectical sleight of hand, we will point him, in passing, to a foundation that leaves nothing to be desired in the way of blunt tangibility. — It is one of the most difficult questions of philosophy whether, and in what sense, the fundamental principle of all thinking, the principle of identity, is able to do justice to actuality at all. Kant offers a ground of its applicability — and indeed an indispensable one — with the following remark: "Were cinnabar now red, now blue, now light, now heavy, were a man changed now into this, now into that animal shape, were the land, on the longest day, covered now with fruits, now with ice and snow, then my empirical imagination could not even find occasion, upon the representation of the colour red, to bring the heavy cinnabar to mind." This [p. 77] expresses, among other things, that our thinking rests upon what is temporally persistent in objects, and that the fixing of the thus-existent presupposes the experience of similarity. That is undoubtedly correct. But it takes no great acumen to notice that the possibility of willing rests on exactly the same objectivity. Were my drinking-glass not a persisting something with persisting properties, I could not use it for drinking; and were I unable to use it for drinking, it would be inconceivable how even the mere intention to do so could ever have arisen in me. Accordingly we may define: things are fixation-points for lawful regularities of comportment.

Now there are two widely diverging directions of human thought. The one seeks to win back from the objects of thought what actuality lies within them; it is called the metaphysical (or better, the study of essence, to which, incidentally, psychology too belongs), and it tends to be conscious that [p. 78] it has no immediate relation to practice, that is, to the sphere of operation of the will<sup>12</sup>. The other — occupied exclusively by mechanics in the widest sense of the word, but nearly by the whole of "science" — seeks to objectify actuality, more precisely, to capture it in an ever more tightly meshed net of relations between objects, and is thus, as against the former, research into facts. But in researching facts, it researches lawful regularities of comportment, or — with respect to things — it researches that sequence of processes and events which can be strung along the thread of "cause and effect". Now, that is exactly the same sequence which the will requires for the realization of its purposes. The theory of causality supplies the will with the practice of means and end ! If it is true — and what student of the soul doubts it?<sup>13</sup> — that the meaning of a doing shines forth from its "fruits", then the drive to knowledge, aimed at ascertaining causes, serves the human will to mastery; and so we might indeed invert our guiding proposition, without taking from it the least of its weight of truth: "at the end is the deed" — namely [p. 79] as causa finalis, for the sake of which the equipment of the understanding was created and set in operation!

Were we, without regard to its ontological worth or [p. 80] worthlessness, to make use of the Cartesian cogito ergo sum in order to uncover the course of self-discovery, we would do better to take one further step at once, and could then indeed be certain of having ascertained, with a (Bouterwek-Biran) volo ergo sum, the source-point of the consciousness of existence<sup>15</sup>. Just as little as we become conscious that we are accomplishing something when we perceive something, just as little does the object of perception suffice by itself for a reflection back upon the perceiving I. This we find only at the resistance of our willing. I-consciousness is existence-consciousness; but we arrive at existence-consciousness only because, thanks to its coupling with an ever-occurring vitality, the existent becomes the self-asserting. Will and self-assertion are one and the same, and to become aware of oneself as one who wills means: to become aware of one's own I. But have there not always been, and are there not still, human beings who with full consciousness stake their lives for a beloved being, for their family, their fatherland, for a religion, a conviction as such; and is it not thereby proven that the will [p. 81] is by no means necessarily directed toward self-assertion? There is undoubtedly such a thing, and we shall presently take into consideration the ground that makes such volitions possible; but this changes nothing in the fact that the state of willing is a state of self-assertion. We could not set this forth more briefly or more aptly than with the help of two quotations from our "Grundlagen der Charakterkunde". "When a great artist, by means of hammer and chisel, strikes the statue of a god out of the block of marble, it is certainly not his will that has given him the outline of the divine image, but rather his life-favoured faculty of vision; and to this the willed action of the chiselling stands related exactly as the chisel does, which can never do anything but shatter the stone! The example shows with perfect clarity why the destructive nature of willing does not, to be sure, exclude a utilization of willing in the service of affirmative stirrings and creative formative impulses, but equally shows that volition itself always consists in a series of acts that are simply destructive, and that the will [p. 82] is a through-and-through negating power." Further: "Love too commits itself with deeds, but only under bitter self-denial; for in the moment of acting — love does not act! Let us imagine two persons of different sex bound by a mutual inclination of passionate strength ... and let us suppose the case that, before the eyes of the lover, the beloved falls from a swaying bridge into a raging stream, and he, without reflection, plunges in after her to save her; it could of course be the threatened drive of possession that had prompted him to this, but just as certainly also real love. Yet the whole content of his action is struggle with the current, struggle with the downward-pulling weight, struggle with the perhaps highly counter-purposive movements of the drowning woman, and the basis of impulse for this is the will to prevail, the will to overcome, the will to power; in accordance with which the rescuer would be lost were he, instead of struggling, to go on loving and to surrender himself lovingly! And it is always so, whenever acts of will break forth out of inclinations of self-surrender. If one compares with this the conduct of the [p. 83] record-seeker who strives to outdo his competitor in a swimming race, or even that of the wrestler who, wrestling, wills nothing other than to throw his opponent, then it stands plastically before our eyes that every action is prompted directly by the drive to self-assertion, but only indirectly by the drive to self-surrender." With this last citation we have, admittedly, already anticipated ourselves.

By ontology one understands the theory of being, and this in turn forms the principal subject of traditional metaphysics. This fundamental problem exists, but tends to involve the confusion of two concepts that, according to our account, must be kept strictly apart: the concepts of being and actuality — and this has been so in the highest degree since Descartes. Striving first of all to call everything into doubt, he believed he had found, in the proposition cogito ergo sum, the unshakeable footing from which judgments about actuality could proceed. Translated literally that would mean: I think, therefore I am (= I exist ); but the Cartesian sense is: I have consciousness, therefore I exist. Now, no doubt, insofar as one has consciousness and therefore knows it, one also knows that one exists as the bearer of this knowledge. But if, as we have shown, consciousness of existence is not the same as consciousness of actuality, then it is impossible to ground a system of judgments about actuality on this proposition alone. That is why we said that no regard should be paid to the "ontological" value of the phrase in question. It does seem, on the other hand, as though the fact of self-discovery is thereby aptly expressed. For if I become conscious that I have consciousness, I have thereby also become conscious of myself as an existing something. — Closer analysis shows, however, that we would not find our consciousness, and with it the existing I, at all, were this I not, besides, also a willing something. The primal act of consciousness, the sensory act of apprehension, always relates to a state of affairs that is foreign to consciousness. Peoples in their early ages had long since formed judgments such as "there stands a tree" before they were able to judge "I (see, perceive) judge that there stands a tree"; and the very same holds for the child. — With regard to the problem of self-discovery, the German philosopher Bouterwek (and after him the French philosopher Maine de Biran) was therefore right when he held that one must proceed from a: volo ergo sum = I will, therefore I am. Only by experiencing ourselves as willing beings are we referred back, from the object of apprehension, to the act of our spirit and hence to the I that underlies it. — Finally let it be noted that Bouterwek was among Schopenhauer's teachers at Göttingen, who thereby received the first impulse toward his metaphysics of the will.

If we now restate what was said earlier about drive and interest with the proposition: in the will-endowed being, every stirring, of whatever kind, ordinarily finds its goal only with the help of self-assertion and, as it were, through it, then this proposition has a peculiar reverse side. If the existent experiences itself as in need of self-assertion, then it has evidently also experienced that against which it feels driven to assert itself; and if it experiences something of this kind, this experience can also overwhelm its will to self-assertion, whether by a robbing of consciousness (deep sleep, fainting, narcosis, driven actions, enthusiasms, ecstasies), or, on the [p. 84] other hand, by itself mobilizing the will for the purpose, that is, of realizing goals of life. We shall speak of this in more detail in the last chapter. Here something else about the matter concerns us.

If, in man, the I-centre stands opposed to the life-centre, or, as we shall call it henceforth, the It (as, so to speak, the actual centre), then it cannot fail that, at least in waking life, an uninterrupted traffic takes place between the two real points. Between them, to speak once in a simile, messengers run ceaselessly back and forth: I-messengers, bearing commands to the It; It-messengers, working upon the I with incalculable arts of persuasion. If the former get through, then vitality stands in the service of some "will to power", whose normal effect is the acting bearing; if the latter get through, then either the I is temporarily disempowered (now as driven, now as enthused), or it in turn places itself in the service of the It, realizing, with the instrument of will, the [p. 85] goals that were instilled in it by vitality. Let us for the moment leave aside such a duality of decisions between the two rivals, and grasp the I as occasioning ground; then we know indeed that volitions in any case proceed from it, that is, purposings of what lies beyond all vitality, of the object that ought to be. If, on the other hand, we direct our attention to the traffic of messengers, or — less figuratively — to the constant oscillation of the life-processes between the I and the It, then we hold in our hands the meaning of the feelings described only peripherally above. ## VI. Zur Theorie der Gefühle — VI. On the Theory of the Feelings

[p. 88] [p. 89] 1. It goes without saying that what is being discussed here is not feelings in general, but human feelings; for in the animal, for instance, the I is lacking. For simplicity's sake, however, we shall henceforth leave the epithet "human" aside.

2. There is no feeling in which vitality is not involved, and likewise no feeling in which the I, and thus spirit, is not involved; or: all feelings have both an I-side and an It-side. — In consequence of the logocentric main direction of the whole of European thought, official psychology has overlooked the It- or life-side almost entirely, from which stem — among other things — first, the confusion, already familiar to us, of drives with interests; second, the false doctrine, not yet eradicated [p. 90] even at present, that feelings are dark, indistinct, "confused" thoughts; third, the division of all feelings into feelings of pleasure and feelings of displeasure.

We have dealt with the first point, and on the second we need waste no more words. Whoever holds every experience, every state, to be something of the nature of thought, ought then at least, consistently — after the manner of Christian Science, say — to explain sensations too, headache too, consumption too, as achievements or misachievements of the intellect! The discussion of the last point, however, may be [p. 91] eased for us by a second schema (Figure 2). — In the third chapter we already let it be glimpsed that the organism is polarized, resembling an ellipse with two foci: the specifically bodily and the specifically psychic focus. From the alternation of traffic with the I there would accordingly arise the difference between predominantly sensory and predominantly psychic feelings. With this premised, let us consider: if without exception every feeling has an I-side, then all feelings must, among other things, admit of being ranged between a maximum of pleasure and a maximum of displeasure. In accordance with its tendency to self-assertion, the I experiences as pleasing every expansion, as displeasing every diminution, of its sphere of power, and in accordance with the participation of all feelings in the I, exactly the same must hold for the feelings; or: "pleasure" means success, "displeasure" means failure . This comes to light readily in all feelings with an easily<sup>16</sup> demonstrable component of impulse — that is, in seeking, wishing, longing, hoping, craving, avoiding, warding-off, [p. 92] attacking, competing, and so on, feelings. Finding, attainment, fulfilment, satisfaction, gain, relief, securing, victory, outbidding, and so on, means success, hence pleasure; the opposite in each case means failure, hence displeasure. To make matters more than sufficient, however, Th. Lipps has furnished proof that even in the so-called object-feelings that accompany the apprehension of colours and colour-compositions, of linear and spatial forms, of noises, sounds, tones, tone-combinations and tone-sequences, the tendency to self-assertion has its say, inasmuch as what weighs decisively upon the degree of pleasingness is the fitness of the object to the conditions of our apprehension .<sup>17</sup>

<sup>16</sup> To page 91. — Practically one may speak of a point of indifference; strictly theoretically, however, there is no feeling-state that does not lie at least a little more either on the side of pleasure or on the side of displeasure.

<sup>17</sup> To page 92. — The "conditions of our apprehension" can of course in principle be traced back to a single condition. Since spirit, or — for this inquiry it makes no difference — the I, is a unity, whatever is in some way unified accords with it, and whatever is non-unified does not, and to that extent the former must arouse pleasure in us, the latter displeasure. In all domains, other things being equal, we "instinctively" prefer the surveyable to the unsurveyable, the clearly articulated to the confused, the regulated to the wholly ruleless. If we further recall the essential oneness of I and will, it becomes evident that, faced with any content of intuition whatever, the tendency toward unification is inherent in us — whence stems the ever-recurring formula of the value of "unity in multiplicity." This, now, is the basic theme of the whole psychology of Lipps. In all images of intuition that arouse pleasure, a "differentiated commonality" can, according to him, always be demonstrated. The tones of musical consonance, for example, are "rhythmically related" insofar as, owing to the simplicity of their vibration-ratios (1:2:3:4 etc.), a common basic representation differentiates itself in them, whereas, as everyone knows, the opposite holds for dissonance. The same holds for the pleasure taken in the concordance "of the parts of a building of unified stylistic character." The sound formed of consonant tones, however, at once also furnishes evidence that the same law applies to qualitatively simple contents of consciousness. The soul, Lipps holds, experiences pleasure when it feels itself stimulated to activity, and pleasure all the stronger the more frictionlessly its activity succeeds upon the object of intuition; or "pleasure increases with the saving of force in the most forceful possible psychic activity" (Leitfaden der Psychologie III, p. 323). In his "Ästhetik," which one should regard, in the old sense of the word, as a contribution to the theory of perception, Lipps has varied his basic idea with acumen and ingenuity, and demonstrated its capacity to analyze the feeling-effects of an exceedingly great number of contents of intuition. But even if we were to hold all of this entirely correct, everyone can see that here the talk is not of feelings as such at all, but strictly only of the I-side of feelings. Not for the soul, but solely for the spirit, can "saving of force" furnish a ground of approval, and not for the soul, but solely for the I's will to self-assertion, does the occasion for activity signify an occasion for the enhancement of well-being. A sharp distinction between the I-side and the It-side of feelings makes possible, incidentally, a theory of mixed feelings, as may be shown by a single example, one which Lipps himself was likewise accustomed to use in his lectures, though to a different purpose. Bodily sensations of pain (e.g. as a result of a needleprick) tend to be accompanied by vital feelings of pain. Let us suppose the case that someone, as a result of a severe illness, suffers from insensitivity (anesthesia) of extensive areas of skin, is for that reason under medical treatment, and is repeatedly tested for his sensitivity to pain by needle-pricks. It can happen, and will certainly happen, that the first needleprick, which occasions a vigorous sensation of pain, arouses on the I-side of the feeling immediate joy, namely as a sign of incipient recovery. But since, at the same time, on the It-side the sensory painfulness of the sensation of the prick is intermingled, a highly characteristic mixed feeling results.

It is precisely in such feelings, however, that the one-sidedness of this way of looking at things becomes manifest. The exciting mood, say, of the colours at the red end of the spectrum, the subduing mood at the blue end (of which Goethe treated admirably in his Farbenlehre), is a matter exclusively of vitality, whose tinge of pleasure or displeasure therefore varies from person to person. If someone is, say, a cheerful [p. 93] and enterprising character, then the exciting red-mood suits his nature better; if he is a quiet, inward-turned, contemplative character, then the subduing blue-mood suits him better. If we further range alongside the blue-mood, say, evening twilight, baritone, cello, and alongside the red-mood midday brightness, soprano, fanfare, then no one will fail to recognize that, notwithstanding certain mood-affinities, each of these contents of intuition possesses a peculiarity of mood all its own, whereby a downright unsurveyable wealth of vital moods opens up. — Notwithstanding this, the life-side of the feelings nevertheless does present polarities (just as the I-side presents the opposition of pleasure and displeasure); and specifically, the predominantly sensory feelings are stretched between lust and fear, the predominantly psychic ones between rapture (exuberance, bliss) and dread. The former two intensify chiefly in strength (violence, intensity), the latter two chiefly in depth. The former are therefore poor in imagery but close to sensation, the latter rich in imagery but remote from sensation. It needs only an unprejudiced reflection directed to the point for one to distinguish the sensation-strong [p. 94] lust from image-saturated exuberance, and both from the image- and sensation-poor feelings of success; or the sensation-strong fear from image-saturated dread, and both from the image- and sensation-weak feelings of failure. — We need not elaborate this, and may leave it to the reader himself to mark off, accordingly, the principal types of personal mood-constitution. 3. However much the I may form the ruling centre of the person, it would nonetheless have ceased to exist if detached from its associated vitality. Accordingly there is no activity of the I, no process of thinking or willing, no acting, that is not accompanied by some feeling or other. Whether I do mental arithmetic with equanimity, or await a visit with tension, or recall the past, or am violently startled, or plan a journey round the world, or converse animatedly, or daydream, or paint a picture, or lift weights: with every comportment a particular mood is bound up, in which is expressed the relation of my doing or leaving-undone, and [p. 95] thus the relation of my I to the simultaneous state of my vitality. Sometimes such moods are so weak that we have trouble becoming conscious of them. But if, say, a state of indifference — of which this holds pre-eminently — persists for a longer time, it passes over either into the quite tormenting state of boredom, or into the still more tormenting state of that particular displeasure best termed "zest-lessness" (a chief mark of so-called constitutional low spirits), thereby unveiling, through intensification, the mood-content that had belonged to it from the very beginning. According to the degree of personal emphasis on feeling in general, and especially of personal depth of feeling, the passionate, ardent natures stand out against the shallow-feeling ones; among the latter, as a variant, the apathetic, whose mental bearing not infrequently bears the character of a certain blasé quality.

Should someone object here: if I must watch a child being run over by an automobile, and feel horror as a result, what has that to do with my vitality? — then the reply to him [p. 96] would be : first, that he could not perceive anything at all without immediately preceding sense-impressions, and these are life-processes; and further, that he would merely ascertain the dreadful event by judgment, without experiencing anything else about it by feeling, were his vitality not thereby drawn into "fellow-suffering". — More important than this scarcely-to-be-mistaken state of affairs is the, in some respects, directly opposite one: that in attentive self-observation we become aware at all times of a fundamental mood which, without stemming from our perceptions and comportments, can exceptionally swell to a strength that colours everything. Here is someone who has "got out of bed on the wrong foot" in the morning, which is to say that he finds himself "groundlessly" in a peevish mood; whereas another time "groundless" cheerfulness and contentment will not really let the sympathy arise which he believes himself obliged to feel for a friend's misfortune. — Such fundamental moods are even more or less "habitual", and consistently show a certain [p. 97] periodicity . Accordingly one has to distinguish, on the I-side, the elated or euphoric from the low-spirited or depressive, and, on the life-side, the truly happy (e.g. Mozart) from the truly melancholic (e.g. Lenau).

It was C. G. Carus who first developed, in his "Psyche" (1846), the thought that autonomous moods are nothing other, and can be nothing other, than the consciousness-capable consequence of the interplay of all the life-processes of the organism, and, as it were, their integral as conveyed to the I. In particular the life-processes that are in and of themselves incapable of consciousness (= vegetative), the adsorptions, absorptions and secretions ceaselessly taking place in the living body, the processes of combustion, nutrition and formation, announce themselves to consciousness in the form of moods characteristic of each case in turn<sup>18</sup>. But to them too the tendency to self-assertion of the I responds (during waking) from moment to moment, which is why euphoria and depression, exuberance and melancholy also deserve to be [p. 98] assessed from the standpoint of peculiarly polar bearings of the personality. (We hardly need add that the vitality of the person belongs to the whole of its life-past, both the part that can be remembered and the part that was merely lived!)

Palágyi, otherwise an outstanding connoisseur of the history of the spirit, did not, however, know the writings of Carus, and accordingly proceeds, in his own reflections belonging here, from facts quite different from those of Carus. All the more may the complete agreement of the results be to us a guarantee of their truth!

4. If we recall once more our image of the messenger-traffic between the I and the It, we shall not close ourselves to the insight that it lies in the nature of feeling-processes (which we therefore call "oscillatory") to be, on the one hand, more or less divided against themselves, and on the other, more or less fleeting. For if the It attains sole rule, a robbery of awareness takes place: the vegetative-bodily It sinks the spirit (and with it consciousness) into sleep; the animal-bodily It brings it "beside itself" through excessive violence of surge, which frequently discharges itself in "drive-actions" only recognized afterward; the psychic It dissolves it in the intoxication of enthusiasm and ecstasy. If, on the other hand, the I attains at least relative sole rule, the commonest consequence today is [p. 99] so strong a preponderance of the activity of consciousness (thinking and willing) that the feelings, which of course are never entirely absent alongside it, become accessible only to inquiry expressly directed at them<sup>19</sup>. Bound in principle to a conflict of the two centres — however mild this may sometimes be — feeling is subject to ceaseless fluctuations, partly in accordance with the alternation of preponderances, but also partly because the I is stable while the It, by contrast, is fluctuating.

The fluctuating nature of vitality brings with it that man persists absolutely in no state whatsoever. If, nevertheless, as we have seen, he can hold fast to one and the same goal for years, this is only because he masters his feelings. One need only think of any record-setting achievement, say the "conquest of the South Pole" which took weeks, and one will not doubt that the participants in it were compelled, and were able, again and again to subject their certainly often wavering and changing feelings to one and the same purpose.

[p. 100] We now look more deeply into the opposition of feeling and willing. In feeling, the I finds itself in a state of undergoing; and if an (impulsive) striving issues from this, that is, seen from the I, a state of being driven. In willing, by contrast, it has subjugated the It (even should the goal also be a goal of life), but precisely for that reason has a prospect of "staying with the matter" and reaching the goal only insofar as it again and again rejects the never-failing messages of the It. Measured against feeling, the meaning of that spiritual deed called an act of will is the never-to-be-begged-off No! According as the feelings pass, heavily or lightly, into that bearing toward life which is immediately receptive to the command of the I, one speaks of feeling-people and will-people. More precisely one can say: the more easily the I-side of the feelings gains the preponderance over the It-side, the more a person is a will-person; in the opposite case, a feelingperson.

5. Countless classifications of the feelings have been devised, e.g. past-related and future-related feelings (the older [p. 101] Stoa), tensing and releasing feelings (Wundt), object-feelings and constellation-feelings (Lipps), life-enhancing and life-diminishing, affirmative and negative, deep and shallow, exciting and calming, naive and reflected feelings, and so on. Such classifications can be multiplied without limit and, for want of insight into the origin of feeling, all hang in the air alike. Once we know that no activity of consciousness takes place that would not indicate some feeling-process or other, we also know that a comprehensive classification of the feelings would coincide with a complete characterology; indeed nearly everything we have set forth so far, and shall go on to set forth, contributes to the solution of precisely this task. — However the classification may in the end turn out, it is certain that it must be multidimensional, if it is to do justice even remotely to the basic possibilities. We have no occasion to go into this further here, but reflect all the more emphatically on what, for every nature of personality, the fact of its [p. 102] capacity for feeling — or more precisely the circumstance that it is the bearer of two mutually rivalling centres — means.

Only in passing do we correct an error into which, as a reaction against the formerly customary underestimation and misinterpretation of the feelings, precisely the most recent phase of psychological thought threatens to fall: the error that feelings are efficacious powers of inwardness and thus suited to (aetiological) explanations. What is efficacious is the It (= besouled body) or, more generally, life, and what is efficacious is the I or, more generally, spirit; feelings, by contrast, are symptoms — indeed by far the most important symptoms — of the two working against one another. Moods, affects, passions, etc. "effect" nothing, but form for the researcher the most passable bridge from the facts of consciousness to the essential traits that bear and condition them.

If every willing is, in some respect, wrested from a feeling and a being-driven, then it also, conversely, belongs to the [p. 103] nature of feeling to resist the commands of the I. No claim of the I to which a claim of the It would not stand opposed, and likewise the reverse. This grounds, for all human mainsprings, a thoroughgoing antithetic, according to which none is found to which a specific counter-mainspring would not correspond, and for all human behaviour a fundamental ambiguity — which any example whatever confirms for us. Even a character of very pronounced self-interest does not altogether lack the capacity for love, together with the delight in giving rooted in it, and thus attains the goals of its self-interest only by rejecting the claims of its impulse to give. Were this impulse scarcely less pronounced than its self-interest — which may rarely be the case, but is possible — the bearer of both traits would rather often fall into inner conflicts. Let us now suppose we are witnesses to a piece of behaviour that can surely be understood only from self-interest; the state of affairs would still remain ambiguous, and we could decide only with the help of further indications whether the personality behaving thus had acted more from the strength of its self-interest or more from the weakness of [p. 104] its capacity for love. The opposite of our example teaches us the same thing. It is the same in effect, but by no means the same in origin, whether someone is inclined to give out of the greatness of his power to love or out of the weakness of his self-interest! The self-seeking of so many psychopaths, which is extremely troublesome to those around them, usually remains far behind, in strength and virulence, the self-seeking of many a tradesman, money-man, intriguer, and so on, and on closer analysis proves only too often to be the mere reverse side of a lack of capacity for love (which is much worse for its possessor). — Whoever knows how to observe and handle this "law," as we might confidently call it, will be in a position to complete, far beyond what is required, our necessarily very sketchy concluding survey of the genera and species of characters<sup>20</sup>.

## VII. Über Gattungen und Arten der Charaktere — VII. On the Genera and Species of Characters

[p. 106] [p. 107] Our characterization of the human feeling-life in accordance with Figure 1 presupposes that both the I and the It are turned toward one another and, precisely for that reason, rival one another. Exactly to the extent that this is so, we are dealing with that genus of characters to which the word "personality" applies in the most proper sense. If we seek a mark for it that comprises all its varieties, however different these may otherwise be, we find it most easily through a brief consideration of self-feeling.

If the I-centre is synonymous with the tendency to self-assertion, and if for that reason it appears in its existence now heightened and confirmed, now impaired and, as it were, [p. 108] refuted, then the experiences (= things undergone ) of the personality, and hence all feelings, must go along now with an increase, now with a diminution of that for which the German language provides the word "self-consciousness" — understanding by it, however, by no means the consciousness of one's own I, but rather the feeling of one's own worth, or more briefly, self-esteem. In accordance with the ceaseless disputes between I and It, all things undergone by the personality, all workings-through of the material of experience, all achievements, and then also all supposed and actual properties, arrange themselves for the personality into the onedimensional graded series of personal values, and are accordingly experienced, at the very least, as either valuable or contrary to value, or finally, at most, as value-indifferent (the adiaphora of the Greeks!) — and by reflective minds even weighed as such. Strictly speaking there is, in the personality, no feeling that would not carry a specific value-coloring attached to it; and it is only with the description of this — including the weighing of its consequent phenomena — that the depiction of the feeling-life is first completed.

[p. 109] Elsewhere, drawing on Nietzsche's collected works, we have investigated in the most exact detail how the drive to self-esteem of the personality works unceasingly to consolidate the consciousness of its bearer in the belief in his own person, and, after shocks have occurred, to restore this belief; by what paths and with what means it achieves this; to what reinterpretations of felt inferiorities it gives occasion; and to what extent all moralities of mankind hitherto owe their origin to such a technique of reinterpretation<sup>21</sup>. Here we content ourselves with merely stating the state of affairs, and with it making visible the mark of that genus of characters whose I and It are turned toward one another, at bottom always working against one another, though at times apparently, and as it were by compromise, also working with one another. The genus in question — let it be designated A — is the chief bearer of so-called "world history," and especially of its great turning-points and breaks.

If we look at these — e.g. at the Greeks' seventh to fifth [p. 110] centuries, at the age of the militant Church and the incipient triumphs of Christianity (Augustine), at the Renaissance, at the seventeenth and, again, the second half of the eighteenth century — we always encounter an "individualism" raised to the highest pitch, or the belief that it is the personality from which the very right of actuality to exist derives. — Half prophets and miracle-workers, half "knowers," the first great thinkers of the Greeks step onto the stage, seasoning their pronouncements with word-mighty self-praise and resounding abuse of the wretched ignorance of the rabble. His fame, Xenophanes proclaims of himself, extends over the whole of Hellas and will never fade away. Parmenides calls the opponents of his teaching wavering, ignorant, and doubleheaded. Pythagoras is worshipped as a demigod, and Empedocles, in his "Purifications," addresses his friends with the words:

No longer a mortal, but an immortal god I now go about [p. 111] among you, honored by all, as is fitting; holy fillets and blooming garlands wreathe my head<sup>22</sup>.

<sup>22</sup> To page 111. — After the excellent translation by Nestle.

Craving for distinction, ambition, and thirst for fame henceforth govern the public as well as the private life of the Greeks and turn it into a continual agon, one growing ever more passionate and terrible. For, running parallel with the drive to prevail, envy develops; in its service stand the mania for slander and the violation of honour, and to these in turn revengefulness responds. Mythical reflections of this are the invention of the "envy of the gods" and the figure of Thersites. For the high tension of envy in civic life a public safety-valve is created: ostracism. The poet Archilochus turns slander into an art-form, and Aristophanic comedy, with its by-name ridiculing of famous and powerful fellow-citizens, represents the scarcely surpassable monument of artistically shaped mockery.

In the Renaissance, self-incensing and the mania for mockery altogether celebrate their orgies. Dante, although [p. 112] still standing on medieval ground in his world-view, describes himself as a man who walks new paths and wishes to be regarded as such. "Dante," says a later compatriot of the poet (Mariotti), "thought of a work in which the names of all his enemies were to be recorded, in which they were to atone, with eternal disgrace, for everything he had had to bear." Petrarch composes a "Letter to Posterity," in which he prefers his future fame to that among his contemporaries. Cities and rulers quarrel over the corpses of celebrities of science, art, and poetry. The poets — full of rivalry to be crowned on the Capitol — write verses not only for the beloved but distinguish themselves above all by the claim that only thereby will she be granted "immortality." A Cardano calls himself the seventh great physician since Creation. A Kepler, a Paracelsus, an Agrippa insist on the incomparability of their achievements. — Parody, caricature, satire outdo one another and call forth in answer the counterblow of devilish crimes. Pamphleteering reaches its peak in the depraved blackmailer Pietro Aretino, [p. 113] before whose infamous journalism the great men of all Italy trembled for a time<sup>23</sup>. But we need not go on. Whoever, even with only some impartiality, considers the periods of "world-historical" surf-waves comes upon the tendency to self-deification of the person, and upon the excesses of envy and ill-will inseparable from it.

<sup>23</sup> To page 113. — It is and remains the great merit of Jakob Burckhardt to have applied, through the manner of his cultural historiography, without really intending it, the characterological view to periods and peoples. No characterologist should leave the following of his works unread: "Griechische Kulturgeschichte," "Die Kultur der Renaissance in Italien," and "Die Zeit Constantins des Großen."

But already the history of the West — of which the American Union is of course only an offshoot — allows us to discern, besides genus A, the intermittent predominance of another genus, which we shall call B. The Middle Ages proper aimed not so much at the mortification of the body as at the humiliation, degradation, indeed the total extirpation of self-feeling, for which the monastic rule "poverty, chastity, obedience" gives us the canon. One would be fundamentally mistaken to assume that anything of this sort stood, so to speak, only on paper, was merely an "ideal" far removed from the customs and conduct of real life, or only the ceremonial dress of a privileged priestly caste. We cannot unroll here the psychology [p. 114] of a Middle Ages that moved amid enormous contrasts; but even without doing so we may state that selfovercoming, self-devaluation, self-denial were at the very least the counterpoint without which the polyphony called the Middle Ages could never be understood. Not merely a band of monks, no — many millions of the broad masses made, for some centuries, the "honour" of a God the guiding rule of their poetry and striving, indeed the guiding rule of even their everyday conduct — a God before whom even the embryo in the mother's womb appeared, conceived as "unclean," in the light of sinfulness, depravity, and worthlessness. If we take away from this disposition what belongs only to Christianity — the agitational fury against shameful "worldly lust" and the voluptuous delight of martyrs and saints in being tormented and tortured — the remaining residue unmistakably resembles certain far older basic attitudes of the Asian human being, as these are condensed, partly in Buddhism, partly in Taoism, into systems of doctrine; in accordance with which Christian mysticism more than once [p. 115] reaches its hand to the Far East (Meister Eckhart!). With this, however, we possess the key to character-genus B, on the sole condition that one has grown accustomed to recognizing, in the actions as in the thoughts of men, symptoms of a constitution of being that would be incapable of thinking otherwise, of acting otherwise.

Just as the I and the It can strive toward one another like two hostile armies and become locked in combat, so too they can strive apart from one another; and the consequences of this turn out differently for the bearer according to whether his centre of gravity must be located in the I or in the It. If — what concerns us first — the latter is the case, the I-side loses more and more the character of the individual I, and in its place steps the dominion of spirit itself, which after all could only become individual spirit in proportion to its interweaving with life. If in the scheme of Figure 1 we substitute "life" for the It-side and "spirit" for the I-side, further hand over supreme power to spirit, and finally let the essence of its bearer be turned toward it alone and away from life, then we [p. 116] have the character-formation B, which, whatever their otherwise far-reaching differences, is common to Taoism, Buddhism, and medieval Catholicism. If it certainly needs no further grounding why the utmost rejection of individualism should issue from this, then the will-religion of Christianity also reveals to us, with its telltale demand that the God to whom one must sacrifice everything be grasped both as pure spirit and as a person, that the origin (arche) of the denial of one's own I is to be sought precisely on the side of the I, and in the turning toward that principle which transforms organisms into persons! Apart from this, to be sure, the differences between Asian and European spiritual cult are quite extraordinary; but we must forgo analysis here.

Finally, however, eastern and western "world history," from the earliest times down to our own day, is interspersed with phenomena — today, admittedly, in the process of vanishing — which, intelligible only from the supremacy of life [p. 117] together with a simultaneous turning away from spirit, link the historical type with the type, on the one hand, of the unhistorical human being (usually called "natural man"), and, on the other, of prehistoric man. Among numerous other marks, it is above all two features that unmistakably stamp this third character-genus C: the cult of images and the cult of intoxication. Buddhist doctrine leads out of the world of images into nirvana, and alongside this permits at most the ever-recurring type of the self-absorbed Buddha; Christian doctrine likewise leads out of the world of images into the purely spiritual beyond, and alongside this permits the similarly stable type of the "Redeemer" nailed to the cross. A Buddhist or Christian bearing toward life, determined by doctrine alone, would be the (inner) imitation, there of the Buddha, here of the Crucified. If one now compares with this the garishly coloured host of demons that Buddhism, more and more as time went on, had to readmit into its temples, and the countless images of the Mother of God and the boundless gallery of saints that Christianity had to admit, one [p. 118] becomes convinced that in both cases an image-cult is at work that stands in irreconcilable contrast to the worldaverted doctrines. But all image-cult is born of the overwhelming power of seeing, or of an intoxication with images. And so we see the whole of "world history" accompanied by the convulsions of an intoxication whose function is to transport the soul into the actuality of images. We could not come to an end with an enumeration, and content ourselves with recalling: the Dionysian frenzies of the Greeks, the Saturnalia of the Romans, the raptures of Brahmin mendicant monks, of fakirs, Pythias, Sibyls, dervishes; the dancing mania of the Middle Ages, which lasted almost half a century (1374 to 1418); the "imitative pandemics" of tarantism, the Children's Crusade (1212), St Vitus's dance, the "jumping fever" of the Scots, and so forth. Many of these phenomena are predominantly pathological in nature; but even through their most extreme distortions there still shimmers a little of that element of image-streaming intoxication which has survived, pure and close to life, among all "natural peoples," and which [p. 119] created the piety, the myths, the cults of mankind as a whole before the beginning of the "world-historical" process of splitting.

However different, indeed opposite, the constitution of genera B and C may be, they nonetheless agree in this, that the basic condition for the emergence of personalities appears diminished: in the one case through the individual being's turning away from the life in it, in the other through its turning away from the spirit in it; in accordance with which both essential forms display more typical than individual traits, and therefore fall to the border regions of a science whose object is the personal characters. Custom, usage, ceremony in every act from cradle to grave, ancestor-worship and the cult of the gods, festivals and rites mark the life of primitive peoples, and mark — though with opposite content — no less the life of the Middle Ages, whose personal characters must stand back as of little weight behind the characters of monasticism, chivalry, and serfdom, of corporations, guilds, trades, and masons' lodges. However certain it is that penetrating research would find all species of character indicated even [p. 120] among genera B and C, it is equally certain that genus A alone permits research to find those species.

Whoever wishes to be instructed on the quite extraordinarily great diversity of species that can be derived with our scheme, we must refer to our "Grundlagen der Charakterkunde" [Foundations of Characterology], whose concluding chapter, "Sketch of the System of Mainsprings," is devoted exclusively to the solution of this task. Here it is enough to point the way. — Let us think of the I-side and the It-side as separated by a vertical section, and let us imagine, in one case, the It fettered by the I, and in the other, the I fettered by the It; we then obtain the opposition of the self-assertion-mainsprings to the self-surrender-mainsprings, of which now the one, now the other, determines the individual character with a slight preponderance, and in exceptional cases with a strong preponderance. If we further divide the I-side by a horizontal section, thereby setting the spirituality of the person over against the personhood of spirit, we obtain [p. 121] the opposition of the general mainsprings, or general interests, or interests-in-the-matter, to the personal mainsprings, or self-interests — for whose preponderances in the individual character the same holds. The general interests may conveniently be divided into theoretical, aesthetic, and ethical; the exceedingly rich variety of self-interests, or egoisms, can be distinguished on the one hand according to the objectclasses of striving (e.g. self-interest alongside craving for distinction), and on the other according to the basic forms of the striving relatedness to the object (active, passive, reactive egoisms). — But with this the surrender- or release-mainsprings likewise fall apart into two analogous groups: into surrenders of spirit, or specific capacities for enthusiasm, and surrenders of the person, or specific capacities for love. This is the bare skeleton, which one finds filled in at the place designated above.

Open remains the immeasurably large field of questions concerning the possible and actual connections between the mainsprings and the properties of aptitude, between both of [p. 122] these and the properties of structure, between the ground-plan of the whole and the principal forms of self-feeling. Open remains, in other words, the question of the law governing the co-occurrence of properties, for which natural-scientific biology offers us a counterpart in the so-called correlations. One may recall, for instance, the "secondary sexual characters," according to which, e.g., the body of the male human being tends to be more hairily covered than that of the female, or a certain variegated colouring of the coat is found only in female cats, and so on. Connections of this kind, thought of as "typical," of course possess actual validity. Further requiring, and capable of, a development that today cannot yet at all be gauged, is the doctrine of the forms of appearance of the essence, of the range and valence of the recognition-marks. But only at the insights that would fall to us along such a path of research would we wish to kindle the torch that would lead us deep down into the drive-layer still thoroughly misunderstood today. And if, running parallel [p. 123] with this and on the basis of what is offered here, the treatment of the metaphysical problem of the body-soul polarity were to proceed alongside it, we might hope that, with the completed doctrine of the characters of man, we shall one day have opened for ourselves the gate to the characters of the world.

## Quellen — Sources

Important pointers to the relevant literature are already given in the notes, which are therefore to be regarded as a supplement to the source-list that follows. — What has been understood, in the school sense, exclusively until recently, and even today predominantly, by "psychology" had its beginning in ancient Greece at the latest with Protagoras, and became, at first, a sub-division of philosophy from Aristotle onward, among whose works, as is well known, there is a treatise devoted specifically to psychology, entitled "On the Soul." Although in the second half of the nineteenth century this psychology made itself independent, by attempting to take up a position between the so-called humanities and the so-called natural sciences, at least ninety-nine out of every hundred [p. 128] psychological writings of the period named still belong, in short, to the Aristotelian genus, as before. What all research efforts to be reckoned to it over two and a half millennia have in common — partly knowingly and intentionally, partly quite unconsciously — is to be sought in the following tendency.

Holding thinking, or at least cognizing, to be the most excellent property of man, one believed one was investigating his soul as such when one examined the inner conditions of the activity of thought — and indeed, as goes without saying, above all with a view to whether they were apt to further or to disturb the activity of cognition; from which, as one notices, is at once explained, among other things, the circumstance that one is accustomed to discuss feeling-processes from the standpoint of obscure or confused thoughts. The more consciously such a goal is emphasized, the more psychology transforms itself into so-called epistemology, which would be better called "science of consciousness." If, finally, the emphasis is placed on ascertaining those procedures which thinking must employ if judgment is to turn out correctly, we have the science of logic. "Psychology," the science of consciousness, and logic hang together, accordingly, in the closest way, however often — indeed precisely for that reason — they [p. 129] have made war upon one another. This kind of psychology has great merits for the science of consciousness; for characterology, by contrast, its yield has turned out to be extremely meagre.

Scarcely less old than "psychology," now, is characterology; indeed it is essentially older, if we include what was made known about human nature by thinking men without any characterological intention. Here we insert an intervening consideration. — For the researcher of character there are two kinds of material: unformed and formed. The unformed is human beings themselves, together with everything they have willfully brought forth since the earliest days: their customs and usages, rites and cults, symbols and legends, their languages and writing systems, world-views and arts, manual skills and inventions, their state constitutions and social institutions, their proverbs, forms of dress, rules of intercourse, and finally their historical deeds. Formed material, by contrast, is the countless pronouncements on human nature from the mouths of the wise and the poets. Were one to gather together everything of wisdom and knowledge about man contained in the linguistic legacies — to pick out only a few [p. 130] Western names — of a Homer, Solon, Aesop, Theognis, Epicharmus, Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Democritus, Aristophanes, Plato, Plautus, Cicero, Catullus, Virgil, Horace, Ovid, Martial, Petronius, Juvenal, Lucian, Apuleius, Wolfram von Eschenbach, Gottfried von Straßburg, Dante, Petrarch, Boccaccio, Machiavelli, Rabelais, Luther, Hutten, Cervantes, Shakespeare, Hobbes, Milton, Pascal, Voltaire, Lessing, Hamann, Schubart, Herder, Pestalozzi, Heinse, Goethe, Schiller, Jean Paul, Arndt, Hölderlin, Novalis, Tieck, Görres, Kleist, Eichendorff, Byron, Gotthelf, Heine, Balzac, Gogol, Dickens, Hebbel, Tolstoy, Keller, Dostoevsky, etc., one would certainly gain the impression that it rivals human life itself in inexhaustible richness, and will for a long time to come remain mere "material" for any effort at scientific mastery. From this we now abstract, apart from a few exceptions (further below). We must, however, single out two categories in particular.

First the great historians, of whom, from antiquity, may be named: Thucydides, Caesar, Sallust, Livy, Plutarch, Tacitus; from the transitional period, the mighty Gregory of Tours; from the Middle Ages, Ekkehard, Marquard; from more recent times, say, J. von Müller, Carlyle, Ranke, Giesebrecht, Freytag, Mommsen, Gregorovius, Friedländer, Burckhardt, [p. 131] Scherr. Then the great self-historiographers, beginning with Augustine ("Confessions") by way of Thomas and Felix Platter, Berlichingen, Schärtlin, Schweinichen, and — best named here — Grimmelshausen ("Simplicissimus"), down to the characterologically exceedingly productive self-portrayers of the eighteenth century — Haupt, Sethe, Trenck, Laukhard, etc. No researcher of character should leave unread: Rousseau's "Confessions," Goethe's "Poetry and Truth," Jung-Stilling's "Lebensgeschichte," Moritz's "Anton Reiser." Only for want of space have we confined ourselves essentially to naming German self-portrayers. For the same reason we leave unmentioned the numerous highly significant correspondences that the eighteenth century in particular bequeathed us — perhaps the most manifold century of mankind so far. At the very least the works highlighted in letter-spaced type are indispensable for the researcher of character.

Turning back to characterology itself, it should be remarked at the outset that it is only now, in our own time, on the point of receiving the scientific formulation that has been proper to Aristotelian psychology for millennia already. A rigorous characterology knows no past, though it does know a by no means inconsiderable literature of valuable preliminary work and contributions. And here again we have, in [p. 132] the first place, to remember Aristotle, and indeed in more than one respect. For it was either he himself, or else one of his pupils, who, in the thirtieth section of the "Problems," resting on the Hippocratic doctrine of the humours, laid the foundation for what was later called the theory of the four temperaments — a theory which, though certainly untenable, continues to have effect down to our own day. Further, he wrote a treatise in six chapters, "Physiognomonica," which, proceeding from predecessors unknown to us, aimed at a theory of signs for the world of characters, and for that purpose recommended the comparison of human bodily properties with those of animals. The lion has coarse hair and is courageous; consequently — men with coarse hair are courageous (and vice versa); the lion has a snub nose and is magnanimous; consequently — men with snub noses are magnanimous. It would be incomprehensible that such trifling should still haunt us today, were there not behind it the intimation of a deeper symbolism. Through the language and poetry of all peoples and times runs the comparison of human characters with animals, and here as everywhere, a perceived similarity of images can point to affinities of essence. But never, never does one hit upon the admissible point of comparison by stringing together the properties of things! The word [p. 133] "sea of flowers" harbours a truth that we irretrievably miss precisely when we hope to discover it by comparing the properties of flowers with the properties of sea-water! The Aristotelian physiognomics was a wrong path. The Neapolitan Giambattista della Porta trod it a second time in the sixteenth century with his book "De humana physiognomia," and as late as 1882 the Dane Sophus Schack published "Physiognomische Studien," which culminate in the wisdom that there are ape-faces, fox-faces, dog-faces! On this dead track stands a long line of railway cars. It is rusted and will fall apart without our having to help it along.

It is, however, no accident that the first steps into characterology are also the first steps into the semiotics of characters. Given the bewildering ambiguity of human action, the study of the alien soul would find itself confined within the narrowest limits without an accompanying study of its immediate forms of appearance. Physiognomics falls into two great domains: organ-physiognomics and function-physiognomics. Whoever wishes to be more precisely informed on this we refer to our works on the theory of expression, "Ausdrucksbewegung [p. 134] und Gestaltungskraft" and "Handschrift und Charakter" (both Barth, Leipzig), further "Einführung in die Psychologie der Handschrift," and finally to the collected volume of our individual investigations, "Zur Ausdruckslehre und Charakterkunde" (both Kampmann, Heidelberg), which at the same time treats several predecessors monographically. From the exceedingly large number of books that, since Lavater, Camper, Combe, Gall, Spurzheim, have been devoted to the subject, we name here only those the researcher of character may not pass by: Carus, Symbolik der menschlichen Gestalt (newly reissued by Kampmann, Heidelberg); Darwin, Über den Ausdruck der Gemütsbewegungen bei Menschen und Tieren [The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals]; Piderit, Mimik und Physiognomik.

In another respect, however, and with better fortune, Aristotle also opens up the science of characters, namely with his so-called Nicomachean "Ethics," whose value lies not in its moral rules but in its consideration of human states of mind and mainsprings. The same holds of Spinoza's "Ethics," which, as is known, encloses an already quite modern-seeming theory of the affects. Quite generally it may be said that thinkers concerned with the conduct and bearing of life, because they had in the first instance to investigate man, contributed the [p. 135] most to the science of essence even when their aim was to make man contemptible.

After the foregoing remarks it will not surprise us that it was a friend and pupil of Aristotle, namely Theophrastus (370–287), who led the peripatetic school after him, to whom we owe the first work of characterology in the most proper sense of the word. Though his thirty "Character Sketches" may be in part reworked and disfigured, they are and remain an ineradicable landmark of thought — even if not only for the good! Theophrastus's procedure — not, to be sure, thought through, but in fact practised — is this: he takes possession of a real or supposed character trait and, by picturing its consequences for overall behaviour (especially in social intercourse), forms out of it a type, which he then, through an astonishingly rich exemplification, transforms back into the most vivid shape. Who would not see before him the "Tactless Man" on hearing: "He is always officiously ready to serve just where one does not want his services and yet can hardly well refuse them"; or the "Miser" at the words: "If his wife has lost a threepenny piece, he is capable of moving aside every piece of household furniture, every bed and cupboard, and shaking [p. 136] out every blanket"; or the "Braggart," when it is said: "If he can catch a fellow-traveller on the road, he knows how to tell him that he campaigned with King Alexander, and how well he stood with him, and of the many jewel-studded goblets he brought home. And in the same breath he boldly asserts that the Asiatic craftsmen are far superior to the European ones — and all this he says without ever having left his native town"! He sketches — in passing, as it were, and without being burdened by learned names — a picture, accurate in its main features, of pseudologia phantastika [fantastic lying], partly in the "Braggart," partly in the "Duck-Hunter," of whom he remarks in conclusion: "They are a strange lot, these duck-hunters, for not only is their whole business nothing but lies and deception, they not infrequently have the loss from it into the bargain as well" (I). Never banal, throughout full of wit, and always superior, he offers nothing that anyone could not confirm even today. Yet, with his genuinely Greek simplification, he sowed an error that continues merrily to proliferate to this day: the, it seems, ineradicable belief in the real existence of types. Nothing perhaps proves more strikingly the dependence of Western man's thought upon the Greeks than the circumstance that one has never again got free of the [p. 137] typological conception of characters<sup>24</sup>! Rightly do the French call the greatest poet of their greatest century the father of their comedy. But what are Molière's characters? Theophrastan types! "Tartuffe," "The Misanthrope," "The Imaginary Invalid," "The Miser," etc. — nothing but properties transformed into persons! Once again put on the stage with wit and with the outstanding knowledge of human nature of the man of the world — truly immortal types! Yet the recipe is always at hand, while wit and knowledge of human nature are not inheritable. The average scholar of today has the recipe; he does not have the wit and the knowledge of human nature, and so he knocks together his types for home use — or rather, unfortunately, not only for home use! (The writer of these lines declares himself willing, should it be absolutely necessary, and then of course only for a corresponding fee, to draft a typology every week — some fifty a year, then, each with a sufficiently persuasive rationale....). — Only in the schools of the Cynics and Cyrenaics does that one-sided individualism develop which places the personality at the centre of actuality, and precisely thereby brings to light, though in a form more epigrammatic than scientific, a good many insights into man. No researcher of character should pass by the legacy [p. 138] of Antisthenes and Diogenes on the one hand, and of Aristippus on the other. Here are a few sayings (always in Nestle's translation) with which we should like to give the reader a taste for turning to the authors named above. Antisthenes: "One must pay attention to one's enemies, for they are the first to notice our faults." Diogenes: "A drop of luck is worth more than a barrelful of wit"; "The sun, too, shines into corners and yet is not defiled"; "Fame is a noise made by raving men." Crates: "In this man's case, it is not he who possesses the wealth, but the wealth that possesses him." (!)

We are not speaking of the recently revived pastime of "nativities," but of the age-old endeavors toward a characterology of the universe sub specie astronomiae. They too remained stuck in the typological, or rather kept falling back into mere doctrines of types. Open any of the old "astrologers" at random, and one will find that the mostly quite transparent analogies follow the lead of a typological prejudice. These are, to be sure, not Theophrastian types, but ones closer to life, drawn from a much more remote antiquity. Yet they purchase the advantage of their descent from the age of humanity's symbolic thinking at the incalculable cost of the downright hopeless task of having to rethink pure symbolisms into the language of rational reflection. In other words: the types of astrological characterology are, in the measure of their greater closeness to life, less determinate than those, say, of Theophrastus (or of any other rational typologist), and admit, in their application to actual individual cases, pretty much every conceivable extension, narrowing or transformation; from which it follows, incidentally, that even if one held "nativities" to be worth discussing at all, any given horoscope is not merely ambiguous but downright infinitely ambiguous! If we are not mistaken, it is connected precisely with this that the very latest fashionable astrology, as it seems, pays homage to the scarcely credible error that one could contest a sidereal characterology today with the same or at least quite similar means as, say, in the time of Ptolemy! However grand and fundamentally correct the idea of a characterology of the cosmos may be, it must seem doubtful whether from it any fructification of human characterology could take place at all. For these reasons we have refrained from including, within the frame of our account of sources, the characterological tradition lodged in astrology, which doubtless harbors many a grain of gold. — Whoever wishes to become acquainted with the factual and historical side of astrology, we recommend the reading of the relevant chapters in Kiesewetter's "Geheimwissenschaften," and quite especially the work by Boll, Sternglaube und Sterndeutung (B. G. Teubner, Berlin).

Historically, the Stoa branched off from Cynicism, and Epicureanism from the Hedonism of the Cyrenaics; and with that we are at the center of ancient character research. No one properly understands this who has not thoroughly studied the surviving sayings of at least the following representatives of both schools: Epicurus, Philodemus, Chrysippus, Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius. If among the later Stoics Marcus Aurelius is the most profound, then Seneca is the most brilliant and characterologically the most productive! An ancient "feuilletonist," if one likes; yet superior to the modern feuilletonist by just as much as the philosophical [p. 139] reflection of antiquity surpasses that of the present. Whoever does not yet know him may be promised not merely instruction but exquisite enjoyment.

The continuers of the Greco-Latin spirit were, as is well known, the French. From the long series of ingenious authors we name only the most indispensable: Montaigne's "Essais," Labruyère's "Les Caractères" (1680), La Rochefoucauld's "Maximes" (1690), and finally Stendhal's "De l'amour." — With the second half of the 18th century the psychological awakening of Germany begins, soon to produce peak achievements that scarcely have their equal. And here we must above all recall two works by poets, each of which, in our estimation, harbors a treasure-house of characterological wisdom unsurpassed in richness — we mean Goethe's "Die Wahlverwandtschaften" and Jean Paul's "Levana," a book which among other things offers the most excellent treatment yet given of the essential difference between the sexes. Standing beside them as an equal is Arndt, thanks to his mastery, never again attained, in the depiction of the characters of peoples and regional groups. — Not comparable to such beacons, yet each in its own way having furthered the science [p. 140] of man nonetheless, are two wholly different works: Kant's, in part perspicacious, "Anthropologie in pragmatischer Hinsicht" and Lichtenberg's ingenious "Aphorismen."

Since the beginning of the 19th century two currents clearly diverge. The one, proceeding from Schopenhauer — who was a far greater knower of the soul than a speculative metaphysician — eventually produced the work that lent the whole field of knowledge its distinguishing name, namely Bahnsen's two-volume "Beiträge zur Charakterologie," published in 1867, and moreover, through a curious mingling with Herbart's researches into the soul, supplied the conceptual apparatus for what is today called psychoanalysis. The other is introduced most magnificently by the thinkers of Romanticism oriented toward psychology and physiognomy. Among the works belonging here, the most important for us today is "Psyche. Zur Entwicklungsgeschichte der Seele" by C. G. Carus (1846, reissued with an introductory preface by Klages at Diederichs, Jena). But Schubert's hymnic "Geschichte der Seele" also greets us across the years as an imperishable monument. In this, however, lies the decisively new element of Romantic psychology: that it seeks the wellspring [p. 141] of personalities neither in consciousness nor in the immediate conditions of consciousness, but in life-processes and life-facts that are in and of themselves foreign to consciousness (usually called "the unconscious"). — The second half of the century brings a high flowering of what we have called Aristotelian psychology, to such a degree that characterological writings increasingly die out alongside it. An amiable straggler is J. E. Erdmann with his "Psychologische Briefe" (1855).

Then, however, the stance of Romanticism is taken up again by the revolutionizer of all soul-research soon to be mentioned, who himself, though, felt far more akin to two remarkable thinkers of the interim period — thinkers who can indeed represent a bridge from the philosophies of those seer-spirits, plumbing the deep and the dark, to the unrelenting daylight of a thinking that tests the metaphysical scale against the hard objectivity of actuality: we mean the American Emerson, with his richly faceted "Representative Men," and the Frenchman Gobineau, with his prescient treatise "Über die Ungleichheit der menschlichen Rassen." And so, not unprepared yet no less surprisingly for that, between [p. 142] every "then" and the "now" steps the revolutionizing creative spirit Friedrich Nietzsche. Whoever today wishes to help build the edifice of characterology may spare himself none of Nietzsche's books. As a compass for traversing this dangerous firestorm of new thoughts we recommend to him our work "Die psychologischen Errungenschaften Nietzsches" (Barth, Leipzig). Here only this one remark: Nietzsche shows that knowledge of essence is impossible unless we have first seen through and dissolved all the self-deceptions with which humanity, seduced by spirit for millennia, has armored itself against the admission of the violation of life. He calls upon us to become surgeons of our own soul, and presses into our hand the knife that cuts to the very marrow of character. — To Nietzsche's psychological discoveries there is now joined, partly furthered by them, a theory of life (biology) that energetically renounces mechanism, whose by far most significant representative, Melchior Palágyi, in his "Naturphilosophische Vorlesungen," carries through with strict conceptual rigor the separation of spirit and life. — The provisional conclusion, finally, is offered by our own work "Die Grundlagen der Charakterkunde." With that we would have arrived at the [p. 143] present, and here we call a halt. Today one talks more about characterology than one actually accomplishes within it. The voices still ring out discordantly and confusedly intermingled; the one reliable guidance has not yet been granted the rank due to it. To this is added that countless specialized workers, e.g. many neurologists, have indeed perspicaciously unearthed individual findings, but for lack of familiarity with what has in fact already been achieved have, so to speak, missed the connection, erecting makeshift structures that collapse one after another. A treatise would be needed to sift through this critically. Finally, we may recall the book by Prinzhorn, "Leib — Seele — Einheit" (in this series), which has made precisely this sifting its chief task. Only one remark do we wish not to suppress in closing: relatively the most promising of all specialized characterological labors appear to us to be the attempts of the anthropologists oriented toward racescience, whose forefather, Gobineau, has just been cited.
