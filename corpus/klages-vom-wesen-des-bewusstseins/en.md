## I. Leben und Kraftbegriff. — I. Life and the Concept of Force.

Introductory remarks. For roughly a century, psychology -- literally "science of the soul" -- has stood at the forefront of research efforts in the human sciences, which in turn presupposes a "biology" or doctrine of life, because the concept of the soul cannot be determined without that of the living being. Yet if we look back upon the achievements of so-called Romantic philosophy, we must confess that since that time we have fallen into a confusion of all fundamental concepts that threatens to culminate in utter doubt (scepticism) and therewith in the renunciation of knowledge. While mountains of "facts" were heaped up after the model of the mechanistic worldview, and extremely precise experiments were conducted with highly artificial apparatuses, it was long ago forgotten to what end all this expenditure was actually required, and that here there is nothing at all to measure, to weigh, and scarcely perhaps anything to count! Therefore, whoever reads through one of the corpulent "Psychologies" without prejudice will be unable to conceal from himself that, while he has indeed learned sundry remarkable data, he has become not one whit wiser concerning the soul in general and the human soul in particular. Indeed, he may feel tempted to apply to this supposed science of the soul the remark with which a Goethe dismissed the erudition of the French Encyclopaedists. Among the Encyclopaedists, so he lets it be heard, "one felt as if walking between the innumerable moving spools and looms of a great factory, and amid all the clattering and rattling... amid the utter incomprehensibility of an establishment whose parts mesh with one another in the most manifold ways, in contemplating what is required to produce a single piece of cloth, one comes to feel disgusted with one's own coat that one wears upon one's back."

We are confronted with a denaturalisation of thought without historical precedent, and let no one be deceived: it terminates at last in utter ignorance and the conceit of superiority peculiar to ignorance alone.

Whoever listens more keenly will not fail to hear that precisely among the guild members the opinion has taken root that judgements concerning the relation of the living body to the soul, of matter to spirit, of being to consciousness, and other such indispensable preliminary questions, are at bottom mere matters of opinion and therefore to be left to the arbitrary discretion of the clever, while science has simply to establish facts and ascertain causes. Let us then examine above all what facts are actually envisaged here!

Since "psychology," in a curious disavowal of its own name, has been inclined to regard the existence of the soul as a popular prejudice, its subject matter has preferably been called the "facts of consciousness" or, what amounts to the same thing, of "inner experience." If, however, one asks wherein consciousness consists -- by what distinguishing marks it may be delimited against non-consciousness, or being, or actuality; how it arose in the universe -- it is still the most honest researchers who candidly confess that this is not known, or else that everyone already knows it of himself through an immediate reflection upon it that admittedly cannot be more precisely described; whereas less scrupulous researchers bestow upon us instructions that invariably already presuppose what is to be explained, usually in the portentous garb of newly coined foreign words. The supposed facts thus hang entirely in the air! And equally hanging entirely in the air are the turbid fabrications of so-called epistemology, whose scholastic artifices do not deceive us into overlooking that it can no more dispense with the science of the soul than the latter can dispense with the doctrine of the conditions of knowing. For if we take the word "consciousness" in its original sense as a substantivised activity -- I am conscious (of something) -- then in any case it is only by virtue of that power or faculty which bestowed "consciousness" upon us that we can know anything at all, whether that knowledge extend to the world of appearances, to the soul, or to consciousness itself! Whoever therefore undertakes to ascertain the conditions of knowing as such must either ascertain the conditions of consciousness, or he has ascertained nothing at all!

The dogma of the insolubility of a question whose answer would manifestly constitute the insight of the threshold at the temple of knowledge of the science of the soul points back to the millennia-long errant paths of metaphysics, which admittedly no one who seriously endeavours to find a solution can avoid pursuing. Provided, however, that he has eyes sufficiently unspoiled, he will perceive that the fearsome difficulties with which the fundamental concepts seem to bristle were fabricated by the drive for power of the spirit in historical man, and that the task is not so much to discover something unheard-of and new as rather to tear apart the close-meshed net of self-deceptions at which, as especially active participants, almost all philosophers have knitted and woven. It was an "interest," albeit of a supra-personal kind, that seduced the thinkers into exchanging life for spirit and thereby, in lockstep, from the very beginning into confusing actuality with being. Once, however, they had first volatilised actuality into a kind of reflection of spirit, it was inevitable that, reflecting upon the soul, they found in its place only consciousness and thenceforth exhausted themselves upon the hopeless, not to say delusional, task of spinning out of consciousness also its enabling ground: experiencing! Whoever now sees through this initial error finds himself forthwith surrounded by a penetrating clarity in which the self-contradictions of academic science disintegrate of their own accord.

To open such a prospect, or, if one prefers, to lead to the threshold, is the purpose of the following exposition. Setting out from consciousness, we immediately take it in the sense of the capacity to take cognisance of something, and thus as conceptually identical with: faculty of judgment, capacity for thought, understanding [intellectus, cogitatio, ratio; διάγνωσις, νόησις, συναίσθησις]. We show that the question of the essence and origin of the understanding requires the answer to the other question concerning the nature of life, and that each of the two can be decided only in relation to the other, but then definitively. In the process, the much-debated problems -- soul and spirit, actuality and being, living body and "dead" matter -- shall find their entirely determinate resolution, as if of their own accord.

Composed as comprehensibly as is conceivable given the subject matter, yet also as briefly as possible, our exposition does not claim to be exhaustive in its demonstrations, but only sufficient, and to that extent it does indeed make considerable demands upon the reader's intellectual collaboration. In order, however, to acquaint even the non-specialist at least to some degree with the prehistory of the concepts under investigation, we ascend to the actual findings by the somewhat arduous detour of refuting widely held erroneous doctrines.

Refutation of the "vis vitalis." The natural-scientific doctrine of life (biology) presupposes the problem of life rather than solving it. For it concerns itself with a group of things that it distinguishes as living from the rest as non-living, but has no answer to the question wherein the being-alive of living things consists. There is no sensory qualitative property by which living things would be distinguished from non-living things. All colours, sounds, smells, tastes, tactile qualities, shapes, and forms of movement are found in principle just as much in non-living as in living things. As for the determination first given by the Pythagorean physician Alcmaeon five hundred years before our era, that the living is "the self-moved" [αὐτοκίνητόν], it does indeed correctly designate an expressive mark of life, but not a property of living things. For quite apart from the fact that objective movement would not reveal to us whence it originates, certain considerations that present themselves today confirm the impossibility of the self-movement of things. With this we come to the refutation of the assumption of a so-called vis vitalis or "vital force."

Force is the cause of movement, or the condition, conceived as possibility, for the fact that ponderable masses (m) receive, now directly (mechanically), now indirectly (electrodynamically), a measurable acceleration (v) per unit of time. If one therefore speaks of "vital force," one would have to be able to specify in gram-centimetres the quantity of vital energy that either potentially inheres in a bearer of life capable of movement, or that comes to effect in its movements at any given time. Were that possible, however, one would thereby have either annihilated the presupposition of the existence of the "vis vitalis" -- namely its attachment exclusively to living things, and therewith the vis vitalis itself; or else one would have advanced to the assumption that in living things kinetic energy continually arises from nothing. The law of the "conservation of energy," currently attested by innumerable experiences, is however merely an unavoidable consequence of the concept of force itself, which is why its annulment would bring the entire mechanistic worldview to collapse1).

Moreover, Rubner and later Atwater have demonstrated through experiments that a living being expends precisely only as much energy as corresponds to the caloric value of the food ingested.

Refutation of the "vis formativa." One seeks in vain to meet the foregoing difficulties by saying, for example, that the vital force is not a cause of movement but rather a "principle" that prescribes to the existing causes of movement the paths of their unfolding. Strictly speaking, it would thus be a "directional energy." However, one can impart a deviation from the direction of movement to a moving body only either through impact (kinetic energy) or through pressure (potential energy).

When the waters of a river change the direction of their course at a certain point, the cause thereof lies in the resistance of the outer bank, the magnitude of which can be determined from the strength of the impact that it just barely counterbalances. If, as a result of a cloudburst, the force of the water masses increases beyond that impact strength, the bank gives way and the river course continues without change of direction; from which it is evident that the change of direction was brought about by a measurable force of pressure.

Moreover, the concept of a "directional energy" rests upon an impermissible hypostatisation of a merely abstract characteristic of the manifestation of force. Direction, velocity, and mass are three states of affairs that are indeed very well distinguishable, but not separable. If, accordingly, "directional energy" cannot be sustained, then the "vis formativa" likewise falls away, according to which the vitality of living things is to be sought in a force of self-formation.

## II. Bewußtsein und Kraftbegriff. — II. Consciousness and the Concept of Force.

Refutation of the doctrine of interaction. -- Let us immediately consider the entirely analogous difficulties that arise from the above erroneous assumption for the conception of the connection between corporeality and consciousness! Already Descartes had invented directional energy in order to bring his conviction of the strictly mechanical causality of the course of the world into accord with the no less important (to him) "freedom of the will." Consciousness, in his view housed in the pineal gland (for him entirely identical with the soul), can, he assumed, not set the bodily "vital spirits" [Lebensgeister] in motion, but can alter the direction of movement of those already in motion; likewise, conversely, consciousness is through the vital spirits exposed to certain disturbances [perturbationes animi] which, taking effect as emotions, have as their consequence confusions of cognition and sinful decisions of the will. From this there developed, albeit only very much later, the theory of interaction between consciousness and body.

It is untenable. For on the one hand, the measurable energy of numerous bodily processes would then be generated from the unmeasurable fact of consciousness of acts of will -- physically considered, therefore from nothing; on the other hand, the measurable energy of all bodily stimuli would vanish into nothing insofar as from them the unmeasurable fact of consciousness, say of concepts, would arise.

Refutation of "psychophysical parallelism." The inconsistency of the master was on this point too evident to have escaped the pupils. Geulinx, completing the separation of consciousness and body, decided without hesitation in favour of the much-discussed expedient that on the occasion of the act of will no less a one than God brings about the corresponding movement of the human body, and on the occasion of the bodily stimulus the corresponding representation in human consciousness (occasionalism)!

Spinoza hit upon a solution no less mistaken but considerably more ingenious. According to him, (unextended) consciousness and (extended) being are merely two distinguishable sides of the selfsame thing (substance), which complement one another with inviolable lawfulness. "The order and connection of ideas is one and the same as the order and connection of things" -- thus runs the prejudice fundamental to his entire system (presented as the seventh "proposition" in the second part of his "Ethica")! Wherever, then, bodily movements occur, some exactly corresponding fact of consciousness is also to be found, and wherever a consciousness, of whatever it may be, flashes forth, there exist exactly corresponding bodily processes. For this reason his doctrine has been given the quite unsuitable name of a philosophical pantheism.

Only Leibniz, in attempting to contrive it still more cleverly, unintentionally disclosed the subreption that had been veiled by Spinoza's artfulness, with his celebrated "pre-established harmony." If facts of consciousness and bodily processes invariably accompany one another without error, without requiring a continual divine mediation for this purpose, this derives from an original arrangement of God, according to which the two relate to each other much as two clocks keeping time with the accuracy of seconds! From this there developed the doctrine of "psychophysical parallelism": consciousness and body do not, it is true, act upon one another; but to every neural process there corresponds a fact of consciousness belonging to it alone, and to every fact of consciousness a neural process belonging to it alone. It is false for the following reasons: a) Here a divine clockmaker would indeed be presupposed; hence faith, not knowledge!

b) But nothing at all would be explained; for one could manifestly think away either one of the two systems at will, the mental or the physical, without thereby impairing the one that remained.

c) The assumption is, however, even impossible, because it annuls its own presupposition! For this was: two systems, consciousness and world, whose reciprocal relation was to be explained. A consciousness existing by itself, however, would know only its own contents and not the slightest thing about a world standing over against it!

Refutation of ideologism. The objection raised in the last place is suited, moreover, to a devastating critique of every form of ideologism (Berkeley, Kant, Fichte, Schelling, Hegel, etc.), as it emerges perhaps most glaringly in Schopenhauer, who opens his metaphysical reflections with the non-thought: the world is my representation. Were he right, it would be impossible to demonstrate by any means of thought how man ever came to the manifestly erroneous belief in a world that is something other than merely his representation! And indeed, since the Indians or since Plato, no mortal has ever even seriously attempted to do so! Because, however, ideologism flatters the drive towards spiritualisation, not to say the spiritual arrogance, of historical humanity and is therefore just now celebrating a verbose resurrection among our "neo-Kantians" and "panlogists," we must, notwithstanding its groundlessness, pause here for a moment at its refutation.

When I look at the empty tabletop before my eyes, I can "represent" [vorstellen] to myself various things that might find a place upon it -- for example, a bowl of juicy fruits. But insofar as I "represent" such things to myself, I am not in a position to perceive them. And were the bowl of fruits now suddenly before me in the same way as the table serving as its support (regardless of whether actually or only in imagination), then in that very instant my directedness towards it would have lost the character of representing and would have exchanged it for the character of perceiving. A person may perceive many things not actually present here and now (illusory perceptions and hallucinations); but never has anyone confused his perceiving with his representing! Our representing can refer to what is perceivable; but to conclude from this that what is perceived is itself something represented would be at least as absurd as if one considered the circumstance that what is illuminated also shines to be a refutation of the existence of original sources of light! Perceiving is one thing and representing, making-present-to-oneself, thinking-of is quite another. Never can the two processes occur simultaneously with respect to the same object. The assertion that what is perceived is something "represented" is the most contrary-to-nature of all fabrications that the philosophically cultured person has concocted! Such a piece of madness would also never have been taken seriously, did it not seem clearly to promise fulfilment of the hubristic desire of the "man of spirit" that the whole earth with its billionfold life, indeed the entire universe with its myriads of stars, should be a product of the understanding peculiarly his own and thus legitimately delivered over to the claims of dominion of human caprice! For what that distortion amounts to is the de-actualisation of the world of appearances!

We need here pursue the difference between representing and perceiving only insofar as is required to bring to light the absurdity of the endeavour, and to this end we point out that the object of perception, as equally but also the dream-image, bears the character of a present actuality whose absolutely simply-to-be-found existence and so-being consciousness has only to confirm. Whether I apprehend the lion rushing towards me in the waking state or in dreaming: in both cases I find myself confronted with an event entirely independent of me, which in both cases perhaps fills me with horror. It is entirely otherwise when I "represent" the same event to myself! Then the present actuality to which I see myself once again related is different from the object of my representing or thinking-of; hence the latter bears the incomparably different character of a formation from within myself! This holds even for the so-called obsessive representation [Zwangsvorstellung], which forces itself upon one but nevertheless manifests itself as originating from the soul. In perceiving, I experience the perceiving state of consciousness as immediately conditioned by something absolutely foreign to the soul; in representing, I experience the representing state of consciousness as immediately conditioned by myself -- since the constraint towards it, perhaps mediately felt, always comes from one's own soul. Were what is represented always to actualise itself instantly, we would know nothing of an activity of representing; and were what is perceived to appear to us like something represented, then we would know nothing of actuality.

We shall return to this below and for the present note that the ideological belief in the consciousness-relatedness of the world of appearances falls all the more under the same fate as "psychophysical parallelism," namely of rendering impossible what it pretends to wish to explain: the assumption of a world existing independently of consciousness2).

## III. Bewußtsein, Erlebnis und Nervenvorgang. Verschiedenheit von Erlebnis und Bewußtsein. Aus dem im ersten Kapitel Erörterten folgt in Ansehung des Lebens: es wird in der Welt der Dinge nicht aufgefunden und ist mit „Kraft“ und „Ursache" unverträglich. Wie aber kommen wir dann dazu, sein Vorhandensein überhaupt zu vertreten? Weil wir selber erleben. Lebensträger ist Erlebnisträger, und die Frage nach dem Wesen des Lebens fällt zusammen mit der nach dem Wesen des Erlebens. — III. Consciousness, Experience, and Neural Process. Difference between experience and consciousness. From what was discussed in the first chapter follows with regard to life: it is not found in the world of things and is incompatible with "force" and "cause." But how then do we come to maintain its existence at all? Because we ourselves experience [erleben]. A bearer of life is a bearer of experience, and the question of the essence of life coincides with that of the essence of experiencing.

According to linguistic evidence, "consciousness" [Bewußtsein] means, as was already noted, the capacity to take cognisance of something, to know about something. One takes cognisance of something through the act of apprehension, and the original act of apprehension is the act of perception. The act of perception can, however, neither coincide with what is perceived nor be a kind of mirroring of it (assumption of the Greeks!). It does not coincide with what is perceived; for: if I see a red thing, I do not thereby produce a red act of perception; if I hear a sound, not a sounding act of perception; if I smell a fragrance, not a fragrant act of perception, and so forth. Nor is it a mirror-image of what is to be perceived; for: all sensory qualitative properties are incomparably different from one another, which is why one cannot give a person born deaf the slightest representation of tones and sounds by describing tones and sounds. Were the act of perception to correspond to each perceived qualitative property (in the manner of a mirroring), then all acts of perception would have to be incomparably different from one another. As a consequence, however, we could have no concept of perceiving and hence also no concept of consciousness. Were our taking-cognisance of the world a mirroring of the world, we would not even know that we had consciousness!

We draw from this the following conclusion: if every act of taking cognisance is necessarily different from every other act of taking cognisance only numerically and not in kind, then the sensory qualitative properties of which we take cognisance in perceiving must have been experienced by us. However the experiencing may relate to what is experienced, this much is certain: that it is entirely different from the act of taking cognisance.

Difference between experience and bodily process.

Experiences

offer, according to present-day understanding, two essentially different sides, one of which is called "sensations" [Empfindungen], the other feelings [Gefühle]. We remain for the time being with "sensation," but designate it by the name sensory experience. The occurrence of sensory experiences is tied to certain processes of the body, namely to the peripheral sense organs, the sensory nerves, and the sensory centres; and the natural scientist is inclined to equate sensory experiences with bodily processes. He thus dissolves sensory experiences into molecular movements of the sensory nerves. This is false for two reasons. a) Were the sensory experience, e.g., of the colour red to coincide with molecular movements in the optic nerve, we would perceive the redness in our head, not outside ourselves!

b) Then, furthermore, person A would also have to perceive the red-experience of person B, provided only that the processes in the optic nerve were sufficiently transparent. However, even if we saw all the molecular movements in the sensorium of a person just then "sensing" red, we would see precisely only molecular movements and not the slightest trace of the experienced redness! One can see colours, but not see seeing; hear tones, but not hear hearing, and so forth.

Consequently, sensory experiences are not objective bodily processes. But then no experience is an objective bodily process. We find experiences in the world of things no more than we found life in it. Since they are also not acts of apprehension, experiencing may be confused neither with existing things nor with the acts of consciousness directed towards them. To these deconstructive propositions concerning the nature of experiencing we immediately add two constructive ones.

Qualitative character of experiencing. -- All experiences are qualitatively different from one another. For if it is necessarily experiencing through the mediation of which we apprehend an unlimitedly large number of qualitative properties, then the mediating experiences must themselves be qualitatively different from one another.

Whoever harbours any doubt about this need only examine the contrary assumption. Were the process of seeing to coincide in kind with the process of hearing, it would be inconceivable how through the one colours, through the other sounds could be offered to us. Were the red-experience to coincide in kind with the blue-experience, then unquestionably for us red would also coincide with blue. As many qualitative differences of sense-data, so many qualitative differences of sensory experiencing! We must acknowledge this, even though for the moment it may be difficult for us to decide how experiencing is nevertheless to be distinguished from what is experienced.

Spatio-temporality of experiencing. No sound can be thought as actual that does not pervade a space and sound for a duration, however short; no colour that does not cover a surface, however point-like in its smallness, and persist for a span of time different from zero. If we remove space and time, we have therewith also already removed: colours, sounds, fragrances, tastes, temperatures, hardness, softness, roughness, wetness, and so forth. All sensory qualitative properties are thus properties of something spatio-temporal. If experiencing now mediates qualitative properties to us, then it must also have mediated to us their spatio-temporality; and if it can mediate the former to us only because it is itself qualitatively "attuned" by them, then the latter only through its own participation in the spatial and temporal being-outside-one-another.

The slightest reflection further instructs us that neither space nor time consists of a sum of indivisible units. We can divide no span of time in such a way that each part would not again be an arbitrarily divisible span of time; no volume of space such that each partial volume could not once more be divided without limit. Vanishingly small space is of one and the same nature as unlimitedly large space; vanishingly short time of one and the same nature as unlimitedly long time. Within the limits respectively set by us, both have the character of boundless continuity. Consequently, the spatio-temporal receptivity of our experiencing must also have the character of boundless continuity. Spatio-temporality, continuity, and qualitative character are extra-spiritual and accordingly incomprehensible.

Refutation of panlogism. -- Against this the panlogist objects: it makes no sense to speak of something extra-spiritual, since our very speaking about it betrays that we possess a concept of what is communicated; since, however, concepts are products of the spirit, we never have to do, in thinking, with anything other than products of the spirit. (Today's most fashionable philosophy!)

To this we reply: it is one thing to mean something judgingly, and quite another to penetrate it intellectually. The concept of redness is indeed a product of the spirit; but it means an actuality that is neither produced by the spirit nor ever intellectually penetrated by it. Were it otherwise, the judgement "this is red" would have to awaken in the person born blind as well the reflection upon the intuitive content of redness! If it accomplishes this only for the sighted person, the cause thereof lies clearly in the fact that it merely reminds him of something experienced; from which it inescapably follows: the experience of redness cannot originate from the spirit. But as not the experience of redness, so too not that of any other qualitative property; likewise not of their spatio-temporality; likewise not of the continuity of the spatio-temporal. As surely as the concepts thereof are produced by the spirit, just as surely do they possess only the one capacity of pointing to experiential contents, which are accordingly presupposed and for their part not to be comprehended.

"Concept" [Begriff] is indeed a (moreover, learned) derivation from "to grasp" [begreifen]; on that account, however, "I comprehend it" [ich begreife es] means by no means the same as "I have a concept of it" [ich habe einen Begriff davon]; rather, it means: I understand it, have intellectually penetrated it, taken spiritual possession of it. We have a concept, after all, even of the incomprehensible; but no one will claim that the incomprehensible is thereby comprehended! If we now maintain the incomprehensibility of all sensory qualitative properties, their spatio-temporality, and the continuity of the spatio-temporal, the question arises as to what in the world can in principle be comprehended and thus entirely penetrated by the spirit. This question can also be put as follows: what in the deposits of the activity of thinking attests only the understanding, without admixture of any experiential material. The answer is: immediately, merely the thought of the unity of what is meant by each concept, recurring in every concept; but in view of the circumstance that, in the face of a world of happening, the positing of unity repeats itself without limit, and the repeated unites once more in rows in the thought of plurality -- mediately also the concept of number. Numbers are what they are independently of personal experiencing, can be generated by the spirit without limit, and do not in the least partake of the fluctuations to which the pointing function of concepts is more or less always subject according to the individual differences of the changing bearers of consciousness.

If I speak to someone of five trees, I shall certainly be able to believe that he connects with "tree" the same concept insofar as to distinguish, as I do, what is meant from grasses, animals, stones; through no addition of however numerous adjectives, however, shall I be able to bring about: congruence of the sensory schemata of his and my concept of tree -- as is most clearly evident in the case of such formations about which judgements diverge as to whether they still count among trees or not; whereas their fiveness is manifestly entirely unaffected by whether one means the trees tall or short, close together or far apart, leafy or bare or needled, and it would be absolutely inconceivable for it to waver towards fourness or sixness! Put differently, in the case of (arithmetic) number-words the concept coincides with the content of meaning, hence they can be unambiguously replaced by formulaic signs of compulsory applicability (calculating machine!), whereby the life-remote nature of number also expresses itself in conformity with the law of expression3)! Accordingly, the world (external as well as internal) can be intellectually penetrated to the extent that it offers a countable side (from which the scientific wishful thought of "exactitude" is explained!).

To this we add: no qualitative property (colour, sound, fragrance, etc.) differs numerically from another; none differs numerically from space and time; space does not differ numerically from time; finally, the continuous does not differ numerically from the discontinuous (for, as is understood, only what is discontinuous can be counted).

## IV. Der Auffassungsakt. — IV. The Act of Apprehension.

Nature of the thing. -- Let us now attempt to set forth wherein the act of apprehension actually consists. There arises first the question: what do we apprehend? Answer: originally and immediately only things, mediately their properties and processes, and only with reference to things, finally, the finding spirit. To things correspond independent (concrete), to their properties dependent (abstract) concepts.

But what are things? First of all, units [Einser]; further, units held to be actual; finally, in each case epitomes [Inbegriffe] of boundlessly many properties. "Epitome" means the spatio-temporal coherence of all possible properties that may ever be ascertained of a thing, and is therefore not to be confused with the concept of a sum. Of no thing can one state how many properties it possesses, since their number constantly increases in proportion to our growing knowledge of it. Through touch I learn many things about the surface constitution of a thing that I had not yet learned through visual inspection, and I learn yet more with the help of a magnifying glass; not to speak of the material properties that reveal themselves in ever-growing number with every change of environment. The senses present to us something actual; but we could not regard the data of actuality as "properties" of something unless the existing one [die seiende Eins] already stood at our disposal, in which those data, so it appears, merely come to presentation.

In a single sentence, the determination of the thing would accordingly properly have to run as follows: the thing is the (posited) point of reference for a spatio-temporally coherent manifold of qualitative properties.

Refutation of sensualism. In complete contrast to this conceptual determination, sensualism has the qualitative properties "sensed" and accordingly sees in things "complexes" or "bundles of sensation-contents." In so doing, however, one moves in circles one way or another! For if by the "sensation-content" of the red, the loud, the hard one understands the perceived redness, loudness, hardness, then these would not be "sensation-contents" at all but inescapably properties of things already presupposed4). If, on the other hand, one understands by them actual sides of what is sensed in experience, then we recall that all experiencing flows temporally and therefore can also correspond only to a temporally flowing actuality. To bundle sense-data together could succeed only if each of them stood still, were an abiding something, and consequently already had the character of the

thing, which was precisely what was in question.

By way of dismantling the sensualist erroneous doctrine, let us go one step further in demonstrating the fundamental difference between thing and sensory experience. Not only does our experiencing flow, it is moreover altered at every instant. Not even in an arbitrary fraction of a second does an arbitrary sensory experience remain self-identical! Of the numerous entirely unassailable proofs of this we give only two.

a) Every sensory impression is subject after a few minutes to a noticeable dulling (adaptation). If we step from a dark room into a very bright room, we feel blinded in the first instant and see the individual things almost not at all. But after only half a minute the glare has noticeably diminished, and after five minutes we recognise everything with full distinctness. If, moreover, impressions repeat themselves sufficiently often, they tend to be entirely withdrawn from our perception. We are so "accustomed" to the pressure that our clothes exert upon a thousand areas of skin that we notice it only most incompletely even when we expressly attend to it. But if every impression is subject within a determinable span of time to a dulling of measurable noticeability, then it must itself manifestly have the nature of a state of affairs that changes, even if imperceptibly, in vanishing intervals.

b) If I have just now perceived an arbitrary thing for the first time (A), and I perceive it an hour later for the second time (B), then it follows from the circumstance that I recognise it again that the impression underlying the act of perception B must have been somehow different from the impression underlying the act of perception A. It has in case B, as one customarily says, attained the character of "familiarity." Compared with the grounding

impression of A, the grounding impression of B has become different by that component which brought it about that the thing now appears familiar to me, whereas previously it appeared new. Consequently, we can receive from no thing two mathematically identical impressions! If, however, the impressions that we receive from the selfsame thing are infallibly other and ever other, it would be absurd to wish to understand from impressional experiences the unity of the thing.

The thought of the unity of the thing touches most closely upon that of the selfsame identity of the thing. If we say of something that it is the same, we have manifestly grasped the something as a unity, regardless of how many properties or sub-unities may in turn be distinguished in it. Proceeding from the selfsame identity of the thing, one has believed it possible to explain its unity from the similarity of successive experiential contents in conjunction with the continuity of the transition from one to the other. However, even if their difference could no longer be noticed and would thus practically coincide with identity, this would always establish only an admittedly close connection between the temporal segments of what is experienced, but by no means the selfsame identity of the object. If I judge: this table is the same as the one I just saw (say, from a greater distance), then I have placed the two tables, the one from before and the one from now, side by side in the spirit and compared their properties with each other. I thus arrive at the concept of the table-thing by no means through comparison of impressions; rather, I compare the present with the remembered impression with respect to the already found and familiar table. In other words: in the second case I have the perception of the table together with a "representation" of that very same table, and I can dispose of this unquestionably only because I already perceived the table, that is, the thing, at the moment of the original perceiving. But what holds of two different impressions holds equally of two temporally different segments of any impression whatsoever. No stringing together of elements of impression would bring about the selfsame identity of the thing, had a first and single glance not already given me that existing one [seiende Eins] in respect of which all comparing (as well as distinguishing) first acquires a meaning.

Durationlessness of the act of apprehension. -- The self-identity of the thing, enduring with respect to time, requires that the thing-apprehending act take place in the temporally extensionless point. If it is timeless, however, then it must also be spaceless and of course qualityless. Considered with reference to time, every thing has the peculiarity of enduring during the span of its "existing." What does this mean? If we represent the time-span during which a thing exists in the form of a line, then our assumption of its equally uninterrupted duration presupposes that we believe ourselves certain of finding at the arbitrary point B of this line the selfsame something that we had found at the arbitrary point A. If we extinguish the points in question, we no longer have the possibility of finding selfsame identity in the temporal flux at a state of affairs, and then not even the possibility of knowing of the existence of the thing. The temporally unextended position of time, however, is posited only by a temporally unextended deed. For were we to make the experimental assumption that the finding deed required a temporal span, however slight, it would itself run in time, or it would flow with time, and would thus be incapable of dividing the temporal line at the unextended point! The time-dividing deed thus does not lie in time; rather, it strikes upon time, encountering it, as it were, just as in the image the dividing mark stands perpendicular to the line to be divided.

Perhaps it will be objected that actual time is nothing at all comparable to an extension, but rather the pauseless coming and going of never-to-be-grasped instants. However correct that may be, it does not at all affect the concept of continuity mediating the comparison, according to which space does not consist of metre-lengths, time not of days, hours, minutes. ## V. Der Bewußtseinsträger. — V. The Bearer of Consciousness.

The selfsame identity of spirit in all bearers of consciousness. -- In apprehending the existing thing, I have at the same time abstracted: a) from the temporality of what is experienced, b) from the spatiality of what is experienced, c) from the qualitative character of what is experienced.

One bearer of life differs from another bearer of life through the peculiarity of experiencing, that is, through its spatio-temporal qualitative character. If he has now abstracted from this, then what is found must be a selfsame both for him at different times and for different finders. In this manner different persons perceive the selfsame thing, notwithstanding that their sensory experiences are qualitatively different from one another. But then manifestly, in all reflective life-moments of all bearers of reflection, a selfsame must also be the disposition through which it comes about that the deed of reflection is accomplished. We call it, taken in and for itself, the spirit, with respect to its connection with the individual living being, the I [das Ich]. The justification of the name "spirit" follows from the simple consideration that it is after all the faculty of judgment that is under discussion, which at the very least belongs to the spirit. The justification of the name "I" follows from the circumstance that every arbitrary judgement requires a judging I. For if I judge: here stands a tree, I can also judge: I judge that here stands a tree; and so in every case.

If, however, the I is in essence the judging I and the judging I is in essence spirit, then it must be the same in all persons. Therefore every correct judgement holds absolutely, for every person, "without respect to the person." And conversely, the I is the same in all phases of life of a given person; in accordance with which everyone says: I did such and such at that time, notwithstanding that "at that time," say fifteen years ago, both his experiencing and the experiencing bodily

nature was entirely different from the present experiencing and its corporeality. As outwardly the thing-point is an abidingly selfsame, so inwardly the I-point. We call the I the "manifestation" of spirit in life, the thing the "projection" of the I into actuality. Things and I's are countable; and whatever else we count, we count with reference to them.

Suppose one has fully understood this, then one already knows: actuality independent of consciousness cannot possibly belong to the thing. Unconditionally actual are images; upon occasion of the impression, however, by virtue of the nature of the spirit, things taking their place are compulsorily merely thought. To state and demonstrate this was ever the endeavour of the admittedly rare Heracliteans among the thinkers, most emphatically and passionately Nietzsche, especially in his posthumous notes for the never-completed "Will to Power." Here are some passages from him bearing on this: "Logic is tied to the condition: granted, there are identical cases." "The belief in the existent proves itself to be merely a consequence: the true primum mobile is the disbelief in becoming..." "Our belief in things is the presupposition for belief in logic." "We need units in order to be able to calculate: therefore it is not to be assumed that such units exist. We have borrowed the concept of unity from our concept of the I... If we did not hold ourselves to be unities, we would never have formed the concept of 'thing.' " "We can only comprehend a world that we ourselves have made." "In fact logic holds only for fictitious entities that we have created. Logic is the attempt to comprehend the actual world according to a schema of being posited by us." "Parmenides said: 'one does not think what is not'; we are at the other end and say, 'what can be thought must surely be a fiction.' " His predisposed belief in the "will to power" prevented him from calling actuality by its proper name and from furnishing the compelling proof that things cannot possibly subsist in an actuality of appearance. By inserting this proof we facilitate for ourselves the more exact orientation concerning the essence of the I.

If the thing necessarily has the index of unity, then the question whether there is an actuality that has the index of thingness amounts to the other question whether unities can be actualised. This is impossible for the following reasons. In view of the plurality of things, what comes into consideration is naturally not unity as such (= absolute unity) but the countable one (= 1), the indivisible element of arithmetic. If we write the equation 1 = 1, we have posited the same magnitude on each side and yet have separated magnitude from magnitude; and upon this rests the whole of arithmetic and ultimately logical thinking itself. All counting and reckoning grows from the capacity to hold fast unalterably the individual one within the unitary union of the sum of ones; for which, with respect to the one, it can be said that it remains in every combination unaffectedly the same. Let us imagine that we could keep the individual one in view through a page-long calculation as if it were a sense-thing: the one thus individually attended to would emerge from our operations of dividing, multiplying, subtracting, adding, finally as exactly the same as what it was at the beginning. Absolute immutability, however, constitutes the reverse side of absolute isolation. The unions into which our one seemed, after all, to enter were accordingly not connections but merely systems of relations. "In the parts of number," Aristotle already correctly observes in his treatise on the Categories, "there is no common boundary at which the parts of number connect, but... they are separate." In order to form the concept of connectedness, we must grasp the connected as a totality within which the condition and the peculiar character of every member belonging to it is co-determined by the condition of the remaining members. The concept of the sum, on the contrary, requires taking the ones together under the strictest isolation of each individual one. A segment of actuality with the index of unity would accordingly be something absolutely isolated and thus outside all connection with the rest of actuality -- which is to say, a de-actualised actuality, because divested of the capacity through which alone an actuality can first attest itself: the capacity to appear, directly or indirectly!

We look upon the stone in our hand like an actualised one; but the stone would not have the property of weight unless it, breaking through the limits of its thingness, stood in connection with the entire planet, which in turn could not move without connection with the sun. Though we may think away arbitrary properties, including those of heaviness, hardness, and impenetrability, we must in the end leave intact the conditions for the fact that the state of affairs held to be actual is able to appear to us directly or indirectly. It appears to us, however, insofar as it is capable of communicating impressions to us, and it conveys impressions to us by virtue of some connection between it and the impression-receptive soul. The isolation attributed to it consequently contravenes its being actual. If, then, there can be nothing isolated in actuality, then there cannot be things in it!

Yet possibly one adduces magnetism as something that we regard as actual even though it by no means "appears" to us, or indeed the hypothetical ether. However, what appears to us is the swinging of the magnetic needle, and what appears to us is light, for whose explanation we have devised the ether; and everyone sees that one would never have thought of speaking of the actuality of either magnetism or the ether, were there not certain appearances regarded as produced by those powers which admittedly do not directly appear to us.

While on the one hand, then, we are unable to hold anything to be actual that does not cohere with the totality of all that is actual, on the other hand, in order to be able to think it, we must take the actual as something thing-like, thereby isolating it and depriving it of the possibility of cohering! Herein the actuality-foreignness of the spirit that compels us to this expresses itself, and from it follows inescapably that logical truth can only point in the right direction towards what it can never reach.

But what if spirit is indeed the unity and the I the form of existence of spirit in the individual being -- would the I then also be something merely thought, a mere noumenon, and thus not present in actuality at all? To this it must first be replied that it would of course be senseless, since it leads to a regress to infinity [regressus in infinitum], to make thingness the reflection of the I and then to volatilise the I, that from which it is supposed to be the reflection, once again into a reflection. As surely as all unities other than the I were first posited by the I, so surely must the positing I be a being actually present in the world of appearances: the I is the only possible noumenon that is at the same time also phenomenon! How this is to be reconciled with the impossibility of the appearance of unity we discuss below; here, however, the other question already -- how the surely absolute unity of spirit, as it were, splits into a plurality of I's; in consequence of which the individual I is just as sharply distinguished, indeed completely separated from the other individual I as every individual one from every other in the union of the sum! The answer is: I am I insofar as, along with all other I's, I participate in the one and the same spirit. I am my own I insofar as my participating course of life is of indivisible peculiarity (individual) and therefore different from the course of life of all other bearers of life.

On the nature of the I. If the personal I is not spirit as such but spirit fettered to life, then it must be not merely thought but also experienced (felt). And undoubtedly everyone feels not only that he is living, but also that it is he, thus his I, who lives. If we attempt to reflect upon the content of the I-feeling, it emerges that it is through and through nothing but the feeling of existence. I feel myself as an I says exactly the same as: I feel that I am. Herein lies a fundamental difference from all other feelings. For these each have their particular "colour" and thereby let me know how I am disposed; the I-feeling, by contrast, has no colour at all, being merely the feeling of being-present. To every mood there is a corresponding counter-mood, just as black stands in contrast to white, blue to yellow, green to red. I can feel sad or glad, busy or bored, active or idle, exalted or depressed, worshipful or contemptuous, loving or hating; only to the feeling of my existence can no polar counterpart be devised (for non-being, as one notes, is merely a concept, not an experience). Our analysis of the act of perception is thus confirmed by retrospective reflection upon the content of the I-feeling, since according to it too the I is nothing other than uninterruptedly persisting self-identity with respect to the passage of time.

Closer examination, however, shows us the existential character of the I in yet another light. If there is no polar counter-feeling to the I-feeling, all other feelings taken together nevertheless stand in a different kind of opposition to it. If I-hood is existence, then in relation to it all surges and movements of the soul [Gemuet] appear as something that befalls the I, and consequently in every befalling the I remains what it is by asserting itself against the befalling. Placed in an actuality of happening, being becomes the assertion of being, the I-feeling becomes the drive for self-preservation of the I. If the being-character of the I constitutes the pattern of the thing, then the assertion-character of the I constitutes the pattern of the efficacious power of things, and therewith of "cause" and "force." Of this too Nietzsche knew as no one else! He had penetratingly recognised, not so much that the con-

cept of effecting originates in ourselves -- which has indeed since Hume been the common property of philosophy -- but rather its inseparability from the thought of thingness, in accordance with its origin in the assertion-side of the I. Here again some attestations: "The attempt to comprehend what happens as a kind of connection and change of position of 'beings'... this ancient mythology has established the belief in 'cause and effect.' " "The properties of a thing are effects upon other things." "What gives us the extraordinary firmness of the belief in causality is... the belief that all happening is a doing..." "The only force that exists is of the same kind as that of the will."

The thing is the point of reference, identical with itself in the course of time, of a coherent sequence of images. If we take into view the fact of its persistence, then the image-qualities become, as was explained above, properties of the thing; if, by contrast, we take into view the circumstance that every thing arises and perishes, then its qualitative property becomes the qualitatively determined efficacious power. Instead of the actuality of happening we now have a derived actuality of movements and processes arising from causes and forces.

The heated oven, for example, has the property of warmth; but by virtue of this also the capacity to communicate warmth to its surroundings, to ignite a match brought near, to bring water to a boil. A stone has the property of hardness; but by virtue of this also the capacity to crush butter and to shatter more brittle glass, and so forth. What, considered from the standpoint of existence, are properties -- that very same, considered from the standpoint of the assertion of existence, are causes or forces7). As surely as the being-character of the I in a world of happening is at the same time the assertion-character, so surely does the concept of the thing already contain the concept of cause and force. The popular opinion that one arrives at the thought of mechanical causality through the observation of "lawful" sequences is so very false that, on the contrary, there are "laws" of the course of nature only insofar as we have posited things as their foundation. As the thing as such is projected existence of the I, so the thing as cause is projected assertion of the I! The world of "matter" constitutes the counterpart of the finding spirit, the world of "forces" the counterpart of the willing spirit. Every "object" [Gegenstand] necessarily becomes a resistance to every other object (which is why formerly "object" was rendered, as literally as it was conceptually precise, by "counter-throw" [Gegenwurf]!).

Soul and living body. We have now arrived at the point where we can answer in succession the questions of which we showed at the outset that the present worldview must renounce their answer.

To be alive means to be capable of experiencing, and the capacity for experiencing demands an experiencing soul. After what has preceded, we need no longer discuss why the soul does not stand in the relation of cause to effect with the living body, and therefore disclose at once the actual connection: it is that of meaning with the appearance of meaning. The soul is the meaning of the living body, and the living body is the appearance of the soul. Neither does the former act upon the latter nor the latter upon the former; for neither of them belongs to a world of things. In conformity with the inseparability of "effecting" from the mutual action of things, the relation of cause and effect denotes merely a relation between separated parts of an already dissolved connection; meaning and appearance, however, are a connection itself, or rather they are the archetype of all connections. For anyone who finds it difficult to envision a relation incomparably different from and incomparably more intimate than the relation of cause to effect, let him call to aid the analogous relation of the sign to the signified.

The cause of a speech-sound lies in certain movements of the larynx, the tongue, and the lips, which in turn originate in the brain; the effect of the speech-sound is a vibration of the air that continues through the movements of the hearing apparatus into the molecular processes of yet another brain. However far we trace back the one or follow up the other, we never step outside the circle of bodily processes, and we would find in neither the body of the speaker nor in the body of the hearer anything of the meaning of the speech-sound. This very speech-sound, however, signifies among other things a concept, is for this reason uttered by the speaker and with reference to it apprehended by the hearer. The speech-sound is the sign of the concept, the concept is what is signified by the speech-sound. Between sign and signified there subsists no relation of cause and effect, and yet we find it difficult even to distinguish them from one another. Whoever wishes to communicate something thinks as a rule not of the how of speaking but of the meaning to be communicated; and whoever understands something spoken hears the meaning out of the sounds without necessarily taking cognisance of the sounds themselves!

In exact analogy, just as the concept is contained in the speech-sound, so the soul is lodged in the living body; the former is the meaning of the word, the latter the meaning of the living body; the word is the garment of the thought, the living body the appearance of the soul. Just as little as there are wordless concepts, just as little are there appearance-less souls! (Souls that, as it were, haunt behind the appearance are chimeras!) And just as little, finally, as there are meaningless words -- for without meaning the sound would no longer be a speech-sound -- just as little are there soul-less appearances. If every soul necessarily appears, then all the more does the converse hold: everything that appears is ensouled. With this we have already gone a step further.

## VI. Die Seele als Bildseele. — VI. The Soul as Image-Soul.

The problem of soul-finding. Not things but images are ensouled: this is the key to the entire doctrine of life. No "natural science" can possess it, because its actuality is not the original actuality of images but merely the derived actuality of posited things.

If things, as we have seen, are impossibly ensouled, then surely the images must be ensouled! We do not repeat the proof for the former, but briefly summarise its result. Everything thing-like stands in a closed "causal nexus"; consequently there is between it and the soul no interaction, because this would tear the chain of causes, and still less a parallelism, because this would annihilate the connection of both. In a world of things, causes, and forces, souls have no room; their home is the actuality of images. Of affirmative proofs thereof, the one drawn from the process of soul-finding alone is already sufficient.

We considered in passing in the second chapter whence the conviction of one's own being-alive stems; but not yet whence the conviction of the existence and character of alien life. The prevailing opinion holds that one arrives at knowledge of alien experiencing through transfer of one's own onto the world of appearances. We pass over the irresolvable self-contradictions into which one falls with this view and seize upon only one circumstance at which it shatters. Transfers of experience, called "empathies" [Einfuehlungen], undoubtedly occur constantly and can never be entirely avoided, but they are no source of cognition; rather, they are the most weighty source of deception. If, in elevated spirits after a brilliantly passed examination, I find all persons I encounter looking more cheerful than usual, then I have indeed empathised my own pleasure into them, but precisely to that extent I have also deceived myself about their actual moods. Our question, however, aims at the source of our knowledge of the actual character of alien souls, not of our illusions about them.

We would not even know that alien souls existed, much less how they were constituted, were not our experiencing of the world-appearance immediately and originally an experiencing of the appearing of living souls. Indeed, the name "appearance" would have no sense without this! Since, as extra-spatio-temporal, neither the point upon which we think the image-characteristics referred in the unity of the thing, nor the concept of the thing, nor finally the positing deed of spirit would be capable of "appearing," the appearance would lack the content that appeared. That what truly appears in it is souls -- which admittedly would not first need to be proved to the "natural man" but does arouse strong misgivings in the cultured person -- we ourselves continually attest with numerous turns of phrase and thereby with our language-forming and language-understanding experiencing.

Does the sensory

For this innumerable attestations offer themselves.

The human body has the property of warmth, a piece of ice the property of coldness; but the colours of a painting are also "warm," those of another "cold." The bark of a tree is rough, but "rough" too is the sound of a bellowing bull. Sharp is the knife, no less sharp the whistle of the locomotive and the smell of vinegar. The thing-property of sounding and the thing-property of colour have absolutely nothing in common with each other; and yet we speak unhesitatingly of "tone-colours" [Klangfarben] and "colour-tones" [Farbentöne]. Height characterises the mountain, depth the well-shaft; but by this very same mark the tonal qualities are distinguished from one another!

∗If we speak of the difference between "high" and "low" [deep] tones, it certainly does not occur to anyone to suppose that the former float down from the stars, the latter up from the chasms of the earth. Yet how do we come to distinguish certain kinds of sounds, whose sensory content is incomparably different from the sensory content of visual space, by means of an opposition of locations in space? It would be idle to refute the various misguided views with which one has sought to explain this, and let it only be noted in principle that the linguistic transfer-theory, here too popular, can satisfy only thoughtlessness.

Language swarms with genuine transfers. "Mountain-ridge" [Bergruecken, lit. mountain-back], "table-leg" [Tischbein], "lever-arm" [Hebelarm], "grasshopper" [Heupferd, lit. hay-horse], "calyx" [Blumenkelch, lit. flower-goblet], "weathercock" [Wetterhahn], "seal" [Seehund, lit. sea-dog] are some examples thereof. But how does the transfer come about whose result is, say, the name "mountain-ridge"? We have, whether consciously or unconsciously, compared with one another the appearance of the animal and the appearance of the mountain, and on the basis of a fleeting similarity of their overall shapes found for the body part of the animal called "back" a correspondence in the shape of the mountain. However familiar such compound words may be to us, we remain nonetheless capable at any time of assuring ourselves of their figurative character. We need only attend to it in order to know at once that the "back" of the mountain is only improperly a back, the "arm" of the lever only quasi an arm, the "belly" of the pot only so to speak a belly; and we find, even without linguistic talent, for each of them immediately a directly designating replacement ("mountain-ridge," for example = crest of the mountain, or upper edge, or profile-line). Were we asked, by contrast, how a high tone differs from a low tone, we would have either to let the questioner hear two tones of different pitch, or else we would resort to circumlocutions that stand on the selfsame line as the one to be explained and require no less an explanation. "High" tone = "bright" tone = "pointed" tone = "light" tone. "Low" tone = "dark" tone = "broad" tone = "heavy" tone. The high tone is thus by no means merely quasi high; rather, it is truly so; and likewise the rough sound is truly rough, the warm colour truly warm, the sharp smell truly sharp! Not everyone need notice the differences in question; but whoever does notice them does so just as immediately as he distinguishes loud tones from soft or black from white!

All attempts at explanation are for now and ever condemned to hopeless failure so long as one has not understood that the statement -- on the one hand, that a colour is red, and on the other, that this very colour is "warm" -- concerns two different actualities: the derived actuality of things and the original actuality of images! When with colour-names we distinguish certain peculiarities in the surface constitution of things, we distinguish by contrast peculiarities of the soul coming to appearance in colour, with statements such as: a colour is warm, another cold. In their thing-property meaning we can apply the names to the things themselves and originally always do so (a red, yellow, green thing); in their soul-property meaning, however, at first only to the properties of things (warm red, cold blue). Although we readily re-thingify the properties and assign to them in turn property-adjectives, we need only compare with "warm red" for instance "dark red" to notice at once that the latter aims merely at the closer characterisation of the redness, the former, however, at the character of the redness. If, accordingly, the same words designate both properties of things and characters of images, then the grounding impression must in each case have been experienced by us as expression of a soul capable of appearing; whereby the sole even conceivable source of all soul-finding would be established.

It is worth pausing another moment at the "high" and "low" tones. Here names are used to distinguish the tones themselves directly by their tone-characters; from which it is readily apparent that other peoples and other ages prefer other property-names; the Greeks of antiquity, for example, "sharp" and "blunt." It would be easy for us, but would draw us away from the main subject, to demonstrate the character-kinship of all these among one another and especially with the characters of visual space8); and let only a glancing light fall on why, in the domain of tones, the designation of the tones themselves seems to have been universally omitted. Compared with tactile and visible thing-properties, sounds and tones, though no less impression-determined, are by virtue of their fleetingness far more difficult to relate to abiding substrates and remain by that much closer to appearance, seeming, as it were, only to play between things rather than to adhere to them. Attending to the meaning of words, we notice that the thingification of visible thing-properties proceeded in the direction of materialisation, that of audible ones in the direction of ensoulment. A colour can be compared to a substance [Sache], a tone only ever to a being [Wesen]. The application of straightforwardly characterising names to tones themselves results from their comparatively thing-independent essential nature9).

Let us recall the word we used above for the soul in order to make immediately recognisable its relation to the living body -- the word "meaning" [Sinn] -- and language offers us for the immediacy of soul-finding a most surprising confirmation, in that it uses the word in question in a threefold meaning: to designate, first, the essence of every thing (according to which a process is either "meaningful" [sinnvoll] or "senseless" [sinnlos]); then, personal character (cf. "light-mindedness" [leichter Sinn], "heavy-mindedness" [schwerer Sinn], "disposition" [Sinnesart]); and finally, the organs of the body that mediate to us in waking the image of the world, the bodily "senses" [Sinne]. Appearance itself is the bearer of meaning; for appearance and only appearance is ensouled! Or:

"The image that falls upon the senses,

That and nothing else is the meaning of the world."

On the intuitive basis of non-intuitive states of affairs. Perhaps, however, one reproaches us for having made a very partisan selection, to which another collection of such names could easily be opposed that designate psychic properties without the mediation of sensory ones; for no intuitiveness whatever, it might be said, is to be discerned in words such as: sorrow [Kummer], grief [Gram], joy [Freude], anger [Zorn], consciousness [Bewusstsein], spirit [Geist], soul [Gemuet], desire [Begierde], soul [Seele]. To this the reply would be that the same holds for them too, as indeed for all only conceivable words for non-intuitive states of affairs, although in many cases the pictorial starting-point of their roots has long since faded in the development of meaning-feelings and sometimes can scarcely be ascertained even philologically.

The original sense of Kummer, for example, is encumbrance, burdening; Gram proves to be cognate with the Greek χρόμαδος = gnashing; Freude, formed from froh, goes back to an Old Norse frár = quick, nimble; Zorn branched from the root ter = to tear (Old High German zëran = to tear apart, to tug); the "knowing" [Wissen] contained in "consciousness" [Bewußtsein]10) leads to the root wid = to see, to find, Greek ἰδεῖν, Latin videre; soul [Seele] comes from the Proto-Germanic saiwolô, which is probably cognate with the Greek αἰόλος = mobile. Whenever we can trace the history of such names sufficiently far back, we unfailingly arrive at a sensory primal meaning and not infrequently find even for today's synonymous soul-words the same or similar starting-images among the most diverse peoples. Thus, to mention only what lies near at hand, the soul is conceived as breath or respiration in the Latin spiritus, the Greek ψυχή and πνεύμα, the Slavic duar, the Indian âtman and prâna, the Arabic nefs and ruh, the Hebrew nephesch and ruach; as something blowing, shaking, agitated in anima and animus, in θυμός (from θύειν = to rush), possibly also in the German "Geist," if the conjecture of its derivation from Old Norse geisa = to rage is correct. The more the investigation spans widely separated linguistic circles, the more compellingly the intuitive basis of all naming whatsoever reveals itself, and therewith the connection of properties of the being with sensory qualities is disclosed as resting upon the determination of the latter to be the appearance of the former!

If our academic scholars still continue to speak of transfers, they might in fairness consider that not even the imagination of a storyteller could hatch out anything more whimsical than scientific psychology! For this science would find itself in the astonishing position of continually using consciously transferred and improper expressions without, however, ever being able to tell us what it actually means! For though it bring forth a hundred or even a thousand words to elucidate a single metaphor, each of them would in turn be a metaphor! Indeed, the matter achieves an almost tragic comedy through the indubitable fact that among the linguistically available names for characters of life, the psychologist most spurns and naturally must spurn precisely those which, like "understanding" [Verstand] or "will" [Wille] or "soul" [Gemuet], no longer reveal their pictorial origin. Understanding, will, soul, spirit, temperament seem to him obscure and suspect of premature "hypostatisations." He hopes to remain more sober if instead he speaks of psychic "processes," inner "movements," mental "activities," or even of "forces," "energies," "associations" [Vergesellschaftungen], "interweavings" [Verwebungen], "fusions" [Verschmelzungen], or of "tension" [Spannung], "damming" [Stauung], "release" [Loesung], or of the "field of vision" [Blickfeld] and "focal point" [Blickpunkt] of attention, or indeed of the "narrowness" and "breadth" of consciousness (!), of "sub-threshold," "supra-threshold," "ascending," "sinking," "self-maintaining," "displacing," "mutually combating" representations (to say nothing of the quietly intimated game of hide-and-seek with learned-sounding foreign words, each of which is all the more borrowed from the world of sensible things). But what has he accomplished with such fraying? In the sense of his own mode of observation, precisely this: to describe the entire inner life with the help of crudely palpable "transfers" from the domain of the senses, whereby, in decided contrast to the colourful and resonant richness of everyday language, he displays a marked predilection for similes drawn from mechanics! Difficile est, satiram non scribere!

We hold that no doubt now remains. Colours, degrees of warmth, spatial properties, etc. serve to describe ensouled personalities only because they are themselves ensouled.

It would be impossible, and it would not even be attempted, to characterise souls with the help of the world-appearance, if it were not precisely the souls and exclusively the souls that "appeared" in the world! Actuality "in itself" is a world of ensouled images or of appearing souls; and were it otherwise, then no one would ever have communicated what stirred his own soul, because there would have been no one to whom the communication could have reached!

At this point we anticipate a finding of the greatest significance, to be elucidated further below, with which we place ourselves in opposition to the entire psychology of the modern era. Even if its most accomplished representatives recognised that the act of apprehension must be added to the impressional experience in order for what is experienced to become an object of perception, yet none of them has investigated and properly understood the impressional experience itself! Caught in the English erroneous doctrine of a five-part analysis of "sensing," they have both misjudged the nature of genuine sensation, to which we shall return later, and above all entirely overlooked the fact that all sensory experiencing possesses, in common with the experience of dreaming, a fundamentally different other side: the side of beholding [Schauung]. That by which the impressional experience enables us to refer what is experienced to things cannot possibly be the same process by which it compels us, and indeed more originally, to refer it to beings. If the former, as we shall yet demonstrate, occurs by means of sensation, the latter does so with the help of beholding, which is its sibling. Whoever touches something hard and again something soft experiences at the same time, with the very same originality, the difference of their character. But if the character of hardness and the character of softness can unquestionably also be experienced where there is nothing to touch, then it cannot have been the genuine process of sensation of touching contact, and must rather have been the process of beholding through which the perception of essence was mediated. On the basis of sensation the ensouled bearer of spirit perceives propertied things; on the basis of his beholding, appearing souls. We further add: beholding can occur without sensation, as the state of dreaming alone already proves; sensation, on the other hand, requires the support of beholding in order for what is sensed to be noticed -- on which more below11).

What lives in the so-called living thing. We have hitherto spoken of the life of images, hence of the original actuality, and therewith of the

life of the universe; for we may of course not distinguish between living and non-living things! Will it be rightly understood when we add: appearances are without exception living, things without exception non-living? Grasped in the sense of images to be experienced, not only plants, animals, and human beings live, but also rock, cloud, water, wind, and flame; the mote of dust lives, the brick, the writing-desk, the starry sky, indeed space and time. Grasped in the sense of things merely to be thought, however, even the human being, like all other things, is merely a compound of mechanically moving atoms. We shall make our meaning most readily clear with the help of the indubitable difference that exists between the life of the corporeality of the dead and the living animal, the dead and the living plant. This difference presents itself to us as follows: in the wood of the living tree appears the individual life of the tree; the logs of the felled tree, by contrast, are partial expression of the life of the earth. We thus distinguish the life of the macrocosm from the life of the microcosm, and our question concerning the so-called origin of life on earth runs not: how did dead matter become living plasma; rather, it runs: how did the life of the earth become cellular life? "In all that is organic, articulated in members," says Carus, the most convincing exponent of late-Romantic knowledge, in his beautiful book on the life of the earth, "the same life, consisting in the continual development of some individuality, is to be recognised, regardless of whether it is the formation of solar systems or of a growing plant that is being discussed; and no less... the rock with its crystalline structures or the spring with its rhythmic currents in relation to the whole of the earth is to be called belonging to a larger organism than is the bony structure with its crystal-fibres or the blood-stream with its pulsating wave-beat in relation to the life of the animal."

Both kinds of life, the macrocosmic and the microcosmic, in turn comprise numerous sub-kinds, about which, however, there is no need to treat further here. On the other hand, we must confirm the doctrine of the life of images, which we have hitherto validated only in the world-image itself, also at that side of the image of the world which, in the spirit of mechanistic thinking, would fall under the genus of living things -- the living organisms (in the narrower sense). To this end we confine ourselves to a single characteristic of the so-called living thing, whose distinguishing value has never been disputed: the characteristic of reproductivity. Not dust, stone, water, cloud, but every living cell reproduces itself (through pairing or by division). Everything that lives today consequently coheres with the very first protoplasms that arose on earth aeons ago; every living being of the present is linked by an unbroken chain to the living beings of an inconceivably remote past! Let us consider the state of affairs somewhat more closely.

From the beech tree falls a beechnut and thrives in the forest soil into a new tree. Does the mother-beech now somehow live on in the beech-child? Certainly not! We can fell and burn the former while the latter grows on cheerfully. Or does something of the matter of the old beech live in the renewed one? Likewise not! For the fully grown young tree contains not a single atom more of the matter of the fruit from which it grew. The matter of a person of thirty is exchanged down to the last molecular units compared with the matter of the same person at ten! If, however, neither the individual being is preserved nor the matter of which it consists, what is it, then, that reaches uninterruptedly through thousands upon thousands of generations? The only possible answer runs: an image! The image of the oak, the image of the pine, the image of the fish, the image of the dog, the image of the human being recurs in every individual bearer of the species. "Reproductivity" is the name for the process, forever inaccessible to physics, of the transmission of the primal image of the species from place to place and from time to time. It is not "matter" that lives, but the image wandering in the cycle of happening from body to body. The wandering image, however, is a self-transforming image: it transforms itself in the individual bearer from birth through growth, bloom, age, and death; it transforms itself in the transition to the new bearer, since no one repeats the other with mathematical exactitude; it transforms itself, finally, in the hundred-thousand-year age of the species; for species and kinds too are subject to becoming and perishing. Plutarch, an initiate of the Eleusinian mysteries, says: "No one remains, no one is a single one, but we become many, while only matter drives itself around a single image... and slips away again"; and Plotinus still calls matter the "receptacle of the images"!

We stand, as one notes, at the source of the concept of the "vis formativa." But everything set forth would be said in vain if our demonstration of the self-revelation of organic life too in images were to be misconstrued as though the image were a "vis," a physical force! In the great machine-house of physics there is room for neither souls nor images. In the world of causes and forces there are absolutely no images; but in the world of images there are still less causes and forces. If one wishes to compare the metaphysical formative power of images to an effecting, this would be the working of a magical force that does not move from without but transforms from within. Genuine transformations are physically impossible; to transform itself unceasingly is the distinguishing mark of everything living. Consequently, life lies outside the thought-world of things in the actuality of happening of images!

## VII. Über den Zusammenhang von Körper und Geist. — VII. On the Connection of Body and Spirit.

The problem of the actualisation of spirit. We know of spirit hitherto the following: coupled with the individual-living soul in the personal I, it constitutes the foundation of relating acts of reflection, or more briefly of thinking consciousness, whose bearer is solely historical man (and in part still prehistoric man). It is found wherever consciousness is found and is no more a part of the living body than consciousness is. But now we stand before a twofold contradiction! The living body, so we heard, is the appearance of the individual soul: but how then would it be the appearance of a personal I? Furthermore, it was shown that only souls can appear. The fact of self-finding consciousness, however, demands for its enablement the actuality of spirit as well, and spirit cannot actualise itself otherwise than by appearing. We recall at the same time what was already settled above: the thing-point is posited by spirit and is therefore pure noumenon; the personal I, however, which accomplishes the positing deed, must be an actually present being. We accordingly called it the only possible noumenon that additionally has the nature of the phenomenon! Even had we not yet found the unity in consciousness, the correlate of the act of apprehension -- the thing to be perceived -- would point compellingly back to a power within ourselves with such a character of unity as we were compelled to stamp upon the continuous flux of the world of appearances. Should, then, not only souls appear after all, but also spirit besides?!

Let us pause for a moment at the difference between the macrocosmic soul of images as such and the microcosmic soul of that particular image which presents itself to us as a living body, and let us think of its most primordial form, the simple cell. We shall then have to consider the soul appearing in it in a double manner: as a mere side of the soul of the universe appearing in the juxtaposition as well as the succession of innumerable images, and moreover as the peculiar soul of the ever-similarly repeating cell-image. By virtue of its belonging to universal life, every individual-living being coheres with the life first of all of the species, then of the earth, finally of the world; by virtue of its individual vitality, it has in addition a local centre of life, comparable to the position of the seemingly constant water-vortex. If we take the cell schematically as a sphere whose particular life is designated by the coherence of all parts with the one and the same centre of the total shape, then the individual life of the cell and therewith of every organic cellular structure proves to rest upon the fact that its image stands in connection with the stream of images only mediately, namely through an immediately conditioning centre. If, finally, with an eye to the planetary form, we call to our aid the revolving-around-itself, we may say that it constitutes the peculiarity of every individually living member of actuality to revolve immediately around its own centre, which for its part is found chained to the stepwise boundlessly extending life of the outer world.

We are all the more willing to forgo pointing out the great perspectives that could be opened up at this juncture for the essential understanding of organic life, since we have hitherto had to pass over the precondition of the entire mode of consideration -- namely, the process of the embodiment of appearance, of which we shall only later have occasion to treat -- and we indicate in passing only this one thing: that, compared with the inorganically living, the organically living appears in the light of an internalisation of life. While the former turns itself immediately outward -- in the language of forces, now pressing and pulling, now warming or cooling, igniting or freezing, illuminating or darkening -- the latter enters into contact with the outer world only by means of a central inwardness, in relation to which the appearing body plays only the role of the messenger receiving and bearing the reply. Here, however, we are concerned solely with the solution, derivable from such a consideration alone, of the captious question of how the extra-spatio-temporal spirit is able to emerge in the actuality of appearance. If the sphere revolving about its own centre is the glyph par excellence of the bearer of life and its midpoint that of its individual soul, then the spirit-endowed bearer of life -- or, put more briefly, the personality -- has acquired a new centre around which the former midpoint is now in turn held to revolve; and spirit-endowment would accordingly be eccentri-

city of the blood! The old saying about the opposition of "heart" and "head" has by no means merely figurative meaning. The transference of the centre of life into the I comes to bodily appearance in the growing preponderance of the cerebrum over heart and solar plexus! If we hear from antiquity of many ecstatics that in the state of "enthusiasm" their whole body had shone, we see in the spiritual religion of Christianity the luminous aura crowning only the head of the "inspired" and finally hovering above it as a dwindling crown of rays: a genuine symbol of the transposition of life to the eccentric locus of the spirit!

On the "proof by deed" of mechanics. Why and how it came about that within a prehistoric people, at some point for the first time, the extra-spatio-temporal spirit was able to intervene in the spatio-temporal living cell is a riddle not yet soluble today. Since, however, it has undoubtedly occurred, we are now in a position to state how spirit comes to effect in the cosmos. It acts neither upon things, nor do things act upon it, since it is after all rather spirit itself that has first transformed the flowing actuality of appearance into a world of isolatedly congealed and resistance-endowed things, for us, its bearers! On the other hand, since it has indeed entered into the living cell, it necessarily acts upon the life within us -- in the sense, to be sure, not of a physical but of a metaphysical acting -- and does so through the usurpation of power over the centre in the direction of the displacement of the soul and therewith ultimately of the de-souling of the living body. Our life, thus in part split within itself, acts by means of its appearance, i.e. the living body, in an equally soul-sundering and consequently destructive manner upon the cosmic as well as the organic life of the actuality surrounding it. For in the same degree as an I-shaped (= spirit-endowed = personal) soul, in judgingly seizing possession of the stream of images, must cut the stream into mutually resistant things through I-points read into it, and in this manner falsifies the happening with the concept of the machine, its willing posture aims, through the mediation of the living body, at an actual mechanisation of the course of the world: but this is available only at the price of the de-souling of the image, or the de-imaging of the soul -- in a word, of the murder of life! As from the received image the judged thing has come to be, so in place of the offspring of creative working there steps the deed that is essentially ever-negating and therefore killing.

The demonstration of the negating essence of the deed, because it requires a complete doctrine of the will, would go far beyond the scope of this investigation, and let it only be illustrated by pointing to some effects that all willing completes itself in destroying. First, however, since the spirit-endowed bearer of life believes himself proven, as it were, by striking blows through "force" and with far-reaching deeds, we must touch upon the scarcely avoidable question of how acts of will can be actualised at all. How is the mechanical intervention capable, one might ask, of altering actuality, if the object of this intervention, the thing, is merely a fiction? To this it would first be replied: as surely as the thing is a "positing of the spirit," just as surely is it something actually experienced with respect to which the positing took place. It constitutes the posited point of reference of spatio-temporal qualitative properties: the point of reference is thought up; but what has changed in qualitative properties in relation to it are impressionally experienced images! Whoever sees on the wall a paint-smeared piece of cardboard where others see the painting of a landscape, and scrapes off the colours in order to study them chemically -- he is neither willing nor capable of directly striking the landscape, since he has no knowledge of it at all; but he strikes it indirectly just as thoroughly as if he had perceived it and intended its destruction!

The alleged proof by deed of mechanics is exactly as old as a humanity that thinks in concepts and has exactly that long rarely failed in its power of persuasion. When before Massilia the legionaries hesitate to fell the primordially sacred oaks of the Gauls, Caesar himself steps forward and delivers the first mighty axe-blow; and because no thunderbolt strikes the blasphemer down, the Roman warriors are forthwith convinced that the daemon of the tree is not present at all, or that it has been slain, or at the very least condemned to impotence. But had Caesar truly refuted the daemonic soul of a tree whose felling he was able to bring about? He had done so as little as the one who refuted the landscape-painting by seeing in its place only flake white and Schweinfurt green and who indeed co-destroyed the painting by removing the spots of colour. Let it be noted, however: we speak here of the daemonic soul, hence a magical power, and by no means of a so-called "aesthetic impression," that most abominable chimera of the cerebralists!

One is so habituated to regarding images from the standpoint of thingness and happening from that of a lawful sequence of movements of things, that every sentence upholding the actuality of images encounters the hard-to-overcome prejudice that by the name "image" only the intuitive outer side of things is meant, perhaps including their effect upon the feeling of the beholder. Now, the thing-apprehension does indeed depend upon a beholding (one that remains, however, unconscious!), since both the qualitative properties in and for themselves and their particular epitomes in each case were co-determined by the compulsion that the spirit underwent through something sensually experienced. Even if the surface of the thing may, as the only thing also experienceable, build, as it were, the bridge to the image, it belongs to the thing no less for that than its material properties. It too decomposes, according to colour, shape, and tactile qualities, into nothing but constitutions posited by the spirit, hence bounded, or, if preferred, "formed," of the existing, in the stream of time resistingly persisting one of the thinkable thing; whereas the content of the merely experienceable image is a wave of the boundlessness of the stream of actuality and therewith an appearance of the essence of the world. Because it is an object of thought, the thing, together with all properties to be ascertained in it (both of its material and of its surface), is found again by every arbitrary bearer of perception at every arbitrary hour as ever the selfsame something; while the, if it is permissible to say so, "primal image" thereof, because only experienceable, never returns a second time even for the same beholder and, in order to be experienced in such a way that in the act of retrospective reflection it itself can be known, demands not merely a receptive readiness rare today, but in addition that favour of the moment which no exertion of human caprice can bring about.

In a humanity of triumphant understanding, which, to speak with Paul, almost exclusively exhibits "Pneumatics" [Pneumatiker], it is difficult to speak convincingly of states of affairs accessible only to the "Psychic" [Psychiker], to whom they appear, however, far more "self-evident" than our belief in mechanics! All antiquity (as every "natural people" still today) knew not only human souls but above all daemonic souls, and has left us precise testimony of how these customarily appeared to the beholder. According to this, the daemonically ensouled image stands in a "nimbus," which means as much as: in a luminously flowing cloud surrounding it; which is why we still occasionally speak of the "nimbus" of a personality and more distinctly of "radiant beauty"12). If we translate

what was then experienced with full power of beholding into a form of communication adapted to the person of the present and specifically using the example of the Celtic oaks venerated as divine, we may perhaps hope thereby for once at least to counteract the tendency to confuse the living image with the lifeless precipitate that the act of apprehension of the understanding retains in the "seen-thing."

When in the fecund moment the "Psychic," instead of merely perceiving the oak (whether with or without "aesthetics"), is able to behold its "primal image," then through the very same state of affairs that signifies to us the variously propertied tree, a daemonic soul has appeared to him, and this is to say: he has felt as overwhelmingly actual the fluidal shudder that murmurs down full of mysterious meaning from the crown of the tree. Something like this may hold for the nimbus within the limits of a character-formation that is still just capable of taking cognisance of the supra-personal content of feelings welling from the depths, groping and divining its way back to the reflection upon the experience of beholding.

Accordingly, the mechanical intervention in an extra-mechanical actuality presents itself more clearly as follows. Grown soul-blind to the nimbus, the "Pneumatic" still perceives the particular tree, including the atmospheric attendant circumstances of the moment, but as cut out from the pantheon of life by boundaries staked out spatially and temporally, in terms of properties and species! Thereby the "primal image" of the tree has been altered to its very core. For in the place of a daemonically living being holding converse with stars and storms, there has stepped the world-estranged one of the species-determined thing, whose supposed individual life, before the more intrusively probing gaze of the understanding, disintegrates into the machine-like play of measurable "energies." Yet this mask is also once again the primal image: however, stripped of its nimbus, peeled out of its "veil of Maya," divested of the protective, because into-the-whole-weaving, covering -- become naked, disenchanted, and henceforth lacking the capacity to make itself heard to a human soul! And therefore the deed directed at things will nonetheless do violence to the image of the world, and because it blindly shatters the appearance, will co-shatter the appearing soul.

Notwithstanding that all similes limp, let this side of the matter also be illuminated in closing with a simile. What would all the books of humankind be for a new race that, however manifoldly gifted

in other respects, had not the slightest inkling that there were written characters with whose help one could banish the most fleeting flash of thought into an eternal present! Were human beings extinct but their books still there, and were the new race now to come, it would see on the printed pages objectively the same thing we saw there, or could at least see it, insofar as it occurred to them to reproduce the types and lines carefully, and would nevertheless not perceive the least of the thoughts that would speak to the initiated from them! And supposing it burned all the books, it would among other things destroy the profoundest teachings of wisdom, although in its sense it would have destroyed only curiously ornamented paper! Just as the meaning of the written characters would by no means have been refuted thereby, and just as little their inherent capacity for bringing about world-changing destinies among those who would have understood how to read them, just so it proves nothing against the extra-mechanical and ensouled nature of images that they are destroyable by mechanical intervention.

The mention of a cultic tree-desecration may at the same time have served as an example for our thesis of the negating essence of the deed. Although the foregoing deliberations tacitly already include a main piece of the demonstration, insofar as they clearly show that the activity of the will constitutes, as it were, the practice to that abstractive operation of the faculty of judgment whose proper meaning is the exclusion of the soul, the intruding memory of many a "good" and excellent deed would yet have to be combated with weightier arguments; which is why we confine ourselves to settling the account of the supposed proof by deed of mechanics alone, by reproducing a short passage from a still unpublished work thereon that, besides the life-value, also calls into question the cognitive value of mechanistic thinking. "The machine -- nature, but nature outwitted and compelled to enslave itself -- can destroy life but never generate it! That we have truly comprehended actuality because we mechanically subjugate it -- this would hold only in a denaturalised world that were itself a machine, whereas the corresponding proof for a living world would first be furnished by the generation of life by way of achievement. However, just as little as we breathe life into the dead, just as unthinkable is it that we should ever create a machine that would sense. And does not the most fleeting glance at the actual state of affairs show us that tool and machine assail the realm of the

living, exterminate organisms in enormous numbers, devastate the countenance of the planet at an ever more furious tempo! Did we penetrate more deeply into the essence of the eagle's soul since we have rifles to exterminate the eagle? Did we unveil the secret of the primeval forest after we erected factories that have soon transformed even the last one into newsprint? Did we solve the riddle of the liquid because we know how to dam lakes better, to canalise rivers in the twinkling of an eye, and reckon the sacred element of the ancients only in horsepower? If, however, the mechanisation of nature, instead of enlivening, rather strikes at the very roots of all life, then we may no longer also persuade ourselves that it is the chief witness of genuine 'natural science.' On the contrary, we ought to take from such experiences that, in contrast to 'energies' and 'substances,' life is no kind of quantum that, consumed here in one form, reappears there in another. 'Energetic' processes can be reversed because they are time-withdrawn semblances of happening and, so to speak, its mimicry; in the temporal actuality of life no eternity brings back the second, and even the omnipotence of a God does not re-awaken what the death-blow of the will has felled! 'Energies' are preserved because they are lifeless; life builds upon the world of eternal transformation, where the concept of force finds no place. The 'proof by deed,' then, looks somewhat different from what is commonly supposed. Just as we can calculate of actuality only what is for us temporally enduring therein and, as it were, within the frame of its lifelessness, so we do indeed mechanically subjugate nature, but only in proportion to the extermination of the life in it!"

However much such examples and reflections may persuade the sympathetic reader to follow, we are prepared for the objection of the philosophically trained that, on the basis of a doctrine of the ensoulment of appearance as such, it should be impossible even to speak of the destruction of life, since according to it the corpse of the animal, the corpse of the plant must infallibly revert to the appearance of a non-organic soul of actuality! To this let only a hint be offered here in reply. First of all, however, let one hold before one's mind, say, the Canadian forests of tower-high giant firs, and in their midst, on a cleared opening, the confused jumble of pole-thin chimneys of a smoking factory-town; let one imagine the Colosseum torn down and from the stones, on the same spot, a sallow-white plastered block of tenements with workers' flats erected; let one turn one's gaze to the ten-

storey hotel barracks that brutal greed for profit has actually erected under the starry sky of the Egyptian desert in sight of the pyramids, and let one now ask whether in each case the primordially grown harmony of images has not been torn asunder and perverted into a grating discord! Even if it remains a hundred times true that some being must appear even in the factory chimney, in the chalky block of tenements, in the hotel barracks, yet no less true is it that this is a being that assails life. How this is to be understood, the following hint must here suffice. Insofar as in us spirit was able to actualise itself, our life as well as our appearance is riven, and that is to say, in us the original life of the soul contends with the parasitic life of the spirit. In proportion, now, as we act from out of it through the mediation of our corporeality, that is, comport ourselves willingly and in deeds, we carry the same rift into the image of the surrounding world. The mechanical intervention -- always a form of iconoclasm -- assails, through the self-division of life, the originally living image with parasitically living monstrosities, thereby suffocating actuality under phantoms, the appearance of the soul under larvae, which owe the possibility of their existence and the mocking semblance of vitality indeed to the blood that they have vampirically sucked from life!

On destiny. We must finally pause yet another moment at a question of doubt that even the most willing-hearted reader will most probably find himself prompted to raise. If the image standing in the nimbus, so he would object to us, is supposed to be a magical power, then a factual correspondence to this power must also be found in the world of things! Our entire exposition seems to run to the effect that, as a result of the weakening of the life of the soul in the human being through a parasitic life of the understanding, some bond was torn that originally subsisted between the world of images and the human soul. Even if accordingly a soul-impoverished humanity sacrilegiously dares to touch and blindly raging destroys what it formerly took into worshipful and loving custody, the images as such must still possess the forces -- living in the metaphysical sense -- whereby they are capable of acting both upon one another and in particular upon the image of the human being, and it would be expected that consequent phenomena thereof would come to light that would somehow also make themselves felt in the world of things! Now, we shall name that power after its manner of outward mani-

festation in the sphere of factuality, and should we thereby speak in riddles, we may commend such riddles to the reader for long pondering. What of the power of the actuality of happening of images works into the mechanical world of things is called: destiny; and because destiny becomes the heavier, the more terrible, the more fiercely the murderous battle between the two worlds rages, destiny almost always means: fate [Verhaengnis]. We ask first of all: can our science, which calculates everything and anything, can it also calculate destiny? Has ever a person, has ever a people calculated its future in advance? Have not, if at all, always only the Cassandras, the seers of the world blessed to their own torment, known it in advance? Did not every destiny always come about differently from what the calculating mind believed it could compute? Humanity destroys life mechanistically: but it will perish from its victories; it will end through the "vengeance of the Erinys!" "What he weaves, no weaver knows!"

Suppose among the watching Gauls before Massilia there had been a Druid: his faith in the living daemon of the tree would not have been shaken by seeing the sacred oaks fall and the fellers triumph with impunity. But he would have waited for the fate that from now on hovered above Caesar's head, indeed above "eternal Roma." And perhaps, if he later heard that Caesar at the height of his glory succumbed to the dagger-thrust of Brutus, he would have believed he knew: that was the daemon's vengeance! But who would refute him?! That could be done only by one who had calculated Caesar's destiny! But no mortal can do that, and never will it be possible! Schiller held that world-history is the world-tribunal, and indeed he understood this erroneously in a moral sense! Destiny, however, is no person, no human-shaped judge who would reward and punish according to "good and evil." Seen thus, only chance reigns! The "wicked" like the "bad" enslaves a thousandfold from the robber-baron's castle of success chain-laden slave-armies of beaten-down "good" people and dies after a life of gluttony softly and insensibly in extreme old age; and the worse peoples have from time immemorial exterminated the better ones! Yet there is a retribution of destiny, impenetrable to the faculty of judgment, and metaphysically Schiller was right. Rome conquered and subjugated the world, only to perish of the putrefaction of the subjugated! Spain murdered Atahualpa, the last Inca and son of the sun, only to perish of the plundered gold of Peru! France

cut off three thousand noble heads on the Place de la Concorde only to perish of the inheritance of the Revolution. (Its "victory" in this war is the consummation of its downfall, as the next fifty years will terrifyingly bring to light!) Germany founded the "Reich" in 1870, that is, the omnipotence of the soul-killing state, only to perish of that very Reich! The ways of destiny are measured by no human reason, and for the second time: What he weaves, no weaver knows! Such is the "proof by deed" of the adept, but against mechanics and the arts of calculation!

And one last word! What are, within the framework of this worldview, those seemingly history-making doers of humanity who mark the turning and breaking points of its calamitous course: the legendary Nimrod, Heracles, Theseus; the historical Alexander, Hannibal, Caesar, Constantine, Charlemagne, Frederick the Only, Napoleon; finally, the creative high priests, the inventors of deceptive values: the Confuciuses, Manu, Moses, Buddha, Zarathustra, Socrates, Christ, Paul, Mohammed? Nothing but executors of destiny. They believe they are driving, and they are driven; and what they consciously strive for is each time immeasurably different from what they actually bring about. They too do not know what they weave! There is as yet no philosopher of destiny; for hitherto there have been almost only philosophers of being, scarcely those of actuality (a few approaches excepted, among which the steepest occurred in the name of Friedrich Nietzsche, which is what, despite their deep inner dividedness, surrounds his writings with an enigmatically threatening sheen). But the symbolic thought of earliest times circled around this most abysmal of all problems -- crystallised in the Chaldean star-cult into the mantic system of astrology -- and from it a ray penetrated the Greek tragedy, which, if consulted, could give us better disclosures than that quite erroneously extracted thought of guilt-and-expiation with which the life-mortifying madness of sinfulness of Christendom captured even the poets. Insisting upon the "freedom of the will," we have become slaves of the machinal. As proof that, in contrast, the whole of antiquity was at bottom pathically disposed, it may suffice to recall those powers to which all peoples still close to the source imagined even the mightiest of their gods to be subject: the Moira, Heimarmene, Dike, Ananke, and the Fates [Parzen] of the Greeks; the Fata of the Romans; the Belisana and the fairies of the Celts; the Wala and the Norns of the Teutons. In all of them is mirrored, among other things, the belief rooted in the depths of life in the mysteriously incalculable rule of a destiny that not even divine caprice, much less human, could resist or escape. And whoever in turn recognised this can no longer "will" anything (except perhaps his "work"!) and looks only with horror upon the madness of the doers. And because he no longer stands in life spirit-emptied -- that would mean in the enduring ecstasis, where there is no more seeking, questioning, striving, judging, willing -- he becomes the deed-avoiding contemplator of life. To have to will is the mark of blindness; no longer to be able to will, the mark of un-blinding. And were one now to ask him: what should we do, what must we refrain from -- he could only reply: hold yourselves ready and worthy of the moment of reception; and should it never come, you would yet have done the only thing you could to save your soul!

## VIII. Entfremdung und Vergegenständlichung. — VIII. Estrangement and Objectification.

The life-process as polarising process. -- We must now close a gap intentionally left earlier. In the first section of the third chapter it was said: "Were our taking-cognisance of the world a mirroring of the world, we would not even know that we had consciousness." In the third section of the same chapter it was said: "If it is necessarily experiencing through the mediation of which we apprehend an unlimitedly large number of qualitative properties, then the mediating experiences must themselves be qualitatively different from one another." What we thus denied of consciousness -- that it mirrors the world, so to speak -- was precisely what we attributed to experiencing. But then the same would now hold for experiencing too: that by virtue of it we would be incapable of taking cognisance of it itself. This might indeed be self-evident insofar as through mere experiencing we do not take cognisance of anything at all, and hence naturally not of experiencing either. We wish here, however, to direct attention to two other points: first, the question of how we know of the experience if the experience itself does not even contain the precondition for it; secondly, the fact that an experience is never experienceable. We begin with the second point.

It was intimated earlier and must now be generalised: we experience something to-be-experienced, but not the experiencing. We see colours, hear sounds, smell fragrances; taste sweetness, sourness, bitterness, saltiness; touch pressures, warmth, cold, wetness, roughness, smoothness, dryness; but we do not see our seeing, hear our hearing, smell our smelling, taste our tasting, touch our touching. Further: we dream dream-visions, but not our dreaming; finally, we feel sorrow, joy, hope, love, expectation, reverence, hatred -- that is to say, something moving the soul; but we do not feel our feeling. Beholding experience is an experiencing of images, sensing experience an experiencing of embodied images, feeling experience an experiencing of the attractive force of images; but beholding is not an experience of beholding, sensation is not an experience of sensation, feeling is not an experience of feeling.

Whoever should doubt this in the case of feeling would be reminded that "to feel" [fuehlen] (originally from: to palpate, to touch), just like "to sense" [empfinden] and "to behold" [schauen], is a transitive process-word that receives its differentiated meaning only from the object to which it "leads over." Just as beholding would remain entirely empty without a beholdable image, sensation without a sensible body, so too feeling without a pull to be felt (whether towards something or away from something). We can here all the less set forth in extenso why a doubt about this tends to arise precisely in the case of feeling, since for that purpose we would have to refute at length the fashionable erroneous doctrine of the absolutely "subjective" nature of the feeling, and wish merely to point out that it can surely not be explained from its supposed subjectivity, but solely from the passional character of feeling, that probably all and certainly most languages have thingified feelings. Were it not something felt that bestowed upon every feeling its sense and content, it would be difficult to understand that human beings have since of old not merely distinguished their feelings from one another but even from themselves, saw them standing over against them like beings, and straightforwardly created gods of joy, of love, of martial courage! Experiencing must therefore be sharply distinguished from what is commonly called experiential content and what is more precisely either image, or body, or the attractive force of the embodied image. If we take as our model the sensory experience and designate what is common to all sensory experiences by the figurative name of mirroring, then our soul mirrors the world, but not the mirror!

With this we have now, almost imperceptibly, albeit seemingly at first in negative form, made recognisable the fundamental character of experiencing, which helps us first of all to the solution of the question, likewise already touched upon, of how -- notwithstanding their qualitative sameness -- experience and what is experienced are able to step apart from each other! It was emphasised that all experiences cohere with one another, comparable to the individual waves of the uninterrupted mass of water; it was further stressed that the image necessarily coheres with the image-receptive soul; but we must now expressly bring to notice why the latter connection is utterly different from the former. If the former is nothing but connection pure and simple, in the sense of parts of a flowing happening that can only conceptually be separated, the latter is the connection of the essentially opposed: a working image with the receiving soul; and precisely therein lies the meaning of experiencing, which we must now make clear affirmatively.

When we reflected before that no experiencing experiences itself, we now lay the emphasis on the reverse side thereof, according to which there would be no experiencing at all without something experienced in the experiencing! What is experienced, however -- and mark well, everything experienced -- stands in relation to the never-to-be-experienced inwardness of experiencing as something essentially external, foreign to the experiencing soul, and therefore and solely therefore as something immediately and irreducibly actual. We behold in waking and we behold in dreaming; but, although for reasons to be touched upon only below we attribute existence only to what is beheld in waking, yet in terms of actuality for the soul of the dreamer what is beheld in the dream stands not in the slightest behind the object of perception that he is able to apprehend in waking with the help of beholding. With respect to the "primal phenomenon" [Urphaenomen] of the contrariety of experience and what is experienced, comparable to nothing else in the world, we can now set forth the proposition: the distinguishing characteristic of that process which we call the life-process is the estrangement effected through it. "Estrangement" [Entfremdung], as one will recognise upon closer inspection, means the stepping-apart of something previously united or fused, and therefore includes as co-experienced the coherence of the estranged members. We demonstrate in a larger work, not yet brought to completion, that the enabling ground of experiencing accordingly to be expected -- namely the fusedness of soul and image -- can indeed be pointed out with full determinacy, while here the clarification that the fact of experiencing itself thereby receives must suffice for us.

The double-sidedness upon which we have thus stumbled has the peculiarity that the one side can never be exchanged with the other, while neither can subsist without the other. Such a reciprocal relation is called polar, and the polarity of what is experienced to experience is the pattern and archetype of all polarities of the world, of which many come to objective presentation, for example in positive and negative magnetism, in light and shadow, "attraction" and resistance-force, space

and time, but most especially in countless processes, states, and conditions of everything organically living. We recall the rise and fall of the movements of pulse and breath, the alternation of wakefulness and sleep, the mirror-image symmetry of every leaf as of every animal body, right and left, man and woman, brain and solar plexus. According to this we can also define thus: in the life-process a working and a receiving actuality are estranged from each other in polar fashion; in accordance with which there stands over against beholding the image, over against sensation the body, over against feeling the pull driving towards union or separation.

The achievement of the spiritual act. The whole of philosophy since Plato confuses the process of estrangement with the deed of objectification! The "psychologists" have driven the nonsense to its extreme by asserting of the spiritual act, clinging to the word "object" [Gegenstand], that it places what is experienced over against us! In so doing, however, they have volatilised the experience into the deed of reflection!! Let us consider: the object of my judging is the table I see, the sound I hear, the sugar I taste; but the object of my judging is also mathematics, unity, difference, equality, similarity, consciousness, thinking, judging, and so forth. Table, sound, sugar are consciousness-foreign objects (in the technical term, "consciousness-transcendent"); mathematics, unity, difference, or virtue and sense of duty, or thinking, judging, opining are consciousness-own objects ("consciousness-immanent"). No one imagines that virtue, difference, the sense of duty could stand over against one in the same way as the table upon which one looks with one's eyes. Were it the deed of thinking, however, that in objectifying would also estrange, then I would need only to think of equality, identity, difference, and instantly equality, identity, difference would stand palpably before my senses! In other words: our concepts would coincide with the actuality of which they are concepts. This is indeed, as we already learned, the opinion of all ideologues since Plato. A pity only that none of those who openly or covertly make the spirit the creator of the universe and openly or covertly seek to persuade us that actuality is a "representation" of the one apprehending it, has even attempted to reveal to us how we came to distinguish our representations, feelings, desires, decisions, and thoughts from the things to which they are directed, or put more briefly: to keep apart the judged world and the judgement about the world! If the world is my concept, then naturally certain concepts are also the world. We would no longer know that there is something fundamentally different from our concepts, and we would know still less that there are -- concepts! With the consciousness of actuality, the consciousness of consciousness would irrecoverably vanish: consciousness itself would have ceased to exist!

It cannot be proclaimed loudly enough and repeated too often: the deed of reflection places nothing over against us; it merely "arrests," separates from the totality of a flowing actuality as from the bond with the experiencing soul, bounds and divides and distinguishes the already existing over-against of the image! The name "object" [Gegenstand], with which in the seventeenth century "Objekt" was rendered anew -- after it had already once before been Germanised as "Gegenwurf" [counter-throw] (thereby emphasising the "against" [entgegen] even more sharply than the admittedly original "over-against" [Gegenueber]) -- contains only in its second component, the "standing" [Stehen], the achievement-result of the act; just as we quite precisely in terms of meaning are accustomed to designating the finding and confirmation of both external and internal facts, indeed of every state of affairs, as "ascertainment" [Feststellung]. Posited or, as it were, "thrown" by the spirit is the unextended point, which, relating the flowing actuality to itself as a time-withdrawn centre, halts it position by position, thereby converting images into propertied things, exchanging for the happening states, processes, and movements of those things, thingifying even the beings though their soul escapes it, and finally equally capturing and consequently solidifying all relations so that it may atomise them! Beheld, sensed, and felt. Were what is experienced not already an over-against of the experiencing soul, the spirit would have nothing to "direct itself towards," nothing at which to "aim" with its throws, nothing it could try to bring to a "stand." But insofar as its activity depends not so much on experiencing in general as rather on the polarity-relation of what is experienced thereto, it is precisely in the fact that it only fixates, thereby annulling the polar connection of soul and actuality, that its "capacity" -- unattainable to experiencing -- is grounded: to be able to place anything and everything at the object-position!

For insofar as the apprehension-capable I perceives a thing, it has also already referred to the outward-hurled point not only the image thereby become an object, but unfailingly also

itself, the positing and fixating power. In place of the sidedness soul-image, with non-interchangeable poles, there has stepped at the same instant the relation of the I to the thing [Sache], or, as one inaccurately says, of the subject to the object, whose members -- as the names already reveal -- both possess the character of objects. Once, then, the thing has been found in the over-against, the I related to the thing can also be found; and if this was found, the finding already contains the knowledge of the difference between what is experienced and experiencing. For in finding myself, I have ascertained that being whose actuality consists in its self-assertion against the befalling of experiencing, and consequently also my experiencing; whereby, incidentally, the question with which we opened the discussion would be settled. This state of affairs requires, however, a somewhat more precise exposition.

The three steps of consciousness. The spiritual act is nothing but a sundering deed; however, since it belongs to the sense and essence of the deed to be the deed of a doer and to be directed at an aim, the achievement of the act consists in the founding of two relations to the "posited" point: the relation, on the one hand, of the positing I to it, and on the other, of the sector of actuality that it was called upon to cut out of the universe.

Each of the two has the nature of a direction, in accordance with which the positing I in particular is originally directed towards the consciousness-foreign, or the thing [Sache]. Conditioned and occasioned by something experienced in the experience, apprehension leads primordially to object-reflection [Sachbesinnung]; hence the dogma haunting the history of thought since Augustine -- that of the "immediate self-certainty of consciousness" -- is thoroughly and fundamentally mistaken. That object-reflection, however, is the relatedness of the I to the thing and not perhaps the reverse, we attest with sundry turns of phrase, as when we express a thinking-of-something with "I refer" [ich beziehe mich] or "take reference to it" [nehme Bezug darauf]. In the achievement-result to be called "object-reflection," the direction of the relatedness is thus indicated by an arrow pointing from the self to the thing.

If now, accordingly, the thinking "subject" not merely simultaneously exists with the thought "object" but, as referred to the latter, is even simultaneously already "posited," then a second step is nevertheless required to "bring the self to consciousness," and this has, in relation to object-reflection, manifestly the character of retrospective reflection [Rueckbesinnung], and would thus be represented by the following schema.

This second step of consciousness follows, both in the history of the species and in individual history, upon object-reflection, but proves itself to be so thoroughly prepared by it that it necessarily occurs sooner or later, and constitutes, as was already set forth by way of a note, the distinguishing mark of (thinking) consciousness. By now showing more sharply why it is precisely self-reflection that deserves the significance of a distinguishing mark of consciousness from experiencing, we bring into clear view, so to speak, what was only sketched in outline in the foregoing section.

Taken literally, our second schema could create the impression that it is not so much the self that is the related but rather the thing that is the relating, which would of course be absurd. The relation is always founded by the self, once in the direction towards the thing, the other time in the direction towards this very self! In the second case too it is the thinking self that finds itself directed or related, but not as in the first case towards the external-worldly thing, but towards the inner-worldly I. We must therefore leave the arrow-direction as it originally was and set the self once more in place of the thing. Before we yet decide how a quite evident doubling of the self is to be understood, we bring to resolution why the distinguishing mark of consciousness from experiencing must be sought in it. Because experiencing does nothing but estrange, it can cohere in polar fashion always only with what is experienced, never with itself; because consciousness, on the other hand, through the sundering deed annuls the connection and in its place posits the mere relatedness of the I to the object, it can also place the I at the object-point. The possibility of making into the direction-determining what was just now direction-determined distinguishes consciousness from the invariably direction-determined experiencing! In the inevitability of placing-itself-over-against is attested the dependence, in the power of disposal over the direction (of attention) the independence of spirit from the polarising life-process.

Notwithstanding, the second schema retains its significance. If the judgement "I find myself," in that it contains the I in the form both of the grammatical subject and of the grammatical object, seems on the one hand to confirm the finding of the doubling of the I in the act of self-reflection, it is on the other hand suited to conveying to us the knowledge of the difference between the two I's. The presently finding I can, after all, impossibly be the same as the presently found I! The psychologists have therefore repeatedly expressed the view that in the case of self-finding the finding I refers to an already elapsed moment of its existence, that is, properly speaking, remembers itself; a state of affairs to which the following schema would correspond:

In addition, let it be noted: the object of thinking, be it sense-thing or be it mere concept, does not as an object of thought find itself spatially over against the spirit. For it is entirely removed from spatio-temporality through being thought! To be sure, the primordial achievement of the act, the founded relation, transfers into the noumenal what in the phenomenal was polar connection between actualities situated outside one another, and would have come about without this not at all. But once the relation to something experienced has been founded, everything relatable to the original thing can thereafter become, in the manner of a thing, a point of reference. Reflection upon the self accordingly takes place only by the detour through object-reflection (just as, incidentally, reflection upon the own-I takes place only by way of reflection upon the alien-I!). Only insofar as the I has already found the thing can it, with the second step of reflection, now also relate itself to the finding-capacity! The finding of the finder requires that a finding has already occurred, and this must have been the finding of the thing.

Here we interpolate: animals too have memory and "remember," if one will (though only involuntarily), but never themselves. The re-experiencing that belongs to them by virtue of the coherence of all experiences with one another makes the content of a present experiencing the distinctively modified content of an earlier experiencing, and by no means the earlier experiencing itself. Only when, through the deed of spirit, what is experienced has become something found and to-be-found, is the voluntary "calling-into-memory" possible, and with it the reflection upon the finding spirit.

Each of the three schemata offers, besides the two mutually related points, also the relating direction, and lets us immediately recognise that a third and final step of reflection can occur, which, once accomplished, presupposes the second just as the second presupposes the first, namely the reflection upon the being-related. Object-consciousness is followed by self-consciousness, and both finally by relation-consciousness. The object of each level of reflection can be decomposed into numerous partial objects, to which correspond just as many sub-kinds of reflection. Thus, for example, to mention only one thing, the relation of subject to object can be considered, but also that of concept to object, further that of thing to actuality of images, and of the concept of it to the actuality of experiencing.

The three steps of reflection set themselves apart with wonderful sharpness in the history of thought of the Greeks. The Ionian hylics, insofar as they were not still symbolists, philosophised on the basis of object-consciousness; the so-called Sophists and especially the highly significant Protagoras on the basis of self-consciousness; Plato on the basis of relation-consciousness. Unfortunately, however, even Greek thought thereby fell upon the fateful wrong path of confusing the respectively favoured object with actuality itself and of dissolving the world-image into concepts of being; from which there arose those three fundamental kinds of philosophical system-building that have repeated themselves incessantly ever since: from predominant object-consciousness, "materialism" (realism, atomism, empiricism, sensualism, etc.); from predominant I-consciousness, "spiritualism" (pneumatology, illusionism, subjectivism, solipsism, etc.); from predominant relation-consciousness, "idealism" (panlogism, criticism, etc.). The ens realissimum of the first genus is called "matter" or substance, of the second spirit, of the third "idea" (category, concept). All three are branches on the trunk of the selfsame fundamental error, in consequence of which the mere substratum was forgotten in favour of the essentia, the cogitare in favour of the vivere, the existere in favour of the fluctuare!

## IX. Die Grundlagen der Empfindungslehre. — IX. The Foundations of the Doctrine of Sensation.

Beholding and sensation. -- We have, even if only with fleeting outlines, elucidated the process of beholding in terms of the beholdable image and have at least suggestively already touched upon the distinguishing peculiarity of the process of sensation. We must now consider the latter more closely, and all the more so since science, having entirely overlooked beholding, has also misjudged sensation -- indeed, has not even ascertained genuine sensation to this day. We maintain the proposition that every sensory experience offers two mutually irreducible sides, the side of beholding and that of sensation, and that neither from beholding alone nor from sensation alone can the occurrence of the spiritual act, through which we arrive at the finding of the thing, ever be explained. -- In order to furnish the proof for this, let us first assume that only sensation exists, and single out a sensation that, while it already possesses the character of genuine sensation in a sense first to be developed here, is fortunately also acknowledged as a genuine sensation by academic science: we mean the sensation of touching contact.

No one will deny that the spatial and temporal presentness of what is touched belongs to the content of a given act of touching. Whatever we touch is for us here present, that is to say, as something touching our touching member; and it is now present, that is to say, at the (empirical) instant of touching. Suppose we could, without changing the place of our existence, touch everything and anything, not only the glass on the table but also the distant mountain ranges or the moon in the sky: then for us there would be no clear distinction of a here from a there; and suppose what is touched did not have the character of temporal presence, then no distinction between the now and the formerly. For this it can be said in brief: everything to-be-touched, whatever else it may be, has in any case the nature of a here and a now; and from this it follows that through mere touching alone we could gain no impression at all.

For if I press my hand against a tabletop, I feel a bodily resistance; if I lift my hand from the tabletop, I no longer feel the resistance in question. Insofar as I could merely touch, at the very same moment that resisting corporeality would have vanished for me! Were I now to place my hand on the table a second time, I would again feel a bodily resistance; but as once more restricted to the now, it would be in no way connected with the previous impression. But even this already claims too much. Time does not stand still for a single instant. There is no now that would persist; rather, scarcely is it there than it already fades into the past to make room for a new present. Because what is to-be-touched belongs entirely to the present, I would, even during the arbitrary span while my hand rests on the tabletop, touch other and ever other resistances, of which each, perishing in the instant, would yield to the next, which would know not the slightest thing of the one just elapsed! In the now and only now to-be-touched resistance there is, after all, never the resistance I touched a minute or a second ago also included! Indeed, the circumstance that the momentary sensation of resistance is infallibly altered in accordance with the preceding sensations of resistance compels us, as we already know, to distinguish every now-present instant of experiencing from every just-past one not only numerically but even in kind; from which it follows that we would not even be in a position to speak of the "impression of a bodily resistance"!

Having thus shown that the content of the touching-experience cannot convey a graspable impression insofar as it is bound to the now, we can moreover still show that the same holds because it is bound to the here. As no researcher disputes, every tactile sensation is "localised." If I call "here" the tactile impression at the index finger of the left hand, then in relation to it a "there" is the tactile impression at the index finger of the right hand. The entire surface of my body thus decomposes into an extraordinarily large number of (of course not mathematical but empirical) spatial positions, each of which, endowed with qualitative peculiarity (which is erroneously and with petitio principii called "local sign"), stands in relation to every other in the relation of a here-position to a there-position. If now the resistance-impression is an event occurring at the here, then it undoubtedly takes place at a particular position of the body. Let us again take the case that I lay my hand on a tabletop: then numerous and indeed qualitatively different pressures arise, each of which, insofar as I were to attend to it separately, would possess the character of the here; but what would by no means arise is that coherence of them all among one another in consequence of which the here-impression at the tip of the index finger would stand out against the here-impression at the tip of the little finger as against a there! What would be touched are qualitatively different resistances; what would not be touched is their being-outside-one-another. Since, however, every bodily resistance necessarily has some extension, it would be senseless to speak any longer of the "impression of a bodily resistance" if the content of the tactile impression lacks extensionality.

One hoped in vain to overcome this difficulty by calling upon so-called movement-sensations. Even if a Melchior Palagyi13) had not conclusively proved that the concept of movement-sensation is a non-viable bastard, it could be shown through a very simple consideration that the organic experiences one has in mind are quite incapable of producing the impression of being-outside-one-another. For even if we were to make the concession that the organic experiences arising during the bending, say, of the arm are those of a transition from state to state, the experience of extension would lie in this no more than, say, in the experience of the brightening of an illuminated surface!

We repeat: if it belongs to the content of the touching-experience to be a temporally as well as spatially determined position, then through mere touching we cannot even enter into connection with the corporeality of the world, let alone be enabled thereby to find things. In so doing, however, we have also already ascertained what it actually was through whose absence the resistance-impression was prevented from "appearing": what was missing was the connection between time-position and time-position, equally between space-position and space-position, and finally between time-position and space-position -- in a word: spatio-temporal being-outside-one-another. Time, however, is nothing other than the most general characteristic of happening, space nothing other than the most general characteristic of images, and the two complement each other in polar fashion. Temporal happening would not be experienceable without the happening-bearer of the spatial image, and the spatial image would not be experienceable except as a transitional moment of happening. As the foregoing considerations will have disclosed, presentness actualises itself only in relation to the temporal stream (and the latter only in relation to it)! With a geometrical metaphor, time would be the longitudinal section of actuality, space its cross-section; to which, however, we attach no weight at this point. Insofar, then, as the experiencing of space requires the experiencing of time and conversely, we are justified, indeed compelled, to speak of the experiencing of spatio-temporal continuity; and this constitutes the most general side of beholding. Only on the foundation of a beholding to whose "contents" spatio-temporal continuity belongs, among other things, can the process of sensation communicate bodily impressions to us.

To this two further remarks are necessary. When we call spatio-temporality the most general characteristic of images and the receptivity for it the most general characteristic of beholding, we thereby oppose not only the astonishing view that space and time reside in the human spirit in order to be pulled over the sense-data by it14), but equally the absurd opinion that one can compose spatiality as well as temporality from sense-data. The visual impressions that an arbitrary thing -- say, a glass -- conveys to us are incomparably different from the tactile impressions that the very same thing conveys to us. Insofar as extensionality is a property of the "seen-thing," it would have to be a different extensionality from that of the "touched-thing." We would have a visual space, auditory space, tactile space, indeed a smell-space and taste-space, and no god would bring them back together into the one and only space that we alone and exclusively know! The same would of course apply to the time belonging to each of these spaces, and it would for this reason alone be a matter of impossibility to refer the visual impressions of the glass to the same thing to which we refer the tactile impressions! The attempt to compose spatio-temporality from sense-data is exactly as sensible as if one thought to erect a house from different photographs of the same house! Only because it is the distinguishing peculiarity par excellence of the soul's power of beholding, and consequently of the beholding power of all the senses, to "mirror" spatio-temporal continuity, do we enter into connection with the image of the world through the mediation, among other things, of sensory impressions.

Furthermore: just as one thought to refute the argument from the spatial positionality of what is touched by calling upon so-called movement-sensations, so one might suppose the argument from the temporal position-

ality can be overcome by pointing to the "faculty of memory." Against this we wish at this point merely to remark, while forgoing a more precise justification, that memory as well as the faculty of recall presupposes, as immediately experienced in the same way, the time-image, just as the so-called movement-sensation presupposes, as immediately experienced, the space-image. Every attempt to derive the time-experience from the memory-experience rather than the reverse has proven to be circular.

Without our having yet elucidated what the life-function of sensation actually adds to that of beholding, we can nonetheless now at least prepare the answer to the question whether it is possible to arrive at thing-apprehension with the help of beholding alone. But a remark about the reciprocal relation of beholding and sensation must be prefaced. Of sensation we have ascertained, even if initially only by the example of touching, that in order to communicate impressions to us at all, it requires the image already mediated to us through beholding. If in this way sensation depends upon beholding, then conversely it by no means holds that beholding too depends upon sensation. There are no sensing beings that are not also beholding ones; but there could be beholding beings that did not sense in the slightest! For we ourselves are, for example, in deep sleep entirely sensation-dead and nonetheless capable of beholding the spatio-temporally extended world of dream-visions, which shares with the waking world the character of actuality.

Turning thereupon to the above question, we reflect that it belongs to the peculiarity of every conceivable thing to be at a place at each instant; in accordance with which the finding of the thing would in any case include a finding of the place. But in order to be able to find the place, we must be able to experience it, and we experience it, as was shown, actually for example with the help of tactile sensation, provided only that a spatio-temporal image is already given on which it can be placed. Such an image would be any arbitrary visual space with moving shapes within it. But if we now think of it as mirrored, we are confronted with the astonishing fact that we are no longer in a position either to locate it within the factual world-space or even to determine the locations of the things moving in it. If we nonetheless believe we see within it their distances from one another, this cannot in any case rest upon the beholding-power of seeing; for the latter conveys to us spatial images, but not factual distances. The landscape painting, for example, seems to possess a foreground, a middle ground, and a background that may lose itself in bluish distances, notwithstanding that the physical object forms a surface parallel to the wall. Were our seeing endowed only with beholding-power, and were we nothing but beings seeing in this manner, we would find ourselves in the placeless space of Faust's "Mothers" -- "around them no place, still less a time" -- which we truly enter in every nocturnal dream, where we exchange our location a hundred times in an instant without it requiring the traversal of intervening space. Should, then, beholding lack the capacity to give us the impression of place, then on the basis of this life-process alone the "localised" thing could never be found either.

To this prelude to the following considerations we immediately append their most important concluding result, which has indeed already been repeatedly applied in the course of our work but can only now be fully understood. All experiencing estranges; yet there are two kinds of estrangement: the beholding kind, which places us in connection with the image of the world, and the sensing kind, which connects us with the corporeality of the world. Since corporeality actualises itself only in the already actualised image, sensation can also be taken as the process of embodiment of images. From the opposition of the bodily life-process of sensation to the soul's life-process of beholding, the opposition of the Greek concept of σώμα to the concept of ψυχή is both justified and precisely definable. Inasmuch as we further see the achievement of the νοῦς, or spirit -- namely the deed of judgement -- bound to the sensing state of wakefulness, we shall feel compelled to see in sensation the immediate precondition for the accomplishment of the spiritual act; from which there arises for us a task entirely unknown to all previous psychology and epistemology: the task, namely, of demonstrating in sensation, erroneously held to be a "fact of consciousness," rather the vital occasion for the emergence of consciousness, or in other words, of demonstrating in the merely experienceable corporeality of the world the enabling ground of its merely thinkable objecthood.

Everything fundamentally important for this has been adduced partly in the preceding chapters, partly and above all in the present section of the current chapter. On the other hand, a book of at least the size of this book would be required to apply these new findings to the entire field of the doctrine of sensation. Placed before an all-or-nothing, we choose a middle way by attempting to indicate the upheaval that the foundations of all sensation-research would thereby undergo, and we lay the emphasis on the precondition, hidden in the genuine concept of sensation, for the solution of the to this day unsolved problem of the intensifiability (comparability) of all adjectives of property; doing so, however, in an abbreviated mode of presentation that strings together the decisive propositions thesis-like with the barest minimum of intellectual mediation.

Quality and intensity. We spare ourselves the enumeration of the current textbook definitions of sensation; suffice it that in the sensation-experience one quite generally distinguishes quality (kind) and intensity (strength). One example instead of many: the heard tone e differs from the heard tone d qualitatively (in kind); the loud e from the soft e by its intensity (in strength); and so everywhere. It is supposed, then, that what has been sensed is: firstly a quality and secondly the intensity of this very quality! The complete untenability of this view has been noted and proved by keener minds; its difficulty did not escape even the laggards; but neither the former nor the latter have found the solution. We preface a few words about the untenability in question and for a short stretch follow a line of reasoning that, through Bergson's essay on the immediate facts of consciousness, has been brought to the forefront of scientific interest -- one that combats the belief in the graduability of kinds from the standpoint of the inapplicability of the concept of quantity to sensation-contents. We shall see later that even this line of reasoning misses the core of the matter.

We compare intensities by degree and thus, as it initially appears, quantitatively. Louder is more loud, brighter more bright, hotter more hot. Indeed, one estimates: that white surface is perhaps three times as bright as this one; and indeed one subsequently measures the degree of brightness by "candle-power," hence certainly numerically. The smaller number is contained in the larger, the unit in every number. But now, when I hear a very loud tone, does my hearing of the loudness consist in my having mentally multiplied the softness? Is the experience of loudness an experience of added-up softnesses? Do I not rather have an equally unitary, undivided, and indivisible impression, whether I perceive a loud tone or a soft one?! To be sure, if, comparing brighter and darker with each other, I look to the occasions of the brightness-differences, I rightly arrive at the concept of a doubly or triply large light-source; but in the brightness that is simply perceived there is not the slightest trace of multiplied brightness-units. So would bright differ from less-bright, loud from soft, also qualitatively? Not a few psychologists answer: "Yes, intensity is, psychologically considered, only a particular quality." Yet wherein would this particularity consist? No one has been able to say! For there cannot be the slightest doubt that I distinguish e from d fundamentally differently from the way I distinguish loud e from soft e. If I charge a street-urchin who has never heard anything of acoustics to tell me when he hears a new tone and when the same one again, and if I now strike a tuning-fork c and again c and once more c, only each time somewhat louder, he says without hesitation that it is always the same tone, though differently loud; but if I suddenly strike f at the same volume, he says now it is a different tone! The "other" tone is quite certainly -- and no one has ever doubted it -- a different tone-quality. But suppose I were also to call the mere increase in volume a change of kind: I would then contradict the fact that it is, after all, judged by the hearer precisely as a modification of the selfsame kind.

The dilemma is therefore this: if I speak of intensity-differences of the selfsame kind, I appear to have asserted a multipliability of kinds, which would be an absurdity; if, on the other hand, I call the degree-difference a difference of kinds, I have annihilated the distinguishing mark of the concept of kind! Kinds cannot be added together, and intensities cannot be made into kinds.

Sensation as contact-sensation. We draw from this at once the necessarily required conclusion: kinds have no intensities at all, and degrees of intensity are entirely without kind. But then two entirely different life-processes must cooperate in order for the impression to offer us both a qualitative and a gradual side. If we call one of the two life-processes "sensation," then we may not call the other "sensation." But we must call "sensation" the one that helps us apprehend the degrees of intensity. Here some of the most important reasons:

a) All visual experiences differ in kind from all auditory experiences, from all olfactory experiences, from all gustatory experiences, from all tactile experiences. We cannot hear colours, see tones, touch fragrances (the so-called disparateness of the senses). Without exception, however, every sense gives us, within its zone, differences of intensity: bright and brighter, loud and louder, sweet and sweeter, warm and warmer, cold and colder, rough and rougher, smooth and smoother, blunt and blunter, sharp and sharper, and so forth. Since the concept of sensation aims to characterise the distinguishing peculiarity of sensory experiencing, we must draw upon the capacity common to all senses without distinction for its formation, that is, the capacity for the experiencing of degrees of intensity.

b) We already know why it would be senseless to make space the content of sensation. For as surely as the visual content red is something incomparably different from the tactile content cold, just as surely would visual space and tactile space then fall apart; the inevitable consequence of which would be that no concept of space could come about at all. To this let it further be added: the "riddle" of space lies not in breadth-extension and not in height-extension, but entirely and solely in depth-extension. For a being situated in a mathematical plane, breadth- and height-extension would merge into a point. Both can first step apart only for the beholder before the plane, hence with the help of depth-extension. To sense space would accordingly mean in truth: to sense the depth of space. Suppose now that touching contact is in any case a sensation and what is touched is in any case something here-present: then the content of at least this sensation could not include spatial depth and accordingly not space. But what a particular process of sensation lacks is no longer suited to characterise sensation in general.

The fact of the insensibility of space, however, constitutes an essential support for our proposition that the experienceable degree of intensity founds the distinguishing peculiarity of sensation. For if what is sensed is the strength of the impression, we recognise without further ado why space cannot possibly be sensed! There are -- as all advocates of the insensibility of space also tend to emphasise -- to be sure larger and smaller, but quite certainly not more or less intense spaces. It occurs to no one to call distance (= great spatial depth) a more intense nearness (= small spatial depth)!

c) Yet one more characteristic all senses have in common, and this is the open-sesame to the essence of degree-differences: namely, the capacity to be painfully excited. And indeed every sensory experience without exception becomes a pain-experience as soon as a certain degree of intensity of excitation is exceeded. Every intensification of intensity culminates at last in the pain-experience! The capacity for pain, common to all senses yet again, thus proves to be a mere intensified form of the experience of intensity.

Having agreed to call the experience of intensity sensation, we must call the experience of kind beholding. In every sensory experience two functions cooperate: the soul's function of beholding images and the bodily function of sensing degrees of intensity.

It is a general principle of research: if a graduable state of affairs is difficult to see through in its middle degrees, one chooses its very highest degrees in order to see through it. For the investigation of envy, a case of intense envy recommends itself; of jealousy, a case of intense jealousy; of intensity, accordingly, a case of the strongest intensity, hence pain. Pain, now, is always a painful contact-experience!! Proof: If I hear a gentle melody, I have in the ear not the slightest contact-experience; if I see a landscape painting, in the eye not the slightest contact-experience. The small child must first slowly learn that it needs its eyes for seeing, its ears for hearing, for in seeing or hearing of middle degrees of intensity there is only what is seen and heard, but absolutely no sensation of the mediating organ. If, however, a cannon is fired immediately beside me, the report seems to tear my hearing organ apart; as it can indeed actually burst the eardrum. I now have not merely the perception of a sound, but in addition a painful contact-sensation in the ear. And if on a hot summer noon I gaze unprotected into the sun, I not only see something very bright, but in addition have a stinging contact-sensation in the eye. Likewise, sharp smells can literally scratch and bite, intense tastes (such as alum) painfully constrict, to say nothing of the particular properties of painful contact-sensations of the actual tactile organs and of the inner-bodily ("visceral") sensations. If, however, all highest degrees of intensity are undoubtedly contact-experiences, then the intensity-experience in general is a contact-experience, even though its weaker degrees may not be noticeable by us as contacts, and we have accordingly to establish: sensation as such is contact-sensation and nothing more.

Contact-experiences presuppose two things: firstly a tactile organ, secondly the self-movability of this tactile organ. The older doctrine of sensation had recognised that the sense of touch is the mother-sense of sensation, all other senses merely branch-senses; the newer doctrine has slain this truth, much to the detriment of science, with the fundamentally erroneous proposition of "specific sense-energies" (Johannes Mueller!). We must return to the older doctrine but add to it a new finding that was from the very beginning left unnoticed. The "primal phenomenon" of contact is the pressure that either my tactile organ exerts against a resistance, or that a resistance exerts against my tactile organ. That my tactile organ must possess self-movement in order to exert a pressure everyone sees; but it must also have self-movement in order to receive a pressure. For the received pressure is nothing other and can be nothing other than sensed counter-pressure. We thus arrive at the conclusion: every sensation is contact-sensation; contact is touching; touching, however, occurs through self-movability of the tactile organ. There are no "specific sense-energies." All senses are sensation-organs in exactly the measure in which they are tactile organs; in other respects, however, they are organs of beholding, which, as the dream during the sleep of the senses proves, is a function of the soul.

Condillac had, for the foundation of "sensualism," famously devised that well-known statue into which one need only insert living sense-organs piece by piece to make it into a perception-capable being. Now we know: equipped with all the senses, said statue would nonetheless not have the slightest impressional experience and would accordingly not be capable of a single perception, so long as it lacked self-movability15). We can therefore assert, for example, with full determinacy that every self-immobile being receives not the slightest impression either. Plants are entirely sensation-dead, although they doubtless enjoy a special kind of beholding experience. When we break a flower, it causes it not the slightest pain; indeed, placed in water, it blooms on just as cheerfully as if it still sat on the stem. Self-movability, finally, is the expression of drivenness [Triebhaftigkeit]. Driven beings are capable of sensation.

For this we can now also say: if beholding consists in the estrangement of the image, then sensation consists in the localisation of the beheld image; for the contact-experience, as was set forth at length, constitutes the starting-point of the concept of the here (and therewith of the concept of the now). What is the here? Originally, that which I can touch. What is the there? Originally, that which I cannot touch. Without the contact-experience there would be no distinction of the here from the there (and therewith also not of the now from the once). The beholder "gazes" into the farthest distance, because as a beholder he is

everywhere; the one who senses is touched only by what is present, because as one who senses he is evermore here: but only from the here-ness of what is sensed does the far-quality of what is beheld gain the character of an unlimitedly further-removable there. The sense-organs differ not "specifically" but according to the degree of the predominance of the sensation-function over the function of beholding. The former predominates considerably in the near-senses of touching, temperature-experiencing, and tasting; it already recedes considerably in the middle-sense of smelling and is vastly outweighed by beholding in the far-senses of hearing and seeing. The tactile organs are in the highest degree sensation-organs; the eye is in the highest degree an organ of beholding.

Peculiarity of bodily estrangement. --- We come to our most important proposition. In the sensed here, what separate in polar fashion are: not the mirroring soul and the mirrored image, but a touched body from a touching body. Both, then, what is touched and what touches, fall under the one genus of corporeality! Here-finding is therefore one and the same as body-finding, and body-finding is inescapably the finding of two bodies, namely the touched and the touching! In the here-experience there lies not merely a polarity but at the same time a duality, and this now enables the entry of the sundering deed of spirit. Thing-finding [Sachfindung] means originally thing-finding [Dingfindung]. Thing-finding originally means finding of the corporeality of the thing, and the finding of the corporeality of the thing can only occur because the content of the experienced corporeality is a corporeal twoness.

We do not omit to point out the surprising confirmation that our derivation receives from the name "empfinden" [to sense]. As the philologists instruct us, the word consists of the prefix "em" and the verb "finden" [to find]; the prefix "em" arose from "ent," this from "ant" -- still preserved in "Antlitz" [countenance], "Antwort" [answer] -- which, cognate with the Greek ἀντί, originally means the spatial "against" [Gegen]! Into the primal meaning of the word was thus first of all taken up the finding mediatable exclusively through sensation; and then also the circumstance that the sensed "content" stands in the relation of something resisting to the corporeality of the one sensing. Notwithstanding that the word "fuehlen" [to feel] also originally means "to palpate" and in the usage of today's everyday language alternates almost freely with "empfinden" (one "palpates" [befuehlt] an object, "feels" [fuehlt] warmth, cold, etc., but also sorrow, joy, etc., and likewise one "senses" [empfindet] brightness, but also again grief, pleasure, etc.), the psychologists were nevertheless right to restrict "sensation" [Empfindung] to the bodily life-processes and "feeling" [Gefuehl] to the soul's life-processes; as is moreover justified by the compound "sensory sensation" [Sinnesempfindung], which has become common usage, whose counterpart "sensory feeling" [Sinnesgefuehl] was rejected by the language. The deed of apprehension thus sunders something already halved by sensation, and the spirit finds itself connected immediately not with the soul but with the body of the soul! (Only thus does it become understandable, incidentally, that it directs itself against the soul and can be richly developed even in the case of comparatively great soul-poverty of the bearer-person.) From the proof thereof:

a) What is a body? It has been said: something "in" space. Palagyi has compellingly demonstrated in his "Logik am Scheidewege" [Logic at the Crossroads] why this is incorrect. For one cannot pour the body out of space as one pours peas out of the sack in which they are "inside." It would be equally incorrect to say: space is "in" the body, or the latter "encloses" a piece of space; for space too cannot be poured out of the body. The correct answer runs: the body is the resistance opposing the penetration of space. Let us consider, however, what it means when we accordingly attribute the characteristic of impenetrability to the body! Space is something absolutely penetrable, and the absolutely penetrable in turn penetrates everything, hence even the body held to be impenetrable. The latter consequently has its impenetrability only in relation to a second state of affairs to which impenetrability likewise pertains. The resisting resists only the resisting, or: corporeality actualises itself only in the contact of two bodies.

b) In space, every conceivable position is connected with every other position through a continuous medium. By virtue of the boundless penetrative power of space there is absolutely nothing that, with respect to space alone, could separate one spatial position from another. If, by contrast, the corporeality of space consists in the possible opposition of two bodies, then it is necessarily the actualisation of positions; or: corporeality means locality, and resisting space is localised space! In the actuality not of space but of the corporeality of space, a real here separates itself from a real there. Contact-experience, then, = experience of resistance = experience of

place = experience of corporeality = experience of the impenetrability of two bodies.

We are now in a position to show the primal source of the intensity-experience in the continuous intensifiability of pressure. Intensity-differences are experienced pressure-differences. From the proof thereof: all highest intensities are, in absolute contrast to all qualities, sensed not merely at the experienced object but also at the experiencing organ, and the increase of "objective" intensity goes hand in hand with an (only not objectively equal) increase of the "subjective." The loudness of a pistol-shot fired at the closest range unfailingly produces in the ear a painful contact-sensation; the still greater loudness of a cannon-shot fired at the same range produces in the ear a far more painful contact-sensation. The increase of objective intensity thus corresponds to an increase of subjective being-touched, and only from the magnitude of the latter do we originally gain the measure for the magnitude of the former.

Sense of intensifiability. -- Now, however, it is time to return to the starting-point. One could, with full justification, argue: even if it may be true that in the immediate perception of a loud sound there is by no means a multiplied softness, it is equally true that in the immediate perception of the pressure exerted by a kilogram weight on the hand there is nothing of the thousandfold multiplication of the pressure of a gram weight either! That intensity-differences are something other than qualitative differences one has seen; by no means, however, has one been enlightened as to how the intensity-difference can be interpreted as a quantity-difference.

To master this difficulty we must first oppose the view tentatively introduced above according to which degree-differences belong to quantity-differences. And it does not suffice, in the face of the view (represented, for example, by Bergson) that the relation of more to less is always a relation of the containing to the contained, merely to point to yet another more and less; rather, we shall even have to show that the containing gains its meaning of more in relation to the contained only derivatively. One discusses this, however, most expediently in stages, and we therefore first set about making two kinds of more and less recognisable.

If I say: 20 is more than 15, then 15 is indeed contained in 20. In every number, with the exception of unity itself, unity is contained so-and-so many times. Counting, as we know, is the achievement already potentially included in the positing of the thing and exclusively of the deed of spirit, and consequently the concept of quantity is also a product solely of spirit. But there is also an experienced more whose

origin lies entirely elsewhere.

We recall that resistances can only be experienced through the counter-pressure of the touched organ, and take the case that the touching hand does not receive the pressure but effects it, say, through a push against a heavy but movable ball: then the ball yields, and the self-movable organ thereby overcomes the resistance. If conversely the ball rolls up with great force so that we try in vain to stop it, the self-movable tactile organ yields and is thereby overcome. If, however, self-movability, as we further recall, constitutes the expression of drivenness, then the displacing of the pressure must appear in the light of a prevailing, the having-to-yield-before-it in the light of a succumbing. In other words: in the former there is co-experienced a more, in the latter a less of power, and accordingly the conflict of pressure and counter-pressure rooted in the sensation-experience founds the concept of force and with it the concept of surpassing. The more beautiful is not multiplied ugliness; rather, it surpasses ugliness; acuteness [Scharfsinn] is not multiplied stupidity; rather, it surpasses stupidity; courage is not multiplied timidity; rather, it surpasses timidity; and conversely, the more wicked surpasses a wickedness, the rarer a rareness, the more ghastly a ghastliness, and so forth. The experienced archetype of all possible intensifications lies in the experience of the degree of intensity, and this in the experienced overcomability of a pressure by our self-movable corporeality. Intensities are in and of themselves no more quantifiable than kinds, but they are intensifiable, and the more in question is a more of driving force. It thus remains true: kinds cannot be graduated; but we must now add: the undoubtedly experienceable intensifiability of intensity is not the same thing as quantifiability.

Having advanced this far, we find ourselves, however, confronted with the following still more captious question: if the more of intensification is something other than the more of increase in quantity, how do we then come to grasp both with the same concept and to posit, in both counting and graduating, the relation of a more to a less, indeed to express the degree-differences even through numbers?! Ten is more than five; but brighter too is more bright than bright. There the less is contained in the more; here, however, it is supposed to be merely surpassed by the more. How can we apply the same concept to the relation of the surpassing to the surpassed as to the relation of the containing to the contained?!

We place three series side by side: a qualitative series, the intensity-series, and the number-series. A qualitative series would be, for example, the colour-series of the solar spectrum; another would be the scale; we choose the latter. One thing we see at once: when we successively strike c, d, e, f, g, a, b, there lies in none of the steps by which we move from one tonal stage to the next either an intensification or an increase in quantity; rather: all tones are qualitatively different from one another, but the qualitative differences between any two neighbouring tones are somehow similar to one another. Regardless of whether we take the above scale as a basis, or, say, an Asian one with far smaller intervals, or indeed the series of just-noticeable pitch-steps, the differences will always be comparable only from the standpoint of the purely qualitative phenomenon of a peculiarly constituted similarity. If we apply to this the concept of intensifiability -- similar, more similar, most similar -- it is especially to be noted that the similarity between any two neighbouring stages is by no means the greatest! A sung c is usually confused by no one with the d of a piano, but quite frequently with its much "more distant" e of the octave! The similarity between fundamental tone and octave is thus greater than that between fundamental tone and neighbouring tone. The progression in the tonic takes place by means of peculiarly constituted similarities, but even the smallest possible step does not present itself as the greatest similarity. That we arrange the qualitative differences of tones nonetheless in a (one-dimensional) step-sequence or "scale" can, as was already noted, be explained entirely only from the relation of the tone-characters, into which we cannot enter more closely at this point. We turn to the number-series: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7... and set forth point by point the differences between it and the qualitative series.

Point 1: Of qualitative series there are many; the number-series is only a single one.

Point 2: In order to get from any number to the next larger, we take each time the same and not merely a similar step. All numbers are, to be sure, absolutely different from one another; but the step from one to the next is always the same, because always the conceivably smallest.

Point 3: Every next-larger number absorbs each time all its predecessors. This completes the difference from the qualitative series to the point of incommensurability. D does not absorb c, e does not absorb d; but 5 has swallowed 4, 6 has swallowed 5, 7 has swallowed 6! As soon as we have taken the step to the next stage, all preceding stages are extinguished. This being-swallowed of the smaller by the larger number is indeed a being-contained of the former in the latter, and therefore the larger also gives back every smaller one. From 7 I can subtract 6, but not 8. (The algebraic trick of 0 -- 1 we must pass over here, since its derivation would require the analysis of the concept of zero, which would lie entirely outside our discussion.) However, as surely as being-swallowed and being-contained are only two different sides of the selfsame thing, just as surely it is open to us to focus now on the one, now on the other side separately. When someone eats an apple, immediately thereafter the matter of the apple is "contained" in his body; nonetheless, the apple has vanished. Likewise 6 vanishes in 7, and it vanishes no less because it lies, so to speak, forever indigestibly in its stomach and can at any time be retrieved from it again. We come to the intensity-series.

The appearance that there are several intensity-series, such as sound-intensities, light-intensities, warmth-intensities, has proved deceptive to us: they are all the one and the same series of pressure-differences, but on the occasion of the corporeality of different images. As the number-series is only one, so too the series of degrees! (Marginal note: were it otherwise, we could not possess the concept of degree at all!) Number-series and degree-series thus agree in Point 1.

Let us now imagine any touchable degree of pressure: we reach the next degree of pressure by means of the just-noticeable pressure-difference, and this is each time the experienceably smallest! The experienceable pressure-differences between any two successive pressure-degrees are thus equal to one another16). (Mark well, not the corresponding magnitudes of the factual pressure-increments, whose relation to the just-noticeable pressure-increment is instead regulated, as is well known, by the so-called Weber-Fechner threshold law.) We thus find, between any two transitions from the degree-experience of

an arbitrary pressure to the degree-experience of the next-greater pressure, not the slightest differences. The deep-seated difference from the qualitative series is evident. On the other hand, we have ascertained an experiential series that is exactly analogous to the number-series also with respect to Point 2. The following formulation brings the analogy out most sharply: we traverse the number-series by means of objectively equal steps, the degree-series by means of subjectively equal ones.

But how do we come to call the higher degree a more and to model upon the degree-series of what is experienced a numerical degree-series of the objective (weights, temperatures, candle-powers, etc.), when after all the experience of an arbitrary degree of intensity does not contain within itself the lesser degree of intensity?

I press the index finger of my right hand against the index finger of my left hand in such a way that the two hold each other in equilibrium. If I now increase the pressure of my right index finger by a just-noticeable amount, or let up with the left index finger by a just-noticeable amount, the left index finger immediately yields before the right. In general: every just-noticeably stronger pressure overcomes the just-noticeably weaker pressure. And because overcoming a pressure infallibly coincides with the yielding of the resistance, we can also say: every just-noticeably stronger pressure annihilates the just-noticeably weaker pressure. There we have an analogy to Point 3 of the number-series as well. Every number is annihilated by the one larger by one counting-step, every pressure by the just-noticeably stronger one: the former through being-swallowed, the latter through having-to-yield. But with this we have arrived at the primal sense of more and less!

The difference of more and less means originally precisely not the relation of the containing to the contained (unless one already takes being-contained in the sense of being-swallowed), but rather that of the surpassing to the surpassed, and this holds also for the number-series. The number, insofar as it is abstractly grasped, is indeed infallibly a magnitude containing the smaller number; but the number, insofar as it is moreover also experienced, is rather a magnitude surpassing the smaller number, as could be demonstrated a thousandfold! For the all-too-human "will to power" we might without hesitation substitute the "will to the larger number." (Indeed, some mystic accordingly defines the "almighty God" not inaptly as the "greatest number!" ) The child learning geography takes special delight in Mont Blanc simply because it surpasses the other Alpine mountains in the number of metres of

height. Mont Blanc is mightier than the Matterhorn, Kilimanjaro mightier than Mont Blanc, and Gaurisankar surpasses them all! The record-mania of all athletes outdoes with numbers and nothing but numbers. Here a true anecdote: Several boasting Yankees in a railway carriage. Each enumerates the virtues of his home town; these, however, are now the most ships, now the tallest skyscraper, now the largest population, now the most tonnage-rich coal deposits, now the most numerous billionaires, etc. Only the last has no numerical record to oppose and must therefore endure the mockery of the others. But then it occurs to him just in time: it is a statistical fact that in his city the greatest number of annual suicides per thousand inhabitants obtains! His record-honour is saved, the mockery falls silent! Sapienti sat!

Perhaps it will be objected: one can indeed understand that the pressure-overcoming pressure appears to the bearer of the pressure-exertion as a preponderance of power, but this does not yet found the concept of a continuously growing series of pressure-overcoming powers; to which, however, it may be replied that it belongs to the character of every arbitrary degree of resistance to be in turn overcome by a greater pressure, and that each time a just-noticeably greater pressure already suffices. Resistance A is displaced by the just-noticeably greater pressure B, resistance B by the equally differentially greater pressure C, this by the equally differentially greater D, and so forth, from which an unmistakable series of mutually overcoming forces results. If we additionally take into account the experience of the growing exertion that we must oppose to the increasing pressure in order not to yield to it, then the concept of degrees of intensity is the, as it were, inescapable consequence thereof. But it would of course be quite wrong to conclude from this that the concept of number was formed on the basis of already existing concepts of degree. The original object of counting is of course not degree-magnitudes, nor indeed volume-magnitudes, but things. Both in the history of the species and of the individual, what is first counted is: one finger and one more finger and one more finger. The more of the degrees of intensity was long since experienced before it could be counted; but the larger number too was originally experienced in relation to the smaller as "more" for the same reason as the stronger intensity in relation to the weaker -- namely, on account of its capacity to surpass the smaller!

We summarise: the experienceable archetype of all degree-differences

are intensity-differences; intensity-differences are pressure-differences; pressure-differences form a single one-dimensional series; any two neighbouring stages of this series differ each time by an experienceably smallest amount; the just-noticeably stronger pressure overcomes, by making it yield, the just-noticeably weaker pressure; it thus takes from it its self-assertion, its "existence," its being; therein is attested a more of power; and this is the originally experienced sense of the relation of more to less; and because as spiritual beings we can count, the exactly analogously constituted number-series helps us to thingify the degree-series: the stronger displacing-force "objectifies" itself in the larger number17). Q. e. d.

## X. Nachwort über Wissenschaft und Metaphysik. — X. Postscript on Science and Metaphysics.

On the essence of science. Science is the doctrine of facts, and the doctrine of facts is the doctrine of causes. The doctrine of causes has, in the course of "world-history," become so autonomous that we must today regard it as the consequent phenomenon of a particular drive, namely the factual drive for knowledge [Erkenntnistrieb]. If we examine this drive with the eye of the metaphysician, whose essence we shall discuss below, it appears as consummate absurdity, for:

a) The pattern of the fact in general is the thing, but the thing is thought up. There is only a single fact that at the same time has actuality: the I; all other facts owe their existence to a kind of shadow-boxing of the I!

b) If, however, the primal object of factual thinking, namely the thing, is not an actual but a merely thought object, then no research into objects can lead to genuine cognition either. It produces only "knowledge" [Kenntnisse], which we shall presently specify more closely, not cognition [Erkenntnisse]. This can also be shown to someone who has not yet heard anything of all the foregoing.

I look out of the window and see that it is wet outside, with which I could content myself. I ask, however, for the cause of the wetness. Answer: it has rained. Question: cause of the rain? Answer: a cloud. Question: cause of the cloud? Answer: risen water vapour that has condensed again into droplets above. Immediately numerous series of questions open up: why does the water rise; why does it condense; what are the properties of water; what those of heat; where does heat come from; what are the properties of the atmosphere; what is the source of the sun's heat, and so forth! I shall end up at last with the cosmic fire-nebula from which our planetary system is supposed to have formed. But what was its cause; what the cause of its rotations, concentrations, and so on? However far we trace back the chain of causes, we nowhere find the primal cause [Ur-Sache]. Every research into causes leads, as has rightly been said, to a regressus in infinitum. Consequently, so it has further been held, the goal of science lies in the "infinite," and ingenious cleverdicks have added: cognition approaches that goal "asymptotically" without, however, ever being able to reach it! Gently! If it is irrevocably established that an ultimate cause is absolutely unattainable, then there is no goal of science at all. For a goal would be an end. If I find myself demonstrably on a path without end, how can I then claim that by walking I am nonetheless approaching the goal?! Rather: whether I take only one step or millions, I am no nearer the goal there than here! It is therefore absurd to suppose that one cognises something when one establishes causes. (There are, incidentally, even indications that the research into causes may finally reach an end of its capacity to ascertain the next following cause in the sense of regression, whether into the temporally more remote or into the spatially smaller; and there are all the more indications that the pleasure in researching causes may one day exhaust itself -- when, namely, its purpose, presently to be disclosed, shall have been nearly completely achieved. A later generation will therefore see the "scientific" phase of the history of the spirit behind it just as we today see behind us the far longer phase of symbolic thinking; regardless of whether it then turns back to metaphysics or is already approaching the extinction of consciousness, which, as it began to exist at some point, will also at some point cease to exist again.)

Yet even this absurdity has a sense, which we can, to be sure, comprehend only if we ask how the scientific research-interest could arise. For this we need only consider what it actually achieves. Cognition it does not achieve; but it enables the human being to take possession of the entire earthly world. The doctrine of causes is that particular kind of rearrangement of actuality that the spirit within us requires in order to master actuality through action. For the cause relates to the effect exactly as the means relates to the end. By "knowledge" [Kenntnis] we understand the result of reinterpretation that is (compulsorily) carried out upon experienced actuality out of the self-expansion drive of the will. One merit at least belongs to Schopenhauer's otherwise totally misguided philosophy, namely that of having emphasised with great decisiveness that our so-called cognising is merely a μηχανή of the will. What remained hidden from Schopenhauer, however, is this: that there is another aim of

research to which genuine cognitive significance attaches. With this we come to metaphysics18).

On the essence of metaphysics. -- Metaphysics is the struggle, sprung from allegiance to life, of reason against reason (according to the principle: similia similibus rotantur). Metaphysics accepts every fact (= cause), be it a scientific or an extra-scientific fact. But it makes a selection among facts, and it is guided therein by the endeavour: not to ascertain further facts (= causes), but to de-objectify the world and to put essence in place of fact. Facts are for it merely signs ("signatures"), now more important, now less so, for the essence revealing itself in the world-appearance. If science was research into causes [Ursachenforschung], then metaphysics is research into essence [Wesensforschung]. Essence = meaning = life = soul. Hence metaphysics = research into life = research into the soul. Here, however, the talk is of the soul of actuality in general, hence of the universe. Metaphysics, therefore, = cosmic physiognomics = the doctrine of signs of the universe.

It moves, together with science, upon the ground of thinking, yet not for the purpose of solving so-called riddles but in order to tie all that is factual, insofar as it may claim sign-value, intellectually to the world-mystery. It is entirely finished with each particular task as soon as it has succeeded in uncovering the relation between the fact in need of questioning and the mystery.

Mysteries, as one notes, neither wish nor can they be decoded. A unveiled mystery would after all no longer be a mystery. Unveilability belongs to the essence of what metaphysics offers in place of a "solution." For it, to modify a verse of Goethe, the following would hold:

All that is known is but a simile;

The world-mystery, here it becomes event.

And precisely this becoming-event of the primordialy secret is what it calls "cognition." One may not ask it about causes; but one may ask it about the essence of light, of science, of the copula "and"; and it will have to prove that everything supposedly known about them is misleading: and it will have to point to the one and the same primordial mystery, as it is present more purely or more dimly in light, in science, in the copula "and."

One side of the cosmic doctrine of life is constituted by the microcosmic doctrine of life, or the doctrine of the essence of organisms (approximately = meta-biology); one side of the microcosmic doctrine of life by the doctrine of the soul in the narrower sense, or the doctrine of the characters of independent bearers of life; finally, one side of the doctrine of characters by the doctrine of the human personality, that is, what today's psychology intends to be but is not. Only on the foundation of a genuine science of the soul, finally, does there exist a theory of consciousness (= epistemology), of which the present treatise offers a principal piece.

The "proving" of metaphysics therefore presents itself chiefly as refutation of science; its explanatory concepts, however, have, like the concepts of redness, sound, and warmth, a merely pointing significance and will be able to explain something only to the one who, guided by them, finds his way back to the sources of the fulfilling experiencing. Metaphysical research thus does not dispense with logic but employs it, like science, yet in the service of an anti-logical task. That is why the most significant metaphysicians have invariably been the sharpest logicians.

On symbolic thinking. Finally, one might ask whether there is not a fundamentally different kind of cognising that with its concepts manages, so to speak, to capture something of the life meant. It did indeed exist in the symbolic thinking of early times. What we have offered here is an introduction to the doctrine of life with the means of metaphysics; the doctrine of life itself could only be offered in a science of symbols; for which purpose, naturally, it would first have to be explained what a symbol is in relation to a concept. We believe we know this and hope soon to be able to present something on the matter. Experience alone, however deep it might be, does not yet enable anyone to refute science, though it may personally move him to turn away from science. Rather, only that experience is capable of it of which a symbolic knowledge was gained. And so let it be permitted, in closing, to remark that all the pointings-out and refutations given in the foregoing are merely the intellectual consequence of the truth-content of a few symbols whose meaning it was granted to us to disclose.

Bergson, who seems in many ways to stand at the opposite pole from Descartes, in truth merely reverses his proposition: the extended, for him, is the genuinely actual, the spiritual the deviation -- a residue of vitalistic thinking. Since now the degree of intensity is equally unextended, it must be resolved into the qualitative, i.e. be denied as an independent phenomenon.

This philosophy is not so much hostile to images as rather in particular an enemy of sensation, i.e. of the bodily side of life, and therein lies the secret of its popularity among intellectualists who would like to dissolve the body into the mind [Geist].

To be sure, in order to find one's way back to the experience of space, one must take the depth-extension as one's starting-point, and for this the contact-experience of the bodily nature is the guide. Were Bergson to pursue this trail, he would be compelled to revise his concept of duration [duree]; for the experience of time, as we showed, is indissolubly interwoven with the experience of space.
