## Creative Freedom — La libertad creadora

*La Libertad Creadora*

Alejandro Korn — 1922

Translated by Emiliano Salomón

Draft for UC San Diego Filomex<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup> I am happily indebted to Moraima Arias Bejarano, Max Akerlundh, Andrés Cáliz, Daniel Guevara, Daniela Kuthy-Cervantes, Patricia Marechal, Karina Ortiz Villa, Clinton Tolley, and Manuel Vargas for their invaluable feedback and suggestions.

### §1

I do not write for those who still suffer from naïve realism.<sup>2</sup> It is difficult to emancipate oneself from this congenital error. It is as ingrained as the geocentric error and the anthropomorphic conception of divinity once were, and as countless prejudices of which we are not even aware remain, being tacit assumptions of our reasoning.<sup>3</sup> Neither Descartes’s methodical doubt nor Bacon’s repudiation of idols nor, finally, Kant’s critique managed to dispel all presuppositions. In the great philosophical systems, one always finds traces of the naïve convictions of the era. If they were to be eliminated through a logical purification, they would simply resurface as instinctive impulses, as expressions of latent feelings,<sup>4</sup> or as dogmatic assessments.

<sup>2</sup> *realismo ingenuo*

<sup>3</sup> *raciocinio*

<sup>4</sup> *sentimientos*

Picture the astonishment of Copernicus’s contemporaries upon being told that the planet rotates, as if it were a top, on its own axis and in an extensive orbit around the sun. Children now hear this lesson on the benches of elementary schools, and people today accept these ideas without hesitation. But what a secular, slow, and painful effort was necessary to transform the paradox into a trivial truth! Copernicus’s work was published in 1543. Galileo was tortured in 1633. Father Feijóo, taking appropriate caution, dared to disseminate the novel theory [p. 20] in Spain in 1750. And it wasn’t until 1855 that the book was removed from the Index. Even in a spirit as elevated as Hegel’s, vestiges of the geocentric conception can similarly be discovered.

With how much greater tenacity must naïve realism, whose pragmatic value is proven in every moment of life, impose itself. The Eleatics already condemned it. All moderately enlightened criteria reject it. Yet, it has not been able to be completely eradicated. To dispel it is, nonetheless, the precondition of all philosophy.

And, evidently, dispelling it is not difficult. Few reflections suffice to show that this visible and tangible universe, which is spatially extended and develops in time, is only known<sup>5</sup> as a mental phenomenon.<sup>6</sup> How many, though, after conceding this fact, proceed to disregard it, setting it aside as something bothersome and thus continue their dealings<sup>7</sup> without taking it into account? This reproach is not directed at the common folk,<sup>8</sup> who are unaware of the first of philosophical notions. There are cultured spirits who, though knowing the error of naïve realism, fail to accomplish the intimate task necessary to escape the suggestion of habit. Moreover, there are refractory mentalities<sup>9</sup> incapable of shedding off their naivety. With spirits so disposed, one should not speak of philosophy, just as one does not speak of music to the deaf. Although they may otherwise be good people worthy of appreciation and respect, and not lacking in common sense, they lack philosophical sense.

<sup>5</sup> *conocemos*

<sup>6</sup> *fenómeno*

<sup>7</sup> *discurso*

<sup>8</sup> *vulgo*

<sup>9</sup> *mentalidades refractarias*

We must bear in mind, then, that the external world is not a known reality,<sup>10</sup> but a problem that, for now, when it exists, only exists in a consciousness.<sup>11</sup>

<sup>10</sup> *realidad conocida*

<sup>11</sup> *en una conciencia*

[p. 21]

### §2

It is not enough to emancipate oneself from naïve realism up to the point of understanding<sup>12</sup> the totality of things as a mental phenomenon. This is the crudest part of the initiation. It is necessary to pursue naïve realism in all its hideouts, especially where it conceals itself in larval forms.

<sup>12</sup> *comprender*

Space and time, the two magnitudes in which the cosmic process is framed, are also only known as elements of consciousness, and their real existence outside of it is not a proven fact.

Meanwhile, the habit of contemplating reality in spatial or temporal relations takes hold of us<sup>13</sup> even more than the supposed externality of things. We can at least suppress the externality of reality *in mente*, but it is not possible for us to evict space and time from our representation.<sup>14</sup> Such a psychological disposition finds its natural support in a language constructed on the mold of naïve realism, so much so that it places spatial and even temporal relations where they only have a figurative meaning.<sup>15</sup> It takes effort to overcome these suggestions to realize that, in addition to the corporeal world, we also only know the existence of space and time as a fact of consciousness.<sup>16</sup>

<sup>13</sup> *nos perturba*

<sup>14</sup> *representación*

<sup>15</sup> *valor figurado*

<sup>16</sup> *hecho de conciencia*

### §3

Not satisfied with populating the external world with sensible images,<sup>17</sup> we also add to them creations of our reasoning.<sup>18</sup> Through a known psychological process, we abstract general concepts that — initially almost concrete — amplify and superimpose themselves and end up taking on exceptional subtlety. We then expel these logical children of consciousness to [p. 22] place them in space or, perchance, beyond. We begin by baptizing them, by giving them a name, and, in a continuous act, they become escaped specters, as if having forgotten their genesis.

<sup>17</sup> *imágenes sensibles*

<sup>18</sup> *raciocinio*

Matter is the concept of what is extended; ether is that of the medium;<sup>19</sup> energy and cause are that of action; etc. This progeny of the beings of reason<sup>20</sup> settles like a swarm upon things or incorporates itself into them, thereby allowing us to construct our conception of the cosmos.

<sup>19</sup> *vehículo*

<sup>20</sup> *entes de razón*

We give such progeny the mission of ordering, distributing, and concretizing things, as well as of establishing a nexus between successive events.<sup>21</sup> In truth, though, their home is in consciousness, and they have not the slightest thought of abandoning it, for it is there that they exercise their office.

<sup>21</sup> *hechos sucesivos*

### §4

Let us see if it is even possible to conceive<sup>22</sup> of something outside of consciousness. In the very utterance of the word “conceive,” we have already set consciousness in motion. We can anticipate that the result of our attempt will be but another conception.

<sup>22</sup> *concebir*

Think of the most remote beginning of time. Launch your thought into the unfathomable places that lie beyond the Milky Way. Wander through the multidimensional spaces. Never will we be able to escape the boundaries of consciousness. Not even the most audacious imagination can cross such boundaries.

Nevertheless, we want there to be<sup>23</sup> something foreign to the process of consciousness, something that is its negation. This we call that-which-is-not-in-consciousness.<sup>24</sup> And, well, we have once again created a conception. That-which-is-not-in-consciousness itself does not exist except insofar as we think it, and, in the very act of thinking it, it no longer is that-which-is-not-in-consciousness.

<sup>23</sup> *queremos que haya*

<sup>24</sup> *lo inconsciente*

[p. 23]

### §5

But if the sensible and intelligible orders only exist in consciousness, does this universe, despite its apparent solidity, amount to nothing more than a fiction? Not at all. Whether we consider the cosmos as a material or ideal process, in both cases it will be real. What varies is only the interpretation we have given it.

In giving the sun a different position in the planetary system, we haven’t thereby ridded it of its functions. For both realists and idealists, it illuminates today as it did before Copernicus. Indeed, we haven’t even modified the common expressions with which we refer to its rising or setting. We can say that the diurnal movement is a deception, but certainly not that the sun is a deception. What is deceptive is rather our way of seeing it.

Similarly, whether we see reality as material, energetic, or psychical, reality is not thereby altered. We prefer the latter because it is the only way we can know it. The other ways of seeing reality are hypothetical.

### §6

That everything only exists in a consciousness does not, by itself, imply that reality itself is merely a mental phenomenon. It only means that reality presents itself to us in this form and in no other. However, our knowledge could very well be the reflection of a reality that is independent of us. We can, in the abstract, distinguish between the *modus cognoscendi* from the *modus essendi*. To affirm the identity of both amounts to identifying thinking with being.

This position is that of absolute idealism. Its opposite is extreme realism, which considers consciousness to be an epiphenomenon of external activities. In the first case, consciousness would be the center of the irradiation of the cosmos, [p. 24] a power that gives being to its own conception of the world. In the second case, consciousness would be an accidental efflorescence, one whose presence or absence neither modifies nor alters the development of the universal mechanism. In the first case, consciousness would not only be active, but it would be the only existing activity. In the second case, consciousness would not only be receptive, but also completely passive.

All intermediate positions, of course, fall within these two extremes: dualistic compromises, eclectic reconciliations, transfigured realism, mitigated idealism, and critical analysis — multiple ingenious attempts to delineate the domain of consciousness and that of things.

### §7

We will attempt, in due course, to take a position. If we explore the content of consciousness, we discover the concept of an entity that, unlike all others, we do not try to expel. On the contrary, we try to withdraw it to our most intimate and personal space. We detach it even from the physical organism. We place it in opposition to the mental process itself, and, in our eagerness<sup>25</sup> to abstract it from everything and in every manner,<sup>26</sup> we lodge it, perhaps, in the hollowness<sup>27</sup> of a mathematical point. This is the concept of the I.<sup>28</sup>

<sup>25</sup> *afán*

<sup>26</sup> *de todo y por todo*

<sup>27</sup> *hueco*

<sup>28</sup> *yo*

It is the persistent and stable unity that we postulate and to which we refer the successive moments of the changing psychic process. Beyond all doubt, it exists only in consciousness. As does the series of facts that we attribute to it, the I: affective states, volitions, and judgments.

But in no way do we attribute to it all the content of consciousness, for consciousness also includes the representation of a world that the I conceptualizes as foreign and separates [from itself], as internal from external. Yet, though this world is outside the I, it is not outside consciousness. The sensations [p. 25] that are its constitutive elements are psychic facts, and we have no other sign of this world’s existence.

According to realism, in accordance with common opinion, for the portion of consciousness opposed to the I, there exists a duplicate of another order; or, better, an original, whose more or less faithful reproduction is all that is cognizable.<sup>29</sup>

<sup>29</sup> *cognoscible*

The proof of that problematic world is arduous. We cannot escape consciousness, and any such effort is futile. There is no other recourse but to resort to argumentation, which itself reduces to considering the content of consciousness as an effect that must have a cause outside of it, without noting that such a cause is unknown, inaccessible, a pure noumenon. And, for now, we shall not ask with what right the concept of cause is employed, a concept which is nothing more than an element of our reasoning.<sup>30</sup>

<sup>30</sup> *raciocinio*

The existence of this hypothetical world, situated outside the horizon encompassed by our knowledge, does not have, in the literal sense of the word, a reason to be.<sup>31</sup> The affirmation of its reality is but an act of faith,<sup>32</sup> an irrational residue of naïve realism.

<sup>31</sup> *razón de ser*

<sup>32</sup> *fe*

### §8

However, in seeking to convert objective reality to a manifestation of the I — which, as we have seen, is not consciousness but rather that which integrates consciousness — the adversaries of realism only manage to contribute to maintaining the fiction of an external world. This egocentric error characterizes subjective idealism and, strictly speaking, it leads to solipsism.

If realism ends up turning the I into an offspring of the physical world, subjective idealism inverts this order and assumes the world to be a creation of the I. In this, the subjectivist is mistaken in more or less the same way as Rostand’s rooster, who believes that if he were not to crow the sun would not rise.

[p. 26] No sophistry can suppress the fundamental distinction between the I and the not-I, between the subjective and the objective order. The Greeks never conceived of such an absurdity, even though they exhausted almost all possible philosophical positions.

It was Descartes who, in identifying thinking with the I, inoculated modern philosophy with this pernicious germ that later prospered in the monstruous systems of the German Idealists. They confuse consciousness with *my* consciousness; they take the part for the whole; and, thus, they arrive at the conclusion that “life is a dream,” that the world is nothing but “the veil of Maya,” or “the cinematographic reel” passing through our consciousness.

It is the merit of realist schools to have opposed this fallacious conception. In demonstrating the independence of subject and object, they celebrate their greatest triumph because they draw support from an indisputable fact of consciousness. Unfortunately, they hasten to undermine their triumph in their desire to subject the I to a noumenal world. The objective world is certainly outside the I, but it is not outside consciousness. In counting something as external, we refer to the I and not to an unknowable reality. Why should we substitute the known reality with an imagined one?

In truth, consciousness bifurcates itself<sup>33</sup> into an objective and subjective order. We cannot say more than what we know. And this we know in an immediate and definitive manner.

<sup>33</sup> *se desdobla*

### §9

The subject<sup>34</sup> does not maintain a contemplative attitude before the world. The subject is by no means a disinterested spectator. Consciousness is the theater of conflicts and harmonies between the subject that feels, judges, and wills and the object that conforms or resists.

<sup>34</sup> *sujeto*

[p. 27] The mutual relations are established by means of mental forms, which constitute an intermediary zone between internal and external reality.

Sensations would birth chaos if they were not coordinated and made concrete in a determinate object. It is necessary to unite some sensations and separate others; to assign them, most importantly, to their respective places in space and time, so that they constitute unities that then stand in relations to one another. The same must also be done with the multiple manifestations of the activity of the subject. To this end, the countless isolated facts must be classified and linked.

This task is carried out through concepts abstracted from both the subjective and objective orders. Although secondary and derived from intuited facts,<sup>35</sup> concepts are as necessary as these for constructing our conception of the cosmos.

<sup>35</sup> *hechos intuídos*

### §10

The destiny of concepts usually varies. While some always retain the mark of their origin and do not stray from their sources, others become independent and acquire their own jurisdiction, especially if they are denoted by a noun,<sup>36</sup> which almost crystallizes them. Most concepts encompass only a particular, more or less limited domain, while others are so broad that they encompass the entire subjective or objective orders; or, sometimes, they encompass both with a universal meaning.<sup>37</sup> Some always possess a certain concrete content, while others are completely abstract and lack any possible representation.

<sup>36</sup> *un sustantivo*

<sup>37</sup> *valor*

Finally, concepts are born or die when they have completed their task. Their life — sometimes ephemeral — responds to a temporary need and barely leaves the trace of a term in the vocabulary. Many endure — as modest instruments of daily labor — and some stand out as dominant and emancipate [p. 28] themselves. Like rebellious slaves, instead of obeying, they claim to govern consciousness and tenaciously resist their displacement. The history of philosophy is the history of these rebellious concepts. Human consciousness has bowed for centuries before the idols incubated in its bosom, like savages<sup>38</sup> before the charms fabricated by their own hands.

<sup>38</sup> *salvaje*

### §11

There are, however, concepts whose tyranny is difficult to evade. They form a select group, a kind of aristocracy, and they seem so indispensable that they have been regarded as having an origin distinct from the mass of empirical concepts, a plebeian mass sometimes denigrated with the label of pseudo-concepts. Those select concepts have been called innate ideas, *a priori* forms, categories, or, at the very least, they have been recognized as having a distinct lineage.<sup>39</sup> They certainly perform an important role.

<sup>39</sup> *abolengo remoto*

If we suppress concepts like space, cause, and energy, then the entire cosmos would collapse and disappear. If we suppress the concept of time, then the very process of consciousness stops and extinguishes. This is because such concepts are universal, applicable not just to a more or less broad or restricted group of facts, but to all of them, without exception. They serve especially to coordinate and establish a nexus between facts. It is as if they were immanent in each singular case. The validity of particular or general concepts depends on them. For this reason, they have been considered to be necessary.

The practical necessity of their use as forms of knowledge is imposed on us, but this doesn’t go all the way to making such concepts irreplaceable. Let us respect them without exaggerating our devotion. Some members of this oligarchy have already experienced a *capitis diminutio*. Thus, the concept of an unchanging substance,<sup>40</sup> with all its repercussions<sup>41</sup> for bodies and souls, is in full decline. [p. 29] And nothing less than the old, almost untouchable, concept of cause is being replaced by that of function. Space and time, in a more abstract language like mathematics, may also suffer some degradation. And even the multiple categories on whose number philosophers never managed to agree can be reduced to a single one — relation — which expresses the relativity and reciprocal dependence of all elements that constitute a state of consciousness.

<sup>40</sup> *sustancia estable*

<sup>41</sup> *secuela*

In virtue of being constant elements in the logical process, and in virtue of their dialectical development being an abstract reflection of facts, these concepts are used to systematize the data of experience. However, like all other concepts, they are empty<sup>42</sup> without the intuitive content to which they apply. To operate with concepts in place of intuitions is to invert hierarchies and to subordinate<sup>43</sup> the primary to the secondary. It is the river, the active force, that carves the riverbed, not the riverbed that engenders the river, even if the riverbed contains the river when it doesn’t overflow. Thinking presupposes intuiting, as Croce says: *“Pressupposto dell’attività logica sono le rappresentazioni o intuizioni.”*

<sup>42</sup> *vacío*

<sup>43</sup> *supeditar*

Of course, we cannot think except in<sup>44</sup> concepts. But let us not take the logical scaffolding<sup>45</sup> for the essence. Let us not imitate the example of natural sciences, which frame<sup>46</sup> facts in schemata and subsequently confuse the schemata with reality. General ideas, like schemata, are indispensable, but let us remain on guard, since conceptualism is the first step toward verbalism.

<sup>44</sup> *en*

<sup>45</sup> *andamios*

<sup>46</sup> *encuadran*

### §12

The point of departure of knowing<sup>47</sup> is intuition.<sup>48</sup> I understand by intuition the evident fact, the spontaneous and immediate knowledge<sup>49</sup> constituted as a unity<sup>50</sup> by [p. 30] synthetic apperception. I do not add “without discursive elements” because this ideal condition is never realized. Pure intuition does not exist. Analysis always discovers its complexity, but criticism cannot be taken to the extreme of denying the intuitive basis of knowledge without falling into nihilism and committing suicide through absurdity. However, even though it deals with experience, I avoid employing the term “empirical” because this sensualist concept assumes a simplicity that does not withstand even the most cursory examination, and it tends to convert the act of knowledge into a passive affair.<sup>51</sup>

<sup>47</sup> *conocer*

<sup>48</sup> *el intuir*

<sup>49</sup> *conocimiento espontáneo e inmediato*

<sup>50</sup> *En unidad*

<sup>51</sup> *hecho*

Likewise, to avoid misunderstandings, I exclude from my concept of intuition the psychological phenomenon called intellectual intuition, for which I reserve, in a figurative sense, the historical name of vision.<sup>52</sup>

<sup>52</sup> *visión*

### §13

In consciousness there exist only facts,<sup>53</sup> concepts, and words. We intuit, we think, and we speak. We think, which is to say, we establish relations through<sup>54</sup> concepts, just as we express ourselves in<sup>55</sup> words. But, from the fact that we express ourselves in words, it doesn’t follow that we ought to be verbalists, and, from the fact that we employ concepts, it doesn’t follow that we ought to objectivize them.

<sup>53</sup> *hechos*

<sup>54</sup> *en*

<sup>55</sup> *en*

Sometimes words<sup>56</sup> — the Word<sup>57</sup> — have been claimed to possess a reality and action of their own. Magical formulas, many of which persist in religious rites and popular superstitions, claim there to be a mystical power in words. Kabbalistic doctrines attribute such a power to numbers, and the same tends to be insinuated, albeit in an algebraic form, in contemporary logistics,<sup>58</sup> the latest budding of Pythagoreanism. Well, we ought to have convinced ourselves that verbal expressions neither crown nor dethrone kings, that number is but a quantitative abstraction. But is it perhaps more difficult to understand<sup>59</sup> that the instrument of thinking, the concept, is not also an entity, as the rationalist systems have believed? The old Kant [p. 31] saw this well in his affirmation that concepts without intuitions are empty. This doesn’t mean we should stop speaking, calculating, and thinking.

<sup>56</sup> *palabras*

<sup>57</sup> *el verbo*

<sup>58</sup> *logística*

<sup>59</sup> *comprender*

Even a philosophy based on intuition cannot develop through luminous projections but must necessarily<sup>60</sup> employ words and concepts. However, it must employ them with an understood meaning. We continue saying that the sun rises and sets without deceiving ourselves about the real fact, and that is how we shall employ concepts, without granting them any character other than that of a symbolic abstraction, devoid of their own reality.

<sup>60</sup> *por fuerza ha de*

On the other hand, a slight reflection suffices to convince oneself that all universal concepts, when hypostasized, become self-contradictory and absurd, e.g., time, space, first cause, etc. Concepts, like words, are symbols. The action that we endure or exercise is no longer a symbol. It is a fact.<sup>61</sup> The Logos, the immanent principle, ceased to be word long ago. One does not continue considering it a rational concept because, really, it is efficient action,<sup>62</sup> will,<sup>63</sup> and energy.

<sup>61</sup> *hecho*

<sup>62</sup> *acción eficiente*

<sup>63</sup> *voluntad*

### §14

The relation between concepts in opposition<sup>64</sup> is rather strange. Formal logic, according to the principle of identity, considers this relation contradictory, without realizing that this principle only governs things.<sup>65</sup> Yes, this hammer is not those pliers. But I use both tools accordingly, without them excluding each other. Both are useful to me, and the use of one does not prohibit me from using the other. The choice depends on the circumstances and my aims, just as their effectiveness depends on the [desired] practical result. So, too, with the use of concepts that are mere instruments of logical operations.<sup>66</sup>

<sup>64</sup> *conceptos opuestos*

<sup>65</sup> *solo rige para las cosas*

<sup>66</sup> *trabajo lógico*

Affirmation and negation, despite being concepts in opposition, are not, for that, contradictory, and neither are they linked by some mystical bond that fuses them into a *coincidentia oppositorum*. [p. 32] I affirm this and negate that. I affirm today what I negated yesterday, in accordance with the concrete case under consideration. The rapid and fleeting process in which life carries death in its bosom — in which perishing is the condition of birth and contrary tendencies come apart and then together — presents a good occasion to use both abstractions. In the living conflict of consciousness, what takes place is not a game of pale abstractions, but the clash of antagonistic forces, ones which we experience and do not dream. The synthesis of opposites occurs<sup>67</sup> in the concrete, singular, and determinate act.<sup>68</sup>

<sup>67</sup> *se efectúa*

<sup>68</sup> *acto*

### §15

When concepts in opposition have both been abstracted from real facts, they both retain an intrinsic meaning.<sup>69</sup> But if one of the terms is merely a speculative or grammatical construction, it thereby lacks any possible content and becomes an irredeemable vacuity.

<sup>69</sup> *un valor intrínseco*

The verbal possibility of being able to oppose a negative complement to every positive concept induces us to create unreal phantoms that stand in relations to true concepts, as the centaur does to the horse.

Thus, the negation of the limit does not constitute a new fact. It is merely the mental subtraction of an attribute inherent in things. We know only limited and finite objects. The infinite is a poetic creation. A realized infinity<sup>70</sup> is an absurdity.

<sup>70</sup> *infinito*

In contrast, the applicability of concepts is unlimited,<sup>71</sup> as they can be used again in each concrete case. If we were to use the simile of the tools without forgetting that, like all its counterparts, it falters, we would say: this hammer, indeed, is not infinite, but the series of blows it can [p. 33] deliver is infinite. It is a futile endeavor to attempt to reach the origin of things with a concept like cause, because no matter how far we carry mental regression or empirical investigation, it will always be applicable again. A first cause is inconceivable,<sup>72</sup> for a cause is not a thing.

<sup>71</sup> *ilimitada*

<sup>72</sup> *inconcebible*

In light of these considerations, we can appreciate Einstein’s doctrine when he tells us that space is finite but unlimited. That is, that any space subject to our study is measurable, but we will never lack a space in which to locate a fact. Through very different paths from those of philosophical reflection, as a postulate of the exact sciences, Einstein offers us here an analogous conclusion, for space is a concept and not a reality.

### §16

Apparently, the capacity for cognition<sup>73</sup> should precede knowledge,<sup>74</sup> but knowledge in fact also cannot be conceived without the cognizable.<sup>75</sup> Knowledge consists precisely in the act of knowing,<sup>76</sup> and it cannot precede itself. The isolated subject or isolated object are abstractions. One does not exist without the other. When the activity of consciousness<sup>77</sup> polarizes itself, it sets one pole against the other,<sup>78</sup> without thereby ceasing to maintain them united through mutual relations, which necessarily participate in the subjective and objective. Here there is neither an *a priori* nor an *a posteriori*. There is a confluence and concordance, a simultaneous, common action that even the most subtle abstraction cannot pinpoint.<sup>79</sup>

<sup>73</sup> *capacidad cognoscitiva*

<sup>74</sup> *conocimiento*

<sup>75</sup> *lo cognoscible*

<sup>76</sup> *el acto de conocer*

<sup>77</sup> *actividad consciente*

<sup>78</sup> *pone el uno frente al otro*

<sup>79</sup> *puntualizar*

In virtue of this, reasoning,<sup>80</sup> with equally valid arguments, can deduce the necessary concepts of<sup>81</sup> both the subjective and objective orders. Here, as always, the egocentric error goes wrong<sup>82</sup> in that it considers knowledge to be a function of the I, instead of recognizing that knowledge is equivalent to the content of consciousness in its totality. Hence the sterile disquisitions [p. 34] of realism and subjective idealism. As evident as it is that being is identical to thinking, it is also evident that thinking is not exclusively subjective.

<sup>80</sup> *raciocinio*

<sup>81</sup> *de*

<sup>82</sup> *perturba*

The exact boundary<sup>83</sup> between both orders, the subjective and the objective, is an interesting psychological question, though a satisfactory solution remains quite problematic. We know well what falls *grosso modo* on one side or the other: sensations on one hand; affects, volitions, and judgments on the other. To distinguish between what in knowledge is form and what is matter, and then, on that basis, to attribute form to the subject is, however, hazardous. Form is as necessary a part of the object as its matter. To put it in Kant’s idiom (while contradicting him), we would say: both matter and form are given to us.

<sup>83</sup> *deslinde*

The subject distinguishes what is its own from what is foreign, and the subject does not attribute to itself the function of giving form to knowledge in the same way that it attributes, for instance, attention to itself. This is not a naïve impression that could be corrected, for we never possess immediate consciousness of such a form-giving capacity. It seems to me that the matter of knowledge is nothing more than a shadow of the material matter<sup>84</sup> of the realistic dualism.<sup>85</sup> Pure sensations do not exist.

<sup>84</sup> *materia material*

<sup>85</sup> *dualismo realista*

The old scholastic distinction between material and formal elements of knowing reduces to the abstraction of primary elements from secondary ones. But both, in their unity, constitute the objective order that stands in opposition to the I. The psychic process develops in its forms, not through the intervention of the subject, but necessarily<sup>86</sup>: indeed, in spite of the subject. Spatiality characterizes the objective order and distinguishes it from the subjective.

<sup>86</sup> *forzosamente*

### §17

Until now, we have been working with a mistaken concept that we ought to abandon. The term “reality” comes from the Latin *res*=thing, and it involves the idea of stability. But there is [p. 35] nothing that is stable. In consciousness, we only observe a process, an action, a becoming, a continuous flowing<sup>87</sup> and flowing-together.<sup>88</sup> If we do not keep this fact in mind, we run the risk of again postulating things and entities<sup>89</sup> where there are only acts.<sup>90</sup> Subject and object are but synthetic operations in which the complex of mental states or the bundle of perceptions<sup>91</sup> is unified. As for the substratum we suppose for them — matter or spirit — it is but a concept, not a thing.

<sup>87</sup> *fluir*

<sup>88</sup> *confluir*

<sup>89</sup> *cosas y entidades*

<sup>90</sup> *actos*

<sup>91</sup> *sensaciones*

Instead of a reality, we have, then, an actuality.<sup>92</sup> This is the correct word that Aristotle taught us. Facts actualize themselves. They do not realize themselves. Consciousness itself is not an entity, but an action.<sup>93</sup> And it is not even an abstract action. It is a concrete one. A pure consciousness would be one without content, that is, an action without activity,<sup>94</sup> a perfect example of an absurdity. We must cure ourselves of all real-ism, not just naïve realism.

<sup>92</sup> *actualidad*

<sup>93</sup> *acción*

<sup>94</sup> *actividad*

In philosophy, reality is an obsolete concept; that is, it is a superstition. Let us keep the word in accordance with its commonly understood meaning,<sup>95</sup> especially to distinguish facts that are certain from those that are imagined or desired. A philosophical treatise, to be logical, should be written only with verbs, without employing a single noun.

<sup>95</sup> *valor convenido*

The rigidity of names, too solid and massive, does not lend itself to conveying the notion of a dynamic process that is movement, vibration, and the development of energies and rhythms. Around the two poles of consciousness — themselves unstable and movable — swirl and boil currents that are parallel or opposing, that reconcile or resist each other, and which, in each instant, create a new fact that never was nor will again be. The need to systematize the accumulation of facts obliges us to isolate them, to abstract them, to compartmentalize them, and, in doing so, we strip the psychic process of its very synthetic life, in which each element is a function of the others. The intuition of the reader should continuously maintain the unity and correlation that the retelling<sup>96</sup> destroys because analysis, by necessity, [p. 36] converts the active unity of that which thinks<sup>97</sup> into the disintegrated series of that which is thought,<sup>98</sup> and substitutes past phantoms for living reality.

<sup>96</sup> *relato*

### §18

If the only difference between objective and subjective activity lay in spatiality, then one of the two could — though not without great difficulty — be subordinated to the other, as has been attempted with so much perseverance in the monistic systems. In truth, there is a much more fundamental difference between these two opposed currents of conscious activity.

The objective world obeys necessary rules,<sup>99</sup> namely, laws. The subjective world lacks laws. It is free. In the objective world, a series of necessary facts<sup>100</sup> develops mechanistically, in such a way that each fact can be foreseen. In the subjective world, the will is what acts. It wills<sup>101</sup> what it fancies in such a way that its decisions<sup>102</sup> cannot be foreseen. The objective obeys causes lost in the past. The subjective obeys ends<sup>103</sup> projected into the future. Against the mechanism of the physical stands the autonomy of the I. Forgive the redundancy — *autos* signifies nothing but the I itself. The autonomy of the I is autonomy par excellence.

<sup>99</sup> *normas*

<sup>100</sup> *hechos forzosos*

<sup>101</sup> *quiere*

<sup>102</sup> *resoluciones*

<sup>103</sup> *finalidades*

The physical order actualizes itself, inexorably chains effects to causes — amoral and insensitive, without purpose<sup>104</sup> and without ends.<sup>105</sup> The subject, meanwhile, feels shaken by pains or joys; it affirms or denies, creates purposes, forges ideals, establishes values, and subordinates its conduct to the ends<sup>106</sup> it pursues.

<sup>104</sup> *propósito*

<sup>105</sup> *finalidad*

<sup>106</sup> *fines*

However, the freedom of the subject is that of willing,<sup>107</sup> not of doing. The free expansion of the will is constrained by the coercion of necessity, which permits no contingency.<sup>108</sup> The subject is autonomous but not sovereign. Its power does not equal its will. Therefore, it tends, ceaselessly, to increase its power. The eternal rebel never gives up its aspiration to actualize all its freedom. Nature [p. 37] ought to submit to the master. The instrument of this liberation is science and technology.<sup>109</sup>

<sup>107</sup> *querer*

<sup>108</sup> *arbitrariedad*

<sup>109</sup> *técnica*

Freedom should not parade around in a vacuum. Kant’s dove imagined that without the resistance of air it would fly even higher. It would, however, collapse — as our freedom would too — if it did not support itself on the resistance that opposes it. This is the condition of subjective effort, and freedom does not seek to annihilate it. It only seeks to achieve its own ends. Dominion over the objective order emancipates oneself from material bondage and constitutes economic freedom, in the broadest sense of the term. The first person who broke a recalcitrant coconut with a stone and invented the hammer initiated its conquest.

Yet the subject feels constrained not only by the objective world, but also by its own conditions. Its action is disturbed by impulses, affections, and errors. The subject wants to emancipate itself from those too. It should certainly add the dominion over oneself to the dominion over nature. Only the autarky that gives form to<sup>110</sup> the will, in a discipline set by the will itself, yields ethical freedom.

<sup>110</sup> *encuadra*

Thus, along with an economic finality,<sup>111</sup> a moral finality is established, to which — without diminishing self-determination — conduct must be submitted. It is symbolized in a concept whose name and content varies, but which, for now, we will call the ethical concept.<sup>112</sup> It comes to be the most complete expression of personality, the ultimate aim of free action, committed to subjecting the natural to the moral order. Although he couched it within an unacceptable metaphysics, no one has better described the conquest of ethical freedom than Spinoza in the fourth and fifth books of his fundamental work, which deal, respectively, with servitude and freedom.

<sup>111</sup> *finalidad*

<sup>112</sup> *el concepto ético*

What a difference, however, separates the moral law from the physical law! The latter we endure. The former we dictate. The latter is an expression of a necessary order. The former is a postulate of our free [p. 38] will. We cannot imagine that the law of gravity would fail even once. When we rise in space and seemingly counter it, we still obey it. This is not so with the moral law, which we infringe because we retain the monstrous capacity to disobey it.

Indeed, freedom and ethics are correlative complements. The mechanistic conception, in extending physical determination to the subject, takes away the privileges<sup>113</sup> of personality. In place of autonomy, it substitutes automatism, and there is no dialectical display that can construct an ethics on such grounds.

<sup>113</sup> *fueros*

Economic freedom, dominion over the objective world, dominion over oneself, and ethical freedom constitute, in their unity, human freedom — a freedom which, far from being transcendent, is actualized in proportion to our knowledge<sup>114</sup> and power. All of the above elements permeate and presuppose one other. None can exist without the others, for they all are the grounds of the development of personality. The eminent principle is not the struggle for existence, but the struggle for freedom. At every step, the former is sacrificed for the latter. Freedom becomes. From the depths of consciousness, the I emerges like a torso: forehead free, arms free, determined to free the rest.

<sup>114</sup> *saber*

### §19

Kant’s third antinomy plainly announces the philosophical problem par excellence: the joint affirmation of necessity and freedom. It is the final and unavoidable conclusion of a logical disquisition. Any attempt to overcome or undo the antinomy, to reconcile thesis and antithesis in a synthesis, is fruitless. This is so whether one opts for a unilateral solution or appeals to transcendent factors.

Let me explain. The antinomy, despite taking on a scholastic structure, is the expression of the conflict that actualizes itself in consciousness. No speculative solution can eliminate this conflict, which serves precisely as the root of the psychic process. To deny either [p. 39] necessity or freedom is a fallacy contradicted by each moment of real life. To seek a reconciliation between phenomenal determinism and noumenal freedom, no matter how much ingenuity has been spent on this endeavor, is to offer but a fictitious solution. For, the noumenon — and even moreso the noumenal — are not objects of knowledge.<sup>115</sup> They cannot be invoked to explain any actual fact.<sup>116</sup> They have no existence in consciousness except as empty concepts.

<sup>115</sup> *conocimiento*

<sup>116</sup> *hecho efectivo*

The third antinomy is the faithful and conclusive result of the critique of the faculty of cognition,<sup>117</sup> and it cannot be evaded, nor is it of interest to evade it. Reality is not altered one iota by a verbalist solution. Upon encountering the antinomy, theory has fulfilled its mission. It gives us the key to what exists but cannot anticipate the result of a process of infinite projection.

<sup>117</sup> *capacidad cognoscitiva*

What remains is but a practical problem: to take a position. If a negative position pleases us, then we will resign ourselves in ascetic renunciation. If we prefer an affirmative position, we will incorporate our personal effort into the energies that carry out the endless task of creative action.

Both positions are legitimate. Through both we attempt to actualize the freedom of the I. We are free to choose to detach ourselves from the world or to commit ourselves to subjugating it. We cannot generalize our own position, especially in the name of freedom, by prescribing how the matter should be settled. Francis of Assisi and Leonardo da Vinci represent two human types, both equally great and beautiful.

Only the herd instinct demands the same path, a dogmatic norm for all. The free person<sup>118</sup> — not without danger — will seek their own course. For this, the free person arms themself with knowledge and inner strength. But the risk is inseparable from freedom.

<sup>118</sup> *hombre*

[p. 40]

### §20

The Wise King says: All the creatures of the world naturally love and covet freedom, especially those<sup>119</sup> who understand<sup>120</sup> all things, and most of all those who are of noble heart.<sup>121</sup>

<sup>119</sup> *omes*

<sup>120</sup> *que han entendimiento*

<sup>121</sup> This comes from the *Book of Laws*, also known as the *Siete Partidas*, compiled by Alfonso X of Castille, the “Wise King,” between 1252 and 1284. This specific quote comes from Partida 4, Título 22. *— E.S.*

Freedom, thus, is the intrinsic trait of the subject. To affirm it is the most genuine expression of one’s being. Personality and freedom are two names for the same fact.

In the struggle waged for the conquest of freedom, the subject distinguishes the circumstances that favor or oppose this essential aspiration and judges and evaluates them from such a vantage point. The subject judges objective facts to be either useful or harmful, and its own acts to be either good or bad. The first is a pragmatic judgment, the second an ethical judgment.

Naturally, the subject wills<sup>122</sup> what is useful and good, and this it does without any possible exception, for such willing flow from its inner nature.<sup>123</sup> The subject works from singular cases and rises to generalizations, forging appropriate conceptions of the useful and good. The useful is what contributes to one’s economic freedom. Good is what affirms one’s ethical freedom. These two concepts, then, are not mere abstractions but possess actual content. This content obtains only in relation to the interests and the will of the subject. Objective facts in themselves are neither useful nor harmful. They are simply necessary. Acts are likewise neither good nor bad in themselves. They are only so in relation to the will they obey.

<sup>122</sup> *quiere*

<sup>123</sup> *condición íntima*

Even if there is no doubt about the concrete meanings of the useful and the good, it is nevertheless necessary to appraise each individual case or series of cases. Here, the subject — rightly or wrongly — fixes values and remains prepared to deny or transmute them once they have fulfilled their mission or demonstrated their ineffectiveness.

Not infrequently, we see that what yesterday was considered useful is today deemed harmful. In the moral order, values that have governed for centuries end up being replaced. To imagine [p. 41] that the values created in the struggle for freedom are everlasting and objective is to ignore their transitory character. They are means to realizing an end, and so they are issued just as much as they are removed from circulation.

The concepts of the useful and the good are likewise rooted in the very nature of consciousness and its fundamental conflict. [However,] they do not change no matter how much the evaluation of particular cases changes. It is the categorical imperative of spontaneous action that is reflected in the feeling of obligation, duty, and responsibility. Moral sanction<sup>124</sup> is the actualization of freedom or its privation, the servitude imposed by vice and ignorance.

<sup>124</sup> *sanción*

### §21

Although economic and ethical freedom are equally fundamental, the two corresponding concepts should not be confused. What is useful is not always good, nor is what is good always useful. One refers to the object. The other to the subject.

To take the useful for the good is the sin of all utilitarian morality and the error characteristic of systems that tend to deny autonomous personality. To insist solely on the ethical concept is to ignore that the full expansion of personality is only possible in a subjugated world.

The lack of economic freedom leads us to exchange ethical freedom for a mere plate of lentils. The absence of ethical freedom submits us to the dominion of instinct and dogma. The lack of both subjects us to foreign powers, annihilates our personality, and prevents us from living our own lives.

The tight correlation between economic and ethical freedom is reflected in language. All words that express servitude have at the same time a pejorative moral signification: slave, villain, lackey, etc. To do away with all economic subjection is, thus, a precondition for human liberation.<sup>125</sup> But it is not the only one.

<sup>125</sup> *liberación*

[p. 42]

The desire<sup>126</sup> to locate the grounds of our conduct and to distinguish the lawful from the unlawful has preoccupied the human mind since its earliest days. In this case, as in all others, the first solution was religious. The norms of conduct were placed under divine authority. The continually renewed attempt to resolve the grave problem through transcendent factors is the residue of this primitive position. To be sure, an ethics requires, as its essential elements, a free and responsible subject along with sanctions. Without the first, we would be left with nothing but the development of a predetermined process, of which we would be “useless witnesses.” Without the second, it would not matter whether we choose good or evil. But, what use is metaphysical freedom or posthumous sanction after the death of the individual? The grotesque postulate of the immortality of the individual was a necessary consequence of the transcendent interpretation.

<sup>126</sup> *deseo*

For their part, the anti-metaphysical schools, while pointing out the emptiness of these fictions, attempted to construct a purely human ethics. However, such schools fell into hedonism or utilitarianism, and the logical development of their deterministic principles led them to a morality without freedom, without responsibility, and without sanction; that is — though they don’t admit it — to the negation of ethics. On such bases, one can write a manual of good conduct or rules for achieving the greatest success in business. But such bases cannot resolve the age-old problem that afflicts the human soul.

Demonstrating that our current ethical concepts are the result of biological or social evolution is of great interest, but it is irrelevant to the matter at hand. We investigate not the historical fact of evolution but rather the reason for that evolution, the principle that guides and informs it. All of these positivist systems miss the question.

Biological systematization, in reminding us that we derive from [p. 43] animals, should tell us why we have surpassed them and tend to shed our bestial residue. Or does it rather believe that we should return to our origins?

The work of the herd instinct<sup>127</sup> and the consequences of social coexistence are worthy of examination. But to ground an ethics on them is to forget that social organization is as much a source of the moral as it is a source of the immoral. This small truth is hidden from sociological moralists, and they would do well to reread every now and then Rousseau’s paradox.

<sup>127</sup> *instinto gregario*

Historical materialism is undoubtedly the most coherent and serious doctrine of the positivist era, as it still carries with it the breath of Hegel. But it remains one-sided. It only addresses half of the problem, and it fails to notice that its dogmatic application would rob us of spiritual freedom.

Neither the determinism of the objective world nor the rule<sup>128</sup> of utilitarian egoism can be denied. But neither can the consciousness of our freedom and responsibility be suppressed. Attempting to suppress this duality is a vain endeavor. It is to ignore the psychological conflict in which human personality struggles in defense of its dignity and in procurement of its ever-broader liberation. To make service of objective determinism is only a pragmatic means to realize our material freedom. But to limit ourselves to this purpose is to make ourselves slaves to the machine invented by our own hands. Let us remember the profound precept of the Gospel: *What does it profit a person to gain the whole world yet forfeit their soul?*

<sup>128</sup> *imperio*

### §22

A conception of ethics that lacks sanction is ridiculous. It is to suppose that there are acts that are devoid of finality.<sup>129</sup> Even a utilitarian morality must suppose that an act brings about useful or harmful consequences. Now, is there to be no difference between good and evil?

<sup>129</sup> *finalidad*

[p. 44]

No one doubts the pragmatic effects of error or success, of ignorance or knowledge.<sup>130</sup> Our acts, thus, turn out to be either effective or harmful, and, in this, they carry an explicit sanction.

<sup>130</sup> *saber*

As for the ethical act, it cannot, as such, receive a utilitarian sanction because, if it were to receive one, then it would cease to be ethical. In this it resembles aesthetic pleasure, which also does not possess objective utility. Should we, in light of this, renounce being good or deprecate beauty? In the ethical and aesthetic cases, the sanction is circumscribed in the subjective domain. It is not, for that, however, any less important, since it affects the development of life, particularly in its most intimate sphere. If ethical finality<sup>131</sup> aims at realizing freedom, then the sanction of immoral acts is the privation of freedom, the degradation of human dignity. The good act, in turn, has its reward in itself, that is, in the consciousness of freedom actualized.

<sup>131</sup> *finality*

Let us recall Spinoza: “Man’s lack of power to moderate and restrain the affects I call bondage. For the man who is subject to affects is under the control, not of himself, but of fortune… though he sees the better for himself, he is still forced to follow the worst.”<sup>132</sup>

<sup>132</sup> Translation taken from Edwin Curley, *Spinoza’s Ethics* (Princeton University Press), 4preface, p. 197. *— E.S.*

Though the useful receives an economic sanction, and though the ethical a subjective sanction, we must nevertheless remember that life is not constituted by these abstractions. Instead, it is constituted by the series of concrete acts which, in varying proportions, are at once useful and ethical. We must not overlook the extent to which the ethical character of personality influences the solution of practical problems. Through this guiding influence, ethical attitude also plays a part in the moral sanction that accompanies every human life.

The intuitive instinct of the folk<sup>133</sup> has never suspended its belief that punishment is essential to culpability<sup>134</sup> — that whoever did the crime will pay for it, that whoever kills with the sword will die by the sword.

<sup>133</sup> *del pueblo*

<sup>134</sup> *en la culpa va el castigo*

When, through a long experience, one contemplates the whole of life from a bird’s eye view, one is overcome with the sensation of an [p. 45] immanent, almost inexorable, justice. The balance of existence appears as the difference between our merits and our faults.

This does not imply falling into a cheap optimism, nor does it imply believing that rewards for virtue, or punishments for faults, are apportioned continuously according to the casuistry of a bourgeois code. It is not a matter of arithmetic relations or anything measurable, but rather of an intimate fact of consciousness, which allows us to prove how, in the conflict of life, it is not a vile task to strengthen the soul<sup>135</sup> and conquer the freedom of the spirit. Even before the accidental event, it arms us with virile strength,<sup>136</sup> like that of the Stoic before the inevitable.

<sup>135</sup> *ánimo*

<sup>136</sup> *entereza viril*

If we were to relate the pain of existence to its faults, we might perhaps find a compensation, which often we find justified in the case of others but certainly not in our own. How many complaints do we voice that, upon a sincere examination of consciousness, turn into reproaches against our own weakness?

There is a close relation between the ethical development of life and the ills that afflict it. In the profound theory of Karma, the world is, at every moment, the expression of its ethical value. Hindus can make this affirmation because, for them, the individual and the universal I are identical. We would have to translate this thought into less mystical language and say: at every moment, life is the expression of its ethical value. According to the popular superstition of transmigration, each person lives the Karma of a previous existence. In reality, each person lives their own Karma, that is — to put it plainly — each person is the child of their deeds.

[p. 46]

### §23

Long before Darwin identified “the struggle for existence” as the reason behind biological evolution,<sup>137</sup> Schopenhauer already believed that he had found the noumenal principle of the universe in the “will to live.”

<sup>137</sup> *la razón del devenir biológico*

He was undoubtedly right to consider the will as the most complete expression of the I. However, he erred in identifying the will with cosmic energy because subject and object, which are opposites, cannot be reduced to a single principle except on the condition of annihilating one of the two.

Indeed, the will, which is a teleological agent,<sup>138</sup> cannot be confused with energy subject to the category of causality. Thus, Schopenhauer, like the other Romantics he so denigrated, becomes entangled in the difficulty of combining the necessity of phenomenal processes with a supposed transcendent freedom.

<sup>138</sup> *agente teleológico*

The will, almost synonymous with the I, belongs entirely to the domain of the subjective; this it affirms or denies. But it cannot be accepted that, in its highest manifestation, it is solely a will to live, the mere affirmation of existence. If there were not, out of fear of falling into the old anthropocentric error, an excessive tendency to equate humans with animals, the simplest reflection would recall how, at each instant, life is sacrificed for a higher value.

This is a historical fact and a matter of daily observation. The very possibility of suicide, which is observed only in the human species, proves that humans can reach the point of negating — and not just in theory — the supposed fundamental principle of the will to live. Humans, either as individuals or as a collective, continually risk their existence for motives that can be either serious or trivial, but which are, strictly speaking, unnecessary. It is even universally agreed-on that an exaggerated attachment to life — cowardice — merits derogatory appraisal.

For the evolved organized mind, life has ceased to be an end. It is now reduced to a means for the realization of purposes without which it would lack value. The Stoics already knew this. Our contemporary liberal positivists ignore it.

It is not a vain conceit if humans have always considered themselves different from animals; or if, at least, we aspire to be different from them. It is the consciousness of one’s freedom that reveals itself in that old presumption and enables one to die for their ideals or superstitions.

Nietzsche saw this point with clarity, and he sought to replace the freedom to live with the “will to power,” a condition for the development of a superior human type. This concept, however, lends itself to a crude interpretation, which was never, despite his amoralism, in the author’s mind. The will to power is nothing other than that of actualizing freedom in its fullness, because, in the human, the will to live has elevated itself to the will to live freely.

Nature and I share the same home, though we are somewhat divided in that nature often subjugates me<sup>152</sup> and even takes advantage of my weaknesses. But, little by little, I manage to domesticate and impose my will on nature. I do not seek to untie myself from nature, since [p. 51] my existence is linked to its; plus, nature is not without its charms. I desire only to subdue and use nature for my purposes, to enjoy peace and my full freedom. I shall achieve this through my own effort and with the help of science.

<sup>152</sup> *tiranizarme*

*Psychologist:* Very well, that is precisely the task to which I contribute.

*Subject:* I am very much in agreement, but you must respect what is rightly mine.<sup>153</sup> I am not a puppet. I am a free subject.

<sup>153</sup> *fueros*

### §25

In our examination of the two opposite, fundamentally distinct, tendencies of conscious activity, we wouldn’t want to take the dualism to the extreme of forgetting the unity of consciousness. Despite their divergence, subject and object are inseparable, components of one and the same psychic process. One cannot be modified without affecting the other.

The clash of opposing currents was already identified by Heraclitus. Diremption<sup>154</sup> is a necessary condition for understanding the activity of the cosmos.<sup>155</sup>

<sup>154</sup> *dualización*

<sup>155</sup> *actividad cósmica*

In the most minute atom, we suppose there to be, without perturbing its unity, forces of attraction and repulsion; the organic cell is a battleground between assimilation and disintegration; the reproduction of the species requires sexual duality; planetary gravitation cannot be explained without centripetal and centrifugal tendencies; the dialectical process unfolds through the coinciding of thesis and antithesis. Wherever one seeks unity, one finds the conflict of two opposing<sup>156</sup> principles: power<sup>157</sup> is a function of resistance. It is unsurprising, then, that consciousness, the common mother of all that exists, offers us the same spectacle. And even if it were surprising, it is a fact, not an [artificial] invention.

<sup>156</sup> *contrarios*

<sup>157</sup> *potencia*

The difficulties in reconciling unity with multiplicity [p. 52] have been demonstrated to us by monists and pluralists in their eagerness to impose on us their unilateral interpretations. The concepts of quantity — unity, plurality, totality, number, measure, magnitude — are employed in accordance with the case at hand, without excluding or contradicting one another. Let us examine the concept of unity and let this serve as an example of the use of concepts in general.

There does not exist even one proven unity. We have looked for it and affirmed its existence. But, in fact, we have never found it. The physical unity — the atom — doesn’t qualify, even though the atom has never been an empirical fact, only a hypothesis. Yet not even under that title can it now subsist. The organic unity — the cell — has turned out to be an organism of infinite complexity. The psychic unity — sensation<sup>158</sup> — is never simple, and even less so are states of mind. Neither in the domain of the objective nor in that of the subjective can we fix a unity. Nor is the I, nor the intuited object, a unity.

<sup>158</sup> *la sensación*

All the real unities that we postulate are meta-empirical. There are only conceived unities. I can call, as I please, unity to the human body, to a book, to a battalion, to a people, then immediately break them down into the multiplicity of their integral parts and consider them as a plurality. Nothing prevents me from also conceiving the totality of existing forms as a unity and calling it ‘universe.’

The unity of consciousness is ideal, and, if we affirm it, it is only to deny the existence of two distinct substances or the possibility of an actual fragmentation.<sup>159</sup> Thus we save ourselves from all the meanderings of occasionalism, pre-established harmony, parallelism, and dualist doctrines in general.

<sup>159</sup> *fraccionamiento efectivo*

By ‘unity of consciousness,’ we must understand that, in both its objective and subjective manifestations, it is conscious action<sup>160</sup> and nothing more.

<sup>160</sup> *acción consciente*

We are well within our rights, in accordance with the evidence, to consider consciousness either as simple or as [p. 53] complex, and to interpret it as the unity that unfolds into complexity or as the synthesis that engenders unity.

It is worth remembering here the misstep of Spinoza, the most brilliant of the monists. His *Deus sive natura* suddenly bifurcates<sup>161</sup> into *natura naturans* and *natura naturata*. The insidious dualism attacks him when he least suspects it, for his entire system is an attempt to overcome the dualism of matter and spirit. It is regrettable that the cruelty of facts disturbs the peace of rationalist speculation. After all, isn’t the distinction between the sensible and the intelligible, between phenomenon and noumenon, also a shameful dualism?

<sup>161</sup> *desdobla*

We do not need to embroil ourselves in such disquisitions, for both matter and spirit are concepts that are sometimes useful and sometimes troublesome, especially when their origin is unknown.

Consciousness, thus, is as much one as it is multiple. But it is unique, for nothing exists outside of it.

### §26

The axioms here expounded can neither be demonstrated nor refuted. They are the expression of immediate data,<sup>162</sup> not the conclusions of a dialectical argument. They are an enumeration of facts that each person can verify. They are present at each moment in intuition, and they constitute the most direct experience imaginable.

<sup>162</sup> *evidencia inmediata*

They cannot be defined. Every definition is a relation, the explanation of one term by another, known term. We cannot so relate primitive facts to others. We can only intuit them. We either see them or we don’t. We know them or are ignorant of them. But there are no means of transmitting them, nor can words replace them.

There will be those who demand from us the definition of the terms we have employed; for instance, that of freedom. Whoever [p. 54] articulates this objection will, however, be unable to define even what is bitter or sweet.

Here is Cohen’s definition of freedom, which is typical and not among the worst: “Freedom is the energy of the will.” Tell me — sincerely — if someone could with this definition know what freedom is without having experienced freedom themselves.

I could, in turn, define freedom thus: freedom is the absence of coercion, just as coercion is the absence of freedom. We gain nothing with such a tautology. Whoever does not know by the immediate testimony of their consciousness what freedom is should give up trying to understand me, as I already renounce, with regret, their valuable turnout. And what I have said of freedom applies to all other terms that express immediate knowledge.

There is also no place for refutation. Only denial is possible. Facts are not debated. They are affirmed or denied. But it is not permissible to replace them with the verbal abstractions of scholastic jargon. This is not about opposing one reasoning to another.

1. There is nothing outside of consciousness. *Identify one fact that is not thought.*

2. Consciousness divides into subject and object. *Eliminate one of the two terms.*

3. Consciousness generates concepts abstracted from the subject or object. *Think without concepts.*

4. Consciousness is action. *Find within it a stable element.*

5. The objective order imposes itself with necessity. *Create or annihilate an objective fact.*

6. The subject is free. *Set a limit to its will.*

7. Objective action constrains subjective action. *Assert that the subject fulfills its own will.*

8. Consciousness is one. *Divide it.*

9. Consciousness is complex. *Affirm its simplicity.*

10. The physical law is unavoidable. *Perform a miracle.*

[p. 55]

11. The moral law is precarious. *Live without infringing it.*

12. Intuition is the only ground<sup>163</sup> of our knowledge. *Discover a fact through pure induction or deduction.*

<sup>163</sup> *fuente*

### §27

We cannot go beyond the facts known through intuition and of which we have immediate knowledge. It is not possible to establish knowledge with certainty<sup>164</sup> on other grounds. The logical function of reasoning<sup>165</sup> is limited to establishing relations between facts, without ever being able, by itself, to affirm the existence of even a single one of them.

<sup>164</sup> *conocimiento cierto*

<sup>165</sup> *raciocinio*

It was a rule of scholasticism, much repeated though little observed, to not to create beings of reason without necessity. This precept demands greater rigor, and it should be plainly stated that a being of reason can never be created, for any such being is, without exception, a mere concept. When appropriate, and provided that pragmatic confirmation permits it, mental operations may be extended until their logical force is exhausted. However, if the created concepts fail to make contact with the content of intuition,<sup>166</sup> they amount to wordplay, no matter how correct the accumulated syllogisms may be. Scientific investigation is not possible without the employment of appropriate concepts as working hypotheses. However, only intuition can lift them out of their precarious status.

<sup>166</sup> *Si no resisten esta piedra de toque*

Consider the ether: the intra-worldly and intramolecular medium;<sup>167</sup> an imponderable, immobile, and elastic matter; as soft as gas; as rigid as steel, though it offers not the slightest resistance to the passage of bodies. Let us grant that this inconceivable absurdity is for now a viable hypothesis. It is a large gap from here to the verification of its existence. One must lack, like Haeckel, any philosophical sense to count these scientific superstitions as facts. Empirical proof is lacking and cannot be supplied.

<sup>167</sup> *vehículo*

[p. 56]

If this occurs in the relatively firm terrain of the sciences, which aspire to be exact, then what shall we say of philosophical speculation, in which one claims to discover the true truth<sup>168</sup> by conjugating abstractions of increasing vacuity! This is to remove reason from its hinges, from its honest work, in order to oblige it to perform leaps of faith<sup>169</sup> and, at last, to harm itself. In accordance with the Socratic doctrine, according to which truth resides in concepts, Plato constructed the archetype of dialectical systems, and all of his successors have merely taken the inheritance without improving it. To date, no one has proven what we might call, in relation to consciousness, the extraterritoriality<sup>170</sup> of a concept.

<sup>168</sup> *verdad verdadera*

<sup>169</sup> *saltos mortales*

<sup>170</sup> *extraterritorialidad*

No less vain is the pretension of scientific empiricism to regard sensation, in relation to what is external, as the sole element of knowledge. It is naïve to continually invoke experience and ignore that it is not a foreign fact<sup>171</sup> but a mental process. This very naivety prevents us from distinguishing the most audacious meta-empirical creations from observable facts. Let us dispense with crude materialism, which doesn’t qualify as a philosophical doctrine, and consider the most honest representatives of the scientistic school.<sup>172</sup>

<sup>171</sup> *hecho extraño*

<sup>172</sup> *la escuela*

Such representatives undoubtedly have the advantage of relying on actual facts, ones that are systematized in science through concepts. But when they deepen their investigations, they reach unforeseen conclusions. Matter dissolves, its attributes turn out to be subjective, space is extension, time is succession, and, finally, there remains nothing but distinct activities, which the monistic tendency tries to reduce to a single cosmic energy, subject to fixed laws. Whether this energy is interpreted as physical or organic, it is never anything but action. So far so good. Only that action outside of consciousness is a monstrosity that cannot be experienced.<sup>173</sup> To reach such a result, scientifistic individuals,<sup>174</sup> neglecting the rigor of the method, and going beyond all possible induction, superimpose one hypothesis over another [p. 57] with the same fatality<sup>175</sup> with which the scholastics strung together the series of their syllogisms, until they arrived at a little being of reason, such as that phlogiston (R.I.P). In the process, however, human personality, the entire subjective world, has been forever imprisoned in the meshes of an implacable determinism.

<sup>173</sup> *esperpento inexperiencial*

<sup>174</sup> *cientificistas*

<sup>175</sup> *gravedad*

Human reason<sup>176</sup> cannot be prohibited from transcending the limits of what is known.<sup>177</sup> This is one of its high functions. Precisely, so that this work can be fruitful, in both science and philosophy, it is necessary to demarcate with exactitude what is known from what one desires to know, as well as to not confuse the positive with the hypothetical, the real with the fantastical. Above all, one must maintain the clear appreciation of the methodological instrument being handled and not use it where it is inapplicable.

<sup>176</sup> *la razón humana*

<sup>177</sup> *lo conocido*

Science should not go bankrupt. But those who seek to use it as an amoral weapon in the enterprise of degrading human personality should. Science constructs the causal nexus of the universe, and it certainly does so to good benefit. There it exhausts its mission. If it invades the domain of philosophy, which establishes final values, then it fails. From such a consummation of science and philosophy, monstrosities are born that are neither science nor philosophy.

The theoretical position of the positivist schools was apparently more solid, as it implied the renunciation of knowledge of first causes and ultimate principles. They only aspired to synthesize what is scientifically cognizable. Let us say in a hushed tone that<sup>178</sup> such a synthesis is not possible, for not even the systematization of the special sciences can be carried out without metaphysical elements, much less the systematization of the whole. But in its historical manifestation, positivism has developed into realist systems, subordinated to a supposed unity or hierarchy of the sciences, in which moral or social facts would be just as subject to laws as the physical or chemical facts are. That is to say, positivism has fallen into mechanism, which entails [p. 58] the annulment of the subject. Hence the series of pseudo-sciences which, like sociology and experimental psychology, still wander in search of the exact laws that they so emphatically announced to us in their first hour.

<sup>178</sup> *Silenciamos que*

### §28

Now, are we satisfied once the data of immediate experience are synthesized into general conclusions? Has the final secret been unveiled, all mystery dispelled? Undoubtedly not.

We can, on such grounds, construct a worldview that responds to all our practical and theoretical needs. But wherever one problem is solved, another arises.

New doubts, new questions spring forth from the depths of consciousness and demand answers. It is not enough to evade them or dismiss them as improper. Even the most naïve question merits an answer.

How are we to conceive of an action without an agent?

It is no more difficult to conceive of action<sup>179</sup> [without an agent] than it is to conceive of the substance to which we try to attribute it. We do not solve an enigma by adding another. Above all, action<sup>180</sup> exists in a development of acts.<sup>181</sup> Substance is a concept. Perhaps the difficulty is entirely grammatical. We do not use a verb without referring it to a noun or pronoun. This modality of language, suggested by the relative stability of things, derives from naïve realism and brings us to error.

<sup>179</sup> *acción*

<sup>180</sup> *acción*

<sup>181</sup> *un desarrollo de actos*

Does the conscious process have a beginning, a moment that initiated its evolution? The idea of time applies — and applies with necessity — to each isolated fact in its relation to those that precede or follow it. But to what antecedent should we refer consciousness, if it is the source<sup>182</sup> of all ideas, [p. 59] including that of time, which is its creation and could not have preceded it?

<sup>182</sup> *fuente*

Has the psychic process, then, engendered itself or does it depend on another principle? We can imagine a creative power that, in giving light to the world, birthed inseparable twins. But this creator is created by consciousness itself.<sup>183</sup> It is a noumenon, a being of reason. No intuition supports its existence. As for engendering itself, it is as inconceivable as preexisting itself. We gain nothing with verbal solutions like Spinoza’s *causa sui*. It happens that, when speaking of process, evolution, and action, we involve the notions of before and after; that is, the idea of time, a category whose relative meaning is not hidden from us, though we are unable to dispense with its employment. This is why Bergson is at such great pains to distinguish the concepts of pure duration and time, but he appeals to a vision that is certainly not immediate intuition.

<sup>183</sup> *por la misma conciencia*

Yet, in the end, something should exist through itself.<sup>184</sup> To exist is to be in consciousness, and in consciousness there exists no fact that does not have its reason in another. And consciousness itself? Consciousness is a process. It is the set of its actual content.<sup>185</sup> It is always consciousness of something, never pure consciousness. We therefore cannot aspire to anything more than a science of the relative, and there will never be a science of the absolute. Neither empiricism nor rationalism can achieve it.

<sup>184</sup> *algo ha de existir por sí*

<sup>185</sup> *el conjunto de su contenido actual*

Does a great enigma thus remain? There remains, at least, a distant end<sup>186</sup> that, despite governing the present, remains unactualized in human consciousness.

<sup>186</sup> *finalidad*

“But that finality is not enough for me. I necessarily need to believe in a Being!”

“You are free to do so, but that is an act of faith.”

In the presence of this cryptogenic world, rationalists and empiricists have, for centuries, striven to conceive a metaphysics to explain the known by the unknown. They built [p. 60] nothing but systems of concepts without representable content. If we in turn harbored the desire to imitate them, a metaphysics would no longer suffice for us. We would need a meta-psychics to penetrate into the superconscious.<sup>187</sup> To enunciate it is to demonstrate its impossibility. Plus, the attempt would not even be original. Plotinus already thought of it.

<sup>187</sup> *superconsciente*

Let us try, then, without leaving consciousness, to address the last and most terrifying of the problems.

### §29

Conscious action<sup>188</sup> is the alpha and omega, the beginning and the end, the creative energy of what exists. It develops the cosmic panorama in the infinite variety of its scenes and gives it the infinite range of intimate emotions. No beyond is conceivable. It is, of course, the absolute,<sup>189</sup> the eternal.

<sup>188</sup> *La acción consciente*

<sup>189</sup> *Es, desde luego, lo absoluto*

We know, however, only the non-extended instant between past and future, the perpetual and fleeting present. We know only the incessant passage of particular and relative facts. Neither the eternal nor the absolute are in our intuition.

If we knew the absolute with certainty, if the essential nexus of facts were more than a concept, then intellectual tranquility should come over us, no doubts would remain, and Being would cease to be a problem. We are witnesses to the action that acts<sup>190</sup> in consciousness, but we do not know it in itself. We intuit only the process of its manifestations. Indeed, even less than this: the series that unfolds in individual consciousness. Are we to take this fragment for the universe?

<sup>190</sup> *acción actuante*

No egoism has reached this extreme. The evidence of our relativity pesters us all too vividly, and the aspiration toward the absolute emerges imperiously, never as an actualized fact, but rather as a logical demand, as a yearning of sentiment, as a willed end.<sup>191</sup> [p. 61] No intuition, no empirical datum, no reasoning<sup>192</sup> elucidates for us the concept of the absolute, although it is the unavoidable complement of the relative.

<sup>191</sup> *finalidad*

<sup>192</sup> *raciocinio*

Cornered by the methodical doubt in the solipsism of the egocentric position, Descartes appeals to the consciousness of our relativity in order to relate it to the absolute. He puts it in the language and prejudices of his time, and it is possible that, stripped of such contingencies, many would find his argument convincing.

We do not stumble upon the hurdle of solipsism, which is inevitable for subjective idealism, since we have not identified the I with the totality of what exists. Nevertheless, the difficulty persists. For, even when the existence of the absolute is affirmed and believed, we only have an abstract concept of it, which is completely empty if we locate the absolute outside of consciousness. A hundred different names have been given to it: a conclusive proof that we are ignorant of the true one.

In consciousness, the absolute presents itself as aspiration, as tendency toward an end<sup>193</sup> valued as ultimate and supreme, as the overcoming of the subject-objective duality. In this sense, we can ground ourselves in the very nature of the conscious process to determine the absolute. We know that the conscious process is a conflict, a struggle without truce, between freedom and necessity. To actualize absolute freedom through the conquest of economic dominion over nature and ethical self-mastery,<sup>194</sup> to subject necessity to freedom, to achieve the full development of one’s own personality: such is the aim, not imposed by foreign powers, not invented by fantasy, for it is the very root of becoming.<sup>195</sup>

<sup>193</sup> *finalidad*

<sup>194</sup> *autodominio ético*

<sup>195</sup> *del devenir*

We have fought for our freedom since we detached ourselves from the penumbra of animality. For it we continue in demand. When the conquest is complete, necessity and freedom will have reconciled. Consciousness will rest in [p. 62] its own peace, and the last doubt will be silenced. But in the meantime, there is no such reconciliation or peace<sup>196</sup>: philosophy doesn’t have the last word, for life is action, a perpetual task, not a theorem. *Cosa fatta capo ha.* Theory marches half-heartedly<sup>197</sup> behind the facts. But let us note the principle that moves the facts: call it creative freedom.

<sup>196</sup> *Entretanto no*

<sup>197</sup> *claudicante*

### §30

The problem of the absolute has yet another facet. In identifying the absolute end<sup>198</sup> as a fact of consciousness, we broach the most difficult matter. Is this end<sup>199</sup> realized in the consciousness of an individual, or is each individual but a part of a universal process of realization?<sup>200</sup>

<sup>198</sup> *la finalidad absoluta*

<sup>199</sup> *finalidad*

<sup>200</sup> *un caso dentro de un proceso universal?*

The certainty of super-individual historical processes does not allow us to assume that existence is exhausted in the individual. Yet, we also do not know the universal as existence. How can we find the relation between the particular and the universal, the ephemeral and the eternal, existence and Being?

Humans<sup>201</sup> employ three means to answer the most vehement interrogation of their spirit: metaphysics, art, and religion. None of these means excludes the others. In fact, they are mutually supportive, and, just as they serve the same purpose, they also stem from an analogous psychic fact.

<sup>201</sup> *el hombre*

Metaphysics offers systems that are no longer expressions of what has been proven but are rather hypothetical constructions of the creative imagination. They are, in spite of the material from which they are forged, works of art, dialectical poems, ideal symbolisms. They maintain, indeed, the pretension of being logical conceptions, but this is merely their formal aspect. They are not born from reasoning.<sup>202</sup> Through a quite complicated psychological process, to which volition is not foreign, convictions take root in the mind when faced with the haunting problem, convictions which appear sometimes as the result of a slow incubation and [p. 63] other times as spontaneous inspirations. They are a sort of intellectual vision that takes control of the author’s spirit and constitutes the author’s work.

<sup>202</sup> *raciocinio*

The argumentation that sustains metaphysics comes afterward. Reason,<sup>203</sup> which has never denied its favors to anyone, performs its logical functions and is ready to demonstrate whatever one wishes, whether it be a brilliant conception or an implausible falsehood. There is no absurdity that has not been supported by a defense plea.<sup>204</sup>

<sup>203</sup> *La razón*

<sup>204</sup> *alegato*

Systems distinguish themselves from each other by their logical coherence, ethical value, and persuasive power, not by their greater or lesser material veracity. They are rational myths, attempting to express the ineffable through a felicitous metaphor.<sup>205</sup>

<sup>205</sup> *metáfora feliz*

At bottom, they are but individual ways of seeing. However, the genius, carrying on the age-old task,<sup>206</sup> has the privilege of expressing with their own vision the thought of a people or an era. This is why the great metaphysical systems, despite being children of their time and products of ethnic and personal factors, endure like imperishable works of art and are always a source of intense intellectual emotion. Each generation in turn attempts anew the proper expression of its thoughts and feelings in new philosophical forms.

<sup>206</sup> *continuador de la labor secular*

If, grounding oneself on intuitive knowledge, one were to establish the conception of a universal process that, without losing its unity, individualizes itself in autonomous monads, actualizing the eternal becoming in a struggle for creative freedom, then the value of such a system would depend on the intellectual vigor of its author. The ideal of creative freedom could also be synthesized in a unique action that reveals itself in consciousness, and we would expose ourselves to the ironic reproach of having fallen again into an old anthropomorphism.

Despite everything, humans<sup>207</sup> continue to resolve the ultimate antinomies and silence the conflict in which consciousness is ensnared: no critique extinguishes the need for metaphysics.

<sup>207</sup> *el hombre*

Art, in turn, satisfies this need through its reconciliation [p. 64] of the opposition between the subjective and objective worlds in aesthetic emotion. Poetry and, above all, music, which employs more abstract materials for expression, submerge isolated accidents in the bosom of the universal and contemplate the eternal in the concrete. The work of art also has its genesis in an intimate vision, which the artist then actualizes in accordance with their creative capacity.

Poetic or artistic creation, is not, by virtue of being free, arbitrary, nor does it conflict with truth. It’s precisely the opposite. Shakespeare’s characters possess more life than the human fragments of our daily dealings, and Othello, e.g., has lent itself to a psychological analysis of jealousy with greater efficacy than a common clinical case.

This ideal truth, created by aesthetic vision, is analogous to what metaphysical vision can achieve.

Lastly, there is the religious solution. It inspires the vehement conviction that we call faith.<sup>208</sup> Its foundation is not, as is often claimed, supernatural revelation, but an emotional state that can reach ecstasy and gives rise to mystical vision. This is the religious phenomenon par excellence.

<sup>208</sup> *fe*

The accompanying myths, dogmas, and rituals are accessory elements and are sometimes artificial. These external forms can be suppressed without diminishing the intensity of religious feeling.

Religious experience, in all countries and at all times, always contains the same fact: the *coincidentia oppositorum*, the overcoming of the dualism of consciousness in the fullness of ecstasy, the mystical union in the identification of the individual with the eternal Being.

Intimate vision — whether it be intellectual, aesthetic, or mystical — is not the immediate intuition that gives us common evidence.<sup>209</sup> It is a complex phenomenon that, though suggesting profound convictions, can only give them a subjective value.

<sup>209</sup> *evidencia común*

[p. 65]

### §31

A century after the *Critique of Pure Reason*, it should no longer be necessary to demonstrate the impossibility of metaphysics as a science. The post-Kantians, for however bold and brilliant some may have been, have neither invalidated<sup>210</sup> nor surpassed the fundamental work of contemporary philosophy.

<sup>210</sup> *desmentido*

A century after the critique of reason, it should also no longer be necessary to demonstrate to skeptics and positivists that we cannot think or live without metaphysics.

The apparent antinomy is resolved in this simple truth: we must do metaphysics, but not as a science. Moreover: we must do it, investing in it all the sincerity of our convictions, without attributing to them a dogmatic value.

For both, it is necessary to arrive at a clear notion of our capacity of cognition,<sup>211</sup> so that we can emancipate ourselves from empirical realism, and so as to not fall naïvely into transcendent wanderings.<sup>212</sup>

<sup>211</sup> *capacidad cognoscitiva*

<sup>212</sup> *divagación trascendente*

For the worst metaphysics is done without suspecting it. This metaphysics abounds in the works of all who deny transcendent wanderings and yet, at every step, give us otherworldly news<sup>213</sup> or convert their poor concepts into a mythological hypostasis.

<sup>213</sup> *noticias pasmosas*

We must, instead, practice metaphysics conscientiously.<sup>214</sup> Our worldview would remain incomplete<sup>215</sup> if, in that zone where the certainty of our knowledge<sup>216</sup> ends, we did not adorn it with symbolic creations adequate to our knowledge<sup>217</sup> and will.

<sup>214</sup> *a sabiendas*

<sup>215</sup> *trunca*

<sup>216</sup> *conocimiento cierto*

<sup>217</sup> *saber*

We must, in spite of everything, do metaphysics. The instinctive shudder of the troglodyte before the hidden powers that they feel revolving around them — whether such powers be adverse or favorable — is transmuted into the vision of the sage when, in the fluctuation of phenomena, they glimpse the harmony of universal forces — the expression, perhaps, of a single and eternal cosmic energy.

[p. 66]

In opposition to the attack of foreign agents, the poet puts forward, with lyrical yearning, their own tribulations: their complaints, their rebellion, their joy. Then arises in the philosopher’s mind, with logical urgency, the profound problem that demands the paradoxical synthesis of the necessary fact and free act. And the consciousness of human suffering, in the mystical emotion of the apostle, evokes the certainty of the final redemption.
