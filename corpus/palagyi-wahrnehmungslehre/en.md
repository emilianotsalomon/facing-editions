## Einführendes Vorwort — Preface

On the 13th of July 1924 Melchior Palágyi died suddenly in Darmstadt, aged sixty-four, of the consequences of a stroke. Apart from a handful apiece of select specialists in the fields of logic, physics, and biology, there can hardly be many who know of him, still less who would be able to appreciate what mankind, and what German letters in particular, have lost in him. For in an age of slogan-drummers and heroes of publicity, the few great men are wont to pass by far off to one side, often known or merely surmised by personal friends alone. Admittedly it cannot be denied that the thinker Palágyi, although master of a mode of exposition capable of rendering matters transparent, contributed his own share toward remaining unknown to the "world," inasmuch as he treated predominantly technical problems and even began the exposition of the deep-reaching and native findings of his "Lectures on Natural Philosophy" almost regularly with a critical examination of some highly learned portion of intellectual history, the assimilation of which demands thoroughgoing collaboration on the reader's part. Yet how little the cause of his relative obscurity is thereby hit upon may be shown by the following example. What reader has to this day closed his ear to the uncommonly noisy clamor about the so-called principle of relativity? But how many of those who have not done so would be in a position to state what the matter is actually about? Unintelligibility of a subject thus appears at times to heighten its celebrity, for which even among primitive peoples instances are to be found! Now the upheaval in physics in the midst of which we stand (whether it signify dissolution or reform) has, however, this peculiar character, that at least one of its aspects, the "four-dimensional manifold," was founded by none other than Palágyi. Not Minkowski with his lecture of 1908 on the "union of space and time," which became famous, but Palágyi it was who, in his treatise "New Theory of Space and Time, Sketch of a Metageometry," published seven years earlier in 1901 and running to only forty-eight printed pages, first furnished the compelling proof that time is to be annexed to the spatial system of axes as a fourth axis, and this without robbing either time or space of their characters, polar to one another, proper to each. With this composition, classical in the strictest sense, the first stage is laid, or better the foundation wall, of that "System of World Mechanics" upon which Palágyi labored for some three decades and which, had it been completed, would incidentally and beyond question have meant the twilight of all relativistic fantasies. Fate willed it otherwise. At the researcher's death only one further storey of the edifice lay before us in book form: "The Theory of Relativity in Modern Physics" (a reproduction of the lecture of the same name delivered at the eighty-fifth Congress of Natural Scientists in Vienna), in which relativism is decisively opposed and in its stead a theory of correlation embracing the whole of physics is developed, culminating in the demonstration of the two poles of reality: matter and ether. But our example has already led us deep into Palágyi's researches and has likewise already let the ground of their outsider standing shimmer through. "He made no secret of his decided contempt for the powerful philosophical fashions of his time": these words, with which he commemorates a Bolzano in praise, hold in perhaps still higher degree of himself. There is a manner of protesting that fructifies, because against half-heartedness, addiction to doubt, and mimicry it calls life onto the field, thereby first bringing life itself to an unsuspected unfolding. Palágyi belongs to the species of the discovering combatants: out of a passionate love of truth a combatant against skepticism and relativism, out of the strength of an ancient racial root of life against rationalism and ideology. Each time it begins with an attack; it ends with a discovery! Thus his "Quarrel of the Psychologists and the Formalists," which appeared in 1902, unsparingly discloses the untenability and the dissembled nihilism of the presuppositions of the author of the "Logical Investigations," not without at once pursuing the descent of formalistic extravagances back through the centuries. Of the same campaign there is still more comprehensive testimony in the parallel work which came out at the same time, "Kant and Bolzano," which all in all serves the rediscovery of the unjustly forgotten adversary of Kant, notwithstanding that it lets both philosophers, so to speak, wear one another down upon their differences. But immediately upon the work of clearing away there follows the construction, with a "Logic at the Parting of the Ways" (1903), which, guided by the insight that to this day we do not know wherein the essence of judgment<sup>1</sup> consists, seeks to give the connection of the subject with the predicate a foundation in the science of life, and which in its second half represents, in our estimation, the most significant achievement in scientific logic since Aristotle. Fundamentally the same spectacle, namely that of creative feud, is offered us finally by his principal work mentioned above, which as a reproduction of twenty "Lectures on Natural Philosophy concerning the Fundamental Problems of Consciousness and of Life" was first published in 1908, was thereafter for years unobtainable, but has just now come out anew, almost unaltered and in the most solid presentation, from the press of Barth. And here it is the foundations of biology, psychology, and the science of knowledge that are demolished piece by piece, in order to make room for a system of judgments which alone would deserve the much-abused name of neo-vitalism. "Science" will require decades even to approximate an exhaustion of the essentially new findings which this epochal work conceals. We can only give the reader of the "Theory of Perception" the counsel to take cognizance also of the "Lectures on Natural Philosophy," since the two works interlock in such a fashion that each appears as the complement of the other. To the new edition of his "Lectures" the author himself yet added a preface that is, as it were, a biography of his soul; the "Theory of Perception," assembled from his literary remains, stands in need of such a preface for the purpose of knitting the connecting threads between it and the principal work. For this it is now important to know from the outset that the first seven investigations arose indeed after the "Logic," but before the principal work. Inasmuch as a mind so passionately inquiring, so restlessly clarifying, and never satisfied with itself as was Palágyi holds up against one another the largely new solutions of many problems which had first become visible to him alone, scrutinizing the lineaments for their fitness to be formed into a concordant whole, we cast an exceedingly gripping glance into the workshop of the thinker. But if it excites a kind of suspense to be present at the coming-to-be and self-development of essential findings of self-reflection, still the gain in knowledge as well as the intellectual enjoyment of him who, already somewhat familiar with the goal, can look back upon the path the discoverer takes, and thus sees before his eyes what obstacles had to be circumvented and what all too obliging byways had to be avoided in order to hold to the direction beheld with the soul, is heightened considerably. For this reason there shall be set forth here, at least in outline, the new findings which have accrued to the theory of perception out of Palágyi's collected work. I. Whoever has followed with sufficient attention the more recent development of the science of consciousness (commonly conceived too narrowly as "theory of knowledge") no longer doubts that the English so-called sensualism, and likewise its counterpart, continental ideologism, have alike run themselves into the ground. Every level-headed investigator of the soul knows today, or can at least know, that out of "contents of sensation"—drive the analysis however far one will—no object of perception coalesces. He will also render account: only the activity of the mind stimulated by processes of sensation—frequently called "apperception"—confers upon what is sensed the character of properties of things remaining identical with themselves. But were one to ask him wherein the activity of the mind is distinguished from the processes of sensation and what it accomplishes upon them, he would either be at a loss for an answer, or we should get to hear sham answers whose untenability would be proved by this alone, that they would annihilate one another. For now the mind is supposed to possess the marvelous faculty of "projecting" outward the experiences running their course within us, and in such wise evidently of bringing forth the external world in the first place; now it is supposed, with scarcely less expenditure of world-creating skill, to be able to transform the contents of experience into a state of affairs standing over against the bearer of life as though independent of him; now an original duality of "subjective" and "objective" experiences is assumed, of which naturally the objective ones must serve for the construction of the world of perception; now, still more bluntly, the sensualistic non-thought of two modes of perception is fetched forth again, an "inner" and an "outer," whereby the problem is simply enough conjured out of the air; now again mysterious "forms of intuition" are supposed to dwell within us which know how to transform, as though at a stroke of magic, the seemingly chaotic jumble of qualities and intensities of sensation into a lawfully ordered world of objects appearing spatially and temporally! We could continue for a long while yet, did we wish to enumerate all the principal varieties of "genetic" and "nativistic" theories, but we fear that in the process it might grow ever more confused in the reader's head and ever darker before his eyes. Nor is it today particularly difficult any longer to show the untenability of them all. If we are endowed with the power to externalize the inwardness of experience, why do we commonly omit to do so with our emotional agitations? Why, for instance, do we not now and then stumble against our expectations, apprehensions, and hopes just as against the supposedly outwardly transposed tactile contents which bear the names of trees or stones? And if our mind already has the faculty of objectification, we should like to learn how it contrives to distinguish external objects, e.g. things, from internal objects, e.g. concepts, and until we have been given a plain and clear answer to this, let no one boast of possessing a theory of perception! The famous doubling into "outer" and "inner" perceptions does indeed thrust the problem aside without the batting of an eyelash, but finds itself troubled by the embarrassing question why with the help of the "inner sense" we do not become aware of the thoughts of our fellow men just as sharply and distinctly as with the help of the outer senses we become aware, say, of the color of their hair. And as regards the celebrated "forms of intuition" of space and of time, we should have to inquire once again what induces us to clap both of them over the contents of sensation, whereas over our states of feeling (according to the conception at least of the Kantians in particular) only the form of intuition of time! But however much such false doctrines lie at feud even among themselves, they nevertheless agree one and all in the assumption that experiences are facts of consciousness ("perceptions," "representations," "ideas"), and therefore founder one and all in the undertaking of disclosing to us the conjuring trick by virtue of which consciousness sets forth out of itself a world of processes taking place merely within it. Precisely here, now, the as it were Copernican endeavors of more recent sages set in, who have grasped once and for all that consciousness is reflection upon what has been experienced and consequently is not itself experience, and that experiences run their course on this side of consciousness, in and for themselves lacking all reflection. This insight was at least broken ground for by the philosophers of Romanticism and in part already put to the test in the elucidation both of the instincts and of the "night-polar" states of consciousness such as presentiment, clairvoyance, dream life, somnambulism, "mesmerism," and so forth; it is forged by Nietzsche into a flashing weapon in the struggle, though not consistently carried through, against the value of consciousness; and it forms the point of departure of several contemporary thinkers (as for instance, for some quarter of a century now, of the investigations in the theory of expression and in the science of the soul undertaken by the writer of these lines), among them in the first rank Palágyi, whose most outstanding achievements in the service of the new set of problems concern the thoroughgoing reconstruction of the theory of perception in particular. We hold it a piece of good fortune without parallel that a metaphysician so highly gifted as Palágyi was at the same time one of the greatest mathematicians of the present age. For therein may lie grounded not only the unerring sureness of aim with which his thinking sets out to ascertain an exact mark of distinction between mind and life, but also the weightier circumstance that he actually finds it by means of the pair of concepts of discontinuity and continuity. Experience, so he teaches and proves, has the nature of a process and hence of continuity, whereas the mental act takes place instantaneously, that is, in the temporally unextended moment; and consciousness, however its closer characterization may turn out, consists under all circumstances in a succession of acts which are held together by the life of the individual being. Let it be observed: the distinction between experience and mental act was known. It remained, however, without effect and afforded no protection against the confounding of the distinguished states of affairs, so long as the difference between the two had not been uncovered in the opposition of untemporal instantaneity to temporal continuity. But as soon as this has happened, a well-nigh immeasurable prospect opens out, flanked by questions of which some, as it were within reach of the hand, bring their solutions along with them at once, others, already more remote, appear significant, and yet others allure into the indefinitely far. We touch only upon the most important with reference to their bearing upon the theory of perception. The periodic alternation of waking and sleeping, now a special form of the general discontinuity of consciousness, appears in the light of a periodic and extraordinary retardation of the beats of the mind's measure, or according to Palágyi of the "pulse frequency" of consciousness, whereas the heightened "concentration of attention" evidently corresponds to an acceleration of the pulse of consciousness. The fundamental problem of the countability of mental acts announces itself, and with it questions of the following kind light up: how many acts must at the least take place per second in the waking state, how many at the most can be crowded into a second; in what degree the pulse of consciousness is subject to fluctuations in the individual life, particularly at the change of the ages of life; whether there are typical disturbances of the pulse of consciousness, and much else of the sort. Far more important, however, are the consequences for the science of knowledge. Processes in general can theoretically be divided into boundlessly many parts. If, then, consciousness, because discontinuous, is never able to accompany a flowing experience,<sup>1</sup> it penetrates immediately into partial experiences also only in the measure of its pulse frequency and not beyond it. That well-known fusion of contents of impression which arises not through covering and mixture but out of the rapidity of succession, particularly beautifully demonstrable by means of the color wheel, which upon sufficiently swift rotation offers in place of the juxtaposition of, say, red and green a uniformly opaque whitish gray, finds its explanation in the lagging of the tempo of the acts of apprehension behind the tempo of the change of colors. If we call to mind also the well-known snapshots which show us running dogs, leaping horses, gymnasts in postures never seen, or indeed the "stretched-out" films (achieved by the taking of thirty to forty phases of movement within the second), then it comes before our eyes past all contradiction that with alterations in the tempo of the mental acts there would have to go complete alterations of the world of perception. In his "Lectures" Palágyi recalls an address delivered in 1860 by the celebrated biologist Karl Ernst von Baer on the theme "Which Conception of Living Nature is the Right One?", in which, on the basis of "moments of life" differing in frequency, far-reaching considerations are advanced concerning the differences in the world-consciousness of "minute-men," "month-men," and so forth. Considerations of the same kind, as we should like to add, are to be found, in part leaning upon Baer, in Sigwart, in his treatise, still worth reading today, "On the Nature of Our Consciousness of Spatial and Temporal Magnitudes." Without indeed having recognized the punctual character of the act, he discusses in a manner as entertaining as it is instructive what transformations the world-picture would undergo upon a sudden alteration of the faculty of discriminating small differences of time. If, for example, an act of reflection took place only about once a minute, then sun and moon would "rise into the heavens like balls of fire, the bright stars like rockets, and sink again; and with magical swiftness the earth in springtime would clothe itself in a green which would as swiftly change its hue. But a multitude of the movements now visible would vanish entirely from our perception; we could no longer discern the movement of an animal's legs, as little as we can now follow the vibration of a string; a long symphony would be an instantaneous roar, and a speech we could understand only if the syllables followed one another at intervals of minutes."<sup>1</sup> Were we, conversely, able to reflect some ten times more often in the second than at present, "then the impression of the swiftness of movements would be diminished by just so much; with unbearable slowness living beings would move, much that we now see in motion would seem to stand still like the hour hand of a clock, because through a long series of moments of time we could observe no perceptible advance. In solemn procession the raindrops and the hailstones would float down from the sky, deliberately the floods of a waterfall would sink and leave us time to count the drops it scatters. The vibrations of a string we should then be able to follow as we follow the to-and-fro of a clock's pendulum, and the whirring of an insect's wings would seem to occur more slowly than the rare beats with which a circling falcon holds himself hovering aloft." Were we even to imagine the mental tempo multiplied a thousandfold or a millionfold, then the qualities of impression would decompose, and a world of wholly new appearances would open up: tone would no longer be tone, color no longer color. When a Sigwart concludes his exposition with the sentence: "Thus our estimation of temporal magnitudes, and with it every representation of the velocity of movements and changes in the world, depends upon the velocity with which our consciousness passes from one moment to another," one has the feeling that he stands before a path-breaking discovery. Yet in all this he thinks only of the derivative opposition of subject and object and not remotely of the original and metaphysical opposition of consciousness and experience; and so his thoughtful reflections remain without consequence for the theory of perception. Rightly does Palágyi lay no weight upon fanciful embellishments of this kind; for they only too easily seduce one into ascribing to consciousness a sovereignty which it could never possess! But they accomplish what all exaggerations accomplish: they shake awake our attention for a state of affairs not yet, or not sufficiently, heeded. If, however, the wholly indubitable co-dependence of the perceptual image of the world upon the tempo of reflection derives from the fact that the mental acts, as it were, mark in each case strictly instantaneously the wave-crests of a flowing experience, then there is therewith definitively established<sup>1</sup> their fundamental difference from experience itself, and the devastating false doctrine that experiences are facts of consciousness has once and for all played out its game. One can arrive at the same result by other paths as well; here, however, we have to do with Palágyi's path and wish to orient ourselves at once somewhat more exactly concerning the place where we have taken our footing. Whoever still stands wholly or in part under the spell of that subjectivism of thought which took its beginning for Europe with the Eleatics and Plato cannot impress upon himself often enough the negative sense of the proposition of the difference of consciousness from life; wherefore indeed Palágyi, especially in the "Lectures," does not weary of attacking the confounding of the two with disparaging similes. He speaks of the "prison of consciousness," of the "Bastille of consciousness," the "cage of consciousness," the "cave of consciousness," and sums up his passionately emphasized aversion to subjectivistic thinking most succinctly in the following sentences: "Whoever speaks of occurrences, processes, appearances that run their course, flow away, or haunt 'within consciousness' will never be able to attain to a rational conception of consciousness. For there are no occurrences, no processes, no appearances within consciousness, because consciousness is no cage, no kennel, no dungeon, no container, no hollow space, and in general no site with respect to which there could be an outside and an inside... Let one hold fast in the first place to this, that our consciousness exists only insofar as it is active, and that consequently one must speak not of processes of consciousness but of acts of consciousness. If one accustoms oneself, when consciousness is spoken of, to think not of a cloud-cuckoo-land but in the first instance of nothing further than one's own mental actions, then it will be self-evident that these mental actions are not hollow spaces... It is further to be remarked that the psychologists of strict observance, who proceed from Berkeley, make of the individual cage of consciousness an infinite cage, which accordingly comprehends the whole universe within itself, so that nothing more comes to lie outside it. The other class of psychologists makes of the cage of consciousness a finite one, so that within it come to lie the psychic processes, outside it the physical processes. Which of the two conceptions is the more comical will probably never be decidable. Nevertheless the former deserves the higher esteem insofar as it is original and brings its fundamental error to frank expression with bold straightforwardness. The second conception, which apprehends consciousness in the shape of a finite kennel, is not merely comical, it is also base. It lacks originality, is only a feeble and dispirited copy of the former, contains within itself all the errors of the former as well, and merely replaces the exuberance of the former by that mental narrowness and inferiority which comes to the fore everywhere that a child of man is unable to distinguish figurative from non-figurative speech." (pp. 74–75.) But when Palágyi sets about illuminating experience, and in particular the experience of the senses, with the insight thus won, the first thing to fall victim to it is the dogma of the possible simplicity of sensation. As little as the quality of whitish gray upon the rotating top is anything simple, so little can any content of sensation be anything simple, since indeed every one of them may be thought of as having flowed together out of arbitrarily many partial contents. Let no one take offense when Palágyi accordingly beholds in the content of sensation as such a product of fusion, a sum, an integral out of infinitely many partial processes; for what matters is not the turns of phrase borrowed chiefly from mathematics, but the recognition that of the allegedly simple constituents or "elements" of the perceptual image of the world not a single one can be exhausted by judgments. And in fact this too forms the point of departure for the conception of reality which Palágyi puts into practice in his researches, as one may convince oneself from the "Lectures" (pp. 237 to 239), whereas the thought pointing in the opposite direction at the close of the first treatise of the "Theory of Perception" lets one suspect transcendental-philosophical backgrounds which in the perception-theoretical investigations of our thinker play, we should say fortunately, no role. Upon the ground of a doctrine of the intermittence of consciousness there now emerges at once with the greatest sharpness what had often been surmised and occasionally also attempted to be formulated, namely that acts of reflection can be linked without exception only with changes of the state of life. Already a Hobbes had declared: always to sense the same is to sense nothing at all. Palágyi, however, proves that in every case even two fundamentally distinguishable vital processes are required if a consciousness of what is experienced is to arise. For in relation to the punctuality of the act the vital process appears either as one just passing away or else as one just arriving. If we set the act in relation to the former, then, strictly taken, it would concern something already past; but if to the latter, then something, strictly taken, not yet present at all. How do we avoid the aporia? "We must distinguish in our sensing," he says in his Logic, "two opposed or antagonistic currents, of which the one lends our sensing the character of impression, the other on the contrary the character of memory. For in our sensing the tendency may prevail to let every temporal segment of the sensation drop at once for the sake of the segment just arriving, as though it had not been there: we storm forward with our sensing into the future, we are fettered by the impression, it sweeps us along with it and does not let us come to a judging reflection. The antagonistic tendency in our sensing consists in this, that every new segment of the sensation is warded off in order to preserve the past segments: it is the memory-tendency in our sensing, by which we free ourselves from the fetters of the impression in order to be able to come over into judging reflection. We cannot with mathematical simultaneity be subject both to impression and to memory: we come from impression to memory and from memory to impression. Our sensing reflection is a rocking to and fro between two antagonistic states. We can also express this by saying that our sensing reflection has a pulse or a breath, so that it can be represented by a wave-line whose wave-crests represent, say, the states of impression and whose wave-troughs the states of memory. One commonly regards as memories only those states in which after a considerable time we come back to a past fact. But if one considers that that faculty which one calls memory must always be present when one wishes to establish an impression, since otherwise the impression would in a trice be forgotten as well, then one sees at once that the transition from the impression to the judgment about it necessarily occurs on the basis of memory even when we pass the judgment in the face of the fact." (pp. 177–178.) It is these distinctions to which the third treatise, bearing the title "Awareness," attaches itself. But here we must come to the reader's aid with a supplementary elucidation, since otherwise, being unacquainted with the "Lectures," he would run the risk of falling into a misunderstanding. The term "awareness" does not, for the rest, occur in Palágyi. Instead he regularly distinguishes consciousness of things from self-consciousness and lets the former come about through "direct," the latter through "inverse" reflection; by which is to be understood the reference to an already accomplished act of reflection by the help of a second act of reflection. Nothing else is evidently meant by "awareness." For Palágyi proceeds from the question whether and how a consciousness of consciousness arises, and teaches that this occurs through double judgments, in accordance with which the primary act of reflection upon things becomes an object of thought for an act that is for that very reason of a higher stage. Just as the primary act is borne by a vital process, so of course a vital process is required for the reflexive ("contrary," "inverse") act. Thus far all is in the best order. But when it is subsequently set forth that a vital process comes to consciousness only by the help of another vital process, and that consequently all perception rests upon double experiences, the appearance might arise as though the primary or thing-directed reflection were afterward again denied its character of consciousness. In fact the further course of thought turns aside entirely from the problem of self-reflection (= awareness), in order instead to make us familiar with the necessity, just now developed by way of quotation, of double experiences for the perceptual judgment. Here we see the researcher at his work and must concede that a final clarification remained denied him. If now we bring in the "Logic" and the "Lectures," we believe we ought to remove the difficulty in the spirit of the author as follows. First it stands beyond doubt: reflection upon things is not self-reflection, and vice versa. If we refer with a second act to the primary act, then there results either a knowledge of our judgments and concepts, or a knowledge of the judging I, or finally a knowledge of the living personality; and each of these kinds of knowledge is thoroughly different from the perceptual judgment. Were we then to direct our gaze upon the double experiences which are supposed to make such pairs of acts possible, and were we to succeed in recognizing in them the features to which "reflection" really owes its origin, then these would have to be other features than those by whose help the perceptual judgment takes place. (Palágyi occupied himself with them in the last of his "Lectures" and demonstrated that self-reflection is rooted in symbolic, that is, sign-giving, experiences of movement.) Secondly, however, it likewise stands fast that the double experiences described above are indeed required for the possibility of perceiving. With regard to these, however, it is according to Palágyi in no way necessary to associate two acts with them once again; rather the perceptual judgment arises in this way, that by virtue of a phantasm of memory the mental act refers to the impression that has in fact just now already elapsed. If we are finally to venture a conjecture as to whence the confusing half-light of the treatise on awareness derives, we believe we find the cause in Palágyi's conviction that consciousness of things and self-consciousness belong together polarically, that self-reflection completes the process of taking cognizance, and that only in regard to it can one speak of knowledge proper or of the specifically human consciousness. If Palágyi's discovery of the punctuality of the mental act, and a general characterization of the vital process first made possible thereby, forms the one corner pillar of his theory of perception, the other is formed by his equally path-breaking finding of "virtual" or "imagined movement." To it the treatises IV–VII lead over, and of it an application leading not inessentially beyond the "Lectures" is now given by the "Theory of Fantasy," which arose after them and in which we have to behold the principal and central piece of the whole posthumous volume. What we should like further to communicate to the reader about this too, for the easing of his understanding, will therefore rest chiefly upon the treatise just named. Sensualistic as well as ideological theories of perception founder in no way merely upon the incarceration of sense experience within consciousness, but at least as much upon the impossibility of bringing into connection with one another contents of sensation presupposed as "disparate" and, according to the chosen mode of consideration, really disparate as well. Even one who had not yet lost faith in the explicability at least of the physical world by atomistic thinking will nevertheless have to concede that no hope any longer subsists of expecting from atomistic thinking the explanation of the living world, were it only in view of the simplest fact of perception. For if we attempt with the help of the concepts of the customary scholasticism of sensation to analyze the perception of, say, an apple lying before us on the table, we see ourselves constrained to the following most peculiar findings. The apple, when taken into the hand, gives us manifold contents of tactile sensation such as of a somewhat displaceable resistance, of smoothness interrupted by roughnesses, further of the slight pressure of its weight. Already of these it is not to be seen why we necessarily refer them to one single something, to the apple. But this same apple further gives us contents of visual sensation, reddish alongside yellowish tones, further once again smoothness (!), peculiar highlights, and so on. It gives us, upon approach to the nose, contents of smell, upon being bitten into contents of taste, finally contents of hearing, for example upon being torn apart. One could go on recounting for a long time, did one wish to describe all the kinds, degrees, and refinements of sense experiences that can be distinguished upon this one object, and one would in such wise find oneself confronted with an equally long series of contents, each of which is wholly incomparable with every other. How in all the world do we come, notwithstanding, not merely to believe them connected with one another, but even to see in their occasioning grounds mutually belonging and mutually interpenetrating properties of one and the same thing?! The gentlemen sensualists have from of old squandered much superfluous acumen upon the proof that substrata or underlying supports are not "perceivable" and consequently are fictions, but have remained our debtors for the answer to the question just raised. To be sure, we have been told of "associations," in German Vergesellschaftungen. Yet what we wish to learn is precisely how impressions of objective incomparability can associate themselves so intimately that we are no longer able to tear them apart. Why does the moon circle the earth? Through "association"! The answer would be exactly as ingenious as that of the sensualists. We become convinced, however, that this word borrowed from commercial life has contributed like nothing else to burying the problem. If by association is to be understood that there subsists in us an inclination to associate with one another images that often followed upon one another or often accompanied one another, then it would precisely not concern the state of affairs here discussed. The warmth of the flame is a property of the flame; but no one has yet held the lightning to be a property of the thunder because upon the sight of the lightning he presently expects to hear the thunder. Whoever fell in love with a pretty girl to the strains of a waltz will henceforth be unable to hear the waltz melody, or even so much as to think of it, without at once thinking also of the figure of the beloved; yet for all that it does not occur to him to hold waltz melody and girl's figure to be mutually belonging and mutually interpenetrating properties of a thing remaining identical with itself! Scholasticism is readily reproached with punning hairsplittings; but the whole of scholasticism never brought forth a coinage of words that could vie in hollowness and perniciousness with "associations"! Did our sense experience really consist of nothing but disparate, that is, mutually incomparable and mutually irreducible contents of sensation, then, despite our knowledge of the essential difference of consciousness, perception would remain a state of affairs not merely totally inexplicable but even unthinkable. For it would require of us that without mortar, cement, or clamps we build up intuitive images out of atoms of perception, and it would require of us beyond this that in the finished edifice the said atoms should occur not, say, as its constituent parts, but as merely distinguishable and in no way detachable aspects of it. How do I come to refer the sourish taste of the apple to that very same thing to which I refer the reddish-yellow coloring of the skin, indeed to let the cause of taste and the cause of color interpenetrate one another, where after all reddishness and sourness fall objectively wholly apart? How do I come to hold the seen smoothness to be one and the same with the smoothness felt by touch, notwithstanding that even the most ingenious art of analysis would be unable to discover anything that a visual quality had in common with a tactile quality? Should anyone, however, believe that something spatial attaches to the visual contents and likewise to the tactile contents, we beg him to consider that we should then find, on the basis of the visual contents, a visual space, on the basis of the tactile contents a tactile space, but could as little hold the two to be the same space as we hold the hardness of the stone to be the same as, for example, the gray color of the stone. Rather we should have to arrive at the most singular assumptions: for instance, that the tactile space surrounds us as a hollow body of the diameter of the span of our arms and is movable along with us; that the visual space reaches into arbitrary distances, but in return occasionally perishes, namely in complete darkness, and occasionally arises again, namely in sunlight, and further, according to the illumination, now extends itself well-nigh boundlessly, now shrinks together more and more, and so forth! But Kant's evasion of the forms of intuition dwelling within us would help us not at all either, because it fails promptly at every particular question. Why, for example, do we see differences of distance, although the more distant things are quite as much a "raw material of sensation," or a "bundle" of visual contents, as the near and the nearest? Why, on the other hand, do we hear nothing of spatial extension in tonal qualities, and so forth? Sketchy as our expositions had of necessity to turn out, we nevertheless hope to have convinced every still unprejudiced reader that by virtue of merely so-called contents of sensation the act of perception would be unperformable. But then there must be a second and quite differently constituted class of vital processes, through whose linkage with the processes of sensation that first arises upon which alone acts of perception could fittingly direct themselves, namely the intuitive image. But these are not, say, the feelings, long known as different from sensations, but rather they are what Palágyi calls processes of fantasy or processes of imagination. We ourselves have designated the function of the soul in question by the perhaps still less mistakable name of beholding or vision, and in our work "On the Essence of Consciousness" (Barth) have furnished the demonstration that in any sense experience whatever the component of beholding must be distinguished from the component of sensing, and that accordingly the sense experience offers two sides, that of sensation, which hitherto has alone been appreciated but, from unacquaintance with beholding, thoroughly misunderstood, and that of beholding, whose investigation is only just now taking its beginning. We have had the joy of finding that a thinker drawing so exclusively from himself as Palágyi assented to our choice of words and adopted it, to which the concluding treatise "Space and Time" bears witness, which was destined to form the prelude to further investigations. The words appearing there such as "tactile beholding" and "tactile vision" signify, however, exactly the same as the earlier preferred "tactile fantasy" and "imagination of movement," and let it be permitted us to add that our concept of vision means a more widely spanned state of affairs, in relation to which Palágyi's investigations are devoted to one side of it, and precisely to that side which we ourselves have merely touched upon and never penetratingly worked out. In his findings relating thereto, which we hold to be just as decisive as his discovery of the punctuality of the acts, we have therefore no share whatever. We may be brief, because everything that matters is set forth in the two last treatises in a manner so free of presuppositions, so comprehensive, and so clear-sighted that even without knowledge of Palágyi's other works one gains an almost gapless picture of the new insights and means of knowledge. If we saw above that out of mere contents of sensation no intuitive image is to be fabricated, the intervening remarks have already taught us that out of mere contents of sensation there is likewise not to be fabricated the most general property of all intuitive images, their spatial extendedness. "Because space in itself," it is said on p. 78, "is not able to awaken any sensations in us, it is also not sensation taken by itself through which its existence is disclosed to us. But although space is nothing sensible, it is nevertheless something perceivable: an apparent paradox in which the enigmatic nature of space attains its most concise expression." A few sentences following soon after bring the complete resolution of the "paradox." We adduce these too and take the liberty, as just now already, in the reader's interest of spacing out the most decisive words. "It is not the sensations that make a shape known to us; sensations are there only to excite our imagination, and it is only this imagination that lets us grasp the shape. If, for example, I cover with the palm of my hand the circular opening of a beaker, I do not perceive the circular form of the opening by means of the sensations which the rim of the glass awakens, but these sensations must first excite my imagination and prompt it to an imagined movement round about the rim of the glass, in order that I may be able to grasp its circular form. Sensations are indeed an indispensable condition for the perception of any really existing shapes of things, but it is not the sensations themselves, but the virtual movements awakened by them, that disclose to us the shapes. One stands here before the wonderful fact that the real spatial order of things is made known to us through fantasy, so that without it, and namely without virtual movements, we should not have the slightest inkling of the real arrangement, disposition, and configuration of the actually subsisting world of appearance." Should a reader who gets this book of our thinker into his hands for the first time, in the face of such sentences, hold the principal thought laid down in them to be something already more or less known to him, or on the contrary to be something very hard to comprehend, then we advise him to read the treatise on fantasy twice, five times, ten times, in short as often as it takes for such feelings to disappear entirely. For the rest, the reader with the feeling of familiarity is worse placed than the one with the feeling of resistance; for he would first have to free himself from certain reverberations which presumably haunt his head from so-called sensations of movement or even from the "kinaesthetic sensations," before he could so much as make the attempt to understand virtual movement. Perhaps the following propositions will support him in this, the grounds for which he will find for the most part already in this book and entirely in the "Lectures."

## I. Empfindung & Wirklichkeit — I. Sensation & Reality

1. If we survey the developmental history of the theory of knowledge as it were from a bird's-eye view, we find that the investigation of the human understanding diverges in two antagonistic directions. For one can grasp the understanding there where it is not yet in activity but is only being stimulated to such: namely at the impressions or sensations of the senses; but one can also confront it there where it presents its opining or knowing in sensibly graspable signs, namely in linguistic or also mimic symbols. It is now interesting to see that the ancient Hellenic theory of knowledge, at least as it was developed by its immortal chief representatives Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, shows a predominantly symbolistic character, that is, thrusts the investigation of our life of sensation rather into the background and with all the greater zeal fixes its eye upon the mental content of our linguistic symbols, and strives to create a doctrine of concepts or of ideas; whereas the modern age indeed seeks to take over and further develop the conceptual logic of the ancients, yet also stands over against it defensively, indeed inimically, and would base the investigation of our understanding chiefly upon the exploration of our life of sensation. While then the ancients built up a symbolic logic, the moderns aim rather at an impressionistic theory of knowledge. It lies in the plan of this work to present the system of my own theory of knowledge upon the historical background of the hitherto development of this central science, not merely in order to be able, through the historically critical illumination, to work out my own trains of thought as sharply and plastically as possible, but conversely also in order, by the light of my own epistemological research, to set the whole history of the theory of knowledge, at least according to its main lines, if possible in a new illumination. From a purely theoretical point of view it would be most expedient to begin at once with a more exact consideration of the symbolic logic of the ancients, but regard for the intellectual disposition of the modern reader commands that the consideration of the modern impressionistic theory of knowledge be placed in the foreground. For the mind of the modern reader is impregnated to such a degree with theories of impression, sensation, or feeling, that it is almost impossible to come to an understanding with him otherwise than by making the investigation of our life of sensation the point of departure of all epistemological consideration. Whence this ardent longing for a theory of sensation? It hangs together in the most intimate manner with the fundamental trait of character of our modern culture. This modern culture is distinguished from all its predecessors chiefly by this, that it shows itself filled with an unbounded greed for dominion over the forces of nature. The whole earth is if possible to be kneaded into such a shape that it becomes a single, well-appointed, supremely fashionable manorial seat of the human race, and that all terrestrial forces of nature may represent a well-drilled body of valets and chamberlains of that same human race. It is this exuberant intellectual lust for power that must in the first place be designated "modern," and by which the culture of the last five centuries distinguishes itself in the most impressive manner from all preceding cultures. To be sure, every human culture consisted at all times first of all in a certain superiority over the surrounding nature, but no culture was yet so wholly and entirely filled with this urge toward superiority over nature as is ours, and none had made bold to make the highest powers of nature, the all-flooding forces of light, of warmth, and of electricity, subject to its purposes as though they were domestic animals. Of the great mechanician of antiquity, of Archimedes, we are told that he was ashamed of his magnificent machines with which he defended Syracuse, because he held every practical exploitation of science to be a debasement of it. How differently did the apostle of the modern philosophy of experience, Francis Bacon, think about this matter! He was indeed no Archimedes, nor was the building of machines his strong side—if only on account of the mathematical insights which are requisite for it—but in return he felt himself wholly free of the false shame of the great Archimedes (as of Greek theoretical philosophy in general). To the classical Greek mind knowledge was pure end in itself, the highest good of the spirit, blessedness of blessednesses. To a child of the modern age, to a Bacon, on the contrary, knowledge is "power." Namely a means to power over nature. Bacon remained, even in the face of the human urge to knowledge, the politician that he was according to his whole being. With penetrating political sagacity he recognized that the modern human mind was seized by a novel lust for power to subjugate the forces of nature, and that not only his own age but also the coming centuries would treat knowledge as a means to the realization of that lust for power. Thus Bacon personifies, as it were, the highest lust for power of modern humanity: the lust to make the forces of nature subject to oneself through knowledge of them. We are by now already so filled with this lust that we wax enthusiastic in Baconian fashion over the overcoming of the powers of nature, even when we have not taken the trouble to become acquainted with the Baconian spirit from his own works. In short, Bacon is in our blood, and we are for that very reason from the outset inclined to equate knowledge with experience (observation and experiment). But since experience can be acquired only by means of the sensations of the senses, we find nothing more comprehensible than that the science of human knowledge (or of experience) must set out with a theory of the sensations of the senses. The Baconian mode of thought presses irresistibly toward an impressionistic theory of knowledge, and history in fact shows that English philosophy under Bacon's influence strove in the first rank to develop an impressionistic theory of knowledge. This impressionistic (sensualistic, psychologistic) theory of knowledge presents itself historically in the three principal works of Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, all of which make the human understanding the object of their investigations, and hang together so intimately that they seem to be spinning out but a single thought; namely the fundamental thought that we must regard the sensations of the senses as the essential elementary constituents of our knowledge. Although these three modern investigations concerning the human understanding show a very diverse individual stamp and also arrive at very diverse final results, they nevertheless agree in the guiding chief tendency, that of resolving all knowledge if possible into sensible elements, to such a degree that they may be regarded as variations upon the same leading motif. The reader does well to study these three principal works of the modern sensualistic theory of knowledge in their interconnection, for he thereby procures himself the enjoyment of seeing thoughts which the one philosopher throws out thought through to their end by his successors. What the amiable and upright Locke still knows how to veil and to bring into accord with common human understanding comes forth in Berkeley and Hume with inexorable clarity, indeed in shrill dissonances, and one learns to recognize the whole range of that revolutionary modern striving which presses with all its might toward the destruction of every distinction between the deed of knowledge and the function of sense.

2. The impressionistic revolution that lies concealed in Locke's Essay comes to wholly unveiled expression in this, that he designates our sensations of the senses by the name "ideas" and in such wise lets processes of sensation appear in the light of mental acts, of activities of the soul. We are already so accustomed to this intermingling of sense function and mental deed that the revolutionary innovation contained in Locke's rebaptism of sensations into ideas no longer strikes us at all, indeed already affects us as something self-evident. This hangs together with the fact that we have translated Locke's "ideas" by "Vorstellungen" (representations), that is, by an expression which does not point so decidedly to a mental deed as do Locke's ideas. A "representation" may still after all pass for a product of the functions of sense, but on the other hand it can also be made equivalent to the expression "concept," inasmuch as one lets "concepts" pass as "abstract representations." Thus the word "representation" is quite particularly suited to veiling that impressionistic revolution which would conjure out of the world the distinction between sensations and mental actions (deeds of the understanding). Modern philosophy has created no second term so characteristic as the expression "representation," beneath whose ample cloak concepts and sensations of the senses are alike able to conceal themselves, and which possesses downright incomparable fitness for deceiving us about the distinction between sense function and activity of knowledge. Precisely because the term "representation" is able to render such excellent services, we see that impressionism everywhere passes at once from sensations to "representations," in order at least in appearance to give itself a more mental character. In doing so, however, impressionism holds fast behind our backs to the conception that representations are nothing further than a composition of sensations, and so the modern "philosophy of representations" dissolves gently into a pure "philosophy of sensations." If need be, however, a philosophy of representations can quite gently assume the look of a "philosophy of concepts," because, as has been said, the term "representation" is able to signify exactly the same as what we are otherwise wont to designate by the expression "concept." Thus the expression "representation" occupies a happy middle position between "sensation" and "concept" and can consequently be played over as easily into sensation as into concept. Whoever knows how to handle the term "representation" adroitly can in the one moment appear as a philosopher of sensation (impressionist, sensualist), in the other as a philosopher of concepts (rationalist, symbolist), and can set himself up as a mediator between two opposed camps of philosophy. One therefore does well to meet every pronounced "mediator" between two opposed philosophical tendencies with some suspicion, for it is a question whether his art of mediation does not consist in that trick by virtue of which he lets a middle expression such as, for example, "representation" be played to and fro, now in the one extreme sense (as sensation), now in the other extreme sense (as concept). We shall later have to occupy ourselves still more penetratingly with the famous term representation; now, however, we return to the overturn which Locke prepared in more recent philosophy, inasmuch as he strove to set up sensations as ideas and, what is the chief thing, as simple ideas. Locke means hereby to assert nothing less than that our sensations are our simplest thoughts, and that out of these simple fundamental thoughts there arise through mere composition those higher or more complicated thoughts in general which we possess as composite ideas of the external world. What may have misled Locke to this conception is easily grasped: sensations seem to us to be the best known of everything known in this world, and so we hold nothing more superfluous than to make yet clearer with words the sense of such expressions as hard, soft, warm, cold, red, green, and so forth (cf. Essay, Book II, Chap. 4, § 6). Accordingly our sensations would be our simplest thoughts, because incapable of further decomposition, so that all our remaining thoughts about the external world would have to be conceived as a composition out of these elementary thoughts or sensations. This conception of the elementary ideational character of our sensations became a fundamental dogma of modern psychology, which comes to expression in the fact that sensations are treated in modern psychological works as "elementary psychic functions." As the father of this conception we may regard Locke, who is moreover rightly set forth by all historians of philosophy as the father of modern psychology in general. (Cf. Windelband, History of Modern Philosophy, Vol. I, pp. 241–245.) Over against the Lockean conception it will be our task to show that our sensations of the senses have no ideational, thought-like character whatever. But in order to be able to prove this, we must first furnish the demonstration that sensations are by no means of a simple nature, but must be regarded as the most composite thing in this composite world. Every sensation is a happening which as such flows away in time and takes up a certain duration. If we consider this, then it is at once evident that every sensation must be thought of as consisting of temporal segments or phases whose number goes into the unbounded. To be sure, in reality we are not able to keep apart the smallest temporal segments out of which every sensation is composed, but it is for that very reason of the greatest importance to become clearly conscious of this incapacity of our power of perception. To this end it is desirable first to think of series of sensations occasioned by moving living beings, say by a fluttering butterfly, by a running horse, or a leaping man. We are never able to follow with the eyes all the phases of any movement, so that, for example, instantaneous photographs of a leaping man contain for us something alien and unknown, which comes to expression most tellingly in the fact that no painter would dare to represent the leaping man to us in such postures as we see in instantaneous photographs. For our natural seeing the individual phases of a movement flow together in such a manner that the individual very small temporal segments of it must necessarily remain wholly hidden. It is one of the facts of our life of sensation most significant in principle that the happening within very small segments of time remains hidden from us. I should like here in passing to point to the well-known experiments with the color top. Were our power of perception able to penetrate into the processes of the thousandth of a second, then the various colored sectors of the top would be unable to fuse with one another even upon very rapid rotation. Were it even granted us to follow the happening of nature down into arbitrarily small temporal segments, then such fusions of sensation as are called forth by the rotating color top could not exist for us at all. It is therefore necessary to bring home to oneself once in the most thoroughgoing manner that everything which happens before our eyes, however much our attention may occupy itself with it, remains for our perception wholly hidden within small and smallest temporal segments. Not only moving but also so-called resting appearances conceal from us the smallest temporal phases of their movement or rest. If we look, for example, for the space of a second at a resting red patch of color upon the wall, it commonly does not come to our consciousness at all that this second comprehends within itself an immeasurable happening, namely a continual being-produced of the red sensation of color, and one must have recourse to artificial means in order to convince oneself of the presence of a continuous happening during the said second. One must say to oneself that during a second one could after all close and reopen one's eyes several times and thus obtain from the resting red patch of color not merely a single but also a twofold or threefold sensation. Hence a whole second is not required in order to get a sensation of the patch of color; one has such a sensation already during the half or the third of a second. Nevertheless one notices nothing of the twofold or threefold repetition of the sensation during the second, and it seems to us that at the end of the second the very same sensation of red would still be present which was present at the beginning of the second. The sensation is, so to speak, frozen, and we obtain the peculiar illusion of the existence of a standing sensation. Now it is, however, of the highest importance for our conception of the world to overcome this illusion and to say to oneself that every process of sensation is a continuous flowing happening, that therefore even the perception of a resting red patch of color is possible only because the sensation of the red patch of color is continually produced anew in the course of the second. Peculiarly, however, we notice nothing of this continuous being-produced-anew of the sensation, because we are in general unable by means of our natural power of perception to penetrate into the smaller and smallest temporal segments of our sensation. If, then, it is permitted to speak of several waves of sensation which in the course of a second proceed from a resting red patch of color, we must say to ourselves that the succession of these waves is as good as lost to us. I have already pointed to this fundamental fact of our life of sensation in my "New Theory of Space and Time" (p. 26) and there made use of the metaphorical expression that the one phase of the sensation "covers over" the other. In my "Logic at the Parting of the Ways" (pp. 391–395) I express the state of affairs by saying that the sensation of the resting patch of color lacks the temporal pulse, or also that we are incapable of keeping the individual waves of sensation of the red temporally apart, of temporizing them. "Ten waves of sensation of a red patch of color give us just as much only one sensation of a resting patch of color as do a hundred waves of sensation following upon one another. All the trouble taken by the waves of sensation to make manifest their temporal succession is wholly lost; we remain blind toward this temporal succession." By all this I mean only to have said that there are no standing-still sensations, because all sensing is a continuously flowing happening. When I examine the famous sentence of the obscure Heraclitus: "Not twice can we step down into the same river; for new and ever new waters stream into it," I am able to grasp as its proper content of truth nothing other than the restlessly unstoppable flux of our sensations. And once we conceive sensation as a continuously flowing happening in time, then it is all over with the Lockean fundamental dogma of modern psychology, as though sensation could be something simple. As surely as a second must be thought decomposable into arbitrarily many temporal parts, so surely must we also think every sensing which takes up any second or any other duration of time as composed of arbitrarily many temporal segments.

3. In modern psychology the concept of the fusion of sensations has since Herbart played a significant role: to be sure, still never one such as really befits this concept. For the sensations themselves, which in present-day psychology pass for something simple, are nothing further than fusions. It seems that a whole series of modern investigators is approaching this conception; quite particularly do I find in one of the most original psychologists of our day, H. Cornelius, significant beginnings toward an overcoming of the Lockean dogma of simplicity (H. Cornelius, Psychology as an Empirical Science, pp. 117 to 141); but one still hesitates to take the last decisive step in this direction, because precisely this step would have to lead to the overthrow of the impressionistic theory of knowledge and to the construction of a new philosophical conception of the world. One is still wont to hold fast to the view that in the case of sensations, in order to think them in all purity, one must abstract from their temporal duration, which then indeed has the consequence that such sensations robbed of their temporal duration appear as something simple. But as little as it makes sense to speak of an extensionless cube, so little can there be talk of durationless sensations. A concrete sensation necessarily has a temporal duration, however short this duration may be, and there can never anywhere take place a sensation whose temporal duration is equal to zero, which would therefore be restricted to a mathematical point of time. It belongs to the essence of sensation to flow temporally, and one annihilates the concept of sensation if one abstracts from this temporal flux, just as much as if in the case of a movement one wished to abstract from its flowing in time. If this now stands fast, then it is manifest that every sensation must be thought of as composed out of arbitrarily many temporal segments, and that in consequence of this compositeness every sensation must conceal within itself a boundless mystery. Peculiarly, everyone says to himself that small and smallest areas of surface of our sensation always contain something unattainable for our armed gaze: but that not only small and smallest areas of surface but also small and smallest intervals of time of our sensing conceal within themselves immeasurable riddles seems to be a thought very little current. In order now to make friends with this significant thought, it is expedient to conceive the small intervals of time of sensing by analogy with the small areas of surface of the same. The more minute a surface is over which we sweep with our gaze, the harder will it be for us to perceive any qualitative differences in it, whereas the microscope conjures up a world of the most enormous manifoldness where our unarmed gaze was able to discover only uniformity. Now just as that which borders extremely closely upon one another in space blurs for our eye, so also will the exceedingly small successive temporal segments of a happening flow together for our perception. But whereas in the microscope we possess an astonishing instrument for penetrating into the neighboring small areas of surface which blur for the unarmed eye, there is nowhere any such optical instrument which would permit us artificially to keep apart and to distinguish the mysterious happening which flows together in the millionth parts of the second. Indeed, we may confess to ourselves that we are usually not accustomed to think at all of the exceedingly small temporal segments out of which every sensation, however brief in duration, is composed. This hangs together with the fact that our concept of time is in general far less developed than our concept of space. While, for example, every man in any way educated and versed in geometry does not confuse an empirical point of space, such as we draw with the tip of a pencil upon paper, with the so-called mathematical point of space, to which we ascribe no extension whatever, there will surely be found even among trained and ingenious men such as it has not yet so much as occurred to to think of the distinction between the empirical and the mathematical Now. By the empirical Now we always understand a small interval of time, thus say a second, half a second, which may serve as a bridge between past and future; by the mathematical Now, on the contrary, is meant the absolutely sharp, entirely durationless boundary between past and future. If anyone does not form the concept of the mathematical point of time, then his thinking (at least with regard to time and to temporal happening) remains standing at a stage as primitive as the spatial intuition of a man who had no inkling of Euclidean geometry. There are thinkers who are unable to come to terms even with the concept of an extensionless point of space, for they assert that such a point of space cannot be thought at all in consequence of its unintuitability. Over against this refractory mode of thinking I will remark only so much, that we are unable to think of a quite determinate spatial distance otherwise than by delimiting it by means of mathematical points, since we should otherwise have to think the boundary as blurred and could consequently never speak in geometry of a quite determinate spatial distance. But exactly so does it stand with temporal distances, and if there is to be any talk of a wholly fixed temporal distance not confusable with any other, then we must think every fixed duration of time as fenced in by mathematical initial and terminal points. Our sensations are never restricted to the mathematical (durationless) Now, but always have a certain, even if only brief, temporal duration, that is, they fill out some interval of time which we can think of as decomposed in thought into arbitrarily many smaller and ever smaller intervals. What now goes on in such exceedingly small intervals remains just as unknown to us as, without the help of the microscope, we let ourselves dream nothing of what may be present in exceedingly small areas of surface. As the world within the thousandth of a millimeter, the micron, is unknown to us, so also is the happening within the thousandth of a second, the microsecond, a mystery to us. And as biology strives to explore the structure of organisms within the micron, so physics in general sets out to gain an insight into the thousandth and millionth of a second of the happening of nature. A natural science that were not filled with this tendency would be no natural science, for it would have nothing truly new to tell us. The whole of modern natural science with all its astonishing discoveries remains unintelligible to him who does not make clear to himself that we do indeed always become acquainted through our sensations with some happening, but that this happening withdraws in its smallest temporal segments from our immediate cognizance. Thus, for example, false notions had for a long time to prevail concerning the free fall of heavy bodies, for this reason alone, that it is not granted to us men to penetrate with our natural faculty of perception into such small segments of time as the falling of a body takes up. But supposing that it were to last an hour before a dropped piece of gold reached the ground: would even then a Galileo have been needed to discover the laws of the motion of fall? Could not then anyone have found that the falling piece of gold had by the end of the second quarter-hour traversed a path four times as great as by the end of the first quarter-hour, and that in general paths increase in the ratio of the squares of the times? But since our faculty of perception refuses its service with regard to small times (just as with regard to small spaces), the human race had to await the appearance of such a genius as Galileo was, before it could attain to any in some measure adequate concept of the motion of fall. In order, then, to comprehend the progress which we are able to make in the knowledge of nature, we must say to ourselves that every happening must be mysterious for us for this reason alone, that it consists of smallest temporal segments which we are unable to perceive. Sensations are therefore by no means so wholly known or given as they have been held to be since Locke and Kant. To be sure, we are thoroughly familiar with our own sensations, and one may well say of them that they are given to us, but with this being-given there is a quite peculiar state of affairs. Sensations are given to us as only some riddle can be given to us. The smallest temporal segments out of which they compose themselves are unknown to us, so that—to speak with an arithmetical simile—we have before us in every sensation a summation whose result is indeed known to us, but whose individual summands remain hidden. All sensation is only summary sensation (total sensation). Only this conception makes it comprehensible to us how there can be a natural science which explores happenings more exactly than they become known to us through our natural perception of the senses. Were it as impressionism supposes, namely that our sensations are wholly known to us, then every striving to explore natural happenings more exactly than is possible for us through mere perception would be nonsensical. Were the sensations of a tone or of a color known to us in all their smallest temporal segments, what in heaven's name should we still have to fathom in them? Impressionism thus leads to a conception of the world in which everything that is given through the senses can contain nothing whatever unknown. But if there is nothing unknown in what we sense, then it is all up with every natural science, for this wishes precisely to give us information about that which in all our sensations remains unknown despite their being sensed. The impressionistic theory of knowledge originally wishes to render the natural sciences the greatest possible services of love. It presses all knowledge toward experience, all experience toward sensation, and supposes thereby to have obliged the investigator of nature God knows how greatly. But inasmuch as it sets up sensation as the simple thing or as the element of our world-picture, it lets the world-picture of each moment consist of elements wholly known to us, of sensations, and thereby denies the unknown which is contained in the world-picture of each moment despite its being sensed. It denies precisely that unknown which it is the investigator of nature's most ardent longing to explore. It makes the world-picture of each moment, as it is given through our senses, into a problemless world-picture, and thereby manifests the dangerous overzealousness with which it is filled toward natural science. Like an over-tender mother who out of sheer solicitude deprives her darling son of light and air, so impressionism robs natural science of its element of life, of the unknown, of the great X, without which it must perish of problemlessness. If the sensible world-picture of each moment contains nothing unknown, then this means that we must be wholly content with the world-picture of each moment. We have nothing further to ask about it, we are silenced. One sees: impressionism makes us far happier than we wish to be. The blessedness of problemlessness drives us to despair. We wish for an unknown, in order to be able to search after something, and if the unknown is stolen from us, then the thought is awakened in us that we are in general able to explore nothing, to know nothing. Impressionism would make us blessed through the complete knownness of what we see and grasp with our hands, that is, of what we sense; but meanwhile it presses us toward the desperate thought that for us in this so well-known world of sensation there is nothing to seek, to explore, and to know. Thus impressionism drives toward two opposed extremes: toward the blessed problemlessness of everything sensed, and toward complete despair of our faculty of knowledge in general. The two extremes come historically to expression through the impressionistic doctrines of Berkeley and Hume. The former makes impressionism into a doctrine of blessedness, of "real and perfect knowledge"; the latter, on the contrary, teaches us more than any other modern thinker to doubt our faculty of knowledge altogether. If a natural science is to be possible, then we must give up the doctrine of the simplicity of sensation. For if sensation is something simple, then nothing further can be asked about it. But if we regard every sensation as something boundlessly composite, then every sensation becomes for us, despite its knownness, a boundless problem. We learn to comprehend that an investigator of nature seeks to penetrate into the millionth and billionth particles of a second; for he is by no means content with what he grasps with his natural faculty of perception, but seeks to penetrate into ever smaller segments of time of our sensing, in order to raise us as far as possible above the inadequacies of our faculty of perception. In short, it is not the dogma of the simplicity of sensation, but the doctrine of its boundless compositeness, that corresponds to the spirit of the natural sciences and of philosophy in general. When we seek to penetrate into ever smaller segments of time of our sensation, we are really seized by the question what after all may take place in the durationless mathematical point of time. This is the problem of actuality or reality. Actual or real we call in ordinary life that which is just now taking place, whereby, however, we are not wont to distinguish the empirical Now from the mathematical Now. From this springs that universally known aberration in consequence of which the naive man readily confuses the actual with his sensation. Actual is always only that which takes place in the mathematical Now; this actual can, however, never be immediately grasped by man, because our faculty of perception is never able to penetrate into the durationless Now. Instead of the durationless Now we grasp through our perception of the senses always only an empirical Now, that is, some fraction of a second in which our sensation runs its course. Through temporally flowing sensations we grasp that which really takes place in durationless mathematical instants. Instantaneous actuality or reality dissolves for us into temporal sensations. We refer our temporal sensations to a nontemporal actuality, the sensible world-picture to a nonsensible reality. In this proposition the fundamental metaphysical character of the human mind comes to expression.

## II. Empfindung & Bewußtsein — II. Sensation & Consciousness

1. In the foregoing considerations we have arrived at a new conception of our sensations, according to which they must by no means be regarded as something simple, but as something boundlessly composite. Out of this insight there will also result for us with inescapable necessity a new conception of human consciousness in general and in particular of our perceiving consciousness. For if it really stands thus—and doubt of this is surely excluded—that the smallest temporal segments of a sensation, which elapse in the millionth and billionth and so forth parts of a second, remain unknown to us, then it follows from this that our perceiving consciousness does not keep step with the flux of sensation, or in other words: that our perception does not flow along with the sensation. For if our perception kept perfectly equal step with our sensation, that is, flowed along with it as an inseparable parallel stream, then not even the smallest temporal segment of a sensation could remain hidden from us, and we should have to possess the most exact knowledge of what takes place in the millionth, billionth, and so forth particles of the process of sensation. Equipped with such a flowing power of perception, we could look down smiling upon all the achievements of instantaneous photography, for we should perceive every phase of movement which it presents as decomposed into billions of smaller phases, by virtue of that natural faculty of perception innate in us which would be able restlessly to fasten itself to the heels of sensation. No happening upon which we had once fixed our eye could still conceal anything from our infinitely sharp perceiving gaze, for we should without any difficulty be able to penetrate into the trillionth particles of a second; indeed even that mathematical Now which forms the absolutely sharp boundary between past and present would have to disclose to us its last secret: that is, we should have to be able to perceive naked actuality itself, without any mediation through any sensation. That we are in reality not able to accomplish such a thing, indeed that even our fantasy is incapable of somehow depicting such an infinitely fine power of perception, hardly needs to be discussed. But it is no superfluous exertion if someone seeks with all his might to fire his fantasy in order to gain some inkling, however pale, of what may take place in the timeless Now; for the complete shattering of such an attempt of fantasy instructs him best that our human consciousness and in particular our perceiving consciousness is of a restricted nature. We express this restrictedness of our faculty of perception perhaps best by saying of it: it is unable continuously to accompany sensation in its continuous flux. One can give this thought yet another formulation. The limitedness of our perceiving consciousness consists namely in this, that we are incapable of reflecting upon our process of sensation in the course of a second as often as we please. For could we in the course of a second reflect a million times upon what is sensed, then precisely thereby the sensation would be decomposed into a million temporal segments, and precisely that would be accomplished which in consequence of the restrictedness of our consciousness we are unable to accomplish. To us men a second seems to be a short time. But no level-headed thinker gives himself over to the delusion that a second is already in itself something of short duration. It is short to us because during its course we can perform very few acts of reflection, in particular acts of perception. Could a man during a second reflect a thousand times upon his sensation, then his measure of time would be another than ours, for he would then operate with thousandths of a second where we content ourselves with taking seconds into the reckoning. What we call a second would for such a being be a considerable time, because in the course of a second it would be able to transact some hundreds of times more affairs than we. Length and shortness of time thus depend upon the nature of our consciousness, for an interval which now seems to us very short would at once acquire a millionfold significance or extension if during it we could perform a million times more acts of reflection than in the present constitution of our consciousness. Now, however, it lies in no man's power to overcome that appearance according to which, say, a second or even a tenth of a second is a very short time, and there comes to expression in this merely the fact that a man is able to reflect during a second or a tenth of a second only very few times. We perhaps express this state of affairs still more clearly if we say that human reflection and in particular perceiving reflection has an intermittent character. For while sensation flows on without interruption, the human mind rouses itself only by jerks, from interval to interval, to a new act of perception. To be sure, we are caught in the illusion that our perception is a wholly uninterrupted or continuous one, but were this really so, then we should need no research into nature any longer, because every happening of nature would have to pour out its last secret before our perceiving gaze. In such wise we recognize the most fundamental illusion in which the human mind is caught concerning its own nature: we all suppose, namely, that our perceiving consciousness is a continuously flowing one, engaged in an uninterrupted act—at least so long as we find ourselves in the waking state: this, however, is mere appearance, for we are able only by jerks to carry out one act of perception at a time, quite as we are able to move ourselves forward only step by step and not to roll on of our own activity in uninterrupted continuity. As we are not in a position to execute a thousand running steps in a second, so it is also not granted us to accomplish a thousand acts of perception in the second. With this conception we take the first positive step upon the thorny path of self-knowledge. We speak much of the barriers of human knowledge, but it always remains fairly nebulous what is meant by such barriers. I believe that the restrictedness of human nature can for a first beginning be characterized by nothing better than by the limited velocity of our bodily locomotion and by the analogous limited velocity of our reflection, in particular of our perceiving reflection. That even the most developed sport of running is unable to bring it beyond a definite measure of velocity need not be expounded here; but we must all the more insistently point to the analogous limitation of our perceiving reflection. There is a limit to the velocity of our perceiving both upward and downward which no human being is able to overstep. It is indeed merely a metaphorical expression when we speak of the "velocity of our perceiving reflection," but it lets itself easily be translated into unmetaphorical speech, for by this metaphor we mean nothing other than that for the number of our acts of perception in the course of a finite time, thus for example of a minute or of a second, there is both a lower and an upper limit. However indifferently we may comport ourselves during the waking state, there must nevertheless come about in the minute or second a certain number of acts of perception, since otherwise we should have to fall asleep or go toward some catastrophe of our consciousness. On the other hand, however, we are unable, even with the greatest exertion of our attention, to exceed a certain number of acts of perception during the second. Hereby there falls at once also a new side light upon the difficult theory of attention. Our attention is namely the more concentrated the more frequently our acts of perception follow upon one another, but it becomes the looser the more thinly these acts are sown. We have up to a certain degree power over our attention, that is, we are able by means of our will to regulate up to a certain degree the density of our acts of perception, but we very soon reach that outermost density of our acts of perception which we are unable to exceed. If we are to express quite palpably that our consciousness is able during the second to accomplish only a determinate number of acts, we do best to speak of a pulse of consciousness and in particular of a pulse of perception. Just as the pulse-beats of an artery occur at determinate intervals of time and in a determinate rhythm, so there belongs also to the acts of our consciousness in general and in particular to the acts of our perceiving consciousness in each case a determinate frequency and consequently a determinate rhythm, which must be investigated experimentally. We shall, once this path of research is entered upon, arrive at the formation of a new branch of knowledge, the experimental doctrine of consciousness or of reflection, among whose first tasks it will be to subject the intermittent character of the human perceiving consciousness to a systematic examination: namely to ascertain the lowest and highest limits of the pulse of perception, to establish lawfully the influence of various external circumstances upon the rise and fall of this pulse, to explore the differences of the pulse of perception in the male and female sex as well as in the various ages of life, and so on and so forth. If such a new physiological discipline is to become possible, then the theory of knowledge, which at bottom would have to be nothing further than a doctrine of human consciousness, must above all seek to raise the intermittent character of our acts of perception to a clear concept. We said that our perception does not flow continuously along with the process of sensation, that therefore imperceptibly small intervals of sensation must elapse before we are able to rouse ourselves to an act of perception. It is now a question what temporal duration we may ascribe to such an act of perception. In order to secure for our fantasy a concrete bearing, let us for the moment assume that a process of sensation must last about one tenth of a second before it can be taken cognizance of by an act of consciousness. It is then precisely a question whether this act of consciousness too takes up any temporal duration for itself. A very simple consideration shows us that an act of consciousness can take up no measurable time at all, since otherwise our consciousness would have to have a flowing character. For supposing that the act of consciousness too were to last one tenth of a second, this would have the significance that our consciousness is in uninterrupted action during the tenth of a second, that is, that it is able to be active also in the hundredth, thousandth, millionth, billionth, and so forth particles of a second, in short that it is able to penetrate into arbitrarily small particles of time. Were our consciousness of such a flowing nature, then it could indeed have flowed along with the process of sensation itself and disclosed to us every temporal phase, however small, of the process of sensation. Precisely because our consciousness is unable to accomplish this, we had to conclude that our consciousness does not flow along with the process of sensation, and therefore is able to flow on neither for a tenth nor for a hundredth of a second nor in general during any interval of time whatsoever, that is, is unable to be in continuous action. Inasmuch, then, as we established that our perceiving consciousness has not a flowing but an intermittent character, it lay already enclosed in the sense of this establishment that an act of perception considered for itself must be wholly durationless, that is, can take place only in the mathematical point of time.

2. I have already set up the proposition of the limited number of our acts of reflection during the second, as well as the proposition of the mathematical punctuality of these acts, as a fundamental article of the doctrine of consciousness, in my attempt at a monistic theory of knowledge, "Logic at the Parting of the Ways." To this work there fell the honor of being thoroughly criticized and appreciated by Professor Uphues of Halle in a separate treatise, "On the Crisis in Logic." Uphues, so far as I see, concurs with my conception that it is not granted us to reflect upon what has been experienced as often in the second as we please; he supposes, however, that I remained his debtor for the proof that our acts of reflection must have a timeless, that is, a mathematically punctual character. I believe, however, to have shown in the preceding consideration that the intermittent character of our consciousness at once also involves its punctuality. We have but to choose between two assumptions: either our perceiving consciousness flows along at the same tempo with the process of sensation, or it does not flow along. In the first case our faculty of sensible perception would have to be an infinitely fine one, which contradicts all the facts of our perception; there remains for us therefore nothing but to deny the flowing character to our perceiving consciousness. But if perception does not flow on continuously in time, then it can flow on for a tenth of a second as little as for a hundredth, as for a thousandth, and so forth, that is, it must have an intermittent, punctual character. Only by discerning that our mental acts take place in mathematical points of time, and hence are not of a sensible nature, have we also acquired the right to speak of mental acts as distinct from vital processes (vital, biological, physiological processes) such as the processes of sensation are, and only hereby do I believe to have uncovered the fundamental aberration of modern philosophy of sensation. For the modern impressionist or psychologist the sensation is in its whole course a so-called "process of consciousness," a "psychic happening," a "psychic phenomenon." Were this really so, that is, were the sensation in its whole course a process within our consciousness, then the very smallest temporal phases of a sensation would have to be known to us just as well as the larger, that is, we should possess an infinitely fine power of perception, which contradicts every human experience. If, however, the smallest phases of a process of sensation must remain unknown to us—which cannot with good means be doubted—then the process of sensation in its temporal course cannot be regarded as a process of consciousness at all, and a mental character may be ascribed only to the punctual acts of reflection between which the process of sensation is temporally enclosed. Thus my investigation leads to results which can be well brought into accord with so-called common understanding, with common sense. Every thinking person who is not influenced by the modern philosophy of sensation will at once concede that he conceives processes of sensation as bodily life-processes, and that by mental acts he understands such as have not a bodily but a nonsensible character. To be sure, he does not know how to state upon what he grounds this distinction, and he therefore lets himself easily be persuaded to hold even the processes of sensation in themselves to be something mental or "psychic." For this very reason I had in my above investigation to set forth the difference between a flowing happening and a punctual act, in showing that in our consciousness no flowing process can take place, since we should otherwise have an infinitely fine faculty of perception. The acts of our consciousness are necessarily punctual, and only because they are punctual (hence not sensible) have we the right and the logical duty to distinguish them from the flowing, temporal (hence bodily) processes of sensing. The proposition of the intermittent character of human consciousness is thus no chance aperçu about the nature of our mind, but in my conviction the only firm foundation for a science of the mind. So long as we are unable clearly to distinguish mental acts from vital processes, we also have no firm scientific basis for a doctrine of the mind as distinct from physiology, as the doctrine of vital processes. The distinguishing mark between the mental and the physiological can, however, be found only if we regard the human mind not with the eyes of an enraptured idealist, but seek to know it as it really is constituted, hence in its weakness and inadequacy. We are strong only through this, that we remain mindful of our weakness. Since now we directed our gaze from the outset upon the limitedness of our consciousness, we found this limitedness precisely in that discontinuity or punctuality which became for us the most valuable mark of distinction between mental act and flowing vital process. Had we from the outset regarded our mind as something perfected, then its distinguishing mark would thereby have escaped us. The question whether our activity of consciousness and in particular our perception is a continuous or a discontinuous (leaping) one was peculiarly not openly discussed by the great pioneers of modern philosophy; but whoever follows the labyrinthine passages of modern thinking with some attention will soon become aware that this significant problem was continually touched, as it were, with the fingertips, but ever again pushed aside. Already Descartes stands, with his doctrine of the two substances, the material and the mental substance, upon the ground of the said problem, and he decides for the principle of the continuity of our mental doing or thinking. It is, he holds, the essence of material substance to be extended, and it likewise belongs to the essence of mental substance to think and always to think. A mind that does not think and does not always think can exist as little as a body that were not extended. Thus the father of modern rationalism is at the same time the first representative of the principle of mental continuity. It is certainly a proposition of the very greatest boldness that a mind always thinks, and the author of such a proposition was surely himself a large-minded, exalted spirit: but it seems to me that that proposition sprang more from the striving after a glorification of our mind than from the objective consideration of our human nature. Doubtless a mind which neither bodily torments nor exhaustion after work, indeed which even sleep and swoon are unable to hinder from thinking, would be a quite extraordinary being, but surely we men do not hold ourselves to be such beings. A mind which always thinks and can be hindered in thinking by nothing is a mind wholly independent of all sensible experiences, so that it becomes incomprehensible how it can be connected with a body at all and what share it could take in the vital destinies of this body. Thus already in the Cartesian principle of the continuity of mental activity a dualistic conception of the world is contained. One need only ascribe to the soul a continuous thinking in order to estrange it wholly from the body, indeed to make the two into incompatible opposites; as which opposites they really appear in Descartes. Against the Cartesian doctrine that the soul always thinks, Locke, as is well known, set himself in his investigations concerning the human understanding. "For my own part," says this amiable thinker, "I confess that I have one of those dull souls that does not always range in the contemplation of ideas, and which holds unremitting thinking to be as little necessary for the soul as unremitting motion is for a body; since the apprehending of ideas is (to my understanding) for the soul the same as motion is for the body: not its essence, but one of its operations. However much, therefore, thinking may be regarded as the proper activity of the soul, the soul need not be assumed as always thinking and in activity. This may be the prerogative of the infinite Author and Preserver of things, who never slumbers nor sleeps; but it does not befit a finite being, at least not the human soul." There speaks in these words that sound and sober sense which affects us so beneficently in English thinkers and in empiricists generally. Locke sees through the idealizing exaggeration contained in the proposition that the soul always thinks, and he feels very well that this exaggeration rests upon the intermingling of a human with a superhuman consciousness; unfortunately, however, he bases his whole argumentation upon the fact of sleep as an interruption of our consciousness, which cannot be called happy for the reason that sleep is an exceedingly little explored state of our life-process and of our consciousness. Descartes had set up the principle of the continuity of thinking, and thus the historical moment was given to point, over against this principle, with all the emphasis of experience to the intermittent or leaping character of our consciousness. If, then, it was Locke's concern seriously to dispute the Cartesian principle, he ought not to have pointed merely to the sleeping man—for such a riddle as sleep is can prove nothing—but he should have shown us that even the waking man cannot perform acts of consciousness without interruption. Now, however, Locke supposes that a waking man is able to think continuously; indeed, he holds the unremittingness of thinking to be the essence or the fundamental condition of being awake: "I grant that in a waking man the soul is never without thinking, because this is the condition of being awake..." (Essay concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chap. 1, § 11). With this one sentence Locke had lost the game over against rationalism, for he himself assumes that there is a continuous human thinking, and merely restricts the continuity to the sixteen hours of our daily waking. He thus takes up no position of principle over against the Cartesian conception, but makes it his own after having riddled it with holes. Waking and sleep thereby become complete incomprehensibilities, for if the soul thinks unremittingly during waking, then waking is a perfect consciousness, while sleep stands over against it as the absolute lack of consciousness. Falling asleep would be an absolute annihilation of consciousness, so that there could be no talk of a gradual slumbering off, of an ever deeper falling asleep, of a dreaming and gradual reawakening. Waking and sleep by no means stand over against one another as absolute awareness and absolute unconsciousness. Their relation to one another is, despite the mighty advances of physiology in the nineteenth century, still shrouded in deepest darkness. The doctrine of intermittent acts of consciousness seems to me, however, to spread a light also upon this enigmatic relation. In the sense of this doctrine we must accomplish a certain number, fluctuating between fairly narrow limits, of acts of reflection per hour, in order to be able to maintain ourselves in the conscious state. Our consciousness must show a certain rapidity of pulse if we are not to be deprived of our human awareness. If our pulse of perception sinks below a certain number of acts in the second, then our waking is threatened, and we may picture sleep to ourselves as a state in which our pulse of perception is greatly lowered, that is, in which individual acts of reflection referring to the sensations follow one another at such long pauses that in consequence of this isolation no perception such as in waking can any longer come about. During sleep, as is well known, breathing is retarded, the individual breaths are deeper and at times periodically cease; the metabolism is correspondingly slighter and the giving-off of carbonic acid diminished by about a quarter in relation to that in the waking state; the pulse too is retarded, digestion less intense, the peristaltic movements of the intestines, in particular of the large intestine, weakened, and so forth. To these physiological marks of sleep we should now have to add an epistemological one: the decrease of the pulse of perception. To be sure, even with this addition we are still very far from possessing a theory of the difference between waking and sleeping, but the proposition of the leaping character of our consciousness makes it at least comprehensible to us that there can very well be transitions from waking into sleep and conversely from sleep into the waking state. One has sought to establish the depth of sleep by determining the strength of the auditory stimuli which are necessary in the various periods of sleep in order to awaken the sleeper. According to Mönninghof and Piesbergen the depth of sleep increases in the first five quarters of an hour only quite gradually, in order then, falling off abruptly, to reach in the course of a further half hour the greatest possible depth. From then on the firmness of sleep decreases abruptly for half an hour, in order thenceforth to grow quite gradually shallower. About the middle of the fourth and fifth hour of sleep, sleep reaches its greatest shallowness, that is, a greatest approximation to awakening, yet deepens again, though only in slight measure, for about an hour, in order from then on to approach awakening. We can bring this sinking into the arms of Morpheus and this gradual return to waking into very good connection with the proposition of intermittent consciousness, by assuming that during deep sleep some key of sensation sounds in consciousness only at long temporal intervals, whereas such a sounding becomes the more frequent the more the sleeper approaches reawakening. One can also render this conception sensible by the following simile: if one strikes the keys of a piano only at very long intervening pauses, then no piece of music can shape itself out of such isolated tones, and a certain rapidity in the succession of the tones is required in order that our fantasy of tone may grasp them as constituents of a unitary musical whole. Similarly does it stand with our acts of reflection, which, if they occur at all too long intervening pauses, lack that coherence which is necessary for perceiving; but at a certain more rapid tempo yield precisely what we call waking perception.

## III. Die Bewußtheit — III. Awareness

III. Awareness. The concept which we have hitherto been able to furnish of human consciousness and of its first chief mark, discontinuity, had necessarily to be an inadequate one, because we always aimed only at learning to distinguish the act of consciousness from the vital process, and hence always considered our consciousness only in relation to the vital processes and not also in relation to itself. Now it lies, however, in the nature of human consciousness not only to possess a relation to the vital processes but also to stand in a relation to itself. At least we demand of a mature, that is, a judgment-capable human consciousness that it be not only a consciousness of some physical processes but also a consciousness of itself. But what one is to think of a consciousness of consciousness has at all times been one of the most dreaded questions of philosophy, because no other question has so pronouncedly a sense-confusing character as this one. For if there is a thinking of thinking, then there is further also a thinking of this second thinking, and so on. Since it is now most burdensome to write one and the same word two, three, four times alongside one another (as for example thinking of the thinking of the thinking, and so forth), let it be permitted me for brevity's sake to speak of the second, third, fourth... and in general nth power or stage of consciousness or of thinking. The number n can be arbitrarily large, and nothing hinders us from letting it grow into the boundless, so that there may fittingly be talk also of an infinite power of consciousness. But what is now to be thought of the higher powers, or indeed of the infinitely high power of thinking or of consciousness, is a mystery which holds a charm for the fewest of men. For that one can reflect upon the thinking of man, and that one has a knowledge of one's knowledge, most intelligent men will still concede, that is, one can still negotiate with them about the second power of consciousness as well; but were one to expect of them that they should approach higher powers too, they would declare this a "most superfluous," "quite unfruitful," and in any case sense-confusing undertaking. Even the knowledge of knowledge seems to many to be an empty manner of speaking, since it is after all enough simply to know something, and it is quite superfluous even to speak of a second power of consciousness. From this state of affairs, however, only so much emerges: that we men in fact have a very slight knowledge of the nature of our consciousness, and, what is still worse, that it does not attract us to explore the nature of our consciousness. The proposition of the discontinuity of human consciousness seems to me, however, to be suited to divesting the old problem of the powers or stages of consciousness of its sense-confusing and therefore exceedingly burdensome character. In the sense of the proposition of discontinuity, a human consciousness has at its disposal nothing but punctual acts. If then we consider an act of consciousness a₁ which is connected with a process of sensation, or more correctly which delimits the process of sensation, the question arises whether this act a₁ has a consciousness of itself. Since in the sense of our conception a consciousness has at its disposal nothing but acts, one can also formulate the above question thus: is it possible that in one and the same instant two acts of consciousness a₁ and a₂ take place, of which a₂ is so constituted that it gives us tidings of the act a₁? For if it is possible that two acts of consciousness take place with complete simultaneity, that is, in the same mathematical Now, then we may indeed say that it is possible for the act a₁ to have a consciousness of itself, for this consciousness of itself would be assured to it through the wholly simultaneously occurring act a₂. Now it follows, however, from the proposition of the discontinuity of human consciousness that such a Siamese-twin act of consciousness as has just been contemplated is an impossibility. For in the sense of the doctrine of discontinuity there must always elapse between two acts of consciousness a certain time (be it even only a slight fraction of a second) which is filled out by some vital process. We may therefore set it forth as an immediate consequence of the doctrine of discontinuity that an act of consciousness can never have a consciousness of itself, but that only a second and later-occurring act of consciousness can lend to the preceding a₁ any awareness, yet without this second act of consciousness being able to have a consciousness of itself, since a third act of consciousness a₃ is required in order that the second act a₂ too may become a conscious one. This is the principle of the relativity of human awareness. In the sense of this principle no single act of consciousness is able to produce awareness for itself, but it is always the relation of the one act to the other that brings forth any awareness. It follows further from the principle of relativity that there are various stages of awareness, upon whose formation the development of human consciousness precisely rests. It may indeed excite concern that always only one act can lend awareness to another, while it itself remains as it were in darkness unless a new act casts light upon it, for the appearance arises as though man could in such wise never attain to a proper awareness: but one must not let oneself be disconcerted by such apprehensions, for one must first have explored several stages of consciousness in order to grasp the significance of each of them and to render account to oneself of what significance the climbing of further stages of awareness could have. For if there is such a stage of awareness upon which one can, for example, discern the validity of a truth w₁, so that the soaring up to higher stages of awareness can alter nothing in the insight into the truth w₁ but merely lets one recognize the connection of this one truth with other truths w₂, w₃, ... wₙ, then a man to whom the truth w₁ is in the first place of concern may fittingly neglect the higher stages of awareness, which would grant him a deeper glance into the connection of truths. Now it in fact stands thus, that man in the course of his development must climb only several stages of awareness in order to be able to establish truths at which no higher stage of awareness can any longer successfully shake, so that the higher stages of awareness are suited merely to discovering new truths, that is, to casting a deeper glance into the connections of truth of human science. We can also express this thus: There is for man only a finite number of stages of awareness which are qualitatively different, and these are precisely those stages which are necessary in order that a child of man may be able to penetrate through to some inviolable awareness of truth: all the remaining stages of awareness, on the contrary, are no longer qualitatively different from the lower stages of awareness and can be serviceable merely to the discovery of new truths and thereby to the deepening of our knowledge; to be sure, they often serve also to unmask apparent truths, that is, errors, against which moreover the lower awareness had already bristled, without however being able to summon the necessary mental force which is requisite for the unmasking of widely disseminated errors. Thus the principle of the relativity of human awareness is by no means a "skeptical" principle, but on the contrary the principle of the development and progress of the human mind, as it manifests itself everywhere in the mental unfolding of individuals. Further it follows from the principle of the relativity of human awareness—as I here formulate it—that the typical fundamental character of the human intellect is one unalterable for all future, because the number of the qualitatively different stages of awareness which are necessary in order to set something forth as true—to pass a true judgment with the consciousness of its truth—will remain at all times the same. At the same time it lies in the sense of the principle of relativity that the human intellect is nevertheless capable of a boundless progress: on the one hand through the unlimited widening of experience, on the other hand also through an unlimited deepening of the consciousness of truth by means of the soaring up to unboundedly high stages of awareness, which stages, however, are no longer qualitatively different from the lower ones and can also never really attain an infinite number. These are, to be sure, merely anticipations of a doctrine of consciousness to be developed in detail, in which the qualitatively different stages of awareness are to be characterized with the greatest possible clarity, and in which it will also come to light that beyond these stages there can be no qualitatively new ones. Here it suffices in the first place to have warned against the intermingling of the two concepts: act of consciousness and awareness (or stage of awareness). No act of consciousness has a consciousness of itself, and every stage of awareness consists in the relatedness of an act a₂ to an act a₁. The proposition of the punctuality of the acts of consciousness thus by no means suffices to set the principle of the discontinuity of human consciousness in a sufficiently bright light; there is further required the second fundamental proposition, namely the principle of the relatedness of two acts of consciousness, in order to make it rightly graspable that a mental deed of man is never accomplished with merely one act, because already the first and lowest stage of human awareness can be attained only through the relation of two acts. The leaping character of human consciousness enters only hereby into the right illumination: for although the individual acts of consciousness are punctual, that is, simple (and human consciousness hereby shows a kinship with the infinite intelligence of the Godhead), yet human awareness is no longer anything simple, but also not anything so boundlessly composite as the process of sensation, for it does not flow as the latter does. It is something finitely composite in the sense that it is built up out of a finite multiplicity of punctual acts through their relation to one another. It hardly needs to be said that an act of consciousness a₂ can relate itself to an act of consciousness a₁ only by virtue of a vital process with which it is united. Acts of consciousness are indeed nothing of the sort that could appear unconnected with vital processes, and it is always reserved to abstracting thought to separate in thought a punctual act from the vital process connected with it. Now inasmuch as awareness comes about only through the relation of act to act, it rests necessarily also upon the relation of vital process to vital process. We are therefore never able to perceive a vital process by itself, in its severedness from other vital processes, but a vital process becomes perceivable always only through the help of another vital process. This is the exceedingly important principle of double experiences, according to which in our perception we never have to do with a single process but with a double process, of which the one is as it were the accentuation of the other, so that we perceive neither the vital process l₁ nor the vital process l₂ accentuating it (related to it) by itself, but merely the accentuatedness of the one process by the other—which can be symbolized by the fractional formula l₁/l₂. One can also designate the principle of double experiences as the principle of the relativity of human perception. Only through this principle is the impressionistic mode of thought, which proceeded from Locke, Berkeley, and Hume and forms an essential constituent of criticism, completely refuted. For impressionism treats a sensation like a simple process, and does not notice that sensation, quite apart from its boundless temporal compositeness, must already arise out of the relation of two processes, since we men are always able to perceive only the relations of processes. The one process is, as has been said, accentuated by the other, which one can also express by saying that the one process is limited, constricted, hindered by the other, or that one process strives with the other, one combats the other, and that it is only this opposition of the two processes which becomes noticeable for us. This opposition can be made intuitable by the opposition of a wave-crest and a wave-trough, so that it is always already a whole wave that we can perceive, while the half-waves out of which it is composed can be inferred only through the theoretical investigation of human awareness, as I shall show in this chapter by a fundamental example. Thereby it will become clear what I understand by a rhythm of life or pulse of life and by the rhythm of consciousness or pulse of consciousness united with it. For inasmuch as an act a₂ is related to an act a₁, there arises the pulse of consciousness a₁/a₂ and in union with it the pulse of life l₁/l₂. The next task of the doctrine of consciousness is to set forth the construction of human awareness out of its pulses. This task becomes soluble only in union with biological research, which must fathom the corresponding pulses of life and their connections experimentally and mathematically, and only hereby can approach the solution of the great riddle of life. It is further clear that the above two acts of consciousness a₂ and a₁, of which the one relates itself to the other, cannot be of the same kind. For the act a₁ relates itself only to a vital process; the act a₂, on the contrary, relates itself by means of a vital process to the act a₁, and relatedness to a vital process is well to be distinguished from relatedness to an act. The act a₁ is an immediate one, for its sole determination is to establish a contact of consciousness with the vital process; the act a₂, on the contrary, is already of a mediate character, because it stands in the service of the coherence of consciousness itself, inasmuch as it accomplishes that relating of act to act without which an awareness cannot subsist. We may therefore set up the proposition that human consciousness disposes of acts of consciousness of different kinds, which is likewise a consequence of its discontinuous character. In his book "On Consciousness" Uphues takes up an opposed standpoint in these questions. He speaks, in the sense of the doctrine of the stream of consciousness, not of acts but of processes of consciousness, and raises the question what one is really to understand by the awareness of a process of consciousness. Here is his answer: "It is a knowing of every process of consciousness about itself. The process of consciousness turns back, so to speak, upon itself (reflects upon itself [reflectit super se ipsum]), it comprehends itself, holds itself together, resists division—if it is permitted to speak of it in these quite unsuitable images taken from the sensible sphere." (On Consciousness, p. 24.) However ingenious these considerations may be, they nevertheless sound like the purest mysticism; they turn the problem this way and that without moving it from the spot. Uphues supposes that he illuminates the nature of "awareness" also by setting it forth as a mark of every process of consciousness, whereby, however, all experience concerning the stepwise development of man's awareness is overthrown. Here are his words: "It (awareness) is the mark peculiar to all processes of consciousness (nota constituens, not a property which presupposes a thing as bearer) through which the processes of consciousness become processes of consciousness. It is a fundamental mark of all processes of consciousness, no mere property of them; but yet no generic mark which repeats itself in the same way in all processes of consciousness and could be separated in thought from their differences, like the generic mark tree, which recurs in all trees but does not occur again in the differences of trees from one another. We can speak of various kinds of trees, but not of various kinds of processes of consciousness." ("On Consciousness," pp. 23, 24.) These are certainly fine and ingenious distinctions, but does one really learn from them what one is to think of awareness? I am unable to think out how an act of consciousness could have a consciousness of itself. It would then indeed have to be at the same time subject and object of its consciousness. Or to express this more clearly: let us assume that the acts of consciousness a₂ and a₁ took place wholly simultaneously, and that the act a₂ related itself to a₁. There would then, as has been said, be accomplished by such a Siamese double act the miracle that an act of consciousness, scarcely born, already had a consciousness of itself. But therewith the principle would also be pronounced that our consciousness needs no temporal duration at all in order to come from the one act to the other. Our pulse of consciousness would be infinitely swift. Just as the act a₂ is present simultaneously with a₁ in order to relate itself to it, so with the act a₂ the act a₃ would also be simultaneously present, in order to confer awareness upon the act a₂ as well. But by virtue of the infinite swiftness of our pulse of consciousness we should also not lack an act a₄ which conferred awareness upon the act a₃; in short, we could perform in one and the same instant as many acts of consciousness as we please, that is, we should be no men but gods. This is precisely the fundamental trait of character of human consciousness, that it is never a perfect, self-enclosed, wholly self-comprehending and purely self-grounded, absolute consciousness. Human consciousness is discontinuous, is leaping, and can wrestle its way upward only step by step or jerk by jerk out of the night of unconsciousness to ever higher stages of a luminous awareness. Therein consists the evolution or development of human consciousness. The doctrine of the stream of consciousness (or of continuous consciousness) stands everywhere in contradiction with the stepwise formation and development of the human mind, and it leads—as we shall convince ourselves—either to the confusion of the human mind with the divine, that is, of the finitely swift pulse of consciousness with the infinitely swift one, or else to the doubt whether there can be talk of a consciousness at all, and whether all our thinking (and willing) is not a mere, null dreaming, that is, to skepticism. The doctrine of the discontinuity of the human mind is, on the contrary, a doctrine of mental progress: of a progress which can be won only laboriously, step by step, but which is borne by the consciousness of striving toward the infinite light. We must hasten to the aid of the primary acts of consciousness with secondary ones, in order to help the former to an awareness. Thus the acts of our consciousness are by no means of the same kind—as the doctrine of the stream of consciousness would have us believe—but it is precisely the task of the doctrine of consciousness to explore the various kinds of acts of consciousness and the various kinds of vital processes united with them. For I must emphasize at once here that there is a path for exploring our life-processes which proceeds from the investigation of our consciousness and issues into biology (physiology), and a converse path which, proceeding from biology, presses toward the investigation of consciousness (hence toward philosophy). Only let no one suppose that the one of these paths can be trodden with the complete exclusion of the other. Only the practical principle of the division of labor as well as the one-sided endowment of the various investigators requires that the one lay the chief weight upon this and the other upon the opposite direction of investigation: but the interest of progress also unconditionally requires that the two directions maintain the most intimate contact with one another. The occurrence of primary acts of consciousness is, taken by itself, wholly insufficient to produce any kind of awareness. For our consciousness is unremittingly besieged by an exceedingly manifold stream of impressions, and their unstoppable flux has the tendency always to fetter our consciousness to the Now of the moment, which would have the consequence that in every instant we should have to lose the whole past of our life-process. It is therefore necessary that our life-process react upon the impressions seizing it with antagonistic vital processes, and that our consciousness too at once oppose to the primary acts secondary, antagonistic acts, which loosen the mental gaze from the fetters of the Now of the moment and enable it, despite the restless forward-racing of the processes of impression, to consider what has just passed. Thus in every process of sensation which is to come to our cognizance there necessarily strive with one another two vital processes of opposed character, because without such a conflict a past could never exist for us. So long as the dogma of the simplicity of our sensations passed for a self-evident truth, the insight that in every process of sensation two kinds of antagonistic processes fight with one another could of course not arise; but since we have freed ourselves from the dogma of the simplicity of sensation, nothing can any longer hinder us in the insight that our mental gaze would never be able to wrestle its way through to any awareness if it were always inexorably forged to the Now of the moment, and if therefore not only the past hours and minutes but also the second just elapsed had to fall away from it. (Instead of the term "act of consciousness" I also use the expression "mental gaze" or briefly "gaze" in a wholly synonymous meaning.) Our mind must accordingly not only take firm footing, as it were, in the Now of the moment through a Now-gaze J₁, but it must at once—say after the elapse of a fraction of a second—rouse itself to a retrospective gaze R₁ which relates itself to the already past Now-gaze J₁, since otherwise the Now-gaze J₁ would remain a vainly accomplished, a wholly lost act of consciousness. We dwell upon the just-past Now-gaze J₁ only by means of the retrospective gaze R₁; the Now-gaze J₁ comes into its own only by means of the retrospective gaze R₁. Every human perception is a belated perception. In vain does the Now-gaze J₁ bore itself into a mathematical Now-point of the stream of time, for there is still required a later-occurring retrospective gaze R₁ which by virtue of its relation to J₁ makes possible a lingering upon it. We live in the illusion that with the mere Now-gaze of our consciousness we are able to master what happens before us, and do not notice that even with the most strained attention we are never able to catch a real (that is, mathematical) Now. It is always something just past that we hold to be a present. It is always already a retrospective gaze through which we come to a Now. There must alternate with one another at least two kinds of acts of consciousness in order that any awareness may come about. We shall therefore have to distinguish well the rhythm of the successive Now-gazes J₁, J₂, J₃... and so forth from the rhythm of the retrospective gazes R₁, R₂, R₃..., and in doing so shall have to take heed that the members of the second rhythm insert themselves in each case at some tempo between the members of the first rhythm, so that the two interleaved rhythms can be represented, say, by the formula: J₁, R₁, J₂, R₂, J₃, R₃... I do not, however, wish to have established any determinate law by this formula. For it is very well possible that between two, three, or more Now-gazes there still inserts itself no retrospective gaze, and that this insertion in general occurs at a very diverse tempo. The intensity of the impressions of the moment and the degree of attention with which we follow them will exercise a decisive influence upon the crowdedness and looseness of the successive acts J₁, R₁, J₂, R₂... In any case there is within every sphere of sense a highest rapidity with which an act R₁ can follow upon an act J₁, and which can be exceeded by no human being. But there is also a lower limit for the swiftness of the alternation between J and R. For if an all too great interval is to elapse between J and the corresponding R, then R is no longer able to relate itself to J, and the coherence of the two acts of consciousness, that is, the awareness to be produced, is endangered. There may then, for example, be in train a transition from a waking attentiveness to a waking reverie, or a transition from a waking state into sleep, or some other transition of consciousness. Here it already shows itself that the doctrine of the discontinuity of human consciousness is able to cast a new light upon the enigmatic states of consciousness. To the antagonistic acts of consciousness J and R there correspond, as has been said, antagonistic vital processes. The process of impression proper induces a process of opposed character which we may fittingly call a process of memory. Only it must be remarked that there is here no talk of such a process of memory as would be related to processes of impression which have already taken place several seconds, minutes, hours, and so forth ago. The process of memory of which there is talk here relates itself to the process of impression that has just elapsed, that is, to the process which in ordinary life one is wont to designate as taking place just now. We do not notice that for the perception of a process taking place before our eyes we require not only a determinate capacity for impression but must necessarily also have a memory, since otherwise the process flowing before our eyes would in all its just-elapsed sections completely fall away from us and we should stare senselessly into the world. The vital process of impression awakens an antagonistic vital process, namely a process of memory, and our consciousness leaps, as it were, over from the one process to the other and then back again, and so forth. This is of course only a metaphorical manner of expression, which is however very suited to impressing the discontinuity of consciousness upon fantasy as well. In the one instant an act of consciousness unites itself with the stream of the process of impression; after a short interval of time another act of consciousness unites itself with the stream of the process of memory, which precisely can be expressed intuitively by saying that the mental gaze leaps over and back from the one stream to the other. Once the stream of memory has been induced by the stream of impression, both streams flow on simultaneously, and it is merely the acts of consciousness which occur alternately at determinate intervals of time and thus produce a mental double pulse composed of two rhythms, upon which as its primitive foundation the far more composite pulse of consciousness of man builds itself up. In any case the reader gathers already from the exposition thus far that a doctrine of consciousness which shall wish to pass for a genuine science has in the first place to explore the various rhythms of human consciousness. These rhythms of consciousness are always bound up with rhythms of life, and the former can be definitively and in positive-exact fashion established only in the measure that it also succeeds in establishing the latter by experimental and mathematical means.

## IV. Empfindung & Bewegung — IV. Sensation & Movement

IV. Sensation and Movement. Uphues concurred with me on the point that already in the perception of an appearance now taking place not only processes of impression but also processes of memory must have a share, and he points at the same time to similar conceptions in Hans Cornelius. Whoever reads the expositions of this subtle thinker (in his "Psychology as an Empirical Science") is seized in many places by the impression that the author is steering straight for the principle of the discontinuity of human consciousness. One finds in him sentences such as: that "our knowing of temporal differences" takes place "by no means continuously." (p. 129.) Further: "The only thing we can bring it to is a discontinuous establishing of such differences..." (p. 129.) Still more clearly speak the following passages: "that differences of the successive contents of consciousness are found not continuously, but always only in greater or smaller finite intervals of time..." (p. 133). Further: "We speak in this sense also of a fusion of successive unnoticed partial contents into a unitary total content of a certain duration." (p. 133.) But Cornelius too unfortunately does not draw the consequences of the insights forcing themselves upon him, for he shrinks back as it were with awe from the thought of a mathematical point of time. The real Now is for him the empirical Now, which endures for a time; the Now-point, on the contrary, is a fiction. He does indeed play with the thought whether at the boundary of two experiences—that is, in the mathematical point of time which separates them from one another—any punctual activity of consciousness is to be set, but he decides for the denial of it. He says outright: "We have no right whatever to regard the transition (of two different contents of consciousness) itself as a particular punctual experience of consciousness distinguished from the two successive contents." (p. 143.) Thus this acute psychologist is comparable to a general who storms the walls of an enemy fortress with great energy, but at the moment when those walls begin to totter suddenly gives up the siege. He is on the way to illuminating the fundamental error of that sensualistic or impressionistic psychology which proceeds from Locke, but at the decisive moment he turns away from the light of his own thoughts. For the rest, it gives me joy to find trains of thought which graze closely upon the principle of the discontinuity of consciousness, even if at the decisive instant they shrink back from it. Thus in his day no lesser man than Leibniz was already on the way to refuting thoroughly that "empirical psychology" which lay in Locke's investigations: in particular, his famous thought of the "petites perceptions" was well suited to inaugurate a new era in the doctrine of consciousness, only that reforming thought would have had to be clearly formulated as well, so that it might not perish of its own dark fullness of intimation. The impression which we gain from the roar of the sea, says Leibniz, is composed of innumerable "petites perceptions" which arise through the rushing of every small ripple of the waves, and so he approaches the thought that lies at the ground of this writing: namely the boundless compositeness of every sensation. But he thinks only of parts of the process of sensation which, considered for themselves, are of very slight intensity, and not, as I mean it, of very slight temporal duration; further, he designates those parts of slight intensity as "petites perceptions," where after all the chief weight should fall upon this, that those parts are not perceived and hence can be no "perceptions." Thus Leibniz arrives at the peculiar conception that sensing must be regarded as a kind of confused thinking, where he ought rather to have drawn the conclusion that the process of sensation is no thinking at all but a vital process. I regard the Leibnizian thought accordingly as one which has remained hovering and stuck in the middle between the doctrine of continuity and that of discontinuity of human consciousness: from which it follows that in the many excellent thinkers of his school, as in Herbart, Bolzano, Lotze, E. von Hartmann, various beginnings toward the doctrine of discontinuity will be found. Yet I cannot enter further here upon these historical researches and mention only that Wundt too, with his demand that in the doctrine of sensations we must first abstract from their temporal duration, moves right up to the very border of the doctrine of discontinuity. For if in the case of a process of sensation we abstract from its temporal duration, then we have really to do with a durationless or punctual sensing, more correctly with a punctual act of consciousness, while the process of sensation, insofar as it has a temporal flux, precisely represents a vital process, a nervous process. We have recognized that in this vital process two antagonistic processes fight with one another, of which the one, the process of impression, always leads us on toward the events just arriving, while the opposed process leads us back to the events just elapsed. These two processes are the bearers of the two kinds of antagonistic acts of consciousness, of the Now-gaze and of the retrospective gaze, which only in union with one another form the foundation of our awareness. But it is easy to see that the intermittent occurrence of these two acts of consciousness cannot yet constitute the whole of human awareness, because in their relation to one another they are really always able to bring forth only the consciousness of an empirical Now. The process of impression culminates in the Now-gaze; the antagonistic process of memory culminates in the retrospective gaze, which really accomplishes only so much as to confer awareness upon the Now-gaze. The expression "process of memory" is accordingly confusing, for we are accustomed to understand by memory the faculty by virtue of which we come back to events which do not take place in the empirical Now but have taken place in a more remote past. To be sure, this crude conception leads us to a false concept of memory, inasmuch as we wholly overlook that memory is an indispensable factor also in the sensation of what is present: but since we once bring such crude concepts with us, we must be all the more mindful to distinguish those processes of memory through which we gain an empirical Now from those processes of memory through which we return to events which have taken place not in the empirical Now but in an earlier past. The first kind of process of memory forms only a component of our process of sensation of the moment, and indeed an indispensable component of it, because without it no awareness of the present can come about: it can therefore also be called the memory of the present or the sensory (receptory) memory, in distinction from memory of the past proper, by virtue of which we return to an event of our past. Evidently there can be talk of a human awareness proper only where someone is capable of directing his consciousness back at will to events of the past. To be sure, this arbitrary directing back of our consciousness is by no means in our power to such a degree as we might wish, and this directing back may indeed be regarded as one of the most difficult riddles of the doctrine of consciousness: but so much stands fast, that there can still be no talk of a human awareness proper where the processes of memory stand merely in the service of an empirical Now, and where it never becomes possible to direct memory at will back to an occurrence of the past. Those two antagonistic acts of consciousness of which there was talk in the previous chapter thus by no means yet produce that kind of awareness which could already be called a well-developed awareness, for they still merely set our consciousness upon the empirical Now. This setting of consciousness upon a Now the infant learns in the first three or four months of its development, for the so-called "stupid quarter-year" is taken up with the coordination of the processes of impression with the corresponding antagonists of the memory of the Now, so that after the elapse of these first three or four months the infant can no longer be called wholly devoid of every kind of awareness, since the setting of consciousness upon the Now, even if still in a wavering and uncertain manner, can already be performed. The heavy process of development which the infant passes through in the course of three to four months we adults perform in a small fraction of a second, for we too must in every perception set our consciousness upon the Now and live through the processes of impression with the corresponding antagonists of the memory of the Now; only our consciousness does not remain set merely upon the Now, but also leaps off from it, dwells upon some past, and returns to an empirical Now. In short, we now discover a new rhythm within our nervous processes and a new corresponding pulse of our consciousness, which springs from the alternating setting of our consciousness upon the present and upon the past. Inasmuch as we turn our attention to that wonderful faculty of our consciousness by virtue of which consciousness is able to set itself upon the past, that is, to direct itself toward the past, we arrive at the formation of a new concept, namely of the motor or effectory (executing) consciousness, which exercises a directing influence upon itself, in contrast to the sensory or receptory (receiving) consciousness, which does not yet have this power over itself, and which we have hitherto considered. But there can be no talk at all of a motor consciousness if we do not at once turn our attention also to those vital processes which we may designate as motor or effectory vital processes (in contrast to the sensory or receptory ones). It is a consequence of the predominance of sensualism in modern philosophy that the intimate connection of sensation and living movement is misapprehended from the ground up. For sensualism teaches that our "ideas" come from sensation, or even bluntly that our sensations are simple ideas; but it does not at the same time also teach that our ideas come from the movements of our body and our limbs, or that these movements are simple ideas. Living movement is not set upon the same line with sensation, as natural science and in particular biology must demand: rather these two kinds of animal vital processes are torn apart, and that in such a way that a mental character is granted to the processes of sensation but denied to the motor life-processes. Hereby sensation moves up into the sphere of "psychic or conscious processes," whereas the motor processes of the living body are pressed down into the sphere of mechanics. On the one hand the sensory processes are robbed of their vital character in order to be stamped as psychic processes; on the other hand the motor processes of the living body are likewise divested of their vital character and treated as mechanical processes, so that through this double logical injustice inflicted upon the animal life-process the appearance arises as though peculiar vital processes did not exist at all. The realm of vital processes is divided into two pieces, and it fares with it as with many a realm which has been dismembered by potentates and robbed of its independence. The one piece of the vital process falls into the sphere of power of the psychologists, the other into the sphere of power of those investigators who pay homage to the "mechanistic conception of the world" and strive to "reduce" all processes to "mechanical" ones. I have already pointed out what immeasurable confusion of concepts has arisen from the fact that one did not attempt strictly to distinguish mental acts from vital processes, and so mingled consciousness with the life-process that it could come to no formation of a proper science of consciousness. But now we see that through this mingling the genuine science of animal life too appears endangered in the highest degree, for in part it dissolves into so-called psychology, in part it is suffocated in mechanics. Herewith I believe to have pointed to the fundamental infirmity of modern natural science. Biology, the doctrine of the riddle of life, loses in it its proper autonomous biological character and is falsified partly in psychological, partly in mechanistic fashion. It falls, as it were, between the two millstones of mechanistic thinking, as this has proceeded from Galileo, and of psychologistic thinking, whose originators are to be regarded as Locke, Berkeley, and Hume. A new epoch in the development of modern natural science can proceed only from biology, in that this science takes full courage and defends its peculiar character against every foreign encroachment. There have indeed in the course of the last three or four centuries always been biologists—so-called vitalists—who did not let the peculiar difference between mechanical process and life-process be conjured out of the world, and in our own days too a powerful "neovitalistic" current is again in train (as is to be seen for instance from the interesting work of Hans Driesch, "Vitalism as History and as Doctrine"): but it appears to me that the weapons with which vitalism stands up for the peculiarity of vital processes are often very unphilosophical ones and unnoticed lend aid to the mechanical mode of thought. It is not the vitalistic standpoint that must be defended over against the mechanistic; rather, conversely, the mechanistic standpoint must seek to demonstrate its right to exist over against the vitalistic. For nothing is more certain than that we are able to perceive anything whatever only by means of our vital processes, and that we could perceive nothing at all if our life-process did not subsist. For by perception of the senses we understand precisely that kind of perceptions which a living being, such as for example man, is able to make in consequence of its vital processes and the acts of consciousness bound up with them: and we do not for the present trouble ourselves at all about the question whether there may also be after death some faculty of perception or how the same might be constituted. Since therefore—when there is talk of the perception of a world of appearance—we always think of the living man, who attains to a cognizance of that world of appearance only by means of his vital processes and the acts of consciousness united with them, no rational doubt concerning the subsistence of our vital processes can arise, and it must rather be explained how it is possible that we, who in taking cognizance of any happenings always find ourselves supported by our vital processes, can nevertheless arrive at the conviction that there are not merely vital processes but also mechanical happenings. Precisely from this state of affairs do some thinkers draw the apparent justification for a philosophy of universal animation, which is greedily seized upon by poets and belletrists, but which conceals within itself exactly as much (or more correctly as little) truth as the doctrine of the universal machine, that is, the view that all processes are of a mechanical nature. It is, as I have already emphasized, the most comfortable manner of philosophizing simply to set oneself over subsisting differences; for in such a way one most easily attains to a so-called monistic system: but whoever simply gives up all differences has thereby given up distinguishing, and hence the human understanding. There is no difference, however slight, between two processes or between two nuances of concept which may ever be given up, for if the principle of identity, and hence the possibility of knowledge, is not to be given up, then every process and every concept as well as every difference must be precisely what it is. There is a haggling in the marketplace, but there is no haggling in philosophy. One cannot, through gradual, imperceptible transitions between processes, bring the differences between them to disappear (as one pursues with such great passion in the modern theories of evolution and descent); on the contrary, one must with all the might of the human power of discrimination press toward making the finest possible distinctions and letting them hold in this fineness. For only he who lets all differences hold gains also the right to inquire after the common origin of all possible differences, that is, to strive after a monistic mode of thought. One can never "reduce" a process a to a process b different from it, nor ever a concept A to a concept B different from it, at least not insofar as such a "reduction" should signify an unnoticed deviation from the principle of identity. For precisely this reason it is important to oppose with equal vigor those three kinds of apparent monism: a) the psychic, b) the vital, c) the mechanical monism, by which the logic of modern thinking is endangered. "Psychic monism," which would set forth all processes as "psychic," that is, as running their course within consciousness, is untenable for the reason that there are no "processes of consciousness" at all. Vital and mechanical monism, however, are refuted by pressing for the distinction between the mechanical and the vital process. To this end we must first enter as exactly as possible upon the difference between movement and sensation. We must not let ourselves be misled by the endeavor somehow to reduce sensation and movement to one another. Neither can a process of sensation be resolved into any vibrations, nor a movement of our limbs into any series of sensations. One must let both sensation and movement be what they are, although one may concede that in our life-process they are united with one another in the most intimate manner, so that there could be no talk at all of an animal life-process if sensitivity and mobility did not complement one another. For movement is a process which enables us to interrupt certain sensations that besiege our consciousness and to relieve them by other series of sensations. Thus we interrupt, for example, our visual sensation through the movement—the closing as well as the opening—of our eyelids. Thus we advance, for example, through the movement of the eyeballs, through the turning of the neck, or in general through the locomotion of the whole body, to new series of visual sensations. We exercise through our movements an influence upon our life of sensation; we direct and regulate the sensory processes through motor processes, although this directing and regulating by no means stands in our power to the degree that we might wish. Conversely, our processes of sensation exercise an influence upon our state of movement at the moment, they drive us out of rest into movement, stimulate us to the execution of very complicated movements, lead us back into the state of rest: in short, they interrupt and regulate our motor life just as much as they are conversely interrupted and regulated by this motor life. No level-headed biologist will therefore wish to reduce the life of sensation to the motor life or conversely the latter to the former: he rather surmises a kind of polar opposition between sensation and movement, which precisely in consequence of their polarity are destined to complement one another: indeed, he not merely surmises this opposition and this complementation, but believes he directly beholds them in the whole morphological structure of the animal body. Never will he wish to reduce the receptory nervous system to an effectory one, never the latter to the former, nor will he assume that anywhere an organism could possess a purely receptory nervous system to the exclusion of the effectory (or conversely), and manifests through this conception his fundamental conviction that he holds the "reduction" of sensation to movement (or conversely) to be downright nonsense and beholds the unity of the life of sensation and the life of movement in their oppositeness and in their necessary reciprocal complementation. Only modern "idealism" (Berkeley's) could make so bold as to make even movement into a process within consciousness, hence into a psychic process, whereby the difference between sensation and movement was abolished and it stood free to reduce movement as a kind of composite psychic process to its elements, to the sensations. And only mechanical monism could venture to take the opposite path and to wish to reduce all sensation to movement, which would of course have the consequence that one would have to conceive the sensory nervous system as somehow arising out of the motor one. All such undertakings should be designated not as "monistic" but as "monotonous" philosophemes, as doctrines of uniformity, which would establish the unity of the universe at the cost of its infinite manifoldness. They make the impression as though someone out of monistic overzealousness were to deny the difference between his right and his left hand and to make us believe that he is the happy possessor of two left hands. In order to fix our eye more sharply upon the opposition and the necessary reciprocal complementation of sensation and movement, we must no longer speak of our modes of sensation in general, but only of a single mode of sensation which forms the foundation of the whole life of sensation: I mean the class of our sensations of resistance, which are commonly concealed under the more refined name of the exceedingly manifold "tactile sensations" and thus are unable to come to their well-deserved recognition. I designate our sensations of resistance as the fundamental kind of sensation on a twofold ground: a) on the biological ground that an animal life is wholly unable to subsist without sensations of resistance; b) on the consciousness-theoretical ground that we are never able to attain to the consciousness of any other mode of sensation, such as for instance the sensations of smell, taste, sight, and hearing, without referring it to the consciousness of our sensations of resistance as their unavoidably necessary presupposition (substratum). Since therefore the consciousness of all the remaining modes of sensation is a wholly nonindependent and dependent consciousness, and indeed always points to the only independent consciousness of sensation, namely to the consciousness of our sensations of resistance: every doctrine of sensation should above all point to this fundamental difference between the independent and the nonindependent modes of sensation and emphasize with the greatest insistence the quite exceptional position of our sensations of resistance. It belongs among the sins of sensualistic or impressionistic philosophy that it mechanically dismembers our life of sensation and accustoms us to speak of various modes of sensation, of say five, six, or more senses, without drawing our attention to the fact that there is only one kind of full sensation, only one kind of independent consciousness of sensation, upon which all other kinds of consciousness of sensation are able to climb up as it were like creeping plants about the trunk of a tree. The expression used by Helmholtz, "modality of sensation," by which he wishes to designate the difference of the various circles of sense (hearing, sight, smell, and so forth), is likewise in a high degree suited to concealing that first and most fundamental difference between the modes of sensation which expresses itself in this, that we can have a consciousness of sensations of resistance without having to support ourselves therein upon the consciousness of other modes of sensation; whereas it would be impossible to form a consciousness of sensations of taste, smell, hearing, and so forth without having first formed a consciousness of resistance. For when we speak of material things, of a material world, of bodies which are endowed with properties, there comes to expression herein in the first place that fundamental character of our human consciousness, that the consciousness of the sensation of resistance is a necessary precondition of every other consciousness of sensation. Whoever therefore wishes to investigate how we arrive at the concept of a body or of matter must necessarily proceed therein from the consciousness of our experiences of resistance, and in the measure that he explores the experiences of resistance he will also be able to give us clarifications concerning the concept of body or of matter in general. Among the sensualists it was Condillac who most deeply surmised the quite exceptional position of the experiences of resistance; peculiarly, he nevertheless begins his treatise on sensations with the assumption that his fictive marble statue, which was gradually to acquire one sense after another, disposes first of sensations of smell, then of sensations of taste, and so forth. (A truly French ordering of our modes of sensation!) For he does not make clear to himself that without sensations of resistance an animal life is from the outset impossible; further he does not notice, however much he later enters upon the exceptional position of the sensations of resistance, that one cannot by any means understand the nature of the remaining modes of sensation before one seeks to understand the sensations of resistance. (Insofar as we men are able to understand our sensations at all!)

## V. Die Widerstandsempfindungen — V. Resistance Sensations

V. The Sensations of Resistance. In the investigation of our consciousness of resistance we must not proceed from those sensations of resistance which are occasioned by the pressing of foreign bodies upon our body, but must above all consider those sensations of resistance which one member of our body awakens in another member of the same: as when, for example, we grasp the left hand with the right. This method is of importance because without the consciousness of the presence of our own body we should never be able to penetrate to the consciousness of the existence of other bodies or of the material world in general. Our consciousness of our own body plays everywhere a decisive role also in the consciousness of any foreign body, so that the formation of the latter without that of the former would be an impossibility. But we find our own body only through sensations of resistance which one part of it calls forth in another part of our organism. Accordingly we shall divide our sensations of resistance into two classes: into the experiences of self-resistance and the experiences of foreign resistance, of which the former have a fundamental significance also for the latter. If then in our whole life of sensation we must distinguish well the stem-sensations of resistance from the branch-sensations of hearing, sight, and so forth, it is further necessary within the stem-sensations to bring out quite particularly, as their kernel, the sensations of self-resistance, because these form the foundation for the formation of our concept of a material world. These very sensations of self-resistance also instruct us that they could never be awakened without movements of the body. Only a body which is able to move itself, or which possesses movable members, can precisely in consequence of its movements experience the sensations of self-resistance: but were our whole body as well as its individual parts to lack all mobility, then it would also never be possible for us to become acquainted with any sensations of a self-resistance. Without mobility of the body there are no sensations of self-resistance; without these there is no consciousness of the presence of our own body; without this consciousness there is also no consciousness of a foreign body or of the material world in general: consequently without the movements of our body we could never penetrate to the consciousness of an external world. Herewith a fundamental proposition of the doctrine of consciousness is proved, namely the proposition that movements are conditions of human consciousness, and hence of human knowledge, just as indispensable as sensation. Movement and sensation move into the same line not only for the investigator of animal life but also for the theorist of human consciousness. We here clearly recognize the fundamental infirmity of every sensualistic or impressionistic philosophy. Impressionism is not only a doctrine shy of the mind but also a doctrine shy of movement, for it not only strives to replace mental acts by processes of sensation, but also stops up its ears when there is talk of movement, whether in order to evade this inconvenient problem, or, in case of need, in order to reduce movement to sensation. Hereby modern impressionism acquires that soft, gelatinous, spineless character which makes it wholly unfit to furnish an epistemological foundation for the natural sciences. The investigator of nature is a man who can by no means be called shy of movement but on the contrary most joyful in movement, because he knows very well that all observing and in particular all experimenting is an impossibility without the execution of movements. For precisely this reason we must wonder in a high degree that impressionism was able to penetrate into natural science and to win a leading role in it. For impressionism, as it proceeded from Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, is really no genuine empiricist but on the contrary a speculative philosophy, and indeed it chooses for the object of its groundless speculations the sensations quite in the same manner as some philosophers carry on a speculation with "concepts." The speculative philosophy of sensation begins by declaring sensations to be something simple and thus making every investigation of them impossible in principle, and it arrives on this path at divesting them in a more or less determinate manner of their vital (biological) character and consequently tearing them out of their insoluble connection with the processes of movement of the living being. The joy in groundless speculations shy of movement and of experiment must already have penetrated very deeply into the natural sciences if it became possible for impressionism (or also Berkeleyan idealism) to nest itself in them. Incidentally, Kant too stands upon an impressionistic foundation when he designates in sensible knowledge the sensations as matter and space and time as form; for he does indeed strive, through the introduction of "forms" and "categories," to set a barrier to impressionism. But how much he himself remains captive in the doctrines of impressionism comes to expression not only in this, that he lets the sensations flow in consciousness (spiritualizes, desensualizes them) just as much as Berkeley, but that he makes them the sole matter of sensible knowledge and does not designate our movements too as matter of knowledge. Now I am by no means of the opinion that the expressions matter of knowledge or form of knowledge are scientifically usable concepts, but wish merely to point out that criticism too does not set our movements upon a line with our sensations, which may pass as the most conspicuous mark of the impressionistic mode of thought. The sensations of self-resistance, which after all form the foundation of our whole consciousness of sensation, show us most clearly how inseparable the life of sensation is in its ultimate root from the motor life. If, for example, we grasp the left hand with the right—which is not possible without movement—then we arrive at sensations of self-resistance, and the accounting for this complicated process of sensation compels us to think of it as consisting of four processes. We shall never wholly illuminate the sense-confusing tangle of these four processes, for every sensation contains a boundless mystery, but a doctrine of sensation as a science is possible only in the measure that we succeed in disentangling this tangle. But there must be talk of four processes within the sensations of self-resistance for this reason, that we must place two processes both into the grasped and into the grasping hand. I shall for brevity's sake designate the two processes in the grasped left hand as the left processes, those in the right hand on the contrary as the right processes. But we must speak of two left processes because we distinguish the process of impression (which is produced by the pressure of the right hand) from the motor process by virtue of which the left hand returns the pressure of the right. There thus fight with one another in the left hand two nervous processes, of which the one has a centripetal, the other on the contrary a centrifugal character. The swelling of the process of impression and the swelling of the counterpressure may run their course somehow rhythmically in small intervals of time: we are, however, generally able to perceive nothing of this rhythm. In the right hand too we must distinguish two nervous processes, a motor and a sensory one, of which the same holds as we asserted of the left processes. But if we compare the right double process with the left, we may say that in the right double process the motor process, in the left on the contrary the sensory one, is predominant, since after all the right hand was the grasping and the left the grasped. If we designate the sensory and motor double process in the left hand by s and m, in the right hand by S and M, then there subsists between (s, m) on the one hand and (S, M) on the other an opposition of the same kind as that between s and m, or also between S and M. In other words: the opposition which subsists between the two processes in one hand presents itself also as an opposition between the two double processes in both hands. One will perhaps take offense that I conceive the double processes which take place in each hand as consisting of a sensory and a motor one: probably because one is accustomed to speak of motor nervous processes only where a visible process of movement is present. One should, however, consider that motor nervous processes can present themselves not only in movements but in pressure and counterpressure, pull and counterpull, that is, in static form. Further I wish to remark that between the sensations of self-resistance and those of self-contact there subsists no difference of principle. If I touch, for example, the left hand with the right, then in both hands similar double processes will run their course as those designated above. For even upon the mere (perceptible) touching of the left hand this offers a counterpressure, even if a weak one, or more correctly speaking: a mere touching of the left hand suffices to call awake in it that fight between a sensory and a motor nervous process which I have set forth above. Impressionism thus gives us a false concept of our sensations of resistance or also of our sensations of contact when it sets them forth as simple, purely sensory processes within our consciousness. For firstly they are not simple, secondly they do not flow within our consciousness, and thirdly they are not purely sensory. Sensations of resistance or also of contact can never take place without motor nervous processes having to be drawn into sympathy. This the anatomist and histologist will very soon concede to me, for the juxtaposed arrangement of the sensory and motor nerve fibers or fibrils suggests to them in intuitive fashion the thought of a cooperation of the two in the arising of the sensations of resistance and of contact; indeed, they will perhaps also at once construct that circular current composed of two opposed semicircles through which the sensation of resistance or of contact comes about. It is important thereby to keep before one's eyes that not every motor nervous current which runs its course in some motor fibril must at once also have as consequence the contraction of some powerful bundle of muscles, hence at once also a visible movement. What I sought above to establish for the sensation of self-resistance or of self-contact holds in part also for the sensation of foreign resistance or of foreign contact. Even when my hand is touched by a foreign body, there arise in my hand not only sensory but also motor nervous processes: only it is in methodical respect most inexpedient, in the consideration of sensations of contact, to proceed in the first rank from foreign contact, as the psychologists of the impressionistic school are wont to do. For if I am to form a correct concept of sensations of contact, then the touching body must be to me something as well known as the touched hand: that is, the touching body must be a part of my own body. Inasmuch as I, for example, grasp or touch the left hand with the right, what is touched stands as near to me as what touches, while in foreign contact the touching body is precisely something foreign to me, which conceals within itself a new problem, with which new problem I must not mingle the anyhow so difficult problem of contact. In order therefore to arrive at even a tolerable concept of the sensations of resistance, I must first eliminate the problem which lies in the concept of a foreign body and take into consideration merely the sensation of self-resistance. For only in the sensation of self-resistance do I clearly perceive that I have to do with a double process, indeed with a twofold double process, so that it will later be easy for me to furnish the proof that the sensations of foreign resistance too consist of a kind—even if another kind—of quadruple process. In order therefore to set the sensations of foreign resistance in a correct light, we must first consider more closely the quadruple process of the sensation of self-resistance. If I touch the left hand with the right, then at bottom I become conscious merely of one sensation, which may indeed pass for a wondrous fact, since after all the sensory nerve of the right hand does not break through the epidermis both of the right and of the left hand in order to continue itself in the latter. This circumstance alone should have set the impressionistic thinkers in highest astonishment and held them back from regarding a sensation as something simple. Were the right hand suddenly to grow together with the left, and were the sensory nerves too on both sides, penetrating the skin, suddenly able to grow together with one another, then it would seemingly be more comprehensible that at the place of contact of the two hands merely one experience of sensation announces itself: peculiarly, however, precisely in the case of the organic growing-together of the two places of contact no sensation would be felt at them, because we generally place our sensations of resistance not in the middle of a nerve thread but at the peripheral surface of the skin. Thus precisely because the sensory nerve thread from the right hand does not continue itself directly into the left, a sensation can be felt at the place of contact of the two hands. Precisely because different nervous processes come into relation to one another, an apparently simple experience of sensation can proceed out of their union. But that the experience of sensation thus arisen is only apparently simple, of this we can convince ourselves through exceedingly simple but for that very reason all the more instructive experiments. If I touch the left hand with the right, then in general I have at first merely one sensation, within which neither the left nor the right hand appears particularly accentuated. But in order particularly to accentuate the left hand, one need only—without giving up the contact of the two hands—exert a sudden pressure with the right hand against the left, whereby an intense sensation of resistance is called forth which is distinctly referred to the left hand. If the pressure of the right hand against the left is exerted several times in rapid succession, then there arises at the place of contact a corresponding series of sensations which are all conceived as sensations of the left hand; for although sensations appear also in the pressing right hand, these are less accentuated and do not come into consideration. From this experiment we can draw the following significant lessons: 1. Even when the two hands touch one another only weakly, now the right hand against the left, now the left hand against the right, may exert a preponderant pressure, and some rhythm may subsist between pressure and counterpressure, which remains unperceived only because the corresponding motor processes (which are accompanied by an invisible vibrating of the hands) are weak and because they also last too short a time and consequently flow together. 2. Since the often repeated pressure of the right hand against the left awakens an equally often repeated sensation in the left hand, we convince ourselves immediately that the exertion of a pressure, hence of a motor process, must be conceived as the condition for the appearance of a process of sensation in the left hand. I am able to interrupt the state of sensation of the moment of my left hand through a motor process of the right hand and to introduce a new state of sensation in the left hand. Every new pressure with the right hand puts an end to the subsisting state of sensation of the left hand and gives occasion for a new sensation in it. This simple consideration leads us to an improved conception of the process of sensation, in particular to an improved conception of the sensation of self-resistance. For what I sense is always the interruption of a subsisting and the introduction of a new state of sensation through a motor process. The view that we could ever perceive a purely sensory current out of relation to a differently constituted (namely motor) current is a scholastic aberration which has arisen through the artificial tearing apart of the concepts of sensation and movement. Sensory currents flow continually in the sensory nerve fibrils of the living body, but they generally remain unperceivable. There must in each case take place some interruption in the sensory tonus of a fibril in order that we may arrive at a sensation. This interruption we are able ourselves to bring about, in that we, for example, let a motor impulse of the right hand act upon the left and thereby introduce an alteration in the sensory tonus of the sensation-capable fibrils of the left hand. In short, we perceive a vital process not through itself but with the help of another vital process. In particular we can perceive sensations of self-resistance always only as interruptions of our tonus of sensation, which are conditioned by motor impulses and referred to these. We arrive herewith at a proposition concerning our vital processes which is analogous to the fundamental proposition which we formulated concerning human awareness. No act of our consciousness has tidings of itself, that is, no act bears awareness within itself; only the connection of temporally successive acts, that is, the relatedness of act to act, creates human awareness. Or in other words: one act lends to another an awareness which the latter could not possess for itself. From this principle of the theory of consciousness alone we could have inferred immediately what we have now found through the consideration of the sensations of self-resistance: namely that no vital process can be taken cognizance of out of connection with another vital process. No vital process is a process subsisting in isolation for itself, but it is a vital process only through the connection in which it stands with the whole life-process. Accordingly a vital process makes itself known to our consciousness always through the connection with others. In particular, a sensation of any self-resistance can never make itself known otherwise than by means of a motor impulse to which it is related as its condition. This is the principle of the relativity of human perception, which is only a special case of the more general principle, namely of the relativity of human awareness in general. Every human awareness is relative, that is, only the relation of the one act to the other, only the relatedness of the one act to the other creates awareness. Accordingly every human perception is a relative one, that is, a vital process can be perceived only through its relation to another vital process.

## VI. Vitalistische Naturauffassung — VI. Vitalist vs. Mechanist

VI. Vitalistic and Mechanistic. In the preceding section we have drawn from the proposition of the boundless compositeness of our sensations all the consequences which result from it without difficulty. Thus we arrived at a sharp distinction between the physical-vital process of sensation and the punctual acts of our consciousness. We were able to set up the principle of discontinuity (or of the finitely swift pulse of consciousness) as the fundamental principle of a scientific doctrine of consciousness, and to formulate or at least indicate such important corollaries of this principle as 1. the proposition of the punctuality of the acts of consciousness, 2. the proposition of the relatedness of the one act to the other (proposition of awareness), 3. the proposition of double experiences, 4. the proposition of the qualitatively different stages of awareness, and so forth. There opened up for us also prospects upon such significant propositions as, for example, the proposition of the constancy of the typical character of human awareness for all future, further the proposition of the boundless deepenability of human awareness or of the boundless capacity for progress of the human mind. But if we wish to advance further in our investigations, then we must learn to distinguish vital processes from mechanical processes. For we have distinguished the punctual acts of consciousness merely from temporally flowing physical processes in general, without however also taking in hand the distinction of a physical-vital from a physical-mechanical process, wherefore all our considerations hitherto had to remain in need of a very important supplement. For what a vital process is shows itself only through its distinction from a mechanical process. Herewith we arrive at the fundamental problem of all natural science (or natural philosophy) in general, whose treatment would make necessary the stirring up of all the depths and shallows of the biological as well as the mechanistic sciences. But since in this writing it is in the first rank a matter of the doctrine of consciousness and only in the second rank of the riddle of life, it will here suffice to enter upon the riddle of life only insofar as this is necessary for the establishment of a distinguishing mark between our vital processes and the mechanical ones. But how the mechanical processes and the vital processes interlock, or the manner and way in which the vital and mechanical happenings are united with one another, is an infinite problem which is not yet to be treated in this writing. It must after all first be settled whether we have any right at all to distinguish vital and mechanical processes, in order that then the manner and way of their linkage may also be investigated. It is thus the great disputed question between the vitalistic and the mechanistic conception of nature that interests us in the following investigations: to be sure, not in the sense in which this dispute has hitherto been conducted between the two great parties. For it is already settled for us that there are vital processes; for we do after all have a consciousness of our sensations, and these sensations are after all vital processes. Unfortunately there prevails in modern natural science the conception that sensations are psychic processes: an error which was carried into natural science by impressionistic philosophy and whose uncovering formed a chief part of the investigations of the first section. Nothing is more certain than that we have a consciousness, and nothing can be so certain for those who have a human consciousness as that they live, as that they have experiences, sensations. Descartes's proposition, I think, therefore I am [cogito ergo sum], is really meant to say: I am wholly certain that I have a consciousness (that I think); but this thoroughly correct proposition should at once be supplemented by the proposition: as certainly as I have a consciousness, so certainly am I also in possession of experiences (for example of sensations). Now Descartes, however, wished only to emphasize the having of a consciousness, and in his rationalistic overzealousness he believed he could bring this about only by voting at one stroke the highest mistrust to our whole life of sensation. In order to establish the certainty of thinking (of consciousness), this great thinker supposed he had to proceed from the uncertainty of all sensing (hence of the vital processes): a fatal undertaking, of whose evil consequences the whole of modern philosophy and natural science still ails. Inasmuch as Descartes tore consciousness and life (thinking and sensing) apart, he uprooted not only the life of sensation but also that so ardently adored thinking whose self-certainty had lain so much at his heart. For his one-sided procedure unchained the most violent and in part very justified contradiction of all minds enamored of sensation, and so it could not fail that the mighty wave of sensualistic reaction sought to displace consciousness (thinking) altogether by sensing and to stamp the latter itself as a thinking. Hereby, however, not only was the nature of human consciousness again wholly obscured, but the certainty of the existence of vital processes was likewise veiled. For even if modern impressionism acknowledges nothing so much as the having of sensations, yet herewith again only the certainty of consciousness and not the certainty of vital processes was emphasized. Thus it seems downright as though rationalism and impressionism had conspired with one another to let no doctrine of the vital processes arise. It is therefore most gratifying to see that in our days a deep-penetrating neovitalistic current is in train (compare, for example, the instructive and interesting work of Hans Driesch: Vitalism as History and as Doctrine), but it seems to me that the manner of combat of the vitalists is an unphilosophical or at least a mystical one, inasmuch as they give themselves quite unheard-of trouble to demonstrate the presence of vital processes in such a way that they proceed first from the existence of mechanical processes. Through this perverse procedure, however, they always deliver only new weapons into the hands of their opponents, the mechanists. For whoever sets forth the existence of mechanical processes as what is immediately certain thereby manifests that he is not convinced of the certainty of his own vital functions, and he will also never be able to succeed in proving the presence of his vital processes. It seems to me that in philosophy no other path stands open than that which was trodden by Augustine and Descartes, namely the point of departure from one's own consciousness; only one must seek to get beyond the rationalism of those thinkers by learning to distinguish one's acts of consciousness from the vital processes to which they refer, in recognizing the former as punctual, the latter as temporally flowing. Through this distinction not only consciousness itself but also the life-process united with it is established and raised beyond every doubt. Only when the difference of act of consciousness and life-process is recognized can the great (infinite) problem be raised how punctual acts are really united with flowing processes, how the mind is united with the physical life-process, and what common origin they have. But before one has sharply distinguished the mental act from physical life, it is also not possible to raise a question with regard to their union and their common origin, that is, one cannot be filled with any monistic striving with regard to the mental and the physical before one has distinguished the mental from the physical. But it is further not expedient to pass over at once from the fundamental distinction of act of consciousness and vital process to the infinite problem of their unitedness and their common origin, for one must first still clarify the concept of the physical process by showing how we arrive precisely by means of our vital processes at the knowledge of mechanical processes, and how there could be no vital processes at all if they were not united with mechanical processes. Certain, then, for the philosopher is in the first place the existence of his vital processes, and supported upon this certainty he strives also to furnish the demonstration of the existence of the mechanical processes united with the vital ones, which happens through the uncovering of the difference of the two united processes. At first glance it seems as though there could be only vital processes, but this is an illusion which we must now uncover. Children in fact behave as though they lent their awareness and liveliness to unconscious and dead things, but their behavior rests only upon this, that in consequence of the low stage of their awareness they have scarcely yet emerged beyond the consciousness of their own vital processes, and that the difference between awareness and unconsciousness as well as between liveliness and lifelessness is for them still only a dawning difference. With the ripening of its consciousness the child indeed soon comes to develop and consolidate that dawning distinction in the measure that its practical needs of life require, but the difference proper between liveliness and lifelessness remains in the theoretical sense a riddle as before. Accordingly there are also thinkers who will not acknowledge such a difference at all and incline toward a theory of universal animation (panvitalism). Inasmuch as they declare all processes of nature to be vital processes of a boundless whole of nature and everywhere behold only gradations of an everywhere present life-process, they believe they have gained a "monistic" standpoint. Truly an enviable standpoint, for it relieves us of all trouble of reflecting upon the difference between liveliness and lifelessness, and moreover clothes this divine indolence of thought of ours in the exalted mantle of a boundless philosophical profundity. Aesthetes and belletrists may find pleasure in such play and declaim grandiose phrases about the universal animation of nature, for with them it is after all not a matter of real thoughts but merely of an effective, large-scale pose with whose help they awaken the appearance of profundity among the multitude. But whoever is in earnest about truth knows very well that there is no such exalted pose as could relieve us of the necessity of distinguishing thought. Only he who has attempted actually to distinguish the living from the lifeless, and who knows how to exhibit the distinguishing mark of the two, has also gained a right to trace out the common origin of all that is living and all that is lifeless and to strive after a genuine monistic conception of the processes of nature. But so long as the distinguishing mark of vital process and mechanical process is not set forth, one has in science no right whatever to speak of vital and mechanical processes (because precisely the scientific concepts for this are lacking), and there can consequently be no talk either of a monistic conception of them. For him who has not distinguished there can also be nothing different which he could unite. For him who has not sown there is also no harvest (at least not where it is a matter of the knowledge of truth). The doctrine of universal animation passes over in modern thinkers quite unnoticed into a doctrine of universal ensoulment (panpsychism) or even of universal spiritualization (panlogism). But one must not let oneself be disconcerted by such transitions, for it is after all a fundamental trait of modern thinking that it makes the confusion of life and consciousness downright into a principle, and so one need not wonder that the theory of universal animation is able in a trice to assume also the look of a panlogism. The error which comes to a breakthrough in the panvitalistic mode of thought is moreover still more tolerable than the panmechanistic aberration. For it is philosophically still to be comprehended and excused that a child of man, who after all can stand on so familiar a footing with nothing as with his own liveliness, lulls himself theoretically into the pretty, naive (to be sure also foolish) illusion that all processes of nature are vital processes: but the opposite conception, which would make the world into an infinite machine, is a mode of thought not only logically perverse but also in aesthetic respect quite tasteless. As regards comfortableness, however, the panmechanistic mode of thought does not lag behind the panvitalistic, for whoever bluntly declares the world to be a machine also need reflect no further upon the riddle of life. But whoever in thinking is above all concerned with "comfortableness"—this peculiar inclination has in our days received the distinguished designation of "economy of thought"—would do well to let thinking be altogether. I am unable to imagine any higher "economy of thought" than one which gives up thinking altogether. Nothing can be more certain than that we have a consciousness, and nothing stands firmer for our consciousness than the relation of our acts of consciousness to our vital processes. Inasmuch, then, as in the first section of this writing I set forth the difference between act of consciousness and vital process, I believe to have won the foundation not only for a scientific doctrine of consciousness but also for a scientific biology. To be sure, it still remains to demonstrate the presence of mechanical processes, because the concept of a biological (vital) process is not established so long as one does not exhibit the difference of vital and mechanical processes. A genuine vitalistic doctrine can come about only if at the same time room is created also for a genuine mechanistic conception of the processes of nature. Vitalistic and mechanistic consideration condition one another reciprocally, but in epistemological research the demand for vital processes precedes the demand for mechanical processes.

## VII. Der moderne Idealismus — VII. Modern Idealism

VII. Modern Idealism. We have recognized it as the fundamental aberration of modern thinking that it systematically confuses the mental act with the biological process—in particular with the sensation of the senses. Now the possibility of every theory of knowledge depends, however, upon whether one knows how to distinguish mental acts from vital processes; for if mere vital activity is equivalent to mental deed, then between the science of life (biology, physiology) and the science of the mental act (doctrine of consciousness, theory of knowledge) no difference can any longer be made. It is therefore downright the vital question of the theory of knowledge and of philosophy in general whether the life-process is distinguished from the act of consciousness or not. Popularly expressed: it is the question of existence of all philosophy whether the "tree of life" is confused with the "tree of knowledge" or not. One would, however, misunderstand me in the most thoroughgoing manner were one to believe that I wish to separate the doctrine of the acts of consciousness (theory of knowledge) from the science of the vital processes (biology) as though these two domains of knowledge could subsist independently of one another. On the contrary, it belongs to the fundamental character of these and the following investigations that I deny the possibility of a pure "theory of knowledge." It is impossible to investigate the nature of human consciousness without taking regard for the structure of the human organism and the physiological function of its organs; on the other hand, however, the anatomical structure of the human body and the vital activity of its organs also remains unintelligible if one wishes wholly to abstract from the fact that this body and its whole life-process is bound up with a personal consciousness. The further we advance in the anatomical and physiological knowledge of our body, the deeper insights shall we gain also into the nature of our consciousness, and conversely from the exploration of our consciousness there will proceed the most powerful stimulus to the knowledge of the anatomical-physiological constitution of our bodily organization. Already our considerations thus far show that we seek to set the epistemological and the physiological consideration in the most intimate reciprocal connection. Inasmuch as we recognized that vital processes have a temporally flowing character, it also became manifest to us that the acts of our consciousness are distinguished by an instantaneous (punctual-discontinuous) character. The light which illuminated the life-process was necessarily at the same time a light which brought us clarification about our consciousness. Our life-process is, to express it popularly, not wholly and continuously glowed and lit through by mental activity; there occur mental darkenings of the life-process, as for example sleep shows. But even the waking state of man is not continuously and uniformly lit through by mental activity; rather this mental illumination has a certain alternating pulse. While our vital processes flow on without interruption, the acts of our consciousness never have a continuous flux but are always of a punctual nature. This peculiar relation of the life-process to the acts of consciousness can also be characterized by saying that the vital processes are indeed conditions of our acts of consciousness, but that through their stream they also hinder us in reflection, insofar as they do not permit us to perform arbitrarily many acts of consciousness in the second. I hereby touch upon the great problem of the connection between life-process and act of consciousness (between body and soul), without being able at once also to enter upon it. Let only so much be emphasized here already, that every investigation which wishes to consider acts of consciousness and knowledge in general independently of the life-process is from the outset a wholly hopeless one, because all our acts of consciousness are immersed in the stream of our life-processes and shine as it were only through this stream. This is the principle of the immanence of the punctual mental acts in the continuous stream of life. But precisely because it lies wholly far from me to tear consciousness and life-process apart, I must lay the greatest weight upon an exact distinction of the act of consciousness and the vital process. For only he who makes exact distinctions can also point out the unitary connection of what has been distinguished. If someone does not distinguish the a from a b, how will he speak of a unitary connection between a and b? There are so-called "monistic thinkers"—I call them sham monists—who believe that one need only deny every difference between a and b in order thereby to have unified them. In this sense one would have simply to deny every difference which subsists for us between the things of this world in order to win the laurel of a perfected monist. Truly a leisurely monism, which, denying the difference of white and black and then also all the remaining differences of this world, gives up the trouble of every distinction and cuts off all thinking with the proposition: All is one (that is, there are nowhere differences). Although this singular kind of monism crops up very frequently among philosophers, it nevertheless seems to me unnecessary to waste further words upon it. To hold fast to all existing differences with the most scrupulous exactitude and yet unremittingly to strive after the unity of our knowledge: this seems to me the only rational monistic urge. If then we wish somehow to learn to comprehend the unitary connection of consciousness and life-process, the indispensable precondition for this is that consciousness be distinguished from the life-process with the greatest sharpness. Now the impressionistic theory of knowledge, as it was founded by Locke, proceeds downright from the confusion of the act of consciousness with the vital process, which—as was already remarked above—comes to expression in the first place in this, that sensations are designated as ideas. By virtue of this designation sensation plays over into the thoughtlike, but conversely also the thoughtlike into sensation (or the vital process). This playing over and back of thought into sensation and of sensation into thought became in a twofold sense a fundamental infirmity of modern philosophizing in general. For every confusion of concepts admits of a twofold reading: either the concept a is exorcised out of the understanding by the concept b, or conversely the concept b by the concept a. Accordingly one can either simply declare the acts of consciousness to be vital processes (impressions) or conversely set forth vital processes as though they were mental acts. The one reading sinks all mental activity into the physiological process, the other raises the vital processes to mental acts. At bottom it is in both readings a matter of the same logical error; but as every logical error can assume a twofold coloring of temperament or feeling, so too the confusion of mental act and vital process. To the one it gives pleasure to "degrade" the mental act to a mere physiological process; the other supposes he becomes blessed by "raising" vital processes (sensations) to mental acts. But we must not let ourselves be disconcerted by such degradations and elevations, for they have one and the same confusion of concepts as their common root. The materialist, who would resolve everything mental into physiology, and the idealist, who would resolve all physiology into mentality, may according to their life of temperament perhaps pass for opposed poles; according to their activity of understanding, however, there subsists between them no difference whatever, for the one as much as the other suffers from the infirmity of confusing the concepts of the mental act with the vital process. Locke himself confuses the two fundamental concepts in such a manner that, now resolving thought into sensation, now sensation into thought, he seems at one time to be a materialist, at another an idealist, and thus rocks to and fro between two opposites which at bottom nevertheless constitute but one error. Thus it becomes intelligible that his sensualism assumes in his successors a twofold coloring: a properly sensualistic or physiological (materialistic) coloring (as for example in the French sensualists, positivists, and materialists), which conceives all mentality as physiological activity of our bodily organization, and an idealistic coloring, which conversely idealizes the physiological functions of our life, namely the processes of sensation, that is, sets them forth as thought-processes, as this comes to light in the philosophy of Berkeley. The confusion of concepts which in Locke thus bore a half-dark, ambiguous character became in Berkeley an unambiguous and sharply emphasized tendency to let the sensible if possible be absorbed into mentality. The opposition of the modern impressionistic to the ancient symbolistic logic, which in Locke still remained half-veiled, is now suddenly set by Berkeley in a kind of Bengal illumination. For the ancient mind attempted to ground the theory of knowledge upon the sign-forming or language-forming activity of the human mind: for it the sense of these signs, the sense of human speech, and in particular the sense of the individual words, that is, the concept, was what is properly ideational; Berkeley, however, goes as is well known so far as simply to deny the presence of abstract concepts and to stamp the sensation of the senses as what is properly ideational. It is he, then, who drives the opposition between old and new philosophy to a head. He is the originator of a "modern idealism" of which the ancients let themselves dream nothing. To be sure, Greek sophistry in general and quite particularly that of Protagoras already labors under a confusion of mental activity with the function of sense, but this confusion was far rather sensualistically tinged. It was reserved for our modern age to let sensuality shine in the halo of ideality. Only we moderns weave a supersensible wreath of rays about our sensible processes, and this not only in the plastic arts and belles-lettres but also in science and philosophy. The peculiar mania of setting forth the function of sense as though it were of a mental nature seems to me to be the quintessence of all modern secession from ancient idealism (Plato's). In this sense we may perhaps designate Berkeley as the father of modern secessionist thinking, and I believe that the so-called secession in the plastic arts and belles-lettres of our days points back to that confusion of concepts of the sensible and the ideational which was raised by Berkeley to the highest philosophical dogma.

2. We may look upon the specific idealism of the modern age with cool composure, for we see through its inner perverseness and its artificial, insincere, affected essence. Sensations are no mental deeds, that is, they do not run their course in consciousness, for if they flowed along within consciousness, then this would have to mean that their smallest temporal segments too were known to us. We should then have an infinitely fine power of perception, and nature could conceal nothing any longer before our boundlessly developed, so to speak divine, scent. In general there take place in our mind or our consciousness no so-called processes at all, because a mind or consciousness knows only how to accomplish actions, only how to carry out acts. In our bodily organism so-called processes can indeed take place, but a mind, a consciousness, is able to react to those vital processes only through instantaneous acts. For a consciousness is nothing spatial, nothing extended, hence no theater for all manner of wondrous tales and psychological adventures, as the modern disciples of Locke and Berkeley love to depict for themselves. When we speak of "processes in consciousness," this is a purely metaphorical manner of expression by which we really wish only to say that the vital processes of our body are taken cognizance of through instantaneous acts of our consciousness. Now it is, however, a universally known chief source of all logical aberrations that we are more or less inclined to take metaphorical turns of speech in the literal sense. An educated man will indeed not wish to conceive the metaphor referring to God, "The earth is the footstool of His feet," in the literal sense, but it can be demonstrated that even the most advanced natural science, mathematics, and philosophy teem with metaphors which are taken in the literal sense and consequently cause an incurable confusion in men's minds. For we use our metaphors so long that through their being worn out they fade, wither, and weather to such a degree that we, not noticing their metaphorical character, become despite our astonishing education and erudition amiable children who really conceive the earth as a footstool for God's feet. Thus we accustom ourselves, for instance, so long to the metaphor that "within our consciousness" processes, phenomena, and further wonders of the sort take place, until, in all earnest forgetting the metaphorical character of our own phrases, we fall victim to childish phantasmagorias about the nature of our consciousness. Many a one pictures human consciousness to himself as a kind of blast furnace in which, instead of iron ore, ores of sensation together with all manner of mental coal, mental lime, and other fluxes are charged and mixed, so that the flames of our activity of thought may be able to purify the iron of knowledge from the slag of knowledge. Others may transpose into consciousness whirring looms, with warps and wefts playing over and back, with rollers and cloth beams, heddles and treadles, cords and straps, levers and pulleys, and so on and so forth. In short, the activity of human consciousness lets itself be symbolized by innumerable similes, and provided only that these similes are painted quite vividly and with poetic power of formation, it is out of the question that they could become in any way dangerous to scientific knowledge. Dangerous for scientific thinking are only the dried-up, withered, and soured metaphors which have lost the last trace of their youthful imagery and which, supported upon their penetrating rancidity, come forward with such highly scientific gravity that they dominate the thinking precisely of the educated and learned classes often for centuries. Whoever, for example, has accustomed himself to speak of "phenomena within consciousness" will no longer easily be helped, for he no longer becomes aware of the metaphorical nature of his shriveled turn of speech and consequently behaves in his thinking like a man who takes some carved idol for a real god. The consequences of the manner of speech that appearances take place in our consciousness hardly need to be discussed at length. For immature children and primitive fetish-worshippers are characterized precisely by this, that they confuse metaphorical with proper speech, that is, take in the literal sense what should be understood only by way of simile. The consequences of that conception, that "within our consciousness" processes or phenomena are able to take place, are almost indescribable. For if someone assumes that in his consciousness some processes run their course, then he means thereby that it is these processes of which he can have a knowledge, whereas all that may take place "outside his consciousness" must remain wholly unknown, indeed that we have in general not the slightest right to speak of anything outside our consciousness, since we are after all able to know nothing of it. Since now the so-called real or material things of which naive humanity is wont to speak are supposed to be found not within but outside consciousness, it is clear that a modern impressionist or idealist, who is able to take cognizance only of such adventures as take place in the chambers of his consciousness, must necessarily deny the existence of real or material things. In short, if that of which we are to have knowledge must necessarily be a "process within our consciousness," then it is impossible that we could have knowledge also of what lies outside our consciousness, that is, it is pure nonsense to speak of real or material things which supposedly come to lie outside our consciousness. It can only be a superstition, such as is wont to haunt the heads of learned gentlemen, that there could be such a thing as a material substance outside our consciousness, opines the ingenious Berkeley. He is thus not content with the paradoxical doctrine that there can be no matter, but crowns this doctrine with the second, still more ingenious paradox that such an abstract concept as matter can have its origin only in the sinciput of highly learned gentlemen, but that an uncorrupted, sound, childlike human understanding can never have tidings of a matter but always only of its sensations or perceptions. If, then, the secessionist mode of thought consists in standing the truth if possible bolt upright upon its head, then I hold that herein Berkeley has accomplished the highest that a child of the modern age is able to accomplish at all. And it is only stylistically fitting that it had to be precisely a bishop—the magnanimous, subtle, and ingenious Bishop of Cloyne—who brings before our view, at least philosophically, the quintessence of all modernity. Whoever confuses the processes of sensation with mental acts, whoever therefore conceives them as processes within consciousness, can nevermore avoid simply denying away everything supposedly real or material (or substantial) which is supposed to be found outside consciousness. The doctrine that we can have knowledge only of what is "immanent" to our consciousness has as its necessary consequence that we can know nothing of what is "transcendent" for consciousness, and that in consequence of our absolute ignorance of anything "consciousness-transcendent" we must necessarily deny the existence of such a thing. Sensations and what is composed of sensations (the famous "representations") are immanent to our consciousness; they are what we have tidings of, they are actuality proper, but so-called material things or substances, which supposedly are found outside our consciousness, that is, which would be transcendent for consciousness, cannot exist at all. One should think that so absurd a consequence as the denial of the existence of material things would have to make thinkers attentive to the fact that the premise from which that consequence was derived, namely the proposition of the consciousness-immanence of sensations (or representations), might be suffering from some logical infirmity. Now, however, nothing seemed more self-evident than the unhappy shriveled metaphor that our sensations were processes within our consciousness (hence mental processes), and so the root of all modern aberrations—the confusion of the vital processes with mental acts—remained subsisting with unbroken motive force. One did not rouse oneself to the thought that sensations considered in themselves are mere vital processes of our organism, and that only those acts through which we obtain tidings of these vital processes are of a mental kind. One was unable to wrestle one's way through to this conception during a period of more than a century and a half, because one held sensations to be something wholly known, hence immanent before consciousness. I therefore had above all to furnish the demonstration that sensations are by no means something wholly known, but on the contrary that the smallest temporal segments of every sensation remain unknown to us and hence cannot elapse within our consciousness. Only hereby is the impressionism of Locke and the "idealism" of Berkeley destroyed at its root, for only hereby is the immanence of sensation in consciousness, or, what says the same, the ideality of sensation, refuted. What Kant therefore attempted to accomplish under the title "Refutation of Idealism" in the second edition of his Critique of Pure Reason, but which could not succeed for him because he himself held processes of sensation to be consciousness-immanent, has been accomplished only through my theory, according to which there can be no consciousness-immanent processes whatever. We may very well speak of foods which are immanent to our stomach, but it is childish and irrational to conceive our consciousness as a kind of mental stomach within which our sensations (or representations) would be found, there to be subjected to all manner of mental-chemical processes. To forestall every misunderstanding, however, I must remark that I have nothing to object if someone speaks of consciousness in an expressly metaphorical manner as of a mental stomach; he must, however, be wholly conscious that he is transferring the image of the vital processes in the stomach to the mental acts of consciousness, and that precisely such a transference contains within itself the demand to distinguish the vital processes of digestion exactly from mental acts, for in the moment when consciousness is really conceived as a kind of stomach to which the sensations are immanent, the human understanding sinks to the stage of childish immaturity where the capacity is still lacking to let a metaphor really count only as a metaphor. The proper kernel of modern (impressionistic) idealism is formed by the proposition of the consciousness-immanence of the process of sensation, or also the proposition of the ideal nature of the sensation of the senses. This proposition, which contains a total falsification not only of the process of sensation but also of human consciousness, leads, as I showed, necessarily to the denial of all actuality or reality, for the reason that this actuality or reality has no consciousness-immanent character.

## VIII. Theorie der Phantasie — VIII. Theory of Imagination

VIII. Theory of Fantasy. One commonly thinks that the scientific investigation of fantasy has a fundamental significance merely for aesthetics, and does not notice that the doctrine of fantasy forms one of the chapters most important in principle of the theory of knowledge as well. For one supposes that fantasy is in play only when one is to transpose oneself into other mental circumstances than those in which one momentarily finds oneself in reality: but it can be proved that without fantasy it is impossible to bring to consciousness even the situation in which one really finds oneself. In other words, without fantasy there is no knowledge whatever: neither a mathematical-scientific one nor one that belongs to the domain of logic, of ethics, or of the so-called sciences of mind. Indeed, without fantasy—and this is to be particularly emphasized—there is also no taking cognizance of what surrounds us in reality, hence no seeing of colors and shapes, no hearing of tones and melodies, no observation of corporeal things through touching and grasping, in a word: no sensible perception and no kind of any lower or even higher mental activity. In order to grasp clearly this fundamental significance of fantasy for all human knowledge, one must above all reflect that most men, when there is talk of processes of fantasy, are wont to think merely of a beholding of images which hover before the mind: quite as though there were only a fantasy of sight and as though a fantasy of hearing, but in particular also a fantasy of touch, could not come into consideration at all. If one considers, however, that the man born blind also possesses fantasy, one recognizes at once what dangerous one-sidedness and aberration lies contained in letting the general concept of fantasy shrink together into the special concept of the fantasy of sight. The man born blind too can represent shapes to himself; he can produce in imagination a triangle, a quadrangle, a circle, a sphere, or another arbitrarily formed spatial structure—to be sure not in such a manner as we who see, who are in the happy position of being able also to "behold" in imagination the shapes produced in imagination. Whoever, like the man born blind, never sees the sun, never perceives with bodily eyes the forms of things, can naturally also not let his gaze glide over the outlines of things in imagination, that is, he is incapable of "beholding" these outlines even in imagination, and he remains exclusively restricted to setting those outlines before himself merely through imagined "movements of touch" of his hand. It stands otherwise with persons who did not go blind in all too early childhood, who therefore still remember having seen the sun and having rejoiced in a world full of light, color, and shape. These recognize the whole magnitude of their bitter loss, for they are able to behold in imagination what it is now denied them to behold in reality. The man born blind, however, knows indeed that something most significant is wanting to him which is given to other children of man, but he has no concept of what this thing lacking to him may be, since he is unable to see light, color, and shape even in imagination. If he represents shapes to himself, he does this not through the seeing but through the touching imagination. As he exactly palpates some body, he is able to carry out this palpation also in mere imagination and in such wise to set before himself the shape of the body in question by means of imagined movements of touch. The first and most important proposition in the doctrine of fantasy is gained through the comparison of the fantasy of the seeing and of the born-blind man. For this comparison shows us that we who see can represent spatial shapes to ourselves in two ways: once by drawing them as it were into the air before us through imagined movements of the hand, the other time by letting our gaze glide in imagination along their outlines. We come in such wise to a wholly distinct distinction of our touching and our seeing imagination and recognize at the same time that the two are bound up with one another in the most intimate manner, indeed have downright grown together with one another. If, for example, we draw a triangle before us in imagination, then we have at the same time necessarily also grasped this triangle in imagination with the gaze. The touching imagination necessarily awakens in us who see the seeing one as well, so that what is drawn in imagination is always at the same time something beheld in imagination. Only the man born blind is able to palpate something in fantasy without having also to see it in fantasy, because for him precisely the seeing fantasy is wholly wanting; he produces shapes of fantasy through the purely touching imagination, which we who see are unable to imitate, because with us the shape produced through the touching or drawing imagination is necessarily at the same time also a beheld shape. Through this proposition we gain the first deeper glance into the psychology of the man born blind. Since his sense of sight is extinguished, a piece of his fantasy too is wanting to him, namely the fantasy of sight. One can also express this by saying that the fantasy of shape of the man born blind is merely a simple one, because he is able to set spatial structures before himself merely through imagined movements of touch; the fantasy of shape of the seeing man, on the contrary, is a double one, because he sets a shape before his mind in a twofold manner: through the gaze gliding in imagination over the shape, and through the hand traveling in imagination over the shape. One may perhaps also say that the man born blind possesses an autonomous fantasy of touch, because this is capable—and to be sure also condemned—to produce shapes for itself alone, whereas the seeing man possesses no autonomous fantasy of touch, because his fantasy of touch is so coupled with his fantasy of sight that he is unable to palpate anything in imagination without also having to see it in imagination. The manner and way of the coupling of our imagination of sight and of touch deserves a thorough attention. We cannot let our imagination of touch play without having to let our imagination of sight play along; but it is a question whether this proposition holds also in the converse manner, whether namely the activity of the fantasy of sight also necessarily sets that of the fantasy of touch into co-activity, whether, when for example we behold a triangle in imagination, we must also draw it before us in imagination, or whether the fantasy of sight can be active for itself alone and wholly dispense with the assistance of the fantasy of touch. Let one not be carried away to any precipitate answer in relation to this question, and let one consider above all that the fantasy of sight in most men grows rank in a so to speak undue manner and impairs the other modes of imagination in their development and brings them to stunting. There are men whose fantasy of touch can be designated downright as withered and senile, because they neither learned to draw nor to model, nor sought to acquire any manual dexterity. These will incline to the view that the fantasy of sight can function for itself alone and wholly dispense with the assistance of the fantasy of touch. But by this view they betray only the state of their own fantasy and give occasion for the surmise that their imagination of touch was stunted through the lack of all exercise, hence that a defect adheres to their life of sense. Wholly stunted, to be sure, the fantasy of touch can become in no man whose hands and feet are not withered. One always accompanies what is seen in imagination with some remnants, however scanty, of a retracing and re-palpating movement carried out in imagination. Whoever has learned even a little to draw often catches himself retracing in imagination, quite involuntarily, the outlines of things and of the human countenances that interest him while beholding them, and the same surely befalls also such persons as otherwise have little to do with the drawing pencil. The geometer constructs the figures which he beholds in imagination through lively movements performed in imagination, and when his fantasy is inflamed by a problem particularly interesting to him, he feels a downright twitching in the hand, as though he were performing the movements by means of which he produces his imagined lines not only in imagination but in reality. Whoever has been granted a particular aptitude for the plastic arts accompanies what is seen with an excited imagination of touch which compels him to sketch or to model in fantasy before himself the appearances that captivate his interest; for it is precisely this heated production of movement running its course in imagination that vents itself with elemental force in the real movements of outward creation. Perhaps these indications suffice to stimulate the reader to a sharp separation of the imagination of touch and of sight; for one lets, as has been said, the two modes of imagination usually blur into one another, and in particular lets the imagination of touch go wholly under in the imagination of sight, whereby one at bottom uproots the concept of fantasy. For however much the imagination of touch is outstripped by the imagination of sight—the latter soars up to the stars and roams well-nigh into the boundless, while the former, bound to the clod, holds sway gropingly in the narrowest space—yet the imagination of touch must be regarded as the ground-material of our whole life of imagination, so that without it a human fantasy could not subsist at all. It stands with it as with the sense of touch, which forms the indispensable substratum of all the remaining functions of sense. The example of the blind man shows that a perception of touch can always subsist and mediate the apprehension of the material world even if the perception of sight is extinguished; but a being which from the outset possessed no perception of touch, but were equipped merely with a perception of sight, is a biological nonsense. Peculiarly, however, modern psychology will not acknowledge the primacy of the sense of touch over the remaining functions of sense, for it proceeds from the view that all kinds of sensations of the senses are equally very simple or elementary psychic processes which cannot be decomposed into any simpler processes, among which therefore no kind can have precedence over another. Just as the chemist will grant no element a primacy over the rest, so the modern psychologist too will hear nothing of a primogenitary character of the sensations of touch in comparison with the remaining sensations. Thus Wundt, for example, designates it as a prejudice of the older psychology (of the eighteenth and in part also of the nineteenth century) that it grants the sense of touch some primacy and would make it as it were the educator of the remaining functions of sense. Of such a fundamental significance of the sense of touch for the development of the remaining functions of sense there may, he holds, be no talk, and one must in the sense of modern psychology set the various domains of sense side by side with the avoidance of every distinction of rank. This conception seems to me, however, to lead to a complete decomposition of our life of sensation and hence also of our life of imagination. It will therefore be necessary to furnish a direct proof that the perception of touch is the indispensable precondition for the possibility of the development of a perception of sight, and that the imagination of touch is distinguished by a similar procedure in comparison with the remaining modes of imagination. Let us then assume for a moment that there could be a genus of living beings which had a perception of sight similar to ours as men, but which would be wholly lacking in a perception of touch, and let us attempt to picture what the immediate consequence of such a generic organization, nowhere given in experience and altogether unheard-of, would be! A being of this genus could evidently not distinguish the original image of a body from its mirror image, because it possesses no sense of touch by means of which it could convince itself that a mirror image is precisely only a mirror image and represents nothing materially graspable. For such a being our whole optics would have no sense, because it could not distinguish images which arise through reflection, refraction, or diffraction of light from the original images. It is the unavoidable presupposition of our optical knowledge that we are capable of seeing a thing at that place where it really is, that is, where it offers itself in reality to our consideration. For only a being which is capable of transposing the original image of a body by means of the perception of sight precisely to where it transposes the material existence of the body by means of the perception of touch is also able to form a concept of the fact that through reflection, refraction, or diffraction of light secondary optical images can arise also where the palpable thing in reality is not at all. The concept of the secondary optical image has as its precondition the concept of the primary optical image, and this concept has further as its precondition the palpation of the real thing, so that the primary image of sight may be transposed precisely to where the thing itself is found by the instruments of touch: hence the possession of a perception of touch is the tacit precondition for the formation of any optical knowledge. As little as there can be a roof frame which does not rest upon foundation walls, so little can there be a perception of sight which did not have a perception of touch as its necessary substratum. I have in my "Lectures on Natural Philosophy" furnished also another proof of the dependence of the perception of sight upon the perception of touch, but it does not seem to me desirable to reproduce it here. Inasmuch as we recognize the fundamental significance of the sense of touch for the development of our life of sensation, we have also grasped the imagination of touch as the precondition for the development of our whole life of fantasy. Whoever therefore wishes to become acquainted with the nature of human imagination must in a scientific investigation take the imagination of the man born blind as his point of departure, because only the man born blind possesses an autonomous fantasy of touch in the sense characterized above. The consideration of this independent fantasy of touch now shows that a man born blind is able to represent to himself an arbitrary figure, for example a triangle, only by producing the figure in question through imagined movements of the hand. If he runs his hand over the four edges of a tabletop, he is capable of grasping the top as quadrangular only because he is afterward able to produce the quadrangular form in his fantasy through imagined movement of the hand. Were he not capable of letting the hand glide over the surfaces and edges of a body in reality and also in imagination, then he would also not be in a position to perceive the shape of the body in question. All perception of extension, position, and shape of bodies is bound to the execution of real and imagined movements about their surfaces and contours. This proposition holds in the same compass for the seeing man as for the blind, only its validity is harder to establish for the seeing man, because in him the fantasy of touch is covered over by the fantasy of sight. When we who see lead our gaze round along the boundary line of a hoop, then our hand performs this movement along with it in imagination, only the imagined accompanying movement of the hand usually scarcely comes to our consciousness on account of its slight liveliness. Moreover, we are able to perceive the shape of things by means of the sense of sight too only because we can let the gaze glide along the outlines of bodies in reality as well as in imagination. Without real and imagined gliding of the gaze, as also without real and imagined movements of the limbs, there is for us no perceivable grasping of any spatial shapes. But even the mere grasping of a place in space, the mere transposing of a thing or a process to some spatial site, would be impossible if we were not in a position to point to the place in question through real and imagined movements. When the surface of our skin is touched at some point by a foreign body, we know, even if we do not see, how to state fairly approximately—indeed for certain particularly sensitive places with greater exactitude—where the contact took place; we "localize," as one is wont to express it, with greater or lesser sharpness our sensations of skin contact at the outer surface of our body and no less at certain mucous membranes of its inner surface. In general we shall know how to localize most sharply the contacts at the movable parts of our body and then also in those regions toward which we are wont often and with ease to execute movements in reality and in imagination. For what does it mean to know the place at which our body is touched? It means as much as to be capable of making in imagination a pointing movement toward that place. The localizing movement carried out in imagination we can, provided only that we are not prevented from it by any paralysis, translate into a real movement, that is, through an actually executed movement of the hand ourselves touch approximately that place of the skin where the contact by the foreign body took place. The capacity to perform movements in imagination, or, as one may also say, virtual movements, forms the foundation of our whole life of fantasy. We therefore owe it to our fantasy, more exactly speaking to our imagined or virtual movements, that we are able to localize contacts at the surface of the skin, and that we are capable of assigning to things or processes some place in space. When we say that we perceive a body at a quite determinate place of space, then there is thereby meant in the first instance that we execute in imagination a movement toward that place at which the body is found. It is virtual pointings through which we fix the places of things and appearances in space, and through virtual movements along the surfaces and outlines of bodies we grasp their extension, position, and shape. And so the sense of the fundamental proposition of the theory of perception now becomes clear to us: that without fantasy, and namely without virtual movement, there can nowhere be a perception of spatially ordered, disposed, and shaped things or appearances. The perception of the real world would remain closed to us if we possessed no imagination, for imagination, and namely imagined movement, is unconditionally requisite in order that anything spatial may be perceived. Modern psychology does not know this concept of fantasy, because the concept of imagined or virtual movement, as it is here conceived, is likewise wanting to it. The English founders of modern psychology, in particular Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, are at bottom sensualists, that is, thinkers who reduce perception and finally also every activity of knowledge in general to sensations of the senses and if possible to sensations of the senses alone. Unfortunately their influence extends not merely to the proper adherents of their school but to all philosophical schools of the last two or two and a half centuries, so that their fundamental views work determiningly upon the thinking of all educated men. We all live, so to speak, in the prejudice that perceptions of the senses must be psychologically resolved into mere sensations of the senses. Now sensations are indeed the first and unconditionally requisite conditions of the activity of perception of our consciousness, yet they are by no means the sufficient conditions for it, since, as we convinced ourselves, imagination, or the virtual movement, is likewise as indispensable a condition of the activity of perception as sensation itself. That pure sensations do not suffice for the activity of perception confronts us nowhere so vividly as in the developmental history of the infant. The newborn shows sensation already in the first days of its existence, but of an activity of perception there can scarcely be talk in it for weeks, indeed for months. How does this come about? Were processes of sensation the sufficient substratum for an activity of perception, then already the infant of a few days would have to manifest an activity of perception. But of this there is not a trace present. For the infant at first lacks every kind of virtual movements. It does indeed move its little hands already in the first days of life, but it merely travels to and fro with them aimlessly, travels also into its face and into its eyes and thereby at times injures itself; in short, it lacks all steering or governance of its movements. But I say that a movement is steered when a corresponding imagined movement precedes it, that is, when it is the realization of a preceding virtual movement. Only in the measure that the infant learns to steer its movements of hand and eye, hence only in the measure that it is able through virtual movements to give its real movements a direction and form, in short in the measure that an imagination develops, does it also learn to transpose what is sensed to a place and to perceive shaped things or appearances in space. But because now the modern philosophy of sensation proceeding from England knows nothing of the fundamental significance of imagination for every activity of perception, because it does not know the concept of imagined movement, it falls everywhere into the highest embarrassment where it is a matter of explaining how we arrive at the perception of spatially ordered and shaped things and appearances. In the first place it knows how to give us no information as to how it is possible that we transpose the contact of our body to a determinate place of the surface of the skin even with bandaged eyes. To be sure, the subtle Lotze wished to explain this through his well-known hypothesis of local signs, according to which the sensations of contact would be affected with a something, with a local sign, through which the place of the sensation is made known to us: but at bottom everyone feels that this hypothesis is only a mere paraphrase of the fact that we localize our sensations of contact. What that something may be which must be enclosed in the sensation in order to assign it a place remains shrouded in mystical darkness, and the word "local sign" is only a means of deceiving us about this darkness. Most investigators understand by the local sign some kind of accompanying sensation—I had almost said "aftertaste"—which is associated with the tactile sensations and doubtless also with the visual sensations in order to indicate their locality. Now it is indeed true that the sensations of contact of the various parts of the surface of the body can show qualitative differences, but qualitative differences are precisely of a qualitative and not of a local kind, and are therefore in themselves wholly unsuited to indicate the place of a contact. The whole hypothesis of local signs is only a vivid presentation of the impossibility of getting at the problem of localization upon a sensualistic foundation. One attempts in vain to read into a tactile sensation something mysterious that could make known its place, for what is puzzled into the tactile sensation is after all in the end again only sensation: but a sensation of place there simply is not. For places are nothing of the sort that would have the power to awaken sensations in us. Sensations are excited in us only through physical-chemical processes; but since places are not such processes, no rational person will ascribe to them the capacity to be able to act upon our sensitive nerves. Physiologists do indeed since Ernst Heinrich Weber's path-breaking investigations of the senses of the skin (1846) use the expression "sense of place" for the capacity to localize the tactile sensations at the surface of the skin; but they are more or less clearly conscious that of a sense of place one cannot speak in the meaning of sense of warmth, sense of color, and so forth, because warmth and light are physical energies to which within our life-process proper modes of sensation correspond, whereas spatial places represent no energy which could claim for itself a proper class of sensations of the senses, hence a particular "sense." Critically thinking physiologists, such as for example Thunberg, therefore also admonish that the expression "sense of place," which has become traditional in the physiology of the skin senses, be avoided altogether. Because space in itself is not able to awaken any sensations in us, it is also not sensation taken by itself through which its existence is disclosed to us. But although space is nothing sensible, it is nevertheless something perceivable: an apparent paradox in which the enigmatic nature of space attains its most concise expression. For one commonly supposes that only what is sensible is at the same time also perceivable; but the existence of space is as it were an omnipresent mockery of this apparently correct proposition, for space is indeed what is everywhere perceivable and yet is nowhere able to act upon the sensing nerves. Therefore space is also, so to speak, an omnipresent veto against that one-sidedly sensualistic theory of knowledge which proceeded from England and which strove in crude fashion to reduce all perception, and hence also all abstract knowledge, to pure sensing. Only a being which not only has sensation but is also able to produce movements and—what is the chief thing—also virtual movements can possess a perception of space. It is not the sensations that make a shape known to us; sensations are there only to excite our imagination, and it is only this imagination that lets us grasp the shape. If, for example, I cover with the palm of my hand the circular opening of a beaker, I do not perceive the circular form of the opening by means of the sensation which the rim of the glass awakens, but these sensations must first excite my imagination and prompt it to an imagined movement round about the rim of the glass, in order that I may be able to grasp its circular form. Sensations are indeed an indispensable condition for the perception of any really existing shapes of things, but it is not the sensations themselves, but the virtual movements awakened by them, that disclose to us the shapes. One stands here before the wonderful fact that the real spatial order of things is made known to us through fantasy, so that without it, and namely without virtual movements, we should not have the slightest inkling of the real arrangement, disposition, and configuration of the actually subsisting world of appearance. Imagination is, so to speak, the organ for the grasping of space or of the spatial relations of the real world of appearance. To be sure, the expression "organ" is to be understood only in a figurative sense: in truth it is only meant that without the process of virtual movement no perception of spatial structures would be possible. When there is talk of imagination, one commonly thinks at once of waking reveries or of poetic-artistic activity of fantasy, so that one deprives oneself from the outset of the possibility of understanding the processes of imagination. Even Kant still defines the power of imagination in the following one-sided manner: "The power of imagination is the faculty of representing an object in intuition even without its presence"; but there are found in his "Deduction of the Pure Concepts of the Understanding" remarkable trains of thought which seek to overcome this one-sidedness; indeed, in the first edition of the "Critique of Pure Reason" there is found a most interesting note which begins with the following sentence: "That the power of imagination is a necessary ingredient of perception itself has surely occurred to no psychologist as yet." Nevertheless Kant brings it to no clarity in this direction, because he does not yet form the concept of imagined or virtual movement as the counterpart to real movement and consequently, despite all his resistance, remains caught in the sensualistic psychology of the English. In order now wholly to overcome this sensualistic influence, we shall have to make a distinction between that imagination through which our consciousness is directly chained to the facts of the real environment, and that imagination which frees us from the chains of the real environment. There is an imagination which makes our mind "absent" in relation to the real processes acting upon our sensing nerves, and commonly only this absence-making imagination is comprehended under the concept of fantasy; but there is—as we have just shown—also an imagination which is stimulated by the sensation of the present processes and to which we owe it that the present is able to unroll before us in its spatial order, disposition, and configuration. I designate this latter, which binds our consciousness to what is present and through which what is present is fashioned into a spatial environment, as the direct fantasy, and set over against it as its antagonist the inverse fantasy, through which our consciousness becomes a stranger in the real environment. This is the fundamental distinction in the doctrine of imagination, and because it is not made by modern psychology, the latter is unable to rouse itself to a theory of fantasy at all, but remains a slave of English philosophy of sensation. Our imagination is unable to fasten itself to what is present for any length of time, but leaps in intermittent pauses involuntarily away from the present things, only, to be sure, to return swiftly to them again. This leaping away from reality and returning to it, this wavering between the dominion of the inverse and of the direct fantasy, can be figuratively represented by a wave-line in which, say, the wave-crests may represent the phases of the direct and the wave-troughs the phases of the inverse fantasy. We let ourselves be taken captive by the impressions of the present, but our imagination glides quite unnoticed over to images of the future or of the past, so that for instants we are carried away from the present, only to return to it again and to dwell with it. It is a tremendously complicated play of waves of which there is talk here, for the processes of the environment stimulate the most manifold processes of sensation and thus occupy our direct imagination in an involved manner, while on the other hand surges of feeling, agitations of temperament, affects, through their continual ebb and flow, seek to divert our attention from the processes of the present in order to turn it to the images of hope of the future or the images of memory of the past, or to lead it back again to reality. Where is the psychology which would have seriously taken in hand the exploration of this boundlessly involved play of waves of our life of imagination, as it is influenced on the one side by the impressions of the environment and on the other by the inner floods of feelings and affects? English psychology has devised for the characterization of these extraordinarily complicated connections in the activity of fantasy the expression "association of ideas." A hollower word has surely never been introduced into serious philosophical investigations concerning human nature. To be sure, our experiences are associated with one another, just as the waves of the ocean, the orbits of the planets, and in general everything that ever took place is thoroughly associated with everything that ever will take place. It is only a question what is gained if instead of the expressions "connection" or "linkage" we use the Latin word "association." Through the introduction of the technical expression "association" into modern psychology it has succeeded in diverting attention precisely from those processes—namely the processes of imagination—to which we owe association, that is, the connection of our experiences of sensation and our processes of feeling. Let this be shown at once by an example. If I cover the opening of a beaker with the palm of my hand, then I perceive, as has been said, its circular form. The tactile sensations which are awakened by the individual parts of the rim of the glass do not remain isolated, but combine into a circular form. The modern psychologist says with a learned mien that they have associated themselves and through this association produced the representation of the circle of the glass rim. Yes, but what process is it then really through which the tactile sensations were associated with one another? The association psychologist finds no answer, for he seems to be of the view that the word "association" possesses the magical power of setting tactile sensations into connection with one another. Thus the word association serves him for the peculiar purpose of conjuring out of the world precisely that interesting process through which the association of the tactile sensations was brought about: I mean the "imagined movement," to which we owe it that our consciousness was able to grasp the circular shape of the rim of the glass. Another example. When one contemplates the features of a person A, it can easily happen that one is reminded of the features of a person B resembling him. The psychologist speaks here of an association through similarity. Thereby, however, the fact escapes him which upon this occasion ought in the first rank to awaken his interest. For when we contemplate the person A, our direct imagination is as it were laid in fetters by his facial features; but suddenly these fetters loosen, and our mind leaps away from the real figure which stands before us in order to dwell for an instant upon the features of another person whom we perhaps saw in a foreign land and many years ago. The wonderful thing in this happening is surely first of all that there is in us a power which is able to burst the bonds that chain us to the present through our sensation and direct imagination! Who names this power which with magic fingers looses us from the ensnarement of present impressions and makes it possible for us to dwell for an instant upon the image of a person not present? For the association psychologist this question does not exist at all. For he does not notice that here the direct imagination was for an instant overcome by the antagonistic inverse imagination, since the word "association" covers over for him the processes of the direct along with the inverse imagination as well as their antagonistic relation to one another. It is as though a physicist did not notice that the ray of light which passes through a prism has penetrated from the medium of air into the medium of glass and back again into the medium of air and has thereby twice undergone a change of direction, a refraction. Figuratively we may perhaps also speak of a refraction of our fantasy, and indeed likewise of a twofold refraction, for our attention was diverted from the present person A to the absent person B and directed back again to the first. The association psychologist, however, notices of this wonderful process only so much, that the person B resembles the person A, and draws from this the peculiar conclusion that it must be the similarity which effected the whole process. As though "similarity" were a power capable of extinguishing as it were what is present and of conjuring for an instant something not present before our mind. May one personify the concepts which the human mind forms for itself and raise them to efficacious powers? Similarity is after all only a concept which the human mind gains through comparison of objects, but no personality, no efficacious mind, no force or energy of nature which could free us from the bonds of the present and lead us over to something not present. And the same holds also of the remaining so-called "laws of association" of which Hume, James Mill, John Stuart Mill, and so forth have dreamed. If one considers, for example, the Humean laws of association, one recognizes at first glance, as Wundt too sets forth with fine irony, that they represent a mere playing with categories of concepts. "Just as," says Wundt, "fire, water, air, and earth are categories under which at a naive standpoint of consideration one may if need be accommodate the appearances of nature, just so would associations according to similarity and contrast, according to simultaneity and succession represent convenient logical templates for the transition from one representation to another. If then the representations X and Y are associated with one another, one will be able to say at one time that they were associated through similarity or, if this does not apply, that they were associated through contrast. If these templates do not fit well, then the association will let itself be brought about by the fact that X and Y were observed together simultaneously, and if this too does not fit, then temporal succession can render the most excellent service. In short, the associated representations X and Y will after all always stand in some logical relation to one another, hence this relation will be able to be brought under some logical category, so that one can then say that this category effected the association of the representations." And this theory was devised not by scholastic philosophers of the Middle Ages but by modern thinkers who are regarded as the heads of the modern "empiricist" tendency! That power, now, through which our mind is quite involuntarily led over from what is present to what is not present and led back again to what is present, reveals itself to everyone who really lets himself be instructed by experience in a wholly unforced manner. That power is namely the human temperament with its never-resting surges of feeling, mood, and passion. When some unsatisfied physical or mental need awakens in us and announces itself through ever more loudly throbbing feelings or emotions, then, as is well known, it is all over with the calm lingering upon the sight of what is present. And then fantasy strays off to images of the past and the future or in general of what is not. In themselves our drives and the feelings and emotions awakened by them are indeed blind, but inasmuch as they combine with processes of imagination they become as it were seeing and purposive. It is one of the greatest riddles of our life-process how feelings can combine with imaginings and fashion a world of fantasy which is able to free our mind from the sensible fetters of the environment. For association psychology, to be sure, this fundamental problem of our life of temperament is not present at all. For it does not notice that between the representation X which belongs to the environment and the representation Y associated with it which does not belong to the environment a tone of feeling must step as mediator, since otherwise the involuntary transition from what is present to what is not present would be impossible. For all our sensations are accentuated by feeling, and this accentuation of feeling, which in the face of the same appearance can in general be a very different one in different persons, is really what is able to lead our consciousness over to something not present. If I see, for example, the person A, then a feature X in his face may be so accentuated by feeling in me that I am involuntarily reminded of the person B. Someone else, who sees the person A and also knows the person B quite well, will nevertheless not be reminded of B at the sight of A, because in him the feature X is not accentuated by feeling in such a manner as in me. It is of importance to remark that the transition from the direct into the inverse imagination of which there is talk here happens in a wholly involuntary manner. Involuntarily the imagination glides along the lines of the things which are found in our environment, and just as involuntarily it glides off, under the influence of feelings and emotions, to shapes and images which belong to the inverse imagination. But we are able—and upon this rests the human character of our intelligence—to steer our processes of imagination up to a certain degree voluntarily: we can namely compel our imagination not to stray to absent things but to persist with the environment, so that we may gain as exact an intuition as possible of what is present. A perfect chaining of our fantasy to the real environment can of course never succeed for us, because the imagination, in consequence of the accentuation by feeling of what is sensed, is quite involuntarily drawn over—even if only for brief instants—into the realm of what is not present. Conversely, however, we can deliberately draw the imagination away from what is present and turn it toward what is not present, as is always the case when we wish to give ourselves over to our thoughts. A perfect sinking into thoughts will of course not be possible, because the impressions of the environment lead our imagination back again and again for instants to what is present. Thus our consciousness during the course of its waking state oscillates continually between what is present and what is not present, and this oscillation will now take place in involuntary fashion, now be deliberately brought about by understanding and will. But whether we dwell with our imagination upon what is present or upon what is not present, whether we let it hold sway involuntarily or steer it voluntarily: always the shapes which it brings before us will have to be grasped through virtual movements. It is the same virtual movements through which I perceive the shapes of real things and through which I set arbitrary figures before myself in the inverse imagination. If, for example, I perceive a triangle upon a real pyramid-shaped body standing before me, then this happens just as much by means of imagined movements as when in the inverse imagination I think a triangle drawn before me in the air. This fact, now, is of the highest epistemological significance. For since it is the same imagined movements by means of which I perceive any shapes really present upon bodies and through which I draw fictive figures before myself, I shall be able in the investigation of shapes to hold just as well to my fictive figures as to the forms of real things. Upon this rests the peculiar scientific character of geometry and of pure kinematics. Geometry is the science of space and of spatial structures; but since real space and real spatial structures can be grasped, just as fictive space and fictive spatial structures, only with the help of imagination, and namely of imagined movement, geometry will be no such empirical science as physics or chemistry. An imagined flash of lightning does not, as is well known, have the same properties as a real flash of lightning; hence the physicist will not be able to say that the investigation of real appearances can be replaced by that of imagined processes. But the space of perception has exactly the same properties as the space of the inverse imagination, and so the geometer will rightly assert that the exploration of the properties of real spatial structures can be carried out in the inverse imagination. This is indeed the wonder of space, that in real perception it shows no properties whatever other than in the inverse imagination, because precisely real space too can be perceived only by means of imagination or of imagined movement. But I designate such a mode of knowledge, for which it is indifferent whether its objects of investigation are given in reality or in imagination, as neutral. In this sense geometry is a neutral science, because the propositions which it finds in the inverse imagination must at once have validity also for direct perception, and because it consists in general of nothing but such propositions for whose validity it is indifferent whether they are referred to the world of reality or to that of the inverse imagination. The same holds for pure kinematics or phoronomy, which, considering movement merely as change of place, abstracts from the mass of what is movable and from its mechanical effects. For if one abstracts from the effects of movement and considers it merely from the point of view that it is a change of place of spatial structures in the course of an interval of time, then all the properties of this change of place can be studied upon imagined movement just as well as upon real movement, because after all real movement too cannot be grasped otherwise than through the imagined. In order therefore to establish the peculiar scientific character of geometry and phoronomy, it is by no means necessary to take refuge in Kant's distinction between knowledge a priori and a posteriori. It suffices to discern that the perception of the real world rests not only upon sensation but also upon imagination, and that in particular real movement and the relations of space can be perceived only by means of imagination. Geometry and phoronomy are sciences which have human imagination or virtual movement as their foundation, so that if our imagination were otherwise constituted, our intuition of space and of movement too would have to be otherwise constituted. The axioms of geometry are propositions by which the nature of our imagination is characterized just as much as the physical-chemical appearances which we perceive in space. The proposition that through two points only one straight line can pass, that further the straight line is the shortest path between two points, and that through a given point only one parallel to a straight line can be drawn, as well as the remaining geometrical axioms, are of fundamental significance not only for the real world but in just the same measure for the nature of our imagination. To an imagination other than ours there would also correspond another geometry and kinematics than ours. The new geometries which the great mathematicians Bolyai and Lobachevsky devised, and which one is wont to designate as non-Euclidean geometry in contrast to Euclidean geometry, which refers to our real world, may be regarded as attempts to break through the limits of human imagination and have therefore a quite incomparable epistemological significance. Unfortunately this significance has been clarified neither by the mathematicians nor by the philosophers of our days, so that the non-Euclidean and multidimensional geometries wrought a great devastation even in the best heads and seduced them to the most questionable sins against the fundamental principles of logic, the exposition of which I reserve for another occasion. But the first step which we take toward the correct philosophical appreciation of non-Euclidean and multidimensional geometry is the recognition that it is imagination, the virtual movement, to which we owe the perception of real movements and relations of space. After we have in such wise sufficiently emphasized the significance of virtual movement, we must now raise the question: what is virtual movement? To what kind of processes does it belong? First of all it is to be remarked that it is by no means a mental or psychic activity, of which one convinces oneself through the following consideration. If one pronounces the word triangle with understanding, then one notices with some attention that one has quite involuntarily—and to be sure also with great fleetingness—at once also drawn a concrete triangular figure before oneself in fantasy. Similarly does it stand with the understanding pronunciation of words such as ocean, cliff, waterfall, and so forth, indeed with the whole of human speech: we accompany our text of words and thoughts as it were with illustrations which are produced now by lively, now by stunted virtual movements. But no level-headed thinker will exchange his text of thoughts for the illustrations which his more or less excited fantasy adds. For the sense of the word "triangle" or of any other word is a general one: the word is employed for the designation of all specimens of a genus, whereas the illustration which is added by our imagination to the word triangle or to any other word is always something individual, concrete. The triangle which I set before myself through an imagined movement cannot be at the same time an acute-angled, a right-angled, and an obtuse-angled one; rather, the sense of the word "triangle" comprehends all the triangles that are possible. In short, the imagined movement, as well as real movement, always produces only a single figure. Words, on the contrary, have a sense only because, and are symbols of concepts only because, they can be referred to arbitrarily many individual things belonging to the same species. But although it costs only a slight logical exertion to distinguish our text of words and thoughts from the accompanying and, as has been said, often most rudimentary illustrations of fantasy, there is nevertheless a philosophical school which consistently and in principle confuses the thought with the virtual movement illustrating it, or, more correctly speaking, denies the existence of the thought and puts in its place the concrete individual image produced through virtual movement. The most energetic chief representative of this English-sensualistic-nominalistic school is Berkeley. He boldly declares that all our ideas are individual ideas, that there are no abstract ideas at all, and he proves the proposition roughly in the following manner: let one attempt to form the idea of a triangle which is at once equilateral and scalene and isosceles, and one will convince oneself that this involves an impossibility. But the abstract idea of a triangle would require that we form the idea of a triangle which unites within itself those incompatible properties: hence there is no abstract idea of a triangle or of any other things. Evidently he confuses the idea of a triangle with that illustration which we produce by means of imagination in order to exemplify the abstract idea in concrete fashion. We illustrate, as has been said, all our ideas through virtual drawings, but we must never confuse these illustrations with the ideas themselves. If, for example, a man born blind represents a triangle to himself, then he illustrates the idea of the triangle in quite another manner than we who see, for he is able to illustrate the triangle to himself only through the fantasy of touch, while we do it also with the fantasy of sight. Nevertheless the abstract idea of a triangle remains the same for the man born blind as for the seeing man. One and the same idea can be illustrated in the most various ways without therefore forfeiting its ideality. We whites illustrate the word "man" in our imagination quite involuntarily with the silhouette of a white; the Japanese, on the contrary, will for this purpose just as involuntarily produce the silhouette of a Mongol: nevertheless both have the same abstract idea of man. Someone can, when he thinks of his friend Hans, behold him in imagination sitting or standing, weeping or also laughing, and so forth, and nevertheless know that he always has the one "Hans" in mind; for if he took every illustration which his imagination produces of "Hans" for another person, then it would be all up with his understanding. Whoever therefore denies abstract ideas denies the understanding: Berkeley too concedes the possibility of abstract ideas, but because he is unable to make clear to himself the difference between his abstract concept and its concrete illustration in fantasy, and because he is further filled with a great aversion to abstractions, it gives him joy to dispute them out of the world. Incomprehensible only is why anyone philosophizes, and moreover philosophizes in so abstract a manner as Berkeley, if he is so mortally hostile to abstractions that he would most gladly exterminate them. How little imagined movement is anything psychic comes forth in vivid fashion when one fixes one's eye sharply upon its role within the human life-process. The infant is able in the earliest epoch of its life, as we have already remarked, only to travel aimlessly to and fro with its little hands; were it never to learn to steer the gaze and to lead the limbs toward a goal, then it would also never come to an activity of perception, for whoever is never master of his movements can also never find anything and transpose nothing sensed to any place, and hence is also capable of no perception, because perception becomes possible only where a place is assigned to what is sensed, so that it finds its ranging within the general spatial connection. But how does there arise out of the chance eye movements of the infant a steerable and then also a self-steering gaze? And how does there arise out of the shapeless traveling to and fro of the hands a gradually forming, purposive movement? Not otherwise than through the help of the developing imagined movement, which prescribes to real movement paths at first uncertain, then ever more determinately formed. Only when after the elapse of three to four months the virtual and the real movements of the gaze and of the limbs are sufficiently strengthened can there be talk also of an activity of perception in the infant. From this it is to be seen that imagined movement is a temporally preceding condition of the activity of perception and not this activity itself, and hence can also have no psychic character. Processes of perception are acts of consciousness; imagined movements, however, are no psychic activities, for long before intelligence begins to stir, the aimless movements of gaze and hand of the child must be ground into formed movements through the virtual movements. But the nonpsychic character of imagined movements comes forth still more clearly in the grown man. When we must make a still unaccustomed movement in the handling of an instrument, we easily become aware of the imagined movement which is to show the real one the way; but the more we have practiced the movement, the more imperceptible becomes its virtual steering, and the more it acquires the character of a so-called "automatized movement." What is meant by this expression psychology gives us as good as no information about. And yet it is here a matter of one of the most interesting and significant problems of the science of vital processes. Someone can, sunk in thought and paying no heed whatever to his steps, take a walk, and he does not wonder afterward at all that his legs functioned to good purpose without having been held to it by their commander, by consciousness. One says the legs did their duty "automatically," and believes the matter is disposed of with this word. But what does one mean by this enigmatic word? Does one believe perhaps that the automatically moving legs are robbed of their signpost, of imagined movement? Were the automatic movements to have lost their helmsman, virtual movement, then they would have to sink back to that primitive stage of development which manifests itself in the infant through the aimlessly traveling little hands. An automatized movement remains a purposive movement only because it has not lost its helmsman, namely virtual movement; to be sure, this virtual movement has forfeited contact with consciousness. It is what has been automatized, that is, switched out of connection with consciousness. By an automatized movement we therefore understand such a movement whose virtual rudder has been switched out of connection with consciousness, that is, automatized. The more we practice a voluntary movement, with the smaller an expenditure of conscious imagined movement can it be steered, and if the required minimum of expenditure is reached, then the imagined movement can, temporarily loosening its connection with the activity of consciousness, become as it were independent and go on working without the supervision of consciousness, whereby consciousness experiences a certain relief and can turn its attention to other activities. The chief result of this consideration is the important insight that there is an "automatic imagination" which steers our movements even when it no longer stands under the supervision of consciousness. And this insight signifies the first step in the scientific theory of the voluntary movements of man and of animals. At the same time it comes clearly forth that imagined movements are in themselves nothing psychic. They can indeed stand under the immediate command of consciousness, but they are able also temporarily to detach themselves from it in order to go on working in the most purposive manner. Most impressively do this detachment from consciousness and the nonpsychic character of imagined movement come to light in those peculiar states in which the activity of consciousness of a person appears paralyzed and yet the processes of sensation of the senses and of imagination continue to subsist, as is the case in hypnosis. This is in its essence a specific paralysis of the brain through which consciousness is switched off in such a way that the pathways leading to it nevertheless remain open, so that processes of sensation can take their course undisturbed. The skin can remain receptive to processes of tactile, the half-closed eye to processes of light, the ear to processes of tonal sensation; all these sensory processes are able to stimulate virtual movements which are translatable into real movements, without consciousness having the slightest share in all these processes. The hypnotized person is in such wise a sensing, feeling, fantasying automaton, that is, the processes of sensation, of feeling, and of fantasy which play themselves out in him combine with no activity of consciousness, but are steered by the consciousness of a second person, that of the hypnotist. He therefore presents as it were a distorted image of a thinking being, for he seems to sense, to feel, and to have imagination like a conscious being, while nevertheless consciousness is wanting to him. Whoever cannot distinguish the activity of consciousness from the processes of sensation, of feeling, and of imagination, let him once attend hypnotic experiments and observe attentively the hypnotist and his medium: for here he will find distributed over two persons what he was unable to separate from one another in one person. He will convince himself in vivid fashion that in the medium processes of sensation, of feeling, and of imagination surge to which not a trace of consciousness adheres, and that in the hypnotist there is to be found the corresponding complementary activity of consciousness which is missed in the medium. Whoever now is clear that sensations, feelings, and imaginings are nothing psychic will not be all too greatly surprised by such a paralysis of the brain in which the said three processes can continue while every activity of consciousness is switched off. The proper significance of hypnotic experiments is to be sought in the first rank in this, that they cast a new light upon the healthy mental activity of man. These experiments show what a world-wide difference subsists between mere nervous life and a steering of nervous life through one's own consciousness. In the hypnotized person nervous life remains just as excitable from without as in the waking man: outer impressions awaken in him intense processes of sensation, mighty upheavals of feeling, and stimulate also the passive play of lively phantasms, so that the processes of imagined movement can also lead over to corresponding real movements. Nevertheless in all these processes of a highly developed nervous life nothing is to be discovered of a knowing and steering activity of consciousness. If, then, there is anywhere an opportunity for the experimenting investigator of nature to grasp the infinite difference between autonomous mental activity and the mere processes of nervous life, this is surely the case in the interesting domain of hypnotic experiment. Unfortunately, however, hypnotism has not been exploited precisely in this most significant aspect. There prevails in modern psychology and physiology a boundless confusion of the concepts of the activity of consciousness and of the nervous-vital process: one intermingles the mental with the nervous-living in a quite unpardonable manner, although precisely hypnotism offers vast sums of experimental facts which seem made for the clarification of these fundamental concepts. One convinces oneself upon the occasion of every hypnotic session in a most vivid manner that mental activity is something essentially other than a mere vital stream of processes of sensation, of feeling, and of imagination. Sensations, feelings, and imaginings are, considered in themselves, purely "vital processes," which can indeed come to consciousness or be steered by consciousness, without however being in themselves activity of consciousness. Originally no level-headed man is inclined to count pains or the excitations of hunger, thirst, voluptuousness, and so forth among the psychic activities, and for this reason one distinguishes the "sensible nature" of man from his "mental" one. This is of course an uncertain distinction which in this form is not correct and must be replaced by the conceptual separation of the "vital process" and the "activity of consciousness": but it could very well have become the point of departure of a genuinely monistic philosophy which brings the vital and the conscious in human nature equally into their own. Descartes, who, stiffening himself upon his proud "I think" [cogito], set forth the sensible nature of man as the source of errors and of evil, conjured up through the rationalistic one-sidedness of his otherwise exalted and rigorous mode of thought a counterrevolution which was to become in a high degree fateful for the development of the new psychology. For English science, whose ideal is vitality, took up the cause of the suppressed "sensible nature" of man in such a manner that it raised the "sensible," or better the vital, to the psychic and set forth the sensation of the senses downright as "idea," as an elementary idea which was to lie at the ground of man's remaining ideas. This confusion of the vital with the mental, or more correctly this resolving of everything mental into vitality through an ambiguous "psychology" which contains neither a doctrine of life nor a doctrine of mind, had as its consequence that for more than two centuries biology and the science of mind have suffered from a chronic confusion of their fundamental concepts. And it seems that this confusion reaches its high point precisely in our days, which comes to expression in the fact that "psychologistics" threatens to devour all philosophical thinking and carries the germs of its Babylonian confusion of concepts not only into the sciences of mind but also into the natural sciences. Possibly, however, we already stand before a turn of modern thinking which will lead to the recovery of the modern mind; at least the signs multiply that a clear conceptual separation of the vital and the mental is being ever more earnestly striven after also in the circle of the "physiological psychologists." For the most advanced investigators of this great school declare that psychology must constitute itself as a branch of the natural sciences, which has its complete justification and is joyfully to be welcomed insofar as that "psychology" which is pursued experimentally is according to its whole essence in fact throughout only vitalistic natural science, which at present still suffers from a mingling of the fundamental concepts of the mental and the vital, but which can be freed from this hereditary evil through a rigorous critical-epistemological purification. But still more promising than the pressing of the physiological psychologists toward a clarified natural science is the mighty striving of the "neovitalists" after a reform of biology, which is to furnish once and for all the rigorous proof that the living can never be reduced to something mechanical, that it is therefore impossible ever to resolve biology into physics and chemistry. To be sure, this highly significant tendency in modern natural science still suffers from many an epistemological perversity or mystical vagueness: but at bottom it nevertheless designates the way of that progress which will lead to the distinction of a vitalistic and a mechanistic method in biology. We can take the riddle of life scientifically in hand—as I seek to show in another place—from two directions, namely through direct mechanistic (physical-chemical) research and further through indirect vitalistic methods of measurement; these can lay claim to exactitude just as much as the former. There will therefore have to be formed alongside "mechanistic biology," and in the most intimate union with it, a "vitalistic biology." The theory of fantasy which I here furnish would be a philosophical contribution to this vitalistically minded biology. In my conception, namely, vitalism is an unconditionally victorious doctrine because in reality there are vital facts as distinct from mechanical (physical-chemical) ones. Sensations, feelings, and imaginings form three classes of vital facts which are characterized by this, that they everywhere hang together only with a consciousness, or, as one may express it, can be immediately attested only through a consciousness. Mechanical facts, on the contrary, can have arbitrarily many witnesses or observers. If, for example, two persons see one and the same flash of lightning, then this flash of lightning presents a physical fact, whereas the two experiences which two persons have of the same physical fact must be designated as two vital facts. A single physical event can excite in n persons n experiences, hence n vital processes, so that it is for the discerning a logical impossibility to confuse the concept of the mechanical with that of the vital. If now, on the other hand, one wishes to distinguish the vital also from the mental, let one take refuge in a hypnotic experiment during whose course the hypnotist calls out to his medium: "Now a flash of lightning has struck the tree here before our window!" One will see by what intense experiences the hypnotized person shows himself shaken, without there combining with the vital processes suggested to him even the slightest activity of consciousness. We have thus obtained a quite determinate answer to the question to what kind of happenings the processes of imagination belong. To be sure, our processes of fantasy generally combine with activities of consciousness and, through this being-bound-up with the mind, seduce us into holding the processes of imagination themselves to be something mental, but they are in themselves only vital processes, quite as much as our experiences of sensation and of feeling. Imagination plays in the realm of the vital a role similar to that of movement in the mechanical world. One could designate it as the vital analogue, as the vital equivalent, or as the vital image of movement. Just as a body is able to escape from the society of other bodies in mechanical fashion only through movement, so a life-process can free itself from the clamps of its environment only through imagination. It is a wonder without parallel that life, without stirring from the place where it finds itself, can nevertheless comport itself as though it had slipped away to another place in space or to another place in time. This being carried away of the life-process from the spatio-temporal standpoint at which it in reality persists is called the process of fantasy. Mechanically life may show itself banished to one place; in vital respect it is nevertheless not banished to that place, but can within the mechanical fetters yet be free of these fetters. This self-liberation of the life-process from the bonds which in reality hold it captive is called in the popular manner of expression "the flight of fantasy." If, then, there is any process whatever by which life sets itself off with perfect distinctness from the mechanical, it is the process of imagination. From the mechanistic standpoint a body is always just where it is; considered from the vitalistic standpoint, however, a living body can dwell somewhere quite other than where it is held captive in mechanical fashion. Now as life nowhere so distinctly confronts the mechanical as in the process of imagination, so this is to be regarded as the highest unfolding of the vital. Sensation and feeling are indeed likewise vital processes, yet in them life shows itself still wholly fettered to the mechanical location; for in sensation nothing further announces itself than an immediately present physical-chemical process, and in feeling nothing further makes itself known than an immediately present state of a living being; life is thus bound to the present through the sensations and feelings, and only because sensations and feelings also stimulate imagination is life able to roam into the spatio-temporal distance while mechanically it dwells at one location. Thus it now also becomes intelligible why even what is present cannot be perceived without the help of imagination. For what takes place, say, two meters distant from our body can evidently be perceived at this determinate distance only through imagination. An activity of perception is not possible without imagination. Modern philosophy of sensation (modern psychologism) knows no process of imagination, but speaks of a "projection of sensation" into the external world toward that place where the perceived image is found. It shoots sensation as it were out of a pistol toward the place of the perceived thing and believes through such a "theory" to have found a way to the explanation of our perception of space. Now, however, the word "projection of sensation" says just as little as the word "local sign." All these technical expressions, such as for example also the term "association," are there only to cover over that process of imagined movement through which the perception of a spatially ordered world of appearance is first made possible. Modern philosophy of sensation thus sins not only against the human mind, in that it resolves psychic activity into the mere vital process, but transgresses still far more against this vital process, in that it eradicates a highest component, the process of imagination, and thereby makes the formation of a natural-scientific theory of the vital substratum of all activity of perception an impossibility. Whoever supposes that a perception is possible on the ground of sensations could equally assert that our whole spatial world of appearance is only one-dimensional. As little as a sensible world of appearance can be forced into the course of a geometrical line, so little is it possible to perceive a sensible world with the mere function of sensation without the help of the two other functions of nervous life, namely of feeling and of fantasy. In a complete doctrine of fantasy the following problems will therefore have to be treated: a) the relation of the processes of imagination to the acts of consciousness, whereby the significance of fantasy for all logical, aesthetic, and ethical activities of the human mind is laid open; b) the relation of the processes of imagination to the processes of sensation and of feeling as well as the relation of these three components to the vegetative ground of life. By the vegetative ground of life is here meant the proper life of the cells, tissues, and organs of the organism, that deeply veiled proper life of all the constituents of our body which in its immediacy can never come to consciousness and of whose presence we have mediate tidings only through the changing feelings which rise up to consciousness out of the unfathomable depth of the ground of life; c) the relation of fantasy or of imagined movement to real or mechanical movement, whereby one of the most important problems of modern natural philosophy, namely the relation of vitalism to mechanism, is unrolled. Of these problems we can here only touch upon the first and the third, since we should otherwise have to develop at once the fundamental features of a philosophy of mind as well as a natural philosophy. Here only the relation of the processes of imagination to the processes of sensation and of feeling interests us, that is, we seek to ground a vitalistic theory of imagination. We saw that it is the processes of sensation which stimulate our direct imagination, but it must now be emphasized that the process of imagination thus awakened also acts back stimulatingly upon sensation. If, for example, a foreign body touches our body at the point P, then the sensation of contact stimulates in us the process of imagined movement through which we point to the point P where the contact took place. But inasmuch as we aim with the imagined movement at a point of our body, there is in this point in turn excited a sensation which, to be sure, on account of its very slight intensity usually does not come to consciousness. It is, however, well known that when someone sharply observes a point of our body, we feel a sensation at that point—not, say, because from the foreign eye an exciting fluid streams toward the fixed point, but because our own imagination begins to occupy itself with that fixed point, and the imagined movement which aims at a point of our life awakens a sensation there as well. It belongs among our most everyday experiences that every movement which we perform in imagination is able to awaken the most manifold sensations. If, for example, one stretches out the hand in imagination toward the metal door handle, then there is released in the hand not only a sensation of contact but also a sensation of cold as upon the real grasping of the metal. To be sure, these induced sensations are of very slight intensity, but there are cases where this intensity can also attain a higher degree. If one carries, say, in imagination a slice of lemon into the mouth—which usually happens when someone does this before our eyes—then this imagined movement can call forth so lively a sensation of the taste of citric acid that the induced sensation seems in intensity almost to equal the real one. In short, it is the first chief law of our direct imagination that, when it is stimulated by any sensation E, it for its part induces the essentially identical sensation E₁. Were a sensation not to stimulate the process of imagination and the process of imagination for its part not to be accompanied by the corresponding sensation, then it would never come to an activity of perception. If a perception is to come about, then the organism must react to the received process of sensation, and only this reaction to the sensation leads to an act of perception. But we react to sensations with virtual movements, which for their part induce sensations in us. The most interesting thing in this state of affairs is first of all that imagination is not only a consequent process of sensation but for its part produces sensations, or, as one expresses it more aptly: induces them. One commonly supposes that sensations are stimulated only through some agent of the external world; one should not, however, overlook that the imagined movement is a sensation-awakening process, and that the sensation induced by it is just as much a real sensation as the sensation which is called forth by an external agent. When I say that a sensation must be thrown back into itself in order that it may lead to an act of perception, then nothing mystical is thereby meant, but only that the sensation must induce an imagined movement and this an identical sensation in order that a perception may come about. One must therefore say that the sensory nervous process stimulates a second nervous process, that of virtual movement, and leads this back to the first, so that the nerve pathways of the whole process form a line closed in upon itself. After we have illuminated—if only fleetingly—the relation of fantasy to sensation, we must now fix our eye upon its intimate relation to the life of feeling, because thereby a new light will fall upon the inverse imagination. Life of sensation and life of feeling are so closely bound up with one another that it is most difficult to separate the two conceptually from one another, and that it is hard to succeed in setting forth the relation of fantasy to the one or to the other. Every sensation is also accompanied by a tone of feeling peculiar to it, and conversely every feeling is accompanied by tones of sensation. That this must be so is easy to comprehend. An agent, such as for example a weight pressing upon the skin, stimulates not only a process of sensation but also irritates the proper life of the cells and tissues upon which it presses, and brings about such alterations in their conditions of nourishment, hence in the circulation of the blood, that these alterations of the vegetative life must make themselves known to consciousness through more or less lively processes of feeling. On the other hand we are wont to characterize our feelings of pain with expressions borrowed from the language of sensation, and we speak accordingly of stabbing, throbbing, boring, burning, and so forth pains, as a sign that in our processes of feeling sensations too make themselves noticeable. But although sensations and feelings everywhere enter into intimate fusions with one another, their conceptual distinction is nevertheless of high importance in principle, because ultimately in feeling only the transformation within one's own life-process ever comes to word, whereas sensation always points to physical-chemical processes foreign to life. Between feeling and sensation there subsists then a polar difference analogous to that between the living and the nonliving. Only the nonliving is sensed, only life is felt. But because the vital process is everywhere accompanied by physical-chemical processes within the organism, feeling too will have to be accompanied by tones of sensation and conversely sensation by tones of feeling. The polar difference of sensation and feeling makes itself known also in the whole structure of the nervous system, for this can in a certain sense be designated as a double system, insofar as the processes of sensation are evidently bound predominantly to the brain and spinal cord, the processes of feeling on the contrary to the sympathetic system. When therefore we say that sensations fuse with tones of feeling and feelings with tones of sensation, this has, translated into the physiological, the sense that the excitation of certain sensory nerve pathways sets sympathetic nerve pathways into co-excitation, and that conversely the excitation proceeding from sympathetic fibrils can bring the sensory fibrils into co-vibration. In short, one will be permitted to speak of sensory and sympathetic nerve pathways coordinated with one another. There lies expressed herein a law of the reciprocal coordination of the life of sensation and the life of temperament, of which of course sensualistic psychology knows nothing to say. After these considerations concerning the connection—still shrouded in deep veils—of our life of sensation and of feeling, it will also be possible for us to set forth the second chief law of fantasy, which refers to its refraction or deflection. If some agent excites a sensation in us, then in the sense of the law just set forth a tone of feeling too will sound along in the sympathetic nervous system. But it very easily happens that besides the normally coordinated tone of feeling still other related diffuse tones of feeling sound along, which has as its consequence that the process of imagination which is awakened through the sensation undergoes a deflection. Only so long as a sensation awakens the tone of feeling exactly coordinated with it will imagination return to the present sensation, mirror it in the sense explained above, and linger with it. But in the same instant in which another tone of feeling associates itself with the appertaining one, imagination will be seized by this and suffer a deflection. It is this fantasy deflected from the present sensation that we designate as inverse fantasy, and to which we owe it that our life-process, and in connection with it our consciousness, is able to dwell upon something not present. The process of sensation need only become stronger or weaker, awaken a more or less pleasurable feeling, bring about a greater tension or relaxation of temperament, in order that a change of the tone of feeling may seize imagination and deflect it from the present impression. The deflected imagination will now for its part induce a sensation, and indeed of something not present. But how is this possible? Quite simply through this, that between the sensations and the feelings a law of coordination holds sway, as we set forth above: a law according to which sympathetic pathways are coordinated with the sensory nerve pathways. Let us assume that a process of sensation running its course in the sensory pathway A stimulates a process of feeling not only in the corresponding sympathetic pathway a but also in a neighboring pathway b; then imagination will thereby be deflected, and indeed toward that sensation B which is coordinated with the tone of feeling b. If, then, it once stands thus, that the sensations are coordinated with corresponding feelings, then a new feeling will lead our imagination over to the new sensation corresponding to it, that is, imagination will induce a sensation of something not present. The second law of fantasy therefore runs: our imagination is deflected from our sensations through the alteration of the tones of feeling which accompany them, and indeed it will induce such sensations as are coordinated with the altered tones of feeling. One can metaphorically designate this law as the law of refraction of fantasy. There is no finer example of this law than the dream life of man, which is shrouded in such deep darkness only because the nature of human fantasy was never made the object of a positive research. In sleep our life of sensation is, as is well known, the more lowered the deeper the sleep is. Brain and spinal cord, these commanders of our animal life, are as it were dethroned, and the vegetative life and its regulator, the sympathetic nervous system, take over the dominion in the whole domain of our vital processes. Sleep is really nothing further than a necessary periodic recoil of our vegetative life against the encroachments of the animal life-process in the waking state. The waking labor of life, which stands in the service of the organism, impairs without interruption the proper life of the myriads of cells out of which the organism is built up. How much this is the case emerges, for example, also from the fact that we can protect ourselves from fatigue through skillful alternation in the waking labor of life and preserve our freshness of performance up to a certain degree. The economy of our organization holds out, however, only until the reaction-period of sleep sets in. Under the dominion of the sympathetic nervous system regeneration then takes place. But if the sympathetic nervous system was seized during the waking labor by more intense agitations of temperament, or if in sleep a strong vegetative labor, for instance the labor of digestion, is still being performed, then the excitation of the sympathetic nervous system continues to sound on to such a degree that thereby the processes of imagination too are drawn into sympathy. Dream life begins. The dream is the typical presentation of a purely inverse process of fantasy, that is, of a process of imagination which is stimulated by feeling. If the direct imagination is rooted in the life of sensation, the inverse grows up out of the life of feeling. In the waking state, however, the inverse fantasy never comes to complete dominion, because it is hindered by the lively life of sensation. The waking state is to be conceived as an oscillation between the processes of the direct and the inverse imagination. The manner and way of this oscillation, but quite particularly the magnitude of its elongation, is the gauge of talent and of genius. The more strongly the direct imagination is able to chain itself to what is present and to be absorbed in it, and with the greater force the liberation from its bonds and the sinking into the inverse imagination takes place—the mightier, therefore, the wave-beat of life between the two shores of the present and the not-present—the more is such a life-process called to be the bearer of a great talent. Only it must not thereby be forgotten that this "wave-beat" is a wholly involuntary one and that there is also still a conscious dominion of the mind over the alternation between direct and inverse imagination. It is only the steering power of consciousness over the direct and inverse imagination that constitutes genius. Whereas in waking the temporal phases of the direct and the inverse imagination alternate with one another in some highly variable rhythms, in dream there can be no talk of such an alternation, because the life of sensation is as good as switched off and the process of imagination is stimulated only through the sympathetic nervous system. Now, between a merely imagined and a real movement a distinction can be made only if consciousness possesses the choice between direct and inverse imagination. This is what the dreamer lacks, which has as its consequence that he holds what is imagined to be what is real. The psychology of the dream thereby becomes intelligible. The images which the dream brings before us are distinguished by their extraordinary liveliness; the images of the waking inverse fantasy, on the contrary, are often rather pale and uncertain. For in the waking state the inverse fantasy must seek to assert itself over against its mighty opponent, the direct fantasy, which can always happen only intermittently and for a few instants, because the real environment at once again lays the processes of imagination in its chains; in sleep, on the contrary, the direct imagination is overcome, so that the inverse process of imagination can unhindered induce the most gorgeously colored sensations. At bottom, however, there is according to their whole essence no difference at all between the images of the waking inverse fantasy and the images of the dream; the former can therefore with full right be designated as waking dream images. For in the arising of a dream image two processes cooperate: firstly, tones of feeling stimulate the imagined movement, and secondly, this induces the sensations coordinated with the tones of feeling. But both marks fit perfectly also the images of the waking inverse fantasy, so that, apart from an accidental difference of intensity, no difference at all may be made between them. To prevent misunderstandings, let the concept of "induced sensation" be considered more closely. When in imagination we stretch out the hand toward something, this imagined movement will induce a sensation as though we had really touched the object of our longing. In the waking state, however, the induced sensation is too weak, so that it usually is not noticed at all. But if the dreamer in the dream imagination stretches out the hand toward the object of his longing, then this imagined movement will induce so intense a sensation of the contact with that object that it equals in intensity a direct sensation in the waking state. In themselves, however, induced sensations are processes of exactly the same nature as those awakened through an external mechanical action upon our organism. Let one therefore not be all too greatly astonished if the vital process of imagination too can induce a process of sensation. Induced sensations are just as real processes resting upon a nervous process as the sensations called forth through an external action. Modern philosophy of sensation confuses the process of imagination with the induced sensation and thereby annihilates the concept of imagination. It is not the induced sensation but the virtual movement that constitutes the essence of the vital processes of fantasy. To the sensation of red in dream there corresponds the same vital process as to the waking sensation of red; only the manner and way of its stimulation is a different one. The same holds also of the sensation of red which appears in a waking image of fantasy; here this sensation of red will usually be of lesser intensity, which however alters nothing in its proper essence. English theory of sensation in particular is wont also to designate the induced sensations as "reproduced" or as "revived sensations." And it combines with these expressions the quite naive representation that a sensation which has once taken place does not entirely vanish but leaves behind a "trace" in the soul or in the ganglion cell of the nervous system. This "trace" is conceived as a latent or sleeping sensation which can occasionally be awakened out of its twilight state through "association" and thereby becomes a copy of its original essence. But the induced or fantasy sensations are no remnants or copies of impressions that were once already there, as Hume supposes. Every induced sensation is a sensation in which not even the slightest part can be identical with a process of sensation already earlier there and stimulated from without. That sensations can be induced through which our consciousness makes past experiences present to itself rests by no means upon the conserving of former living sensations, but upon that important law which I designated as the coordination of our life of sensation and of feeling, and which physiologically must be expressed as the working together of the cerebrospinal and the sympathetic nervous system. The role of mediator is therein always played by imagination, or more exactly by imagined movement, which in general represents a mediating or connecting vital function and is always in play where something not yet connected is to be brought to consciousness in a connected manner. Nevertheless it happens continually that we are churned through by dark feelings without their being able to become corresponding sensations, and that our consciousness remains standing at unclear intimations without being able to penetrate through to intuition and to knowledge. The more experiences we gather and the more we reflect upon them, the more do sensations and feelings become coordinated with one another and the more easily does it succeed for our feeling, with the help of imagination, to induce a sensation which helps us to intuition and to knowledge. We are grateful to the genuine thinkers and poets because they ease for us the ordering of our inner life, because they enlighten us. From the coordination of the life of sensation and of feeling it by no means follows that the induced sensations of the inverse imagination are any remnants of the externally excited sensations of the direct imagination: but only that the experiences of perception, sensations, and feelings were coordinated and that the inverse fantasy too can make use of them in reverse fashion. With these propositions the foundation is won for a new rational theory of the activity of recollection and memory in man. It would still remain to be set forth in detail how imagination is related to the various kinds of sensation of the senses, the sensations of touch, sight, hearing, and so forth, and how it establishes a connection between them: but however important in principle these questions may be, I must nevertheless deny myself entering upon them within the framework of this small work. It is here for me only a matter of working out in broad strokes the immeasurable significance of what I designate as virtual movement. For this very reason I must in closing still emphasize that imagined movement is a vital process which presents itself as a real nervous process. The vital process of imagination is in the physiological light nothing further than the conflict of two antagonistic reflex movements which reciprocally inhibit one another, so that it can come to no conspicuous visible movement. All impressions which act upon us would necessarily have to discharge themselves in reflex movements, were it not provided for in us, as also in all more highly developed organisms, that many a reflex movement can be inhibited through a counterreflex following upon it exceedingly swiftly. Where this occurs, there we have the feeling of having performed an involuntary movement in imagination. Imagined movement is thus the vital equivalent of two real reflex movements following upon one another in a very short time and therefore canceling one another. But since between the first and second reflex there elapses an interval of time which, however small, is nevertheless at all events measurable, the first will not be able to be entirely canceled, which has as its consequence that every imagined movement must discharge itself in scarcely noticeable real movements. This was already surmised in his day by Carpenter, who with his ideomotor movements touched upon the thoughts here set forth, but arrived at no clarity because he remained caught in a sensualistic psychology. If one thinks the virtual movements switched out of the vital process, then all functions of sense fall apart, and all coordination between the life of sensation and of feeling ceases: in short, the connection is lacking between all the processes which form the foundation of our perception and of our memory in general, and the whole proud edifice of human activity of consciousness collapses. Now, however, modern psychology does not know the concept of the specific processes of imagination, of the virtual movements, and it resolves fantasy into pure sensations, that is, it disorganizes the vital substructure of our mental activities. To make matters worse, it confuses the vital with the mental and hinders the development of a true science of mind. What sound content it holds it borrows throughout from physiology. The rest is scholasticism, like the theory of association, or else mythology of the most naive kind, like the theory of traces. It requires a reform from the foundation up into all the parts of its crumbling walls. This reform leads on the one hand to a genuine science of consciousness, on the other to a genuinely natural-scientific doctrine of the vital processes of sensation, of feeling, and of imagination and of the nervous processes conditioning them. Generations of investigators will take part in this great labor. Fortunately biological research is caught up in a mighty upswing as never before. It also shows itself animated by a spirit which is most favorable to the formation of a new psychology and theory of knowledge. It is investigators of nature who in our time press toward philosophy, and the leading circles of professional research show themselves permeated by the conviction that the highest blossom of the knowledge of nature is self-knowledge. Truly, in such a time it is worth the trouble to occupy oneself with philosophy!

## IX. Raum und Zeit — IX. Space & Time

1. Our first question with regard to space and time runs: Through what experiences are they made known to us? The common view is that there are no such experiences at all, for what is experienced must after all somehow be sensed or felt. But space and time are nothing of the sort that would be sensible or feelable, even though they at all events become perceivable to us by an unknown path. This, it is said, is precisely the great riddle of space and time, that we do not know how they come to our consciousness. Others, who believe they look more deeply, assume with Kant that experiences of space and time not only do not exist but would also be the most superfluous thing in the world, since space and time have their seat from the outset in our mind and hence would not first have to become known through experiences. Rather, it is our own mind which confers spatial and temporal configuration upon the shapeless material of sensation coming from without. In short, it is we ourselves who create the world-stage—space and time—from within outward. This conception has been much admired, but it seems to us to set forth space and time not so much as a wonder dwelling in the divine as rather in the human mind, a wonder which is everywhere perceived and nowhere experienced. We are, frankly stated, of the view that only that can be perceived which is somehow experienced. We hold this proposition to be the fundamental principle of the theory of perception, and hence also of all natural science. That space and time should form an exception which, notwithstanding that they are everywhere perceivable, would be mediated by no experiences whatever, seems to us only a self-deception into which one easily falls if one renders oneself no exact account of one's experiences. The theory of perception must do everything to point to the actual experiences through which space and time are made known to us. For only when we uncover these experiences can we arrive at a deeper understanding of our own activity of perception, and hence also of all the happening of nature. There is a kind of experiences which have no character whatever of sensation or of feeling and are therefore easily overlooked. To these experiences belongs, for example, the case in which we do indeed fall into an incipience toward a movement, but the movement remains unperformed. Then we are caught up in a peculiar, merely inward impetus, as though we were moving the whole body or one of its members, for example the foot, the hand, the finger, in a determinate direction, whereby nevertheless it comes to no movement. I call this paradoxical but well-known experience of a movement dammed up or canceled already in its incipience, of which only the bare inward impetus is present, a virtual movement. It easily becomes conspicuous to us when we experience geometrical figures with scientific or shapes with artistic interest. Then we surely catch ourselves letting the hand glide along the lines and surfaces of the forms in question, without of course realizing the movement. This is a wholly involuntary virtual miming, drawing, modeling of spatial shapes. We should catch ourselves in such virtual movements far more often were we not under the mighty predominance of our visual experiences guided by practical interests, in whose nature it lies to bring the virtual movements to apparent disappearance. It is consequently expedient to study the perception of shape in the first instance not in the seeing but in the blind man, or indeed in the man born blind. We ask ourselves: How does the blind man perceive shapes? and in connection herewith: how does he come to a purely fantasy-like beholding of shapes, that is, how does he represent them to himself? The answer can scarcely suffer a doubt: he can perceive shapes only with the help of real, that is, actual movements of touch. If he wishes merely to behold shapes inwardly, that is, merely to represent them to himself, he is able to bring this about only with the help of virtual movements. He thus perceives the quadrangular form of a board by executing real movements of touch along its edges, and he represents a quadrangle to himself by producing it inwardly through virtual movement. His intuition of spatial shapes resides as it were in the touching hand, to which he refers not only the real movements of touch but, what is here the chief thing, also the virtual movements of his faculty of spatial intuition. Were he to lack those secret stirrings of the inner impetus of life which we call virtual movement, then he would precisely possess no spatial intuition of shape, indeed no intuition of space at all, because the intuition of shape unites within itself all the components of the intuition of space, such as length, direction, position, and so forth. And however incomparably exalted the intuition of space of the seeing man may be above that of the blind, it nevertheless agrees with the latter in the ultimate fundamental condition—in virtual movement. Thus the blind man gives the seeing man the most fundamental information about his faculty of perception—whereby it is once again confirmed that he who revels in riches attains to reflection upon his life only at the sight of the most elementary want of the needy.

2. It is of importance to remark that virtual movements are still very much lacking in the newborn and that their gradual formation is a chief affair of the first epoch of the infant's life. We shall call this first period of the child's life, still far from sufficiently explored, the epoch of the Hundred Days, and bring out as its chief character that the life of sensation and the life of movement still fall almost entirely apart, that is, that the connecting bridge between them must first be built in the course of this period. Upon this indeed rests the fundamental significance of the virtual movements, that they are called to establish a connection between the unconnected sensory and motor life of the newborn. Since in the embryonic epoch neither the sensory nor the motor life could awaken, it is comprehensible that these two components of life, upon their awakening after birth, need not stand at once in a fully prepared intercourse with one another. The unconnectedness of the sensations and movements makes itself known to the observer in the inner helplessness and disorderliness of the infant's movements. Two great feats the newborn must learn and practice in the epoch of the Hundred Days: gazing in a determinate direction and the steering of the hand in a direction. These are very significant tasks of development which can be solved only through the gradually forming virtual movements. The faculty of beholding must develop and insert itself between the life of sensation proper on the one hand and the life of movement on the other, in order to bind the two together. The virtual movements have therefore a twofold vital task to fulfill: 1. They must combine with the sensations, namely the sensations of touch and of sight, so that the shapeless, merely qualitative sensing may advance to a spatial intuition of shapes; 2. they must gradually confer upon the helplessly disorderly movements of the infant an inner shape, a steering. Peculiarly, it has never been pointed out in positive fashion what is really lacking in the movements of the infant. In our conception, what they lack above all is steering through virtual movement. A movement is called steered when its realization is preceded by an impetus of the soul which determines its path-form more or less sharply in advance. We adults can with consciousness and with intention give ourselves such an impetus of the soul; we produce it voluntarily and are for that very reason in a position to discover it. We feel the impulse to accomplish a movement, for example to leap over a ditch, and discover upon this occasion the virtual movement. But if virtual movement could be brought forth only with consciousness, then we should not have the right to designate it as an original experience of man, because this name belongs only to such experience as can appear also without the contribution of consciousness. For this very reason we draw attention to the fact that the first development of virtual movement falls in the first epoch of the infant's life, where there can be no talk of a consciousness or will directed upon virtual movement. We thus characterize the inner state of the infant by saying of it that its life of sensation is already awakened, since it already shows sensitivity to temperature and brightness. Weeks must, however, pass before the awakening out of the infant's sleep has advanced so far that the life of sensation concludes an ever more familiar alliance with virtual movement, that is, becomes beholding, and confers upon seeing as upon the movement of the hand a new kind of inner liveliness. This is the mysterious time of the development of the intuition of space.

3. The theory of perception finds itself at present still in a fairly initial state, since important building stones for its doctrinal edifice are lacking to it. It does not know the experiences which in their totality make possible the activity of perception of the human mind; it believes it can get along with mere "sensations" and can ground perception upon these alone. The philosophical tendency which is caught in this fundamental error is called sensualism and stems from the seventeenth century, yet reached the high point of its development only in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, but influences even in our days the thinking of educated and learned circles, although its credit is caught in a rapid decline. How far one can get with mere "sensations" is illustrated best by the infant of a few weeks, in whom the faculty of beholding, of directed gazing and of directed movement, is still stuck in its initial development. Had nature provided for man as sensualistic philosophy expects of it, then the human race would be wholly incapable of life, for not only would the vital substratum for the development of its intelligence be lacking to it, but it would also stand in vital respect far beneath the mammals, birds, amphibians, and fishes, to whom after all the intuition of shape is not denied. Mere sensations are indeed indispensable for the coming about of perception, but they represent throughout only a superficial, introductory experience which is able to announce only qualities and not also spatial shape and which still requires an essentially differently constituted supplement in order to be able to furnish a vital substratum for perception. This supplement is precisely offered by virtual movement. Without it not only would the sensory and motor life fall apart, but—what is of equal significance—the various functions of sense too would have to forfeit their connection with one another. In fact the newborn finds himself, particularly during the first weeks, in a state in which the various kinds of sensation lack connection. One could designate this state downright as "anarchy" of the life of sensation, were the expression not liable to misunderstanding. Anarchy signifies not only the lack of a subsisting but also of a becoming connection, and this latter sense does not fit the infant. Its life of sensation finds itself in the state of a becoming coordination, and it is precisely a question how it is possible that between incomparably different, disparate kinds of sensation (such as, for example, sensations of sight and of touch) a connection arises. Since this fundamental question was not raised with all emphasis, no doctrine of the connection of experiences, and hence also no genuine theory of perception, could be built up. The thought lay near that it is the human mind or Nous (intellect, understanding) which binds the disparate kinds of sensation with one another. But it would truly go badly for the infant if in the natural coordination of its functions of sense it first had to await the awakening of its activity of understanding, whereas conversely its mental development has the vital coordination of its functions of sense as an unconditional temporal presupposition. The various functions of sense must, however disparate they may be and however unbridgeable they may seem, be bound with one another through inner life and experience itself, that is, there must be a central experience which makes possible the vital connection of all experience and thereby creates the substratum for the mental activity of perception setting in later. Self-evidently a newly supervening kind of sensations could not procure the desired connection, since it would only increase the unconnected kinds of sensation by one. Now, however, there holds the law of the soul that the special kinds of sensation, such as for example the sensations of touch and of sight, however disparate they may be, nevertheless agree with one another in this, that they awaken a purely psychic "impetus" in us which is accordingly very well suited to setting them all into connection with one another. This impetus of the soul, or virtual movement, is thus the sought central experience which coordinates and binds the various functions of sense with one another and in general the life of sensation with the motor life. Only virtual movement must never be conceived as a real movement, but merely as a psychic analogue of the latter. The physiologists were, since the famous experiments of Ernst Heinrich Weber (1846), close to discovering the virtual movements and thereby founding a new theory of perception. For Weber turned to account with rare skill and success the well-known fact that a test person is in a position, even with bandaged eyes, when touched at a place of the skin, to designate this place approximately. He stood in such wise before the question of principle which lies at the ground of the whole theory of perception: how it is possible that a purely qualitative (and more or less intense) sensation of contact or of pressure can at once also contain an indication about the place of the contact. Contemporary philosophy, however, was unable to solve the fundamental problem, and even the subtle Lotze furnished only a paraphrase of the question, in ascribing to the sensation of contact an accompanying sensation, a so-called "local sign," which makes known the place of the contact. However apt this coinage of a name was, it nevertheless contained no information whatever as to to what unknown kind of experiences such a "local sign" would have to be reckoned.

4. If someone frees himself from the prejudice that all experiences would have to be of the kind of sensation or of feeling, and on the ground of experiences which he makes upon himself concedes that there are "virtual movements," then he will easily be able to explain to himself upon what it rests that even with bandaged eyes we can approximately indicate the place where our skin has just been touched. He will say to himself that the sensation of contact continues itself immediately in a virtual movement which contains a pointing to the place of contact. If then we realize this virtual movement, we succeed in designating approximately the place where the contact took place. It is thus virtual movement which "localizes" the contact; it is the "local sign" vainly sought by Lotze. I designate the place-determining capacity of the virtual movement proceeding from the sensation of contact as tactile beholding. Upon this tactile beholding our perception of space rests in the last ground. It is to be emphasized at once that the newborn does not bring the faculty of tactile beholding with him into the world fully formed, but that it develops only gradually and, stimulated by it, the second kind of beholding, visual beholding, also begins its co-development. Although it may sound paradoxical, tactile beholding nevertheless has precedence between the two kinds of beholding. Visual beholding must learn to subordinate itself to tactile beholding and to adapt itself to it in order to be able to develop at all in a determinate direction and not to remain without support. However highly superior visual beholding may be to primitive tactile beholding, it must nevertheless as it were lean upon this latter in order to be able to subsist at all. One can illustrate this peculiar relation by the following simile: As is well known, the man blind from cataract who has been operated upon must first learn seeing proper after the operation, because his freshly acquired faculty of sight must first come to an understanding with his tactile beholding in order to become capable of performance at all. This is the time when visual beholding learns to support itself upon tactile beholding. Similarly there is in every normal infant an initial epoch in which its visual beholding must as it were go to school with tactile beholding in order to become fledged. This sounds almost incredible, because it appears as though a blind man were temporarily appointed guide to a seeing one. It is, however, easy to see that our organism is originally so laid out as to come into intercourse with the surrounding world first through immediate contact of the surface of the skin, so that this primitive intercourse forms the foundation for the higher intercourse of the eye with the world of light. Orientation in cosmic space is a later affair which of course can be procured only through visual beholding and visual perception, but with the infant it is at first a matter of mere experiences of place and of extension upon its own skin, namely upon the sucking lips and the palpating fingertips. The whole perception of space of man is originally attuned to tactile beholding: a fact which one can express geometrically by saying that the surface of one's own body with the appertaining three principal axes furnishes the natural system of coordinates to which in the last analysis the whole perceivable cosmos remains referred. Precisely because this fact is so elementary, it must be particularly underscored, for the disregard of it led in our days to that system of contradictions which has become known under the name of relativism. In order to overcome these contradictions, we shall have to subject the relation of tactile beholding and visual beholding to a penetrating examination. Above all, however, it is a matter of analyzing tactile beholding, because without this concept the construction of a systematic theory of perception is not thinkable. The "sense of place" of the skin so named by Weber contains a presentiment of what is here introduced as tactile beholding. The popular expression "sense of place" designates a mystical faculty of finding the place where the skin has just been touched. This expression is, apart from the fact that it says nothing about the faculty in question, conceived far too narrowly, for it is here quite generally a matter of a sense of space and particularly also of a sense of direction. Under the impression of the contact of the skin there arises in us an involuntary impetus of the soul to execute a movement in the direction of the contact, which movement may of course remain suppressed. We then really have a directional experience awakened by the sensation of contact, which contains an instruction as to in what direction a movement would have to be executed in order to strike the place of contact (or the touching foreign body). Had we not this directional experience, then it would also be impossible to execute a directed movement toward the place in question. When therefore I say that the sensation of contact awakens a "virtual movement," I mean that in this latter a directional experience is enclosed which can serve as a compass to an eventual real movement. It is to be regretted that the existence of this directional experience was not uncovered, since without it there could also be no finding of place and no experience of place. Directional experience and experience of place belong necessarily and inseparably together and only in union with one another constitute tactile beholding. To be sure, this tactile beholding can develop only under the condition that it manifests itself in real movements. This shows itself in a very instructive manner upon the occasion of the development of tactile beholding in the infant in the epoch of the Hundred Days. Virtual movement forms itself in him in the measure in which he learns to execute directed movements with the hand. We pronounce this fact in the fundamental proposition of the belonging-together of the virtual and the real movements. In the sense of this proposition our intuition of space (which is really contained in the virtual movement of tactile beholding) could not develop if virtual movement did not manifest itself in directed real movements. Thus without directed real movements there would be no intuition of space, in particular no tactile beholding. Conversely, no directed (steered) real movements could come about either if virtual movement did not precede them as a directional experience. These few propositions contain a complete refutation of sensualism, for they show that out of a merely receptive life of sensation an intuition of space could never proceed. Only because sensations are in a position to stimulate virtual movements and the real movements effectuating them can an intuition of space (in particular a tactile beholding) form itself in the first period of the child's life.

5. One can study tactile beholding (or Weber's "sense of place") experimentally either upon blind or upon seeing test persons. I name the blind man in the first place because he is the more suitable object for experiments upon tactile beholding. In consequence of his great infirmity he possesses a wholly independent or pure tactile beholding which not only can but must function for itself alone, whereas the seeing man—who disposes of two most intimately bound modes of beholding—is never able to employ tactile beholding in isolation. If one bandages his eyes and touches a place of his skin, then involuntarily and unconsciously he will seek, even under the bandage, to grasp the place of contact with visual beholding, of course without thereby bringing his tactile beholding even the slightest assistance. Rather this useless visual beholding under the bandage will exercise upon his tactile beholding a disturbing influence, however slight, which nevertheless has the consequence that the blind man in general shows in experiments in tactile beholding a greater fineness and determinateness than the seeing man, wherefore I should like to designate tactile beholding directly as blind beholding. There is thus a kind of spatial beholding in which the blind man is superior to the seeing, for which surely no seeing man will envy him, and this is precisely tactile or blind beholding. Science has hitherto never laid this proposition open, as indeed in general it was unable, despite many presentiment-filled approximations, to form the concept of tactile beholding. In order to be able to accomplish this, the seeing man must learn to feel his way very deeply into the situation of the blind, and this is a far more difficult task than many a superficially thinking person might assume. For what we who see call our visual beholding is never a pure visual beholding, but always contains within itself in mysterious fashion a supporting tactile beholding which does not come to our consciousness. We do indeed experience in learning to draw that our seeing is at first burdened as with a kind of leaden weight which hinders us from depicting upon a surface what is seen spatially, but we do not make clear to ourselves that that inner hindrance which we must learn to overcome in depicting the spatial upon a surface is precisely tactile beholding. It would, however, be an error to believe that the draughtsman and painter would have to free himself wholly from tactile beholding in the interest of the exercise of his art and to acquire a pure "visual beholding." Of this there can be no talk whatever. The draughtsman and painter too can never entirely dispense with tactile beholding; only it ceases in him to be a "hindrance" in the exercise of his art and becomes rather a mightily furthering counselor in his artistic creative activity, without his being able to render account to himself wherein that enigmatic tactile beholding consists to which he owes many a precious inspiration. In short, we should turn in vain even to a new Leonardo da Vinci to have him decompose our spatial beholding into two separate components, into a pure visual beholding and a pure tactile beholding, for this task of division lies outside the frame of his art and of his artistic thinking. The physiologist too and the physicist, indeed the mathematician as well, however deeply interested they may be in the task designated, and however many clarifications we owe to them concerning the two kinds of beholding, nevertheless cling much too strongly to the "actuality" (reality) of what is beheld not to leave us in the lurch when we attempt to penetrate into the mystery of our subjective faculty of beholding. Thus it gradually comes to our consciousness—after we have made ourselves pupils of the great representatives of the plastic arts and then also of the physiologists, physicists, and mathematicians—that spatial beholding conceals within itself an enormous problem which despite all the exertions of illustrious minds is still far from sufficiently clarified. And in doing so we are for the present still wholly silent about "temporal beholding," because we should not wish to complicate our problem still further at the very beginning, although it is easy to see that the problems of spatial beholding and of temporal beholding hang together in the most intimate manner and cannot long be kept apart. That it is most difficult to separate cleanly from one another the two components of our spatial beholding, tactile and visual beholding, has moreover nothing whatever surprising about it. The two modes of beholding have already in the very earliest time of childhood, where consciousness is not yet awakened, bound themselves so intimately with one another that in us who see they unavoidably always act together throughout our whole life, as though they had grown together with one another. In this union, however, visual beholding predominates to such a degree that tactile beholding remains as it were Cinderella-like always deeply overshadowed, so that its cooperation has from of old escaped the investigators of the soul. As at sunrise all the stars of the night sky grow pale and at bright day are as though extinguished, so our visual perception too, from the beginning of its ascent to the throne until the end of life, does not suffer the deeply hidden support which it receives from tactile beholding to come to our clear consciousness. Therefore we do not raise the great problem: How does tactile beholding act within all visual perception, how do we establish its unavoidable share in all visual perception, how do we separate the component of pure visual beholding from the component of pure tactile beholding, and how do we determine the relation of the two components to one another? Let us indicate at once—so far as this is possible at the beginning of the investigation—the direction in which we attempt the solution of this problem. Between tactile beholding and visual beholding there subsists a relation of oppositeness and of reciprocal complementation, which we designate briefly as a relation of polarity. One commonly thinks that opposites must "exclude" one another and that they stand over against one another as thoroughly "hostile," and by so one-sidedly false a conception of the concept one blocks one's own way to the understanding of our faculties of the soul, indeed also of the processes of nature. In what sense tactile and visual beholding complement one another and in what sense they have an opposed character can be uncovered only step by step. As regards the relation of complementation of the two modes of beholding, this is known from common experience, and one commonly pronounces it in the form that the sense of touch is a "near sense," the sense of sight on the contrary a "far sense," that is, that the former is suited only to the disclosure of the spatial relations in the vicinity of the body, but that the latter possesses as it were the power of flight and opens up the immeasurable distances of space. For this very reason one holds the two spatial beholdings to be wholly "of the same kind," so that it has a surprising effect to be asked to assume a relation of polarity between them, whereas already the primitive expressions "near sense" and "far sense" announce the dull presentiment of some opposition. Science has indeed manifoldly prepared the conception of this polarity, but has never pronounced it, because it was precisely never able to penetrate to the concept of isolated tactile beholding (blind beholding). The difficulty with which one has to contend here will come forth only gradually. For the present I wish only to indicate the way upon which one arrives at the discovery of the two polar faculties of beholding. The opposition between the two modes of beholding is brought somewhat nearer when one compares the peculiar character of the movements of the eyes with the movements of the limbs. The eyes have an incomparable special position in our organism; they move in their sockets roughly as in a ball joint, so that they accomplish rotations about a fixed midpoint, whereas the movements of the limbs have a pendular and in the final result a progressive character. Already these forms of movement let one surmise a polarity, which however comes forth clearly only when one compares the virtual movements of visual beholding with those of tactile beholding. Before, however, we enter more closely upon this, let us characterize tactile beholding more penetratingly for itself and in particular bring out the facts pertaining hereto which we owe to the physiologists.

6. Although the physiologists have hitherto not occupied themselves with the concept of beholding, in particular of tactile beholding, they have nevertheless carried out a great quantity of interesting experiments and observations, as though they intended to form a doctrine of spatial beholding. E. H. Weber devised (1829) a native method, surprising through its simplicity, for determining the fineness or sharpness of the "sense of place" in the various districts of the human skin. His classical experiments, in which he sets the two blunted points of a compass upon various places of the skin of a test person, are universally known. The points of the compass must be suitably ground down so that no feelings of pain but mere sensations of contact or of pressure arise. The test person comports himself wholly passively; it suffices that he does not perceive the touched place of the skin with the sight and that he in each case gives an indication whether he distinguishes two separate points or feels merely one. For when two neighboring places of the skin are touched, they can lie so close that their sensations fuse and are no longer distinguished. It is precisely a question how close the two points of the compass must be brought in order for the fusion to set in. Weber himself says of this: "By now opening the compass at first more, but then ever less, I arrived at that distance of the ends of its legs where the two impressions began to be sensed as a single impression. Even then the observer could still determine whether the line connecting the ends of the compass lay in the longitudinal direction of his body and his limbs or in a transverse direction. For he sensed only one impression, but the touched part of the skin seemed to have an elongated shape..." "If now it was recorded at what distance of the ends of the compass two contacts were still distinguished, or at least the direction of the legs of the compass could still be determined, then I obtained a survey of the fineness of the sense of touch insofar as it expresses itself as a sense of place." The finest discrimination of place showed itself upon the tip of the tongue and the fingertips, the bluntest in the middle of the back, of the upper arm, and of the thigh. Later (1852) Weber developed a still simpler method for the investigation of the "sense of place" of the skin, in which the test person undergoes the contact of only one point, but no longer comports himself passively, rather himself seeking to strike the touched place with a point. For the philosopher precisely this experiment, simplified in the highest degree, has a significance in principle, since it lets the problem of tactile beholding come forth purified of all incidental matter. In view of the fact that the test person, who does not see the touched place, is nevertheless able to find it with a certain approximation, the question after the psychic root of this achievement forces itself forward with elemental power, and in it the whole problem of spatial beholding is enclosed. A great physiologist has in such wise through his experiments given the impulse to subject the sensualistic doctrine of the experiences upon which our tidings of the external world rest to a thoroughgoing revision and to create a new theory of perception. Unfortunately, however, philosophy was able to do justice to this mighty stimulus only in limited measure, so that by far the greater part of the requisite labor of thought remains to be made up. Quite evidently our spatial beholding is rooted in the tactile sensations, whereby of course it is not meant that these sensations could make space immediately known to us. By sensations we understand mere experiences of quality, and out of such a spatial beholding can never come about. Experiences of quality are precisely no "experiences of order," and the juxtaposition of appearances in space could be disclosed to us only through this latter kind of experiences. There arises then the significant question what category of experiences we might address as experiences of order. The question is not easy to answer, but the Weberian experiments contain a guide to its solution. For in these experiments (1852) it comes down to executing a directed movement which strikes the place of contact approximately. Thus it need only still be decided what purely psychic factor is requisite for executing a movement in a determinate direction. Now, however, one at times experiences well-noticeable "impulses of movement" (virtual movements) which remain unperformed, that is, preserve a purely psychic character and nevertheless contain within themselves a determination of direction of the movement remaining unperformed. With this recognition we take the decisive positive step toward the solution of the great problem of spatial beholding. That this first and decisive step is a correct one is confirmed to us by unfalsified vital instinct. It would go all too badly for the possibility of our self-preservation if the foreign contact which strikes our body at some point of the skin could awaken no reacting impulse of movement (virtual movement) in us, and if we were from the outset incapable of eventually executing a defensive movement toward that endangered point of the skin. A humanlike life could not be preserved at all in the long run if foreign contacts of the skin did not excite psychic impulses of movement which in case of need also lead over to the execution of corresponding real defensive movements. Inasmuch as we thus recognize the elementary vital necessity of the virtual movements, every doubt of their existence too vanishes. Since now the virtual movements aiming at a place of contact are the fundamental experiences upon which our spatial beholding rests, there has in the above been set forth the vital necessity of the tactile sensations leading over to the essentially differently constituted experiences of tactile beholding. But we can go one important step further. It is indeed indispensable for the preservation of human life, but still far from sufficient, that foreign contacts of the skin awaken corresponding virtual movements (eventually also real movements), since provision must also be made that, before the foreign contact has yet struck the skin, a corresponding defensive movement can be performed. This, however, tactile or blind beholding is no longer able to accomplish. For this very reason it requires supplementation through visual beholding, which is grounded upon an essentially differently constituted (polar) system of virtual movements, as the noble art of the defensive and offensive movements resting upon visual beholding so beautifully brings to view. Thus we are once again set before the great problem how tactile and visual beholding complement one another. Perhaps we may here give expression to the hope that the physiologists will in future bring the questions of tactile and visual beholding into the closest connection with one another and in general take seriously in hand the glorious problem of beholding with their so fruitful experimental methods. To this end there would be required above all a novel and systematic elaboration of the Weberian experiments (upon the "sense of place") with the express and planful intention of making them a foundation of the theory of perception, hence of setting them upon the exploration of tactile beholding. As test persons the blind would predominantly be employed, partly because only the blind man possesses a pure tactile beholding, partly also because the experimenter himself learns to form the concept of pure tactile beholding as the minimal foundation of spatial beholding only in intercourse with the blind. Naturally one would not be permitted to content oneself with the two Weberian experimental methods mentioned above, nor indeed with their further development hitherto by a long series of excellent investigators, but it would come down to working out a regular "school of tactile beholding" for the blind, in order to scout out the outermost limits of the capacity of tactile beholding. What significance this subject would have for the "pedagogy of the blind" need not first be said. But it would have to acquire a still far greater significance for the doctrine of "pure visual beholding," for the working out of a genuine physiological "optics" is possible only through the parallel founding of a physiological "haptics." In what direction this haptics would have to move is shown by the following consideration. There is within blind beholding a capacity which corresponds to what in the seeing man we are wont to designate as "the eye's measure." For the testing of this blind estimation of distances between points the following method could serve. Let one touch in temporal succession two points in the left palm of a blind man and require of him that with his right hand he a) touch corresponding two points in the left palm of a second blind man, or else b) make visible the two places touched upon him upon a suitably prepared block of paper by means of a drawing pencil. Naturally similar three-point experiments can also be made, in which the estimation of differences of direction or of angle also already distinctly comes into question. In place of point experiments line experiments can also be introduced, in which the blind man is required to transfer the quite simple figure drawn upon his palm (triangle, quadrangle, circle) onto the palm of a second person or onto a block of paper. With some imagination a whole course of instruction in geometry and drawing for the blind can be worked out along this path; but what is of no slight importance: along this path a new light can dawn upon the seeing man concerning tactile beholding and in consequence concerning "pure beholding of light."

7. Once one has thoroughly convinced oneself that the blind man is able to form the essential constituents of the apprehension of space (such as place, length, direction, dimension, shape, and so forth) and consequently—provided only that he has the necessary intellectual endowment—can also be introduced into the science of space (geometry), indeed can become at home even in its higher problems, then one frees oneself from a great prejudice in which one is wont to be caught with regard to the apprehension of space. One commonly stands under the mighty influence of a hegemony of seeing, of an imperialism of the eyes: one is to such a degree a man of the eye that one is thereby seduced into grave aberrations in the assessment of the vital sources of our perception of space, indeed of all perception whatever, and in particular one does not learn to raise so deep-reaching a problem as, for example, through what experiences space and time are made known to us. Unfortunately the natural sciences and mathematics too find themselves at present in this little enviable situation. And yet physiology in particular would be in a position to furnish, upon the path characterized above, important contributions to a new doctrine of the perception of space. It could point most insistently to the fact that the sensations of contact are indeed necessary preconditions of the formation of our apprehension of space, but that this could never come about if we were not capable of executing directed movements. The capacity to sense contacts passively would be wholly superfluous and senseless if we did not also possess the capacity to execute movements toward the touched place and actively to touch and to grasp the foreign bodies found there. It is so conspicuous that in touching we can have two kinds of experiences of contact—passive ones in mere being-touched and active ones in moving grasping—that one must wonder why science does not pronounce the principle of their belonging-together and does not characterize the tactile sensations as passive-active double experiences, which would at the same time say that a normal faculty of touch already presupposes the active faculty of movement of the limbs. But therewith it is also already pronounced that the perception of space rests upon two kinds of preconditions: 1. upon receptivity for sensations of contact, 2. upon the faculty of movement of the limbs and in particular upon the sensation of grasping contact, since only the two in union constitute the passive-active faculty of touch. If this stands fast, then it is also clear that the capacity of grasping space can be rooted neither one-sidedly in the experiences of passive being-touched nor one-sidedly in self-movement and active touching, but must be rooted in the secret connection of these two capacities. It is therefore a matter of building a bridge from sensation to directed self-movement. An intermediate faculty must be inserted between the sensory and the motor faculty of man in order to make the grasping of space comprehensible, and this intermediate faculty is precisely beholding. Since we restrict ourselves in the first place to the grasping of space of the blind man, this holds above all of tactile beholding. Beholding is a thoroughly peculiar faculty of the soul which is stimulated in us through sensations and in the first instance through sensations of contact. Inasmuch as we have experiences of contact, we become beholding, and indeed this psychic transition takes place before ever we come to the consciousness of the contact, that is, before we could summon a mental act of perception by means of which we could refer ourselves to the being-touched. Therewith it is also said that the transition from sensing to beholding takes place within a small fraction of a second, whereby it also becomes explicable why it is so hard to become conscious of the experience of beholding separately from the experience of sensation. In general the doctrine of the experiences which lie at the ground of the perception of the external world is beset with great difficulties for the reason that we must reflect upon psychic processes which take place in small fractions of a second. Comparatively easier it is to delimit beholding over against the eventually occurring real movement, for the actually executed movement is a process belonging thoroughly to the external world, whereas beholding is a purely inward, that is, psychic happening. But since mere beholding (in particular visual beholding!) usually leads over to no movements of the limbs whatever, it does not come to our consciousness at all that it could very well lead over to such movements, indeed that it is an experience in whose nature it lies to contain an inward preparation for real movements eventually to be executed. Therefore it is expedient to make above all not visual but tactile beholding the point of departure of the doctrine of beholding, because contacts which strike the skin of our body lead over so easily to impulses of movement that an exertion of will is required to suppress them. If the single beholding is very short in duration, then to be sure we do not notice it at all; but if it becomes more ample, then we fall into a peculiar state in which we experience an inner impetus and at the same time the illusion as though we were already actually moving, while in reality we nevertheless remain unmoved. It is such states of merely "apparent" or "imagined" movement that most easily lead us to the discovery of the experience of "virtual movement," which serves the eventually occurring real movement as a compass of direction and is really nothing other than beholding itself. I should like here in passing to remark that the virtual movements can acquire an extraordinary significance in everyday life, quite particularly when they are supported by affects which lend them amplitude. Affects or "agitations of temperament" contain an urge to movement through which beholding or virtual movement is very easily carried over into a real movement. Since now it can be of extraordinary significance in practical life to overcome the urge to movement of the affect and to preserve the mere state of beholding (or of virtual movement), the struggle between the driving power of the affect and the will to maintain oneself in the state of beholding is a particularly excellent opportunity to discover this latter and to assure oneself that there really are inner states or experiences which one rightly regards as "virtual movement" and which border closely upon the real movements, so that one could figuratively designate them as "experiences at the exit gate of the soul," or more briefly as "exit experiences." Sensations, on the contrary, are experiences which take place at the "entrance gate of the soul" and which can therefore also be called "entrance experiences." These figurative designations render the good service of bringing near the oppositeness or polarity of sensation and beholding (or virtual movement) and of freeing our mind from the grave sensualistic prejudice that there are only entrance and not also exit experiences of the soul. Once one has overcome the prejudice that the soul is a blind alley which possesses only entrance and not also exit experiences, then one is mentally properly prepared to weigh the important question upon what experiences our apprehension of space rests.
