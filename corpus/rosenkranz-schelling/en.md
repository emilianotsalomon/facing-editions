## Introduction — Schelling's Philosophy

Gentlemen,

This is not the first time that I am giving a presentation of Schelling's philosophy here at our Albertina. In the lectures on the history of philosophy in general, such a presentation has continually occurred. In particular, however, in the summer semester of 1835 I gave a detailed lecture solely on the philosophy of the Germans from Kant to Herbart, in which I discoursed thoroughly on Schelling and his school.

At that time these lectures did arouse a lively interest, but they caused no further stir. They were simply lectures like any others. But how the statistics of philosophy have changed since that time!

For many reasons, which is not the place to set forth, one could have believed that Schelling and his system had their proper home precisely in Bavaria, that his system was grown together with that state's present stage of culture in a manner similar to how Hegel's had been with Prussia for two decades. But now Schelling, contrary to every construction that had settled him so comfortably in Munich, is suddenly in Berlin, and from there declares war on Hegel's philosophy — not, however, as he himself says, for the sake of war, but for the sake of peace. He may therefore not be surprised if lectures on him, and indeed against him, are also given. Precisely after the anniversary of Hegel's death he delivered there that promising inaugural address of his lectures, which will be known to you. By this, the whole position of the philosophical parties has now been changed. Schelling has acquired a new interest. People are eager to hear how what he now presents as his completed system connects with his own earlier philosophizing. People are eager to see what repercussions his lectures will have on Hegel's school, on scholarship in general....

## The Tension of the Times

Two things strike the eye at once amid this excitement: first, that we indeed see Prussia proving itself here too to be the state that seems to have, above all in Germany, the vocation for the development of intelligence in its philosophical form — for even though men such as Hegel and Schelling are South Germans by birth, they did not attain the maximum of their effectiveness in their own fatherland. Prussia lets no great philosopher since Kant escape it, and Herbart too was ours, even though, as the metacritic κατ' ἐξοχήν, he finally laid himself to rest off to the side in Göttingen, rather than in that famous Hallesche cemetery of Berlin, which since Fichte has already seen so much philosophy buried within it, along with Solger and Hegel, Schleiermacher and Gans. As long as Schelling was in Munich, one heard only vaguely, only little, said about the content of his lectures. The Belgians and Frenchmen who sat there at his feet seemed to know more than his German students. Here and there something leaked out, but Schelling himself never acknowledged these seepages as genuine currents. How utterly different all at once, since he has been in Berlin! Here he must come out into the open, although one cannot claim that he was not a public character in Munich, or that he held his lectures behind closed doors. The newspapers have swarmed with reports about him ever since his appearance; almost every day brings a pamphlet; everywhere one glimpses nearer or more distant allusions; indeed even ephemeral conversation has seized upon this subject as a favorite topic. In such cases of general agitation it is most advisable to give oneself over to serious reflection on the matter, to a thorough recollection of the entire state of the facts, in order to sail through the storm-waves of praise and blame, of exaltation and mockery, by the compass of criticism, which points unwaveringly toward justice.

The second thing you may notice, however, is the visible agitation of the Hegelian school. When one speaks of it, one tends above all to think of Berlin, where it is now personally checkmated by Schelling. The names Gabler, Henning, Hotho, Michelet, Werder are the ones usually mentioned first, especially among the French, when some of Hegel's disciples are to be named. Of these, however, Michelet is the only one who has never ceased to represent philosophy as such uninterruptedly, indeed even now to stand up against Schelling. Werder occupies himself exclusively with logic, Hotho with art, Henning — though not as a writer — with political economy and Prussian common law, and Gabler speaks a critical word from time to time in the Berlin Jahrbücher. What, then, will Hegel's disciples in Berlin have to say in reply to Schelling? And will they reply to him at all? So one asked everywhere. The quietism of the so-called Old Hegelians, who in Berlin had lulled themselves into a certain fleshly complacency, received a powerful jolt from Schelling. Just as the faction of the so-called Young Hegelians had already won over the youth, had seized the sympathies of those looking toward Germany's future, and had emphasized against the Old Hegelians the system's unfinishedness in particular, and the conflict of its consequences with much that passed as Hegelian orthodoxy — so too, through Schelling's appearance in Berlin, all those who until then had rather grumbled quietly against Hegel's philosophy gained courage. Eclectic natures, hungry for learning, pliable, more inclined toward poetry and history than toward speculation, in particular had to feel emboldened. In short, the opposition to Hegelian philosophy at once found a decisive rallying point. The need of the half-hearted and the weak for an authority was satisfied. The Hegelian system could wish for nothing better to withstand its trial by fire. The earlier accusation raised against it of atheism and high treason had not been a scholarly one; but now it was to come to speculative polemic, and indeed from the very man who had once published, together with Hegel, a Critical Journal of Philosophy.

For this agitation, gentlemen, we must be very grateful to Schelling as its instigator, for it is skepticism alone that creates problems and secures progress for scholarship. Only the indolent or the shallow shun struggle. Conducted with seriousness and dignity, it is the salt of scholarship, without which it would grow dull. To judge Schelling curtly, saying that he is no longer capable of anything, is to say nothing at all, for whatever the case may be with him as a systematic thinker, he has the nimbus of historical greatness on his side. No envy will scratch his immortality out of the annals of world history. The specific self-feeling that pervades him as a philosophically productive nature, the self-consciousness of having an imperishable deed behind him, will always lend him an immediate assurance that lets him despise, with an ironic smile, the sting of anonymous, unscholarly attack — all the more since a haughtiness reaching to the iciest coldness has always constituted a basic trait of Schelling's.

My intention with these lectures, gentlemen, is now to give you, from the documents of the literature, a presentation of Schelling — that is, of his philosophy — so as to make you yourselves capable of judging the present controversy. At the same time, however, I shall have to combine a critique with the reproduction of Schelling's philosophemes, which, insofar as the Hegelian system is the completion of Schelling's, can have no other sense than to show that the stage which speculation occupies with Hegel is the truth of the stage on which it stood and still stands with Schelling. Schelling's laudable effort, ever since Hegel's Phenomenology has existed, has consisted in reaching Hegel's standpoint out of himself. But he shows his ingenious originality precisely in

## The Distribution of History

not being able to do so, for history distributes its advances among different individuals, and precisely the productive individual cannot break through the circle of its own necessity, which is why it is able only to misunderstand, not to acknowledge, the deeds that spring forth further from its own doing. Schelling is not to be reproached for not wanting to let Hegel stand, but rather for how he believes he has refuted him. Or rather, this is not to be reproached in him — instead, his refutation, as merely a supposed one, is simply to be refuted in turn.

In order to follow Schelling in his development from the very beginning, gentlemen, we must transport ourselves back into the previous century, to South Germany. This region had already once had an epoch of intellectual brilliance in the Middle Ages. North Germany rose to such a level only with the Reformation. Its literature became equal to that of South Germany only once the great masses of the Slavic peoples from the Elbe to the Niemen had been completely Germanized. In the eight-

teenth century North Germany and its literature became a real power over South Germany, as Halle and Göttingen flourished and, through Frederick the Great, the sense for the universal, for the seriousness of political life, for the freedom of intelligence, was consolidated. South Germany was at that time splintered into a multitude of small particularities, of which the organization of Germany at the Peace of Vienna left only the narrow strip of the small Saxon, Hessian, and Nassau states between north and south. The Saxon states were swept along by Prussia in its upsurge. In South Germany, the despotism and luxury of the petty courts held on longer. Württemberg too suffered greatly from this mischief, especially under the regime of mistresses, about which you may read in Schlosser's History of the Eighteenth Century. Yet the second half of Duke Karl's reign took a better direction toward more spirited entertainment, particularly through art.

The Enlightenment also made its way everywhere in Swabia, and the products of North German literature, composed in its spirit, were devoured with the greatest eagerness.

The more oppressive outward life often was, especially for the middle class, the more unrestrainedly the agitation poured itself into brooding and into fantasy. In the unfortunate Schubart the floodgates of an enthusiastic eccentricity broke open; Wieland plunged from seraphic exaltation into a Voltairizing eudaemonism; Schiller, a revolutionary pupil of the Ducal Karlsschule, saved himself from the chaos churning within him, especially through the nature of the poetic genre that founded his popular fame, for the theater compels the poet toward respect for reality. A great many Württembergers emigrated to other lands, such as Spittler, Plank, Paulus, Niethammer, and others. In the theological seminary of Tübingen, the nursery of all the great men of Württemberg, the struggles of the age had, so to speak, their innermost sanctuary for the Swabians, and in North Germany at most Schulpforta — which, however, is not yet a university — can be compared to it.

It was here that Schelling arrived around Michaelmas 1790. He was fifteen years old. Extraordinary external favorable circumstances allowed the precocious, excitable youth to enter into contact very quickly with the most significant of the students. There had

formed among them a secret club, in which the enthusiasm for the ideas of the French Revolution — which at that time had not yet entered its phase of terrorism — celebrated its glowing cult. Schelling became its rapturously zealous fellow member, and it was here that he made the closer acquaintance of Hölderlin and Hegel...

Hegel, a full five years older than Schelling, had already spent two years in the seminary as a ducal scholarship-holder by the time Schelling arrived there, and had already, after writing a dissertation on the immortality of the soul, been promoted to Magister.

Like Hegel, Schelling was meant to study theology. From his father, who was rector first in Bebenhausen, then in Maulbronn, he had received, for that time, a quite competent education in Hebrew. When he was promoted to Magister in 1792, he wrote a dissertation on the third chapter of Genesis, on the Fall of Man. One certainly cannot place too much weight on such youthful works, but with Schelling it is surely characteristic that he chose precisely this theme. The crisis of human nature and, as supernaturalism assumes, the crisis of the whole of the rest of nature caused thereby on the one hand, and the converse crisis of the ultimate overcoming of all evil, the sublation of history, and the adunatio rerum omnium in Deum [the reunification of all things in God] on the other, have not ceased to occupy him to this day. He has sought to comprehend the beginning and the end of history in the most varied ways.

It was Herder, among us, in the last third of the preceding century, who accepted in principle the reasonable, critical conception of biblical history that had gained currency in England and France among the so-called Deists, but who in its execution shifted it over into the poetic point of view — something the Englishman Lowth had, admittedly, already done before him with regard to Hebrew poetry. In 1774, in his First Document of the Human Race, he poured out, with declamatory exuberance, a wealth of magnificent phrases which, by fertilizing the theologians' imagination, exercised great influence on their exegesis and above all enchanted the young, as one can gather, among other things, from the delighted words Goethe wrote about it to the consul Schönborn. In his Ideas toward a Philosophy of the History of Mankind he pursued the mythical

with greater sobriety, without, however, entirely abandoning the prophetic tone that was peculiar to him. Gervinus, History of German Poetry, IV, 493, cites Herder's words: "Whoever today were once again to display the whole work of God through times and peoples in all its dignity and simplicity — would he not be a prophet? Consider what Luther did, and shudder — and hope!" To this he adds the following remark: "We see that here speaks the same enthusiasm of the man who, in the pages of German Art and Manner, wanted to regenerate poetry — a man wholly filled with brilliant hopes for the progress of mankind, with brilliant hopes for his own effectiveness, a man of the future, a prophetic spirit himself, who here cannot conceal the impetuous wishes of his breast to become a new Luther to the people. But Luther was not driven by a presentiment of the shining roles he wished to play; he was guided quietly and gradually by the finger of the deity and of the destinies that had appointed him to be a prophet; had the slightest inkling of this prophetic role arisen in him and seduced him into taking his measures accordingly, his work would have been lost." — Of this Herderian

manner, however, a great deal passed over into Schelling, and in the preface — signed September 9, 1794 — to his first small philosophical writing, which appeared in 1795 at Heerbrandt's in Tübingen, running to 62 pages, we find even all the sermon-like catchwords, the turns of practical importunity, that are so common in Herder. Schelling wishes here "that the great feeling should not be entirely foreign to any of his readers, the feeling which the prospect of a finally attainable unity of knowledge, of belief, and of willing — the last inheritance of humanity, which it will soon demand more loudly than ever — must necessarily produce in everyone worthy of ever having heard the voice of truth! — Philosophers have often lamented that their science has so little influence on the will of man and on the destinies of our whole race, but did they also consider what it is they are lamenting? They lament that a science has no influence which, as such, existed nowhere, that no use was made of principles which only one part of mankind — and even this part only in quite different respects — held to be true. Who will follow the guidance of a guide whom he himself does not yet dare to think of as the only true one; who will heal the ills of mankind by a means that is still, even now, suspect to so many, and found among different people in such utterly different quality? Seek first in man himself the marks by which all must recognize eternal truth, before you call it down from heaven to earth in its divine form! Then all the rest will fall to you!"

For Schelling's affiliation with Herder, the latter's dualism between rationalism and supernaturalism is also highly characteristic. He begins with reason but ends with super-reason. Comprehending cognition does not carry through, and poetic intuition steps in where it breaks off. Herder considered the beginning of the human condition at the end of the second volume of his Ideas in 1785. He took Genesis's account of the state of the first humans as an allegory, as a presentation of truth in historical form, without a congruent factuality underlying it. In this respect, then, he was a rationalist. Yet at the same time he also held that the first man had been in need of education (just as Schelling said in 1809: man always needs a help) and that he could not have developed out of himself without the influence of higher beings, the Elohim — which was again supernaturalistic. That the Elohim brought the animals before Adam so that he might name them was, for him, particularly important on account of his theory of the origin of language. Yet here too he swung back once more into the rationalistic, at least in a footnote, by leaving the "how" of the influence undetermined.

Following Herder — as was also the case at that time with Kant, Schiller, and others — Schelling took the narrative of the Fall as the poetic expression of mankind's oldest philosopheme concerning the origin of evils (antiquissimi de prima malorum origine philosophematis explicandi Genes. III. tentamen criticum philosophicum, 40 pp., 4°).

Hegel left Tübingen at Michaelmas 1793 to take up a tutoring position in Bern. He cannot, however, have had all that much contact with Schelling in that year, since otherwise it would be impossible that around Christmas 1794 he should write to Schelling in Tübingen concerning a treatise which the latter had already had printed in 1793 in Paulus's Memorabilia, V, I, 1-68: on myths, historical legends, and philosophemes of the oldest world. Hegel says in his letter that he sees from it that Schelling is continuing on his path of elucidating important theological concepts. This treatise is a continuation of the dissertation, which is also explicitly cited in it. The distinction of myth, legend, and allegorically expressed philosopheme is drawn with lively rhetoric, yet quite sensibly, in Herder's manner, who is a few times, and rightly, contested in the footnotes. The childlikeness of the first humans and the influences of nature, etc., as well as the loose combining of analogies, play a large role in Herderian fashion.

Up to that point, then, Schelling was a philosophical theologian. In the autumn of 1794 he first appeared as a pure philosopher. The little tract appeared for the Easter book fair of 1795.

Reinhold had reduced the problem of philosophy to the formula of distinguishing the represented and the representing from the representation as such. Fichte, connecting to Kant with sharper consistency, put in its place the opposition of object and subject in their various possible relations. Schulze had come forward against Reinhold with a dogmatic skepticism, which Fichte, in 1794, in a critique of Aenesidemus (Jena Literaturzeitung, no. 47 ff.), refuted into its nothingness. In the same year Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre appeared.

From this Schelling drew the impulse for his first philosophical attempt, which he wrote in the autumn of 1794, in a few printer's sheets, under the title On the Possibility of a Form of Philosophy in General, and in which he paid full homage to subjective idealism. Schelling's main thought was that the moments of Kant's category of relation, as Kant himself had indicated, also underlay those of the other categories, but were themselves in turn conditioned by something higher — this higher thing being precisely the I positing itself categorically, hypothetically, and disjunctively; this, then, contained the primal form of knowledge.

Having been an interpreter of the world of myth, he appears all at once as a philosopher who grasps philosophy reformatorily not in a single problem but at once in its totality. In this way he also leapt from topic to topic later on,

We further see him attach himself to something given. Sanguinely excited, he lives with entire devotion for it and does in fact advance it further. He is no common follower, but a truly productive mind.

In his enthusiasm, however, he deceives himself to the extent that he forgets the impulse that another gave him, and afterward regards even what that other had done — because he has absorbed it into his own advance — as his own discovery, his own invention. He becomes ungrateful, at first without knowing it, and, once he has become conscious of this, gives just cause for the unpleasant legal disputes over the theft of ideas, over the mine and thine of thoughts. This is what he did with Kant, Jacobi, Fichte, and Hegel. Now, in throwing himself into a task with passionate absorption, he proceeds assertorically. He does not derive; he says, it is so. The turn of phrase "I believe," "I hold," and so on occurs in him endlessly often. But the assertoric judgment passes of itself over into the problematic. My assurance that something is so is true only insofar as something else, which contains the condition of its existence, is constituted thus and not otherwise. Schelling made an extravagant use of hypothesis. Not calm enough to pursue a consequence through all its members, he helps himself, whenever a stoppage occurs, past it by presuppositions, by possibilities to the right and left, and thereby creates for himself, without becoming aware of it, countless contradictions. Indeed, he goes so far as to leave gaps to be filled in as one pleases.

This lack of circumspection, of critical sobriety, of continuity in thinking also fragments his style of writing. Here a parenthesis nests itself in; there a note attaches itself, which itself in turn has a note. The paragraphs cut through the whole only externally, into indifferent divisions. Schelling never had the patience to work out an organically interlinked chain-mail. His only systematic work written in one continuous flow, not haltingly like the Draft of a System of Natural Philosophy — the work he himself called System of Transcendental Idealism, 1800 — is nevertheless, in form, only a contrafacture of Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre.

The urge of progress, however, led Schelling to a poetic-prophetic tone, whose bold outspokenness aroused great enthusiasm in others as well. It was the very word of greatness, with whose magic ring Schelling captivated; it was the intimation that he was the man of the century for the solution of the "great tasks" he set forth, by which he knew how to keep a constant expectation in suspense. One must not take this — as does Magis Amica Veritas in his Discoveries concerning the Newest Discoveries in Philosophy — as merely a common trick of a cleverness bent on self-promotion; rather, Schelling was unquestionably always, for himself, convinced of the objective importance of his doing and willing.

In the winter of 1794-95 he wrote Of the I as Principle of Philosophy, or On the Unconditioned in Human Knowledge.

In this writing he attached himself entirely to Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre, and it might only be noted that Schelling also inverted the judgment "the I is the Absolute" into "the Absolute is the I." "Whoever wants to know something wants at the same time that his knowing have reality." Such is the first sentence of this writing. The point is to be found at which ideality and reality, thinking and being, coincide identically, for if something higher existed outside of knowing that constituted the condition for knowing itself, so that knowing were thus not itself the highest, then it could not be absolute. Reality, then, must not merely exist as an object separate from it, the way God himself is taken by dogmatism simply as a thing, but must be a moment of knowing itself. Schelling expresses, in this connection, the wish to be able to set up a counterpart to Spinoza's Ethics, in which God is determined as the absolute object.

Schelling did not mention Fichte at all, but all the more Kant and Reinhold, of whose "principle of consciousness" he says, on p. 20: "in it the last stage of abstraction had been reached, on which one had to stand before one could arrive at that which is higher than all abstraction."

In the preface, p. XIV, Schelling asserts that philosophy, up to him, had progressed only from object to object; now the second possible revolution in the domain of philosophy was to follow, the inversion of principles. — But had not Descartes, had not Spinoza, Kant, done this — had they set up no principles? — Toward the end of the preface he pours himself out in depictions of the future perfection of science: "But once the dawn is there, the sun cannot fail to follow. To actually usher in this more beautiful day of science is reserved for only a few — perhaps only for one," etc.

As a seed-tip of his own evolution, one should note the emphasis with which Schelling, on the last page, mentions §76 of Kant's Critique of Judgment (the relation of teleology to mechanism) — conclusion: "The finite I, too, is therefore to strive to bring forth in the world what is real in the infinite, and the highest calling of man is — to make the unity of ends in the world into mechanism, and mechanism into the unity of ends."

To this shaping of subjective idealism are attached the "Letters on Dogmatism and Criticism," 1795, first published without his name in Niethammer's Philosophisches Journal, II, 3, pp. 177 ff. and III, 3, pp. 174 ff., reprinted in the Philosophische Schriften I, 1809, pp. 117-200. In this reprint a change is to be noted. The close of the eighth letter — there are ten letters in all — recalls Lessing's paradox, that he associates the idea of a most perfect being with that of boredom, and would not, for anything in the world, wish to become blessed. To this Schelling had added:

"For whoever does not think this way, I see no help in philosophy."

He struck out these words in 1809. Further, the close of the ninth letter was left out: "If one wants to make the opposition to the demand of dogmatism more noticeable, it is this: strive to bring, not yourself closer to the deity, but the deity closer to you, into infinity."

To make these letters more comprehensible to us, we must recall that Schelling had, in fact, carried on a philosophical correspondence with Hegel in the winter of 1795, which could have given him personally an impulse toward this form; and besides this, that the epistolary form was at that time altogether very popular, not only in the novel but also in philosophy. In the times of Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz, the real correspondences of philosophers had flourished; now came the period of fictitious ones. Schiller had published his pantheistic correspondence between Julius and Raphael in 1786; Reinhold had acquired his chief popularity through his Letters on Kantian Philosophy printed in Wieland's Merkur, and so on. Schelling declared, in a prefatory note, that he had chosen the letter form for the sake of clarity.

He called dogmatism that system which subordinates the subject to the object, and criticism that which subordinates the object to the subject. These, he held, were the only two truly possible philosophies. The dogmatist annihilates himself in order to make room in himself for the presupposed object; the criticist annihilates the object in order to assert himself absolutely. The most perfect example of dogmatism was, he held, Spinozism, because Spinoza, as subject, wholly divests himself in favor of God as the absolute object. Here Schelling immerses himself in admiration of Spinoza's disinterested love of God. He overlooks that, with Spinoza, one must first translate the terminology of popular expression — God, love, and the like — into the speculative terminology of substance, attribute, and mode, in order to understand it in its true sense. He therefore regards him as a mystic; he transforms him into the rapturous intellectualist Malebranche. But it is true that he sets forth, with fine enthusiasm, how Spinoza made virtue a mere consequence of blessedness, rather than making blessedness a result of exertions of virtue. In the eighth letter he goes so far as to say: "The highest to which our ideas can rise is evidently a being that, absolutely self-sufficient, enjoys only its own being, a being in which all passivity ceases, which stands passively toward nothing, not even toward laws, which acts absolutely freely only in accordance with its own being, and whose sole law is its own essence. Descartes and Spinoza — your names are almost the only ones that can as yet be named when one speaks of this idea! Few understood you, still fewer wished to understand you."

Schelling also conceives the distinction between dogmatism and criticism as the opposition of passivity and activity, and sets up the imperative:

Be! as the highest formula of criticism. This conception leads him to Greek tragedy, in which man struggles against necessity and perishes in this struggle, yet proves his freedom precisely in his downfall, since without the struggle he would not have come to ruin either.

In the end — and this is actually the progress unconsciously made in these letters — subjective idealism blurs together with Spinozistic mysticism and Hellenic fatalism into a sublime mood that presentiently anticipates the overcoming of all oppositions. "We want to be glad if we can be convinced that we have advanced to the last great problem to which all philosophy can press forward. Our spirit feels freer," etc. And: "We do not want to complain, but to be glad that we finally stand at the crossroads where separation is unavoidable: glad that we have explored the secret of our spirit, by virtue of which the just man becomes free of himself, while the unjust man trembles of himself before the justice that he did not find within himself, and which he therefore had to hand over to another world, into the hands of a punishing judge. Never henceforth will the wise man take refuge in mysteries in order to

to conceal. It is a crime against humanity to conceal principles that are generally communicable; but nature itself has set limits to this communicability: it has reserved — for the worthy — a philosophy that becomes esoteric of itself, etc."

Immediately following these letters comes a work which Schelling himself later completely ignored, and which has also been forgotten by others who would have had a duty to return to it — by historians of the most recent philosophy. Even Stahl, Schelling's pupil, passes over it fleetingly in his Philosophy of Law, vol. I, 1830, in the development of the history of recent philosophy, p. 260. It is the:

New Deduction of Natural Right, in Niethammer's Journal, IV.4, 1796, pp. 277 ff., and 1797, V.4, pp. 277 ff. In the intervening year, 1796, Fichte's Foundations of Natural Right According to the Principles of the Wissenschaftslehre had appeared, and in 1797 Kant's Doctrine of Right; and the editors of the Journal had to apologize for having interrupted the printing of Schelling's work for so long. Fichte, in the third section of his unpaginated introduction to the Foundations, claims that apart from hints from Erhard and Maimon he had been able to make use of nothing for his work.

Schelling had posited the absolute causality of the I as the unconditioned principle of human knowledge, and in this respect was a Fichtean. He had further united the ethical resignation of Spinozism with the subjectivity of critical idealism. He had abolished the opposition of theory and practice in the wretchedly eudaemonistic manner in which the rabble of the Kantian school took it. The reasoning that I must act unconditionally in a practical sense because I cannot theoretically realize myself unconditionally was not only ridiculed by him — he had also expressly declared that true knowledge would already of itself result in true action. Thus, by rejecting the talk of the weakness of human reason, of the deficiency of the faculty of cognition, and of the correspondingly more strongly felt need to entrust oneself to practical reason, he was led to the attempt to present the I as the unconditioned in human action. Schelling's deduction, although he does not say so, links up with the third and fourth theorems of Kant's Critique of Practical Reason, which had first appeared in 1788. The opposition between the matter of the will, as morality, and its form, as freedom — i.e., here, self-determination — as well as the opposition of autonomy and heteronomy, constitute the categories underlying the deduction. All being-determined by an object is heteronomy; the agent must simply be himself. Schelling therefore also says here: Be! is the highest demand that practice makes.

He posits right as the thoroughgoing antithesis of duty. Duty positively commands me what I ought to do; right only forbids me what I ought not to do. Morality sets for my individual will the limit springing from the essence of duty; right sets only a boundary to my practical possibility. Its technical expression is being-permitted. What is not forbidden, I am permitted to do. The limits of realization here are, on the one hand, nature — physical impossibility — and, on the other, humanity, the general will, against whose power that of the individual will is powerless. Morality has no such limits. Even if it cannot externally realize a duty, it is enough for it truly to will it.

Since right thus stands in a relation to nature, the subject of right must possess a physical causality. Such a causality, in which autonomy and heteronomy are immediately united, is life; the subject must be living. The right of the subject therefore extends only as far as it can realize its individuality according to the form of the will. Schelling finally draws from this the conclusion that the right of nature, because it rests on physical superior force, must destroy itself, i.e. must abolish all right. Freedom occurs in it always only in opposition, because all are in themselves free, all are equal to one another in their practical possibility, and all originally stand in the same relation to objects as selfless things. The individual will must therefore be capable of being forced into identity with the positively universal will.

Practice must precisely for that reason unite the free individuality of moral autonomy with the heteronomous external necessity of right. The concluding paragraph, 163, accordingly leads to a new problem: "to make the physical power of the individual identical with the moral power of right, or to the problem of a condition in which physical force is always also on the side of right. But in passing over to the solution of this problem, we also enter the domain of a new science."

In the postscript Schelling promises a commentary on these aphorisms, complains of the skepticism which, in conjunction with the letter-bound spirit, compels science to derive its principles as strictly, cogently, and literally as possible," and polemicizes against the persecution of philosophers by the rabble. "Once one is certain of the principles, and this has been decided among the philosophers, then they should and must also be brought before the people in a quite different form — only the people must not presume to take part in the investigations before they are completed and have become ripe for a general and public decision."

Indeed, indeed! When are the philosophers in agreement? And where, and with whom, does the unphilosophical populace begin? Who may presume to say that something could be permitted to be taught in the schools of the philosophers which would act as a destructive poison for the people? ## The Leibniz-Wolffian Philosophy

In V, issue 4, p. 315, the Leibnizian philosophy is spoken of as a system that up to now, in regard to the fruitfulness of its ideas, capable of a truly infinite development, was the only one of its kind." Indeed it is said: "The history of our philosophy (for so we may surely call the Leibnizian) -." This remark is noteworthy because in the Letters on Dogmatism and Criticism, as we have seen, Descartes and Spinoza were still praised as the only ones whose names one might name when it came to the true concept of the absolute being. Schelling's insight into the history of philosophy had thus made progress.

Schelling postulated the unity of the whole of reason, emphasized the spontaneity of intelligence, made the transition from nature to freedom, and said, among other things, in VI, issue 2, p. 200: "Spirit wills, and it is free. That it wills — no further ground can be given for that. For precisely because this act occurs unconditionally, it is a willing." And p. 201: "Spirit is an original willing." Here, then, we already encounter that conception of the concept of spirit which Schelling placed at the head of the

final shaping of his philosophy. That willing constitutes a determination inseparable from the concept of spirit, and hence original within it, is certain. Spirit cannot acquire willing only afterward. But it is wrong when willing is elevated into the original totality from which the other determinations of spirit are then supposed to be derived. For in and for itself willing too is only a moment of spirit. Schelling actually substitutes the concept of subjectivity for that of willing. Selfhood, self-determination, is the more general concept, to which both thinking and willing are subordinate. Freedom is no less the element of theoretical reason than of practical reason.

Schelling had this survey reprinted in the Collected Writings, 1809, I, 203-340. But he omitted not only the replies to Heydenreich and Tittmann, the afterword to the formal philosophers (V.3, 258-60), and other minor pieces, but also the introduction, the critique of Heydenreich's Letters on Atheism, and the conclusion of the whole. In this, VIII, 1798, issue 1, p. 128 ff., he wants applied to philosophy, not indeed, as had already been done by Spinoza and Wolff, a mathematical method,

but rather the method of mathematics to be applied. P. 131: "All sciences will ultimately dissolve into a universal science: into a universal mathematics." What one is actually supposed to think by this is hard to say, for if the method of mathematics to be applied in philosophy is not itself supposed to be a mathematical one, then the designation "mathematical" would be entirely superfluous, since the method could then only be either the analytic or the synthetic — which Aristotle called epagoge and apodeixis, and distinguished in the Organon as the only two forms of the genesis of cognition. These two methods even underlie those of mathematics itself, and hence, if there is to be talk of a specific application of mathematics to philosophy, one can only proceed as Kant did, who in the Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science wanted the element of intuition added to that of the concept of understanding, and placed the distinctive character of mathematics in precisely this union. Should the expression "mathematical" be meant only to indicate the rigor of demonstration, this would of course be ridiculous, for exactness and cogency are just as much a duty of philosophy as of mathematics. Schelling has

however not expressed himself more clearly. In his own writings we do not find that, in point of form, he ever went beyond the analytic decomposition he particularly favored, or beyond the pattern of the synthetic method with definitions, corollaries, and so forth.

We have deliberately refrained from establishing, right at the outset, periods into which Schelling's

development might be divided, for once such nodal points have been set, they easily exert an influence on how the matter is grasped. But that with the "Survey" we have reached the end of a second phase of Schelling's, following the first, theological-mythological one, may now well be recognized. It began with the question of the possibility of a form of philosophy, then turned to the investigation of the absoluteness of cognition; it destroyed the opinion of the necessary limitation of cognition by the object, in that Schelling developed dogmatism and criticism as the opposed, sole true forms of philosophy; it deduced the concept of willing and finally, in the "Survey," gathered together all sides of reason, in order to devote itself to the special discussion of content — nature, history, and art. At the same time Schelling had progressed from modest attachment to Reinhold and Fichte in his first little treatise to ingenious scorn for mediocrity and impotence, and could already see himself cited by other contributors to the Philosophical Journal as a weighty authority, as one of those who had most deeply penetrated Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre.

We still lack an adequate history of the epoch of natural science in Germany at the end of the last century. Steffens, in his memoirs, has given us very fitting contributions to this, and in particular has depicted the magnificent activity of Werner in Freiberg, where Humboldt and others first developed. But there is a difference between biographical recollections and a critical, coherent narrative. Such a narrative, however, cannot be expected here either, where we are occupied with Schelling in detail. We must presuppose it. We need only highlight those moments which stand in a more definite relation to Schelling and which he himself mentions and acknowledges as precedents in his writings. We will first name the writings themselves and then what is to be regarded as common to them, since this was precisely what Schelling found the expression for.

Above all here are to be named: Kant's Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science and, for organics, that same author's treatise on teleological judgment in his Critique of Judgment. Of the former, Schelling said in the Ideas, Book II, chap. 5, that the analytic treatment of the concept of matter and the derivation of the principles of dynamics were accomplished in it "with such evidence and completeness that nothing further remains to be done here." For Fichte, nature had been, as it were, covered with a veil. It counted for him really only as the means of intercourse between intelligences. Schelling also entered into that side of the Kantian system which made a new shaping of natural science possible. He never concealed this anywhere. He cites Kant incessantly, makes use of his terminology, links up with his problems, and, bearing this in mind, one must actually wonder how the Schellingian school could so completely forget this relation, as though the philosophy of nature had fallen from heaven with Schelling — something which Schelling himself, at that time at least, did not yet imagine at all. On the more precise relation between Kant and Schelling in this regard, one should consult my History of Kantian Philosophy.

Next to be named is Kielmeyer's address: On the Relation of the Organic Forces to One Another in the Series of the Various Organizations, the Laws and Consequences of These Relations, Stuttgart 1793. Schelling himself said in the World Soul, 1798, p. 298, that future ages would undoubtedly date from it the epoch of an entirely new natural

history." Later he referred back to this in the System of Transcendental Idealism, p. 257. In the First Outline of a System of the Philosophy of Nature, 1799, p. 220, note, he indeed wants to trace Kielmeyer's view back, on one occasion, to Blumenbach's Specimen physiologiae comparatae inter animalia calidi et frigidi sanguinis, and then to Herder's Ideas for the Philosophy of History, 1786, I, pp. 117-26.

Further, Eschenmayer, a pupil of Kielmeyer's, at that time genuinely arousing great hopes for science, wrote a treatise in which he led metaphysics over into chemistry, following the hint given by Kant in the Metaphysical Foundations: principiae quaedam disciplinae naturalis, inprimis chemiae, ex metaphysica naturae substernenda, Tübingen 1796. From this treatise stems that expression of Schelling's philosophy which was to become so fateful: Potenz [potence/power]. Schelling also took up other formulas of Eschenmayer's further on, and he himself, in 1801, particularly names Eschenmayer's deduction of the living organism.

Very important for the philosophy of nature is supposed to be a treatise by Fr. Baader, also often cited at the time, On the Pythagorean Square, from which the mischief with the schematism of world-directions took its beginning. I confess, however, that despite every effort expended, I have never myself set eyes on this treatise, and have found nothing about it in the most thorough historians of the most recent philosophy, e.g. Michelet. Schelling himself cites Baader's Contributions to Elementary Physiology a few times, and praises, in 1803, in his essay on the planets, Baader's schema of world-directions...

Beyond doubt, however, is that Schelling owes an infinite amount to Lichtenberg. The critical spirit of this physicist, as learned as he was knowledgeable, shook up most concepts, and, among other things, furnished Erxleben's Compendium of Natural Science with a host of stimulating additions, almost none of which failed to leave evidence of its effect on Schelling — something the latter never concealed either. Particularly to be cited are the discussion of the various kinds of air and the critique of Crawford's theory of heat... To this was added, in the sixth edition of 1794, the unforgettable preface.

As long as Schelling remained in Tübingen, no genuine engagement with natural science is to be observed in him. Only when he went to Leipzig in 1796, only when he there heard Hindenburg's lectures on mathematics and physics, only when the atmosphere of Jena began to work on him, did his natural-philosophical direction break forth impetuously. It is probably not entirely improbable that Goethe's proximity as well — in whose house he later stayed for days at a time — exerted a stimulating influence on him, just as Hindenburg's inclination toward combinatorics also appears not insignificant for Schelling's manner.

If we now ask ourselves what was common to all these efforts of empirical natural research, we must see it, first, in the fact that through discoveries emerging alongside and independently of one another, much that had until then lain far apart and seemed to have no kinship whatsoever now suddenly drew closely together, whereby a tendency necessarily had to be produced to treat the hitherto received tradition of science as problematic altogether.

Second, duality showed itself everywhere in the positive determinations that were becoming dominant. In mechanics, Kant's theory of the opposition of attraction and repulsion held sway; in chemistry, through the more abstract conception of electricity as positive and negative, its phenomenon was brought closer to that of magnetism; Cavendish had decomposed water and found the union of two gases to be the condition of its existence; in physiology the opposition of irritability and sensibility emerged; in pathology, with Brown, that of the excitable and the exciting, and so on.

Third, the unity of the opposites therefore had to force itself upon attention. The so-called attractive force was a predicate of the very same matter as the repulsive force. Negative electricity could be distinguished from positive by nothing other than the opposition itself, by no means through particular properties. It was thus one and the same electricity that was at once both positive and negative, just as magneticity in itself is everywhere the same, and nonetheless the unlike poles attract one another, the like poles repel one another, and so on.

Schelling's great merit was now not merely to pursue the unity of a single duality, but the absolute unity of all dualisms. He did not posit unity as an abstraction from which the manifoldness of the particular is merely left out; rather he recognized the necessity of the heterogeneous for the harmony of the whole. The main opposition to his endeavor was thus formed by atomistic-mechanical physics, whose classical representative for his time he regarded as the system of Le Sage, which Lichtenberg too prized very highly. In this he relied chiefly, as he himself says, on the reports which Prevost, in his treatise de l'origine des forces magnétiques, Geneva 1788 (German translation, Halle 1794), had given of Le Sage's views. He devoted an entire chapter in the second book of his Ideas to combating these. As late as 1799, in the introduction to a First Outline of a System of the Philosophy of Nature, p. 6, he said: "Our science is, according to what has gone before, wholly and thoroughly realistic; it is therefore nothing other than physics, it is only speculative physics — in tendency wholly the same as the systems of the ancient physicists and, in more recent times, the system of the restorer of Epicurean philosophy, Le Sage's mechanical physics, through which, after a long scientific sleep, the speculative spirit was first reawakened in physics." This judgment is unquestionably one of Schelling's overhasty ones, for Kant surely has quite different claims to this praise than that already forgotten Genevan.

Schelling found himself especially furthered in his new activity by Spinoza and Leibniz.

The one always reminded him of the unity of all the particular, while the other attracted him through the many-sided extent of his knowledge and through his critical spirit in the sphere of natural science. The one nourished in him the realism of universal substance; the other, through his spiritualism, was akin to the standpoint of subjective idealism, which Schelling had at that time in fact not yet consciously abandoned. In accounts of the history of recent German philosophy it has become customary to treat Schelling's philosophy of nature as a single whole, taking individual determinations now from this, now from that of his writings. But with this eclectic procedure one cannot sketch a faithful picture of Schelling, because with him it is not merely the bare What that matters, but equally the How. Along with his philosophy one must also develop his philosophizing. It is precisely the constant becoming, the continual self-transcending, that is the interesting thing about Schelling. But this is bound to be lost as soon as one does not follow him step by step and instead gives, in place of his genesis, a dogmatic outline of his system — a system he himself never fixed. So one must also, in tracing his development, carefully note the point at which he himself, although already seized in himself by the truth of a higher standpoint, could still not overcome the subjective derivation of the concept of nature.

Schelling saw through the vanity of the theories he found before him with a sharp eye. Against the materialism of mechanical physics he ultimately summed up his own polemic in Kästner's words against Le Sage's attack on Galileo's law of falling bodies: "There are certain small particles of time of a determinate size, but one does not know how large; at the beginning of each such particle of time, and never otherwise, something strikes a falling body — one does not know what, nor how strongly. So in this time it travels a distance — one does not know how far. And further, it does not fall according to the law people claim to have found by experience, but according to quite another law, which, however, cannot by experience be recognized as different from the former. And granting all this, what do we learn? — That the falling of bodies can very comprehensibly be explained from things about all of which one knows nothing. The law found is this: the distances of every falling body are as X quantities of an X time-atom. Against Humean skepticism he objected that it leaves unexplained how such a constant uniformity of the succession of phenomena — or rather of their apprehension in us — comes about. Hume maintained that the connection of two facts as cause and effect has only a subjective value, because we have simply become accustomed to regarding them, in their succession, under the category of causality. Schelling thought one could grant this, but must ask not only how, on a mere habituation, the succession could remain constant in its before and after, but above all how we could ever arrive at all at such a representation, which becomes second nature to us. He raised the same objection against criticism [Kantianism]. This distinguished the intuition of the sensible manifold from the a priori concept of the understanding. Experience was supposed to be only the synthesis of both determinations. But, said Schelling, how do I come to it that, as you assure me, the sensible, matter, makes an impression on me? You assume this exactly as the dogmatists do, that there are things outside us. You let the understanding apply its categories of cause and effect, etc., only afterward

onto it. So you posit a matter without form, a form without matter. If only you could make comprehensible how nature comes so readily to submit to this transference of the laws of understanding, the forms of thought! You distinguish the inner from the outer and assert the unknowability of the former. Things in themselves are supposed to be inaccessible to us. But where, then, does the inner begin? Do you not, when you decompose the compact, the sensible, always find surfaces anew? Above all, however, you remain obliged to explain how we — since in fact we can only make our representations the object of our consciousness, not directly the things themselves of which they are the representations — how we are nonetheless able to connect with certain representations the certainty that something outside us corresponds to them in themselves? It could not fail that Schelling took offense at the timidity with which Kant had posited the concept of purposiveness merely as a means for the easier apprehension of nature. He rightly wondered why purposiveness should be found in some natural products but not in others. Does this not prove that the concept is immanently bound up with objectivity itself, that we are subject to a compulsion in transferring the teleological principle onto nature, and that purposiveness therefore exists not merely for us, but in fact also in itself? Here too he concluded: How organized products came to be actual outside of

and independently of me was not at all what I demanded to know, for how could I even form a clear concept of that? The question was: how the representation of purposive products outside me came into me, and how I am compelled to think this purposiveness — although it belongs to things only in relation to my understanding — as nevertheless real and necessary outside me. This question you have not answered." Of a master-builder-like divine understanding as an organizing principle of matter he would hear absolutely nothing, because this would abolish the concept of creating, indeed of nature itself. "A being in which the concept of the deed, the design of its execution, precedes, cannot bring forth; it can only form, shape matter that already exists; it can only imprint on matter from outside the stamp of understanding and purposiveness; what it produces is not purposive in itself, but only in relation to the understanding of the artist — not originally and necessarily, but only contingently, purposive."

Schelling now sought to resolve, for science, the opposition of matter and spirit, of object and subject, by deriving, in the fourth chapter of the second book of the Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature, the first origin of the concept of matter from the nature and intuition of the human spirit," as follows. The mind [Gemüth], as he put it, is the unity of an unlimited and a limiting force. Limitlessness would make consciousness just as impossible as absolute limitedness. Only insofar as the force that strives toward the unlimited is limited by the opposed force, while the limited force is in turn conversely released from its limits, are feeling, perceiving, and cognizing conceivable. Only the antagonism of both forces, hence only their ever-becoming, relative unity, is the actual mind. It is just the same in nature. Matter as such is not the first thing, but rather the forces whose unity constitutes it. It is to be grasped only as the ever-becoming product of attraction and repulsion — hence not as inert crassness, as one might well imagine, but rather those forces are the original; force, however, in the material is as it were the immaterial. Force is, in nature, that which can be compared to spirit. Since, then, the mind presents itself as the same conflict of opposed forces as matter does, they must themselves be united in a higher identity. The organ of spirit for grasping nature, however, is intuition, which takes possession, as object of the external senses, of the space bounded and filled by attracting and repelling forces. So Schelling had to draw the conclusion that the same absolute appears in nature as it does as spirit, and that its harmony is not a thought merely applied to it. "Or if you maintain that we merely transfer such an idea onto nature, then no inkling of what nature is and ought to be for us has ever entered your soul. For we do not want nature merely to coincide contingently (say, through the mediation of some third thing) with the laws of our spirit, but rather that it itself necessarily and originally not merely express but itself realize the laws of our spirit, and that it be and be called nature only insofar as it does this." "Nature is to be the visible spirit, spirit the invisible nature. Here, then,

in the absolute identity of the spirit within us and nature outside us, the problem of how a nature outside us should be possible must be resolved."

This was the standpoint that in general underlay the Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature, as an Introduction to the Study of This Science, Part One, Landshut 1799 [misprinted 1779 in source], octavo. The title of this work recalls Herder's work on the history of humanity. Herder too wanted to give ideas toward the philosophy of the same. But whereas in Herder we find a completely systematic edifice, in this respect Schelling's work is precisely the opposite. The greatest contingency of the objects, of their sequence, of their treatment, and of the sources drawn upon is visible in it. It gives the impression of the reflections of an ingenious, perceptive man upon extensive reading. For a while he let what he read stand as it was, but now he has become critical and begins to discover the deficient, the contradictory. Yet he does not stop at this negative business; rather he himself searches out thoughts to remedy the noted defects

and gaps by means of ingenious conjectures. Great as is the pleasure the observation of imperfections gives him, the desire to create positive improvements and extensions is incomparably greater. The optative and subjunctive forms — the could, should, might, all the turns of phrase expressing perhaps — thus make up the fundamental tone of the presentation. Yet it is certain that Schelling gave an extremely fruitful stimulus with them, and had an effect similar to that of the Problemata of Aristotle, which in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries set tasks for exact research.

After an introduction, whose essential content we have developed above, there follow two books, of which the first treats the following subjects: 1) On the Combustion of Bodies; 2) On Light; 3) On Air and the Various Kinds of Air; 4) On Electricity; 5) On the Magnet (as Schelling spells it); 6) General Considerations as a Result of the Foregoing. The second book treats: 1) On Attraction and Repulsion in General as Principles of a Universal System of Nature; 2) On the Illusory Use of Both Principles; 3) Some Remarks on the Mechanical Physics of Mr. Le Sage; 4) The First Origin of the Concept of Matter from the Nature and Intuition of the Human Spirit; 5) First Principles of Dynamics; 6) On the Contingent Determinations of Matter; 7) Philosophy of Chemistry; 8) Application of the Same to Particular Objects; 9) Essay on the First Principles of Chemistry.

In the preface Schelling expounds on the distinction of philosophy, both theoretical and practical, into pure and applied. "Pure theoretical philosophy occupies itself merely with the investigation of the reality of our knowledge in general; applied philosophy, however, under the name of a philosophy of nature, has the task of deriving a determinate system of our knowledge (i.e., the system of the whole of experience) from principles. What physics is for theoretical philosophy, history is for practical philosophy, and thus from these two main parts of philosophy develop the two main branches of our empirical knowledge. With a treatment of the philosophy of nature and the philosophy of man, I hope therefore to encompass the whole of applied philosophy. Through the former, natural science; through the latter, history is to receive a scientific foundation."

On the relation of this first part to the second, which never appeared, he further says: "the philosophical part of this work concerns dynamics, as the fundamental science of natural science, and chemistry as a consequence of it. The next following part will encompass the principles of organic natural science, or so-called physiology."

He wants the distinction between the two books of this first part to be understood such that the first is empirical and the second philosophical. But this distinction is empty. Things proceed in the one exactly as in the other, and the investigations of the first book contain just as much philosophy as those of the second do regard for empirical fact — particularly with respect to chemistry, in which he cites, with the highest approval, Scherer's Supplements to His Outlines of the Newer Chemical Theory, Jena 1796, and Eschenmayer's treatise mentioned above. It could well be that the latter greatly reinforced Schelling in the merely quantitative determination of qualitative differences. Eschenmayer's two main propositions, as cited by Schelling himself, run in this regard: "Since matter fills space not by existence alone but by forces, and since the varying proportion of these same forces brings about only a gradual distinction, all diversities of matter ultimately reduce to a diversity of degree.

The qualities of matter are therefore gradual relations. — Chemical operations concern themselves with changes of the gradual relations of matter." That quality has a determinate relation to its quantity, and that the quantitative alteration of the qualitative can abolish it and bring about its transition into another quality, is certain. But the other quality is by no means therefore itself a determination to be grasped merely as a gradual difference. This latter is only the difference existing within one and the same quality, e.g. green, greener, greenest. But the white into which green as light-green, or the black into which it as dark-green, can pass over, are no longer mere degrees of the green color, but qualitatively distinct differences. Green is not a lower or higher degree of white or black, etc. It is no different in the process of chemical objects. Quantity is only a moment of quality, by no means its definition. Later, when the untenability of Eschenmayer's conception came to light, in 1801, Schelling himself protested against it, as we shall see further on.

One might now expect an account of the concepts Schelling formed at that time of magnetism, of light, and so forth. But this is, in part, impossible given his subjective, ever-limiting, conditioning presentation, without going into the minutest detail, and, in part, this determination was not the main point. In it Schelling was even, as could not be otherwise, dependent on empirical fact, and others were not lacking in equally good ideas. To be sure, he would not have advanced natural science through mere hypothetical generalities; the perceptive engagement with detail, the sound critique of prevailing notions within empirical science, were the indispensable means for winning the sympathy of actual researchers; but the essential thing through which he had his effect was nevertheless the speculative enthusiasm of the natural researchers. Let us illustrate with one example how this fire of thought broke through everywhere in him. In the chapter on electricity he proceeds for pages entirely critically, analyzing the explanations of Priestley and others. But then passages like the following occur: "It is unnecessary trouble that many have taken to prove how entirely differently fire and electricity act. Everyone who has once seen or heard something of both knows that. But our spirit strives for unity in the system of its cognitions; it cannot bear having a separate principle forced upon it for every single phenomenon, and it believes it sees nature only where, in the greatest manifoldness of phenomena, it discovers the greatest simplicity of laws, and, in the highest prodigality of effects, at the same time the highest economy of means. Thus even every thought — even one still crude and unworked — deserves attention as soon as it aims at a simplification of principles, and if it serves no other purpose, it at least serves as a stimulus to investigate for oneself and to trace out the hidden course of nature." It was this manner of carrying forward Herder's exhortatory sermons toward fathoming things that had so great an effect.

As a second part of the Ideas, Schelling had announced a physiology; instead of this, however, expressly remarking that it should not be taken as a continuation of the Ideas, he gave the book: On the World Soul. A Hypothesis of Higher Physics for the Explanation of the General Organism. Hamburg 1798.

This book is the most solid in the series of his natural-philosophical writings. The chaotic vagueness of the Ideas has already receded; the relation to experience is more decisive, more solid; the language is clear and matter-of-fact, and only occasionally has traces of that mysticism to which Schelling later not infrequently paid homage. For example, when on p. 129 he speaks of fire and adds "which, since Prometheus, has not been extinguished on earth," this poetic-mythical flight of fancy appears strange enough in the midst of an otherwise entirely dry exposition. Or when on p. 132 he says: "Perhaps in the heights of the atmosphere, to which only in summer do clouds sometimes rise, in those regions to which the ancients transposed the seat of the gods." Or when on p. 290 he says: "irritability is, as it were, the center point around which all organic forces gather; to discover its causes would mean to unveil the mystery of life and to lift the veil of nature." Or p. 303: "Thus the contemplation of inorganic no less than of organic nature comes to a halt before that unknown in which the oldest philosophy already surmised the first force of nature." Or when at the close, p. 305, he speaks of the general continuity of all natural causes, of a common medium through which alone all forces of nature act on the sensible being, when in it he recognizes anew that being "which the oldest philosophy, divining, hailed as the common soul of nature, and which some physicists of that time held to be one with the formative and shaping ether (the portion of the noblest natures)" — one asks oneself which philosophy is meant to be designated by the expression "oldest," since the oldest Greek philosophers, Heraclitus included, did not philosophize about a world soul but simply περὶ φύσεως [concerning nature]. The world soul belongs only to the later Pythagoreanism and to Platonism. On p. 4, Schelling himself, after speaking of the presence of a positive and a negative principle in nature, defines the world soul as follows: "These two conflicting forces, taken together, or represented in conflict, lead to the idea of an organizing principle that forms the world into a system. Such a principle the ancients perhaps meant to indicate by the world soul."

The great advance Schelling made in this work was that he grasped nature entirely autonomously and naively abstracted from the relation of intelligence to it, from the possibility or impossibility of taking possession of it as it is in itself. Through this he became more capable of objectivity, and through this he was also probably driven to recall the thought of the world soul as the immanent principle working according to concepts — just as, with a sense of self-satisfaction, he prefixed the second main treatise with the motto:

Sicelides Musae, paullo majora canamus! The whole, which he very modestly called only a hypothesis, falls into two treatises, the first of which concerns inorganic nature, the second organic nature. The first bears the title: On the First Force in Nature. Schelling does not intend to single out a specific force, but rather holds that nature must originally produce out of itself in a positive way. This creating, infinite in itself, is the first thing one must think in its concept. But this first force must be pressed back into itself, in its creating, by a second, negative force. The positive force, without being checked, would only run out into a rectilinear progress; through the negative force, however, it is bent back into itself as a circle. Only the arrested stream of causes and effects, as he once expresses it in the preface, is organization. Of course one must not imagine the negative force as externally added to the positive, but rather think the process of these forces as original.

We see here, then, a repetition and further elaboration of the thought he had already set forth in the Ideas, that nature, matter, is just as much the active unity of the attractive and repulsive force as the mind is the unity of an unlimited and a limiting tendency. The particular determinations he further derived from this were:

⁠1) that light is the first and positive cause of universal polarity;

⁠2) that no principle can excite polarity without itself having an original duplicity;

⁠3) that real opposition is possible only between things of one kind and of common origin.

The latter two propositions are perfectly true, and Schelling later tried to present them in many other forms as well. The expression "real opposition" was meant to deny the merely logical, merely subjective opposition and to designate the opposition that generates itself as activity — e.g., a magnet is in itself the duplicity of opposed poles; for it, real opposition is possible only in relation to another magnet. Toward paper, wood, and so forth it is indeed something different, but it opposes only itself to itself, and does so in such a way that like poles repel each other, unlike poles attract each other. Only this is polarity as exciting-excited, for in the individual magnet it is only in itself

really possible. What one magnet does in the excitation, the other does too. They are wholly homogeneous, and only through their homogeneity is the tension of the poles possible. The truly heterogeneous, polarity, thus so little abolishes homogeneity that, on the contrary, it exists only within it.

The first proposition, however — that light is the first and positive cause of universal polarity — was an overhasty judgment, though a pardonable one for that time. If in old age Schelling fell into an almost anxious reserve, in youth, conversely, he paid homage to every fresh impulse and produced treatise upon treatise, idea upon idea. The decomposition of atmospheric air as well as of water, the relation of oxygen to vegetation and animalization thereby made possible, the connection of their development with heat, of heat with light, the conjectures about the kinship of electricity with these imponderables, and so forth — all these data gave light an extraordinary preeminence. In regard to it Schelling hoped for a union of the Newtonian and Eulerian explanatory hypotheses and a more definite conception of the duplicity existing within light. Yet we see him here still everywhere circumspect, without those adventurous excesses and without

that pride to which he later inclined, when he no longer appropriated the positive discoveries of empirical science with the same care, or, as with Faraday's and Arago's discoveries of magneto-electricity, recalled in 1832 his own intimations as an anticipatory priority.

Entirely in the manner of the "Ideas," he still conducted investigations into the ponderable basis of electrical matter, offered a critique of the ordinary meteorological concepts, and put forward a hypothesis to explain changes in barometric pressure. He wishes to point out that atmospheric chemism cannot be comprehended from the finite relations of experimental chemistry alone — for example, the ever-constant mixture of atmospheric air under the most varied conditions.

The second treatise bears the title: On the Origin of the General Organism. Its basic idea, as is indicated on p. 222 itself, is the further development of the concept of the organic as established by Kant, but more specifically the demonstration that both mechanism and chemism are, for the organism, mere conditions and by no means principle. The organic, in its activity, is its own cause and

effect, and absorbs the chemical, like the mechanical, only as a moment within itself. Life cannot be explained from death; but all so-called forces are in themselves something dead. Force must first be aroused by force; only in conflict, only in play, is force truly force — as Schelling already says in the appendix to the preface, that the last rampart of atomistic mechanics has been surmounted by the thought that a force does not act where it is, but is where it acts. Life, however, generates itself out of its own depth.

Schelling developed, in his unmethodical manner of going straight in medias res, 1) the original opposition of plant and animal, taking it at first chemically in order later to show the inadequacy of this presupposition. He defined vegetation as a deoxidation process, animalization as an oxidation process. The plant produces, in light, a large quantity of oxygen while trying to retain hydrogen within itself; the animal, by contrast, generates oxygen out of itself without being bound to external influence for this. Inhaling is an oxidizing; and while the plant releases carbon only from its non-green parts and in darkness, the animal expels carbonic gas with every exhalation. In it is united what in the plant is kept separate.

⁠2) On the opposed principles of animal life. Here Schelling sets up an antinomy and its resolution.

Thesis: Either the ground of life lies solely and exclusively in animal matter.

Under this heading Schelling seeks above all the necessity that in the organic, matter and form must be thought as immediately identical. The mechanical does not shape itself but is passive with respect to its formation; in the chemical, crystallization shows self-formation, but without excluding the possibility of at once, in dissolution and mixture with another body, taking on yet another shape, one necessary indeed for this mixture. In the organic this is impossible. Without its form it does not correspond to its concept; as matter it can only be thought together with form. From the mere constituent parts, however precisely one may know them by quality and quantity, one cannot assemble an organic individual. Life is therefore beyond mechanism and chemism, and while it cannot be separated from its material existence, it also does not spring from matter as such.

Antithesis: Or the ground of life lies wholly and entirely outside animal matter.

Here, Schelling thinks, one could really only be speaking of Haller's opponents, who deny irritability and put in its place sensibility as a merely passive organ, for pure passivity without reaction exists nowhere. The so-called impression that something from outside is supposed to make on the nerves is possible only through a counter-action of the nerves themselves. Thus he arrives at the:

Synthesis: The ground of life lies in opposed principles, of which the one, positive, is to be sought outside the living individual, and the other, negative, within the individual itself.

That is, universal life as the positive cause is actual only in the living being. The living being is the individual. In it, universal life is a restricted life. Organization is essentially individualization. The individual relates negatively to universal life, because it is the existence of that life only within determinate limits, without whose fixing, however, life in itself as positive cause would have no reality.

⁠3) On the negative conditions of the life-process. Now that the concept of life has been established, Schelling investigates the conditions of its process. He calls them negative conditions, but only means by this that individual life cannot be thought without relation to something else outside itself, which it processes into its own individualization. It is as he later, in old age, called reason the "not-not-to-be-thought," without which nothing at all could be thought. Such negative conditions are the simple chemical elements, whose relation to the organic led Schelling to the following schema, so often repeated by his school:

The living thus subjects the elementary, the inorganic, to itself and produces it out of itself. Schelling endeavors — admittedly in a highly desultory manner that at every moment interrupts itself with digressions — to work out the relation of the negative conditions of life to the formative drive, to irritability, and to sensibility. He holds fast to the true determination that life is only the process of the splitting-apart of unity and the separation of what is united. Both accomplished union and accomplished separation are death. Irritability therefore relates to reproduction just as negatively as sensibility relates to irritability. The heightening of one of these factors of the organism therefore necessarily also alters the activity of the others. Yet although Schelling, with unerring instinct, gropes his way to what is correct, and although in his polemic against inadequate conceptions he is generally quite right, in his own positive expositions he all too often falls back to lower standpoints. In detail he is not yet able to overcome chemism in the domain of the organic. And the unmethodical, wholly accidental character of his presentation is indeed not the least to blame for this. Thus, for example, he quite rightly recognizes that in the organic, matter and form exist in undivided unity. This thought leads him to the determination that all natural products are distinguished from one another either by their interior, their constitution, or by their exterior, their figure, or

by both together. Now, on p. 218, the concept that constitutes the unity between qualitative matter and form is the concept of coherence, for every inner change of matter is just as impossible without a change in its degree of coherence as a change in the shape of matter is impossible without at least a partial change of coherence. From this Schelling deduces a priori that every transition from the fluid to the solid is the beginning of a kind of individuality, and that accordingly the striving of the life-process must go toward individualizing infinitely within unity — as he later explains, for example, that the animal organism at bottom aims to produce as much nitrogen in itself as possible, because nitrogen is the element that chemically distinguishes it from the carbon-based plant. In Schelling, life does indeed keep re-emerging, out of such relative loss in chemical detail, as the higher principle which disturbs mechanism and chemism from producing a perfect equilibrium of forces. Such a balancing, fixed, such a rest of indifference, would be death. Because this awareness never leaves him, he fights not only against mechanical and chemical physiology but even against structural physiology, which wants to make life dependent on the integrity of the shape of the organs, whereas even this shape has only a secondary validity against the force of generative life.

⁠4) Quite without necessity — for the disorder and randomness of the reflection continues — Schelling wishes finally, in a separate section, to deal with the positive cause of life, since this had actually already been under discussion the whole time. Perhaps, to judge from some citations, Baader's Contributions to Elementary Physiology, which had appeared in Hamburg in 1797, still gave him this impetus. Since the fluid and its restless circulation within the frame of the body constitutes its properly own life, the fluid, that is the blood, must possess a peculiar capacity for the reception of the negative life-principle. But the same must hold of the organs generally, since each one draws from the blood, for its specific capacity, the quantity necessary to it. But since positive and negative are reciprocal determinations, the degree of capacity for the positive principle is at the same time the measure for the degree of the negative. Since the nerves are now the organ through which the positive principle acts upon the muscles,

it acts, the voluntary motion must be greatest in those muscles to which the most nerves go, while involuntary motion must be greatest in the organs with the fewest nerves. The organ with the fewest nerves is the heart, for in it the capacity for oxygen is the least. Schelling touches here, on p. 273, on the circumstance that, on account of the delicacy and paucity of the nerves going to the heart, doubt had even begun to be cast on whether they exist in it at all, and himself cites, p. 273, Behrends' dissertation "qua probatur, cor nervis carere," in Ludwig's Scripta Neurologica minora, vol. II, p. 1 ff. Hegel, in the edition of the Philosophy of Nature made by Michelet as the second volume of the Encyclopedia, p. 577, says outright: "One finds no nerves in the heart; rather it is the pure liveliness of irritability at the center, as a muscle that pulses." At this the Berlin Literary Gazette of 1842, p. 202, turned up its nose considerably and opined that "the anatomists (which ones?) found otherwise." The philosophy of nature had every interest in setting the heart against the brain — in whose membranes, too, thousands of blood vessels interweave, without the white and gray gelatinous matter ever ceasing to constitute its true, specifically distinguishing substance — and this is not one of its worst deeds. The thought of capacity leads Schelling to some remarks on galvanism; this leads him again to immerse himself in irritability; this in turn reminds him of sensibility, p. 290, as the negative of irritability. Sensibility, he thinks, can not so much be observed as only inferred. Schelling now wants to assume in the animal a drive to movement whose direction, however, is originally supposed to be indeterminate. If it is determined, this happens through sensibility. The unity of irritability and sensibility is supposed to be instinct, which governs the animal in its most characteristic expressions. From p. 298 on, Schelling endeavors to distinguish formative force [Bildungskraft] from formative drive [Bildungstrieb]. In the former, efficacy is supposed to be absolutely necessary and blind, hence it also belongs to inorganic nature; in the latter, the disturbance of a foreign influence is supposed to occur in an accidental way, which, however, precisely compels the organization to produce a determinate shape. Naturally, this very principle occasioning individualization is now supposed to be the positive cause of life, of which, then, with some solemn Greek expressions, as the ἄμορφον [amorphous] and ἄφθαρτον [imperishable], mention is finally made...

However fleeting, almost improvisatory, Schelling's exposition may have been, and however justly one may heap censure upon it — indeed however much, in its carelessness, in the impatience of its questioning and in the terseness and hypothetical character of its answering, it can even momentarily provoke the scorn it received in abundance at the time — it nevertheless had a core of genuinely speculative nature. One does not fill half a century, one does not fill Europe with one's fame, without having accomplished something great, and it would be the most melancholy thought for me if one wished to hear, out of my exposition, a tendency to belittle Schelling's real merit, or even a pleasure in the censure of him where I may not spare it.

What was so dangerous for Schelling was the impetuosity of his sanguine temperament and the great success he had had from his very first public appearance, at so young an age. The former drove him to precipitancy, the latter spoiled him. To take the applause of the world by storm, rather than slowly to earn the assent of the best minds, became his habitual disposition. He had been called from Leipzig to Jena as a professor. Having recovered from a severe illness, he made his appearance there, arousing the liveliest enthusiasm. Steffens, in his memoirs, has described this inaugural lecture for us. His academic position spurred him to work out a book for his lectures, since this was still the prevailing custom at the time. The listeners not only took notes but usually also had to buy, once again in printed form, what they had written down. Thus Schelling published in 1799 his:

First Outline of a System of the Philosophy of Nature. For the Use of His Lectures. Jena and Leipzig. Octavo.

In the short preface he asks, most modestly, that judgment be withheld until the matter is closed. He wishes to venture, for dynamic physics, what Le Sage did for mechanical physics.

In content, this book does not go beyond what the World-Soul offers. One can speak only of a further working-out in particulars. In form it indulges in the most varied change. Now problems are raised and analytic solutions attempted; now propositions are set down axiomatically, from which whole series of corollaries are derived; now, without conceptual headings, Roman numerals appear, now letters, to mark distinctions; now corollaries follow, and so on. However, the appeal to the authority of facts and the precision of citation recede, and abstract deduction comes to the fore — that is, the work, however much beauty it contains, however stimulating its effect was, already marked the transition to the later manner of construction. It is also remarkable that not this work but only the Ideas and the World-Soul saw second editions. Schelling himself seems to have felt the disorder of its form and therefore to have prefixed a systematically appearing outline of the whole, in which, to be sure, some things are again somewhat shifted. According to his earlier promise, Schelling ought to have given a scientific physiology. He did not give it; yet because it should have followed as a matter of course, it remained constantly in his mind and formed the involuntary center of his research. According to the outline's pretension, the whole is supposed to have three sections, which, however, should not be taken too seriously.

Namely: 1) proof is to be furnished that nature in its most original products is organic; 2) the conditions of an inorganic nature are to be deduced; and 3) the reciprocal determination of organic and inorganic nature is to be given.

Schelling proceeded from the view that in transcendental philosophy, and hence also in the philosophy of nature, the unconditioned, being itself, cannot be grasped in the thing, in the particular, as it is in itself; it cannot fully present itself therein, because it lies beyond all particularization, and the particular by no means provides merely an imprint of it. Science must construct cognition, i.e., grasp it as activity. Consequently, the philosophy of nature too can present nature — as the epitome of all being (as he had earlier, in the Ideas, defined it as the epitome of the totality of experience) — only in its activity, which is why to philosophize about nature actually means to create nature. Not as object, as product, in which the producing activity has been extinguished, but only as producing, only as posited into becoming, does one truly cognize nature.

Hence Schelling repeated once more the thought that the infinite, ideal, productive activity must be checked by an opposed activity lying within nature itself, the retarding activity, if it is to come to finite products. Such products are indeed only apparent products, because the productive activity, striving toward infinity, is co-posited in the individual

product, and consequently generates in it the tendency toward infinite development, and each product can in turn disintegrate into further products. Analysis, as Schelling puts it, cannot therefore come to rest at anything that is a product, but must ascend to that which no longer has any substrate but is rather the cause of all substrate, the absolutely productive. To foresee this is the first postulate of all philosophy of nature. As that which is mechanically and chemically insurmountable, this must be simple action. In such action must lie the principle of determinateness that we call quality, and to that extent it is the basis of a dynamic atomism. If, with such definitions, one might believe oneself standing on the threshold of Herbart's system and his real beings, the matter changes at once in the further course, in that Schelling asserts that the simple actions can be distinguished from one another by nothing but their original figure — in which he agrees with the atomists, yet differs from them in that he can think these basic shapes not as existing but only as self-cancelling, as interlocking. Cohesion is an example of the cancellation of absolute isolation. The

most original product of this interlocking is therefore the most original fluid, that which is just as incomposable as it is absolutely decomposable.

Schelling here had in mind the caloric substance still assumed at that time. Since by means of this principle only an absolute formlessness would arise, an opposed activity must again be assumed here too, through which limitation, and with it the product, becomes possible. In matter, accordingly, the absolutely indecomposable — which is at the same time the absolutely composable — must hold the balance against the general dissolution of all form. This principle is really that of individuality. In organic nature the genus always triumphs over the individual; in inorganic nature, however, the individual exists only as mass, as something fixed. Nature as such does not lose itself in either extreme but is a middle term between both. It proceeds from shape to shape, and can produce the determinateness of the product only through inhibition of formation, which presupposes the splitting of the formative drive into opposed directions, i.e., sexual difference, for in this lies the possibility that the product develops itself further out of itself and the producers likewise

## Continuing Development

continue to exist as further-developing, whereas in the chemical process the differing objects vanish into the neutral product as a finished one. Only through this is the permanence of different developmental stages in nature secured. The whole of nature thus forms a dynamic sequence of stages, in which each product represents a determinate deviation from the original ideal.

Since nature, in this way, contains within itself the opposition between the formation of a general organism and the formation of the individual, the equilibrium between the two must appear as a constant struggle. Reciprocal action must hold the inorganic and organic worlds together, as it were, by a compulsion. Everything organic must be excitable, i.e., receptive; the inorganic must solicit it into expression. Here Schelling had in mind the correct thought that the concept of the inorganic and the organic is relative, insofar as everything soliciting — hence even something organic itself, insofar as it solicits — can take on the meaning of the inorganic; hence even the cosmic relations of the central body and the peripheral bodies could be taken in this way, just as the sun, through the oxygen it emits, excites the earth.

He rejected both the mechanical, that is Newtonian, and the metaphysical system of attraction, that is the Kantian, and put in their place a third, derived from both, that of physical attraction. He assumed, namely, that gravity was the tendency toward general intussusception. Actual intussusception, however, could only be produced by an action added to gravity, since gravity itself is merely the tendency toward penetrating unity, not its accomplished realization, as this occurs in chemism.

For the closer determination of excitability, Schelling went into the particular moments of the dynamic sequence of stages. The organic has, in the nerves, the means of receptivity; sensibility is thus its innermost element, and only through its degree is the degree of its irritability determined. Both unite in the formative drive, as the general force of production, which: 1) accomplishes, in nutrition, the self-preservation of the organic individual; 2) in secretion, separates out from each organ, according to its peculiarity, its specific product; 3) in growth, shows the result of nutrition and secretion in the finished product; 4) in instinct — whose most singular form is the artistic drive and whose highest manifestation is genius — gives the individual its constant direction toward the limitation originally necessary to it; 5) the artistic drive is therefore connected with metamorphosis, and this in turn 6) with the generative drive, in which the individual not merely reproduces itself, as in nutrition, and not merely produces objects that coincide immediately with their use, as in the artistic drive, but in which it produces another individual essentially identical with it. Of these expositions, the one on the artistic drive, pp. 197-215, is one of the most excellent that we owe to Schelling. Particularly surprising was his insight into the connection of the artistic drive with metamorphosis in insects, which Steffens later pursued down to the smallest particulars.

Since Schelling asserted a dynamic sequence of stages, a continuous evolution, he had to demonstrate the analogous moments to the organic within inorganic nature. As such he posited magnetism, the electrical process, and the chemical process. One will accept this parallel to the extent of thinking, by means of it, the production of nature as progressing from lower to higher activity, and to that extent one may equate magnetic polarity with the excitability of sensibility, and so on. But Schelling had to go, beyond the duplicity of nature's double form as inorganic and organic, to the concept of general nature, which hovers as it were above both; and here he now posited — adding, in the outline itself, a question mark — the cause of magnetism, electricity, and light, as the parallel potencies of the organic and the inorganic. This was, indisputably, already an empty schematism. The distinction between electricity and the electrical process, between the cause of magnetism and magnetism itself, was just as merely intended as the identity between light, the chemical process, and the formative drive. Given what Schelling had said in the World-Soul about the affinity of sensibility with oxygen, and what he says here about the connection of light with oxygen, one should rather have expected the parallelism of light with sensibility. On this occasion Schelling began to use a mode of expression which was soon carried to the most extreme abuse in his school. For in constructing the natural activities he postulated, he had emphasized only the quantitative determination. He spoke of the equilibrium of forces, of the retarding of one by another, of dynamic atomism, of the distinction of all natural products as a merely gradual inhibition of the original production striving toward the boundless. He called the distinctions of becoming, the points of inhibition of nature's activity, potencies. Magnetism was the same potency as sensibility, only within inorganic nature, i.e., that nature in which the outside-and-beside-one-another exists only as a tendency toward the within-one-another, etc. Now Schelling also expressed this difference of degree by saying that sensibility was indeed already present in itself in magneticity, only it had not yet appeared or come forth. This form, which followed of itself from the thought of dynamics, was deformed by shallow minds into explaining everything by everything else: that one thing was completely the same as another, only it had not yet appeared; it still resided in the concealment of a lower stage. Just as little as one gets anywhere by dissolving all qualities into merely quantitative differences, just as little does one get anywhere by making all qualities into original, self-subsistent simple actions that are distinguished by their figure and yet, as interlocking, are supposed again to give up their shape. Rather, in the qualitative — as that which is indeed immediately determined within itself — the quantitative change must be noted, but only as something in itself merely external, as the regression into quality.

Schelling actually forgot that already in the opposition of the absolutely fluid and the individual, in the hypothesis of the tendency toward intussusception, and in the demonstration of intussusception in the chemical process — already supplied by Kant and acknowledged by him — he possessed all the presuppositions for deducing the reciprocal determination of the inorganic and organic. Otherwise it would be hard to explain why he did not draw the conclusion with a theory of disease, pp. 254-79, derived from the dynamic sequence of stages in nature, in which he treated the sthenic or asthenic behavior of the particular potencies of the organism entirely in the Brownian manner (in the World-Soul he had criticized Brown's physiology but praised his nosology), but rather once again gave a general theory of the chemical process, in which really only the thought receives further elaboration that the chemical and electrical process are subordinate to magnetism. As Schelling here touches once more on intussusception as the equalization of the heterogeneous into the homogeneous, he sets up the concept of distribution as that of the production of heterogeneity, and finally derives from this the framework of the universe, by making the gravitational force operating in magnetism, together with the duplicity of an expansive and a retarding force, the condition of the evolution of the universe. This hypothesis, critically linking itself to Kant, he calls a "real construction of matter."

Schelling endeavored, on p. 310, to state the core of his view quite definitely:

"There is thus One cause that has brought the most original opposition into nature; this cause we can designate by the (unknown) cause of original magnetism."

"Through this cause an effect extending infinitely throughout the universe by distribution is conditioned; through this, a state of indifference for each individual product; through this state of indifference, the possibility of a difference within the homogeneous; thereby the possibility of a dynamic process (to which the life-process also belongs), and in particular of the chemical process, as a dissolution of the heterogeneous within the heterogeneous."

## The Dynamic Process

"This actuality of the dynamic process for each individual product is conditioned by communication, which takes place infinitely throughout the universe, and whose general medium, for the part of the universe known to us, is light."

Introduction to His Outline of a System of the Philosophy of Nature. Or: On the Concept of Speculative Physics and the Inner Organization of a System of This Science. By F. W. J. Schelling. Jena and Leipzig, 1799.

This little work contains Schelling's engagement with empirical physics, in a very intelligible language and altogether solid bearing. It is full of interesting turns on its theme, even if in substance, in the determination of particular concepts, it does not take a single step forward. Schelling declares that we are able to cognize nature a priori only because it is a priori, i.e., because in it all parts are determined from the whole. He reminds empirical science that it by no means always insinuates only pure facts, but all too often insinuates as facts its own reasoning, its inferences, its fictions. He recalls Bacon's thought that we still lack the experiment by which the central phenomenon of nature would be represented. He impresses upon us that all knowledge can only be self-produced, and that the experiment is to be taken as a priori only in this sense, because it compels nature to answer a determinate question. The opposition of the a priori and the a posteriori does not adhere originally to things themselves but exists only with regard to our knowledge and its manner.

Otherwise, the essential content of this pleasant little work is the further working-out of the thought that all producing of nature must be taken only as a productive product. Every finite product that has come to be exists only as a monument of the general process of production, which immediately advances beyond the particular and at once draws it back again into the continuity of the whole, out of which it emerged, reducing it to a mere moment.

At the close, p. 83, Schelling said: "And with this the author hands over these first principles of a speculative physics to the thinking minds of the age, asking them to make common cause in this science — which opens up by no means small prospects — and to make up, through their own powers, for whatever he himself lacks in strength, knowledge, or external circumstances."

But while he had thus, by his own account, arrived at the propylaea, the gates of the temple of nature closed before him. In the short span of three years he had, rushing headlong in his victorious course, accomplished his deed. Such daring, such boldness — as Schelling himself likes to say of himself — was needed to make, with decided instinct, a leap forward in science. And it is probably this very haste that best explains the remarkable phenomenon that from this point on Schelling increasingly turned his back on natural science. He did still write treatises, but they contain almost only modifications of the views hitherto developed, and often not even the happiest ones. Since 1805 even these utterances ceased, and he turned his attention, it seems, to quite different fields altogether. It is possible that the reception of his views by Steffens, Windischmann, Schubert, Oken, Schelver, Wagner, and others, who worked them into the particulars of their material, made his own further participation seem superfluous to him. One can, by way of comparison, call Schelling's procedure the opposite of Goethe's, who pursued an interest once conceived with the most unremitting tenacity, who collected every small contribution, remained in the closest possible contact with all contemporary researchers as soon as there was even the slightest resonance, and advanced, with the same warm participation, in every sphere of natural science — from nephology and mineralogy to osteology and physiology — right up to his death.

What strikes us above all, looking back at Schelling's works in the philosophy of nature, is the effort to develop the thought of thoroughgoing duality. The difficulty for him was the lack of the dialectical form which he absolutely needed for the truth of his concept. We have seen how he posits the negation of the negation, but how he does this in such a way that he lets one force be added to another, in order to postulate yet another force in which the antagonism of the dualistic potencies was supposed to become differentiated. A force spreading out infinitely is always supposed to be pressed back into itself by an opposed inhibiting force and forced into the finite product as the result of the reciprocal action of the positive and negative force. Schelling always insisted on deriving the development of the lower potency not from itself but from a higher one as the positive cause — almost as Aristotle, above the ὕλη [matter] and the concept of the ὅθεν ἡ ἀρχὴ τῆς κινήσεως [that whence the origin of motion], presupposes the concept of the οὗ ἕνεκα [that for the sake of which] as the αἰτία ἀντικειμένη ταύτῃ [the cause opposed to it] and as τέλος γενέσεως πάσης [the end of all becoming]. That the concept of force is a very inadequate form of reflection he himself knew very well, and he conducted the most apt polemic against its abuse, particularly regarding the vital force then in vogue. This insight, however, could not prevent him from himself remaining stuck everywhere in this same category, and from declaring the dynamic process to be the highest thing in nature. From this it is also understandable how he at the same time always wanted to derive the activity of nature from a specifically determinate force. He asked continually: through what cause did the original duplicity come into the original identity? Since, however, the duplicity did not first come into the identity at all, but rather, as original, is immediately identical with it, the question can either not be answered at all or only awkwardly. Not at all, if one recognizes its ineptitude; or awkwardly, if one either presupposes an unknown force through which this is supposed to have happened, as Schelling himself did a few times, particularly in the World-Soul, or guesses at a special force which is supposed to have brought about the separation of the One, the opposition of the identical against itself.

In the Ideas, Schelling still posited attraction and repulsion as the dualistic forces of matter identical within gravity; in the World-Soul he inclined to acknowledging light as the principle of original polarization; in the Outline of the System, magnetism was finally declared, at the close, to be the absolute agent of all primordial natural formation, after, at the beginning, the opposition of the formless fluid and the individual — or rather, still further back, the distinction of the simple actions as original qualities distinguished from one another only by their figure — had held this position. Schelling remained in debt for a resolution of these contradictions.

Having now traversed the circle of nature from its inorganic forms up to the concept of disease, Schelling returned to the consideration of spirit, but could no longer remain at the Fichtean standpoint and take nature merely as a negative. Rather, he conceived the thought that philosophy can complete itself only in the unity of the development of the real and the ideal. The beginning of philosophy can indeed be made with one or the other of the opposed poles of the whole, with the object or the subject. But the development of the object, the real, must lead to the concept of the subject as such, for it is object only under the presupposition of the subject. Conversely, the development of the subject must lead to the concept of the object as such, for it is subject only under the presupposition of the object. There must consequently be a point at which the objective, the real, attains the subjective and ideal that is posited in itself within it — that is, an organization that is itself identical with the concept of intelligence. This is the human being. Conversely, however, the subjective, the ideal, must also attain, at some point, the objective, the real, posited in itself within it, a point at which it coincides completely with it. This is the work of art.

If, then, transcendental idealism, in presenting the subject, has to lay out the sequence of stages of its self-potentiation, the history of self-consciousness, the necessary series of acts through which the ideal increasingly realizes itself, it must at the same time be shown which forms of the real correspond, as the same, to these acts. The whole system is one only through this conformity. Nature is spirit, only as not yet knowing itself, and spirit is nature, only as having come to consciousness of itself. This was the task which Schelling, on the threshold of the nineteenth century, attempted to solve in his:

System of Transcendental Idealism. Tübingen, 1800.

This book has its precedent, on the one hand, in Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre. Schelling himself states in the preface, as a secondary aim of his work, that he wished to provide a generally readable and comprehensible presentation of transcendental idealism. On the other hand, we must recall Schelling's writing On the Ego, and in particular the exposition which Schelling had given, in the Philosophical Journal, in the general survey of the most recent philosophical literature, of the true essence of criticism. Here he had, with the most genuine enthusiasm — that is, one springing from the matter itself — broken through the barriers that were supposed forever to separate sensibility and understanding, and to present the laws

of nature as something different from nature itself. From this preliminary work one must explain the easier flow of language and the greater formal perfection prevailing in the architectonic, which distinguishes this work above Schelling's other writings. One can indeed say that Schelling still finds himself here on the Fichtean, indeed the Kantian, standpoint; but one must at the same time admit that this standpoint has, under his hand, become quite a different one, which set all problems in a new light. Schelling divided the whole into six main sections:

⁠1) On the Principle of Transcendental Idealism.

⁠2) General Deduction of Transcendental Idealism.

⁠3) System of Theoretical Philosophy According to the Principles of Transcendental Idealism.

⁠4) System of Practical Philosophy According to the Principles of Transcendental Idealism.

⁠5) Main Point of Teleology According to the Principles of Transcendental Idealism.

⁠6) Deduction of a General Organ of Philosophy, or: Main Point of the Philosophy of Art According to the Principles of Transcendental Idealism.

## The Original Form

It is not superfluous to call to mind again the original form of a significant work, since later one tends to remember only its general content, and in such reproduction foreign elements are not seldom mixed in. But for the history of a philosopher, the side of form is no less important than that of content, and even though Schelling, with his synthetic manner of principles, deductions, and corollaries, still retained a form that lagged behind the movement of the content, his procedure was in itself thoroughly dialectical. In particular he succeeded, in the development of consciousness, in separating the act of immediate self-production from the act of reproducing what has been posited, while showing, relative to the following stage conditioned by it, immediacy once again. There is a difference between the concept the ego forms of its own activity and this activity itself, which must necessarily precede its own concept — just as the ego itself is originally, in itself, the identity of subject and object and the separation of these opposites, but all this not yet for itself, only for the intellectual intuition of the transcendental philosopher. Schelling took as his starting point entirely the concept of synthetic apperception a priori, that the judgment "I am" is higher than the judgment "I think." He shows that in the concept of the ego the identity of matter and form is posited, which is necessary for knowledge; he shows that the ego is infinite in its finitude; that it exists only as self-producing, and hence is no thing, no object; that in its eternal becoming it is object only through self-knowing; that intellectual intuition is the organ of philosophy, and that knowledge in general constitutes the object of transcendental philosophy. P. 13: "Now all knowledge is reduced to certain original convictions, or original prejudices; these individual convictions transcendental philosophy must trace back to one original conviction; this one, from which all others are derived, is expressed in the first principle of this philosophy, and the task of finding such a principle means nothing other than finding the absolutely certain, through which all other certainty is mediated." One sees easily that this is entirely the standpoint of subjective idealism. The division is also made accordingly:

⁠1) The ego, as the first in knowledge, finds itself determined by something else: it behaves theoretically.

⁠2) The ego posits something else through itself, generates the objective, determines reality: it behaves practically.

⁠3) Both are an opposition resolved in the highest task of philosophy: "how can representations be thought as at once conforming to objects, and objects as conforming to representations?" This is to occur in teleology.

⁠4) But if, in the purposiveness of the actual world, conscious and unconscious activity meet without consciousness (p. 18), this happens, in aesthetic production, with consciousness. P. 19: "The objective world is only the original, still unconscious poetry of spirit; the general organon of philosophy — and the keystone of the whole vault — is the philosophy of art."

In the theoretical self-intuition of the ego, Schelling distinguishes the various acts as successive epochs, namely:

⁠1) from original sensation to productive intuition;

⁠2) from productive intuition to reflection;

⁠3) from reflection to the absolute act of will.

In the first epoch, the ego posits itself, a, in original limitation by the non-ego. Insofar as this limitation is grasped in its particular positive determinateness, it is posited, b, as sensation. But the ego can only find itself determined by generating its own passivity. The opposition of the objective, which it senses within itself according to its specific quality, is possible only through its own opposition against itself. Grasping itself in this, it is, c, productive intuition.

This becomes reflection through the distinction of concrete intuiting by means of outer and inner sense, since with this the difference of the various categories enters in, which lie in the one category of relation and no less form the basis of the others.

Reflection is finally sublated in the schematism of the imagination, for schema is — unlike image and symbol — a rule for producing an empirical object. With the schema, the ego accordingly places itself at the turning point from theoretical to practical behavior, because with it the possibility, actuality, and necessity of a reality yet to be produced is posited. Schelling calls the peculiar activity of this epoch absolute abstraction, because it separates everything that was united in the original synthesis of inner and outer sense, and because through this separation the opposition of concept-less intuition and intuition-less concept arises. What, on the stage between productive intuition and reflection, inner sense posits in time as accidental and outer sense posits in space as substantial — in the simultaneity of cause and effect as life, as organism, as unity of activity and passivity — consciousness now brings to consciousness of itself through judgment. With the form of judgment, that is, with the self-conscious separation of the original synthesis, the various forms of judgment also therefore appear, and the process of reflection tends toward sublating intuiting, i.e., finding-oneself-determined, so as to make the subject entirely a subject for itself — which absolute abstraction, namely from the given object, accomplishes, and thereby makes possible the absolute act of will, by virtue of which the subject posits itself as object. Strictly speaking, Schelling, following Kant's and Fichte's lead, deduced the categories of the understanding in the second and third epochs of theoretical self-intuition without essentially altering the determinations given by Kant. He therefore also assumed that permanence corresponds to substance, succession to causality, simultaneity to reciprocal action — or space, time, and motion, or outer sense, inner sense, etc. — in a parallel construction. There occurs here, on p. 292, an important passage on logic: "If the categories are determinate modes of intuition of intelligence, then, when stripped of intuition, mere pure determinateness must remain. It is this which is designated by the logical concept. If, then, a philosopher stands originally only at the standpoint of reflection or analysis, he will also be able to deduce the categories merely as merely formal concepts, hence merely from logic. But apart from the fact that the various functions of judgment in logic itself still require derivation, and that, far from transcendental philosophy being an abstract of logic, logic must rather be abstracted from it, it is mere illusion to believe that the categories, once separated from the schematism of intuition, still remain as real concepts, since, stripped of intuition, they are merely logical concepts, but combined with intuition they are no longer mere concepts but actual forms of intuition. The inadequacy of such a derivation will betray itself through other deficiencies as well, for instance that it cannot uncover the mechanism of the categories, particular as well as general, although this is visible enough. Thus it is indeed a striking peculiarity of the so-called dynamic categories that each of them has its correlate, whereas this is not the case with the so-called mathematical ones — a peculiarity, however, very easily explained once one knows that in the dynamic categories inner and outer sense are still undivided, whereas of the mathematical ones, one belongs only to inner, the other only to outer sense. Likewise, that everywhere, and in every class, there are three categories, of which the first two are opposed to each other while the third is the synthesis of both, proves that the general mechanism of the categories rests on a higher opposition no longer visible from the standpoint of reflection, for which there must therefore be a higher one, lying further back. Since, further, this opposition runs through all categories,

and there is one type underlying all of them, there is doubtless also only one category, and since we could derive from the original mechanism of intuition only the single category of relation, it is to be expected that this is that one original category — which is indeed confirmed on closer view." Compare with this statement another, on p. 36, in which logic — as that in which the mere form of other scientific propositions is made into content — is derived from that abstraction in which form and content mutually condition each other, that is, from the ego.

That nature is identical with spirit, insofar as both are Idea, is certain. In this, then, Schelling was completely right. Only in the working-out of the concept of identity did he go astray, and from this aberration we can learn how difficult the step must have been for philosophy to give up the subjective conception of nature. Schelling wanted to "explain" self-consciousness, he wanted to derive its "history." The objective was, however, according to his presupposition, supposed to represent within itself the same moments of development. This identity was not supposed to be that of reason in general, the logical identity, but a parallelism of construction, an equality of becoming through the analogy of the particular moments running through both. This assumption rested on the further hypothesis that the Absolute itself exists wholly, in itself, in both the subjective and the objective, only with the relative difference of an inverted relation of the factors of the real and the ideal. Thus nothing remained but to transpose the acts of self-potentiating self-consciousness from the form of ideality into that of reality. The ego is in itself dualistic and identical with itself in its duality; it is the unity of its infinity and finitude, etc. Objectively, this activity appears as the construction of matter out of its forces; the ego's being-limited by the object appears as the direction of the extension of matter, or as the deduction of its dimensions, from which arises the concept of magnetism as the linear potency, of electricity as the potency acting in breadth, in the plane, of the chemical process as the force acting in depth, etc. Magnetism is therefore supposed to be, in nature, the same thing that the original limitedness of self-consciousness through intuiting is; electricity the same as sensation; chemism the same as productive intuition, and so on. In his eager manner and his luxuriant delight in combination,

Schelling here already again altered a number of the physical determinations — for example, he posited galvanism as the true principle of the chemical process, because it shows itself wherever a process passes over into a product. He also took pleasure, p. 176, in being the first to give an explanation of why matter must be intuited as extended in three dimensions. For Schelling this step was absolutely necessary. Since he had already factually gone beyond merely subjective idealism, yet still held fast to its principle as the highest, nothing remained for him but to deduce realism from idealism in such a way that it appeared, in its various metamorphoses, identical with those of idealism and of equal dignity. It was one and the same construction that was accomplished in the double form of the ideal and the real. One will therefore not be surprised that Schelling, who first developed this thought, immersed himself in it to the point of paradoxical consistency, for example p. 188: "Another remark, which shows us still more closely the identity of the dynamic and the transcendental, and lets us glimpse the far-reaching connection extending from the present point, is the following. That second act is the act of sensation. What, then, is it that becomes an object to us through sensation? Nothing other than quality. But all quality is only electricity, a proposition proven in the philosophy of nature. But electricity is precisely that by which, in nature, that second moment in the construction is designated. One can therefore say that what sensation is in intelligence, electricity is in nature." Just as naturally, Schelling's closest followers exploited this parallelism to excess, but it is scarcely excusable that Steffens, long after that ferment had subsided, still reveled in it as late as 1824, in the first part of his Anthropology, and, quite in earnest, drew a parallel, for example, between the solidity of metals and the solidity of temperament. Schelling really took extraordinary pains to carry through the identity of intelligence with nature. He labored hard to reconcile the succession of acts by which self-consciousness attempts to represent the original synthesis, with the evolution of the universe, in which, as the reciprocal action of all substances, individual organisms struggle with the general organism and with an inorganic nature. In the boldness of his assertions he was not at all timid here — as, for example, when he speaks of light, as indeed the boundary

of our sphere of intuition, but not of the universe, and now adds, p. 259: "it is not mere hypothesis that beyond the world of light, with a light unknown to us, a world radiates which does not fall within the sphere of our intuition." But he immediately passes back into positive investigation: "If, then, intelligence intuits the evolution of the universe, so far as it falls within its intuition, in an organization, it will intuit this organization as identical with itself. For it is intelligence itself which, through all the labyrinths and windings of organic nature, seeks to reflect itself back as productive. But in none of the subordinate organizations does the world of intelligence present itself completely. Only when it attains the most perfect organization, in which its whole world contracts, will it recognize this organization as identical with itself. For this reason, intelligence will appear not merely as organic in general but as standing at the summit of organization. It can regard the remaining organizations only as intermediate links, through which the most perfect gradually works itself free from the fetters of matter, or through which it becomes completely an object to itself. It will therefore also not concede to the remaining

organizations the same dignity with itself."

In theoretical self-intuition, the ego seeks to become conscious of itself in acting. It finds itself determined, through intuiting, as by an unconscious producing. The limitation of its self-determination indeed comes to its consciousness as something posited; the being-limited becomes sensation, sensation becomes productive intuition, productive intuition becomes self-feeling, and, since the ego driven back into itself is inner sense, self-feeling becomes reflection, reflection becomes abstraction: yet the producing never coincides immediately with self-consciousness, but the latter only grasps what has already come to be without its contribution, and each of its acts is always only the condition of the following one. This constant failure of the striving to take hold of itself at the very moment of production is necessary if the world is to become manifest to self-consciousness as object. In it lies the stimulus to go, again and again, beyond whatever stage has been reached. Now, as the subject, through abstraction from intuiting, finally grasps itself in its pure self-determination, its standpoint changes into the opposite of the one hitherto held. It proceeds from itself with consciousness. It is no longer a process that occurs ideally, as it were outside of time, but it produces empirically in time. It idealizes, in that it first posits an object within itself as thought, and it realizes, in that it posits its intuiting as something intuited. It does not take the world up into itself but brings it forth out of itself, or behaves practically. Consequently it is no longer merely a simple but rather a double duality, which at every one of its points is at once ideal and real, for the practical thought is meant to become deed, that is, something intuited, and in the deed that has become objective its thought is posited. But as the subject, in free self-determination, makes itself into object, the moment of unconsciousness of intuiting reappears after all. For self-determination as such is indeed unconditioned and accompanied by consciousness, but that it determines itself empirically in just this way and not otherwise is objectively mediated. In this conditionedness, self-determination is accordingly unfree. And yet what appears as its passivity is its own deed, because it makes into a condition for itself, out of the objective, only that which corresponds to it. And not only things have this significance, but just as much other intelligences, in which the subject recognizes itself (pp. 341-43), because they, like itself, can abstract from all limitations, for through their acting they realize themselves and generate objectivity. That willing constitutes such a unity of the subjective and objective is, according to Schelling, conceivable only through a pre-established harmony, which is that of absolute intelligence, and which, as such, the willing and acting subject itself — since it co-produces it — never gets into its own power.

With regard now to the individuality of self-determination, Schelling goes so far as to make the degree of freedom itself dependent on its own immediate necessity — a doctrine that would destroy all morality, because morality must draw the inference from ought to can. I can be free because I ought to be, but not: I am free because I am. This aberration is connected with the deification of genius prevalent at that time. P. 350: "Only through this restriction of my free activity to a determinate object do I become conscious of myself, and hence also free; consequently, before I am free, i.e., conscious of freedom, my freedom must already be restricted, and certain actions must already, before I am free, have been made impossible for me. To this belongs, for example, that

what is called talent or genius — and not only genius for the arts or sciences, but also genius for actions. It is harsh, but is for that reason no less true, that, just as countless human beings are originally unfit for the highest functions of spirit, so countless others will never be capable of acting with that freedom and elevation of spirit, even above the law, which can belong only to a chosen few. Precisely this — that free actions are already, originally, made impossible by an unknown necessity — is what compels human beings now to accuse or extol the favor or disfavor of nature, now the decree of fate."

But with regard to the identity of the subjective and objective, which acting presupposes, Schelling indeed says that it must be thought as a deed of intelligence itself, just as he also postulates the existence of other intelligences as necessary, so that the individual subject may come to consciousness of its willing, to consciousness of the freedom produced as object; yet he explicitly wants no theism assumed to explain that identity. ## The Moral World-Order

expect the acknowledgment of a moral world-order to follow. But Schelling rejects this, because such an order would exist only so long as every individual willed it. In fact his theory of history is a higher one than Fichte's. Within his systematic development he describes the absolute synthesis of history, in an important passage, p. 433, as follows: "If, then, that higher element is nothing other than the ground of identity between the absolutely subjective and the objective, the conscious and the unconscious, which separate precisely for the sake of appearing in free action, then that higher element itself can be neither subject nor object, nor both at once, but only the absolute identity in which there is no duplicity at all, and which, precisely because the condition of all consciousness is duplicity, can never attain to consciousness. This eternally unconscious thing — which, like the eternal sun in the realm of spirits, conceals itself by its own unclouded light, and although it never becomes object, yet impresses its identity on all free actions — is at the same time the same for all intelligences, the invisible root of which all intelligences are only the potencies, and the eternal mediator of the self-determining subjective in us and of the objective or intuiting, at once the ground of lawfulness within freedom and of freedom within the lawfulness of the objective. Now it is easy to see that for that absolutely identical thing, which already separates in the first act of consciousness and through this separation brings forth the whole system of finitude, there can be no predicates whatsoever, since it is the absolutely simple — not even predicates taken from the intelligent or from the free — so that it can also never be an object of knowledge, but only of eternal anticipation in acting, that is, of faith. If, then, that Absolute is the true ground of the harmony between the objective and subjective in the free action not only of the individual but of the whole species, we shall find the trace of this eternal and unchangeable identity most readily in the lawfulness which, like the fabric woven by an unknown hand, runs through the free play of arbitrary will in history." This concept must have been so important to Schelling at the time that in the preface, p. XIV, he expressly warns against imagining that absolutely identical thing as a substantial or personal being, because this "would be no better than viewing it as a mere abstraction" — an opinion

which could be imputed to idealism only through the grossest misunderstanding."

The particular elaboration Schelling gave to the concept of action pursues the dualism of the unconscious and the conscious from the individual up to world history. In the individual, the objective element that determines him to act, without itself being consciousness, is the causality of nature, the natural drive as the compulsion of his organization, which can be taken generally as pain. Opposed to this is the subjective, which must cast off every given limitation and, as pure intelligence, make the moral law, as it holds for all intelligences, the necessity of its willing. But the actuality of the will is conditioned by the fact that the ego is able to have before it two equally possible actions and to decide for the one or the other (or for neither). This act, through which alone the subject becomes certain that it can be compelled neither by the causality of nature nor by the moral law, is arbitrary choice [Willkür]. The ought-to-be of the law must, incidentally, be distinguished from the ought-to-be of the ideal, which changes at every moment of action, and which

is nothing other than the schema of how something ought to be.

It is thus evident that the individual can violate the moral law, and the further task is therefore to bring forth, among intelligences, a mechanism which, with the inexorable necessity of nature, annihilates such a violation as soon as it occurs. This second nature is the legal constitution. The initial establishment of such constitutions will always be a half-unconscious work of violence, undertaken merely to found some order amid the conflicts of the various wills. The higher organization of the constitution must, like every organization of nature, be grounded on three mutually independent powers. But it is very difficult for the perfect state to be realized, because the executive power, in order to secure its own independence, will retard the development of the legislative power, and so on. We need not go into further detail here, since Schelling only repeats, in brief strokes, the main points of the Kantian theory of the state, which demands a federation of states and a congress of nations as a guarantee for the existence of the legal constitution.

The individual human being, like the individual state, can realize freedom only in a progress extending into infinity. This progress, thought of as that of the species, is history, for a mere aggregate of actions is history as little as a course of mere natural processes without progressiveness. In history there is united what is otherwise separated: the self-conscious intent of the individual's arbitrary will and the unconscious lawfulness of the whole. The problem of history really consists in showing how the willing of the individual is turned, through the connection of the totality, into a quite different meaning than the one he himself had put into it. Every action is objectively a product of everything that has happened up to that point; the whole past, as its condition, is posited in it as result. Subjectively, however, looking forward, it is a product of free will. That, given such a position of the objective and subjective, no more contradictions arise than actually do arise is explicable only from an absolute synthesis in which all contradictions are dissolved and sublated in advance. P. 430: "That from the completely lawless play of freedom, which every free being carries on for itself as though no other existed beside it (which must always be assumed as the rule), something reasonable and harmonious nevertheless emerges in the end — which I am compelled to anticipate in every action — is incomprehensible unless the objective in all action is something communal, through which all human actions are guided toward one harmonious goal, so that, however they may conduct themselves, and however unrestrainedly they exercise their arbitrary will, they must, without and even against their will, through a necessity hidden from them, by which it is determined in advance that precisely through the lawlessness of their acting — and the more lawless it is, the more certainly — they bring about a development of the drama which they themselves could not have intended, arrive at where they did not want to go." If, therefore, Schelling thinks, p. 435, only the objective moment in history, unconscious purposiveness, is held fast, fatalism results; if the subjective moment, the self-conscious arbitrary will of decision, is fixed, atheism results; and only the acknowledgment of the unity of both determinations is religion, and grasps history as the system of providence.

One has, therefore, as the measure of the progressiveness of history, only reflection on the ever-greater development of the legal constitution, since the state of morality, as well as that of the arts and sciences, cannot serve as a criterion for the species, and is compatible with the greatest public injustice — although, p. 422, the absolute realization of the true legal constitution will remain only an eternal article of faith for acting and working human beings. Schelling holds fast to the necessity, for the concept of history, that its process must be progress, a succession of actions, for without this the concept of history could no longer be maintained at all. Freedom would then no longer appear but simply be, that is, the opposition of the objective and subjective would not merely arise and pass away in constant becoming, but would be sublated in and for itself. But as long as history exists, so long is the identity of the conscious and the unconscious also produced. Man, however, because the identity splits apart into opposition, is not merely a passive player but a co-poet of the world-drama. Schelling expresses this thought very beautifully, p. 436: "If we think of history as a drama in which everyone who takes part plays his role quite freely and according to his own discretion, a rational development of this confused play can be conceived only on the assumption that there is one spirit who composes in all of them, and that the poet — of whom the individual actors are merely fragments, disjecti membra poetae — has already, in advance, so harmonized the objective outcome of the whole with the free play of all the individuals that in the end something truly rational must result. Now if the poet existed independently of his drama, we would be merely the actors carrying out what he had composed. But if he does not exist independently of us, but rather reveals and unveils himself only successively through the play of our own freedom, so that without this freedom he himself would not exist either, then we are co-poets of the whole and self-inventors of the particular role we play."

P. 438: "History as a whole is a continuous, gradually unveiling revelation of the Absolute. Hence one can never point to the single place in history where the trace of providence, or God himself, is as it were visible. For God never is, if being is that which presents itself in the objective world; if he were, we would not be — but he reveals himself continually."

This concept of history as a never fully completed revelation of the Absolute already shows us the seed of the theory of the theogonic process which Schelling adopted in later years. Against the fatalism of abstract theism he was thereby just as right as against the apotheosis of chance in atheism. The objective connectedness of events seems to exclude God just as much as the freedom of the individual subject does. But God is precisely neither of these one-sidednesses, but their absolute synthesis, their eternal identity, the eternal ground of harmony between the two. Schelling now wanted to derive the articulation of history from these concepts, and held that the opposition developing within it was that of fate and providence, between which nature stands in the middle as a transition from the one to the other. Let us first present Schelling's own words, which became so important. P. 439:

"The first period is that in which the ruling power appears only as fate, i.e., as a wholly blind power that coldly and unconsciously destroys even the greatest and most glorious things; to this period of history, which we may call the tragic one, belongs the downfall of the splendor and wonders of the ancient world, the fall of those great empires of which scarcely any memory has remained and whose greatness we can infer only from their ruins, the downfall of the noblest humanity that has ever flourished, and whose return to earth is only an eternal wish."

Let us pause here for a moment. Schelling determines the character of this period immediately in negative terms; he speaks of destroying, of downfall, but does not say how the most glorious things could come into existence in the first place. When "the downfall of the splendor and wonders of the ancient world" is spoken of, one must ask which peoples, which cultures Schelling counts to this — for the Chinese and the Indians, who surely belonged to the ancient world as much as to its splendor and wonders, have not perished. Of a fall of great empires "of which scarcely any memory has remained" we likewise know nothing; and it is really only in the case of the Egyptians that we infer the greatness of such empires from their ruins, for the fleeting conquests of nomadic peoples have never left monuments behind; of the ruins of the American peoples, of the remains of Palenque and Tehuetlapallan, which cover the ground for miles and indeed set us the task of inferring from them the fall of great empires, Schelling could not yet have thought at that time. So too the "downfall of the noblest humanity" is incomprehensible in this first period, for as the humanity of the beginning it cannot

already contain the superlative. What we actually know of this humanity of the ancient Medes, Persians, Indians, Egyptians, Phoenicians, and so on, is not such that we could make the "return" of their history a wish of ours — that is, Schelling has here lost himself in vagueness; instead of determinate concepts he has substituted for himself the fiction of a perfected beginning, whose vaunted glory contrasts oddly enough with a downfall through a "wholly blind power." We shall see further on how this fiction degenerated into a corruption of the true conception of history.

Now Schelling sets nature over against fate. In the second period, what appeared in the first as blind night is supposed to appear at least transformed into an open law of nature, which "gradually brings about at least a mechanical lawfulness in history." This period seems to begin with the expansion of the great Roman Republic, from which point on the most unrestrained arbitrary will, expressing itself in a general lust for conquest and subjugation, first bound the peoples generally to one another, and — bringing into mutual contact what up to then had been preserved separately among individual peoples in the way of customs and laws, arts and sciences — was unconsciously and even against its own will compelled to serve a plan of nature which, in its complete development, must bring about the general federation of nations and the universal state. All events falling into this period are therefore also to be regarded as mere natural outcomes, just as even the fall of the Roman Empire has neither a tragic nor a moral side, but was necessary according to natural laws and really only a tribute paid to nature."

This whole contrasting of nature against fate is mistaken. Nature is immanent to fate, as blind necessity, as its organ; but the opposite of fate is, precisely because the concept of nature coincides with its own concept, the predestination of destinies through an absolute, self-conscious subject — which also appears factually to be the case, so that the natural fatalism of the ethnic peoples stands opposed to the spiritual fatalism of predestination among the monotheistic peoples. But it was not at all the Roman Republic that, through the arbitrary will of conquest and subjugation, first brought about a reciprocal contact of cultures; rather, the first conquerors in the world-historical sense were the Persians, and the first mediation of the cultures of Occident and Orient was undertaken by Alexander, who was also great in having a consciousness of this fusion and in bequeathing it to the Diadochi. How events that are ultimately supposed to result in the universal state can be called mere "natural outcomes" is incomprehensible; but had Schelling understood by this word the compulsion of causal connection, then such a mechanism would belong to all of history, including the first and last periods. It is therefore also wholly groundless to regard the fall of the Roman Empire as having occurred merely according to natural laws, for in a general sense the downfall of any state and people can be regarded as "a tribute paid to nature," insofar as a people is an individual and can, in the course of its history, reach a culminating point, beyond which it disintegrates. But the fall of Rome was in fact tragic, and indeed through morality. One need only read Tacitus to become convinced how, in the loss of public political life, in the disappearance of old custom and piety, in the hatred of pleasure-seeking vice against virtue, precisely morality was the one form still left to the spirit in which to fashion for itself a bearing that was not at all conditioned by the state of public affairs. But precisely such a bearing brought the danger of death, and this was tragic. When an Arria handed Paetus the dagger for a painless death, this is a tragedy, opposed by the comedy of immorality, which, lacking the courage to kill itself, like a Nero, like a Messalina, begged for death from its entourage at the approach of its pursuers. Arria killed herself because she bore the burden of virtue, not, like a Lucretia, who spurred revenge with her death after having been violated. Indeed, one can rightly say that Stoicism was the tragic philosophy of this age, in which it was not nature but the most perfect arbitrary will — that is, the very opposite of nature — that ruled.

"The third period of history will be that in which what appeared, in the earlier periods, as fate and as nature will develop and become manifest as providence, so that even what seemed to be a mere work of fate or of nature was already the beginning of a providence revealing itself in an imperfect way. When this period will begin we cannot say. But whenever this period will be, then God too will be." Providence, however, is not the unity of fate and nature, but of fate and predestination, of necessity and freedom, of the wrath of doom and the grace of redemption, of must and will, of becoming and knowing, or whatever other expressions one wishes to posit for the oppositions sublated in its concept. But if this unity is not to remain an empty word, if the solemn sublimity of an announcement of God's being is not to provoke boredom, a closer determination is needed, which Schelling does not give. But while he only looks prophetically forward to this highest period, it has long since entered the world with Christianity. Humanity now knows what the purpose of God is, and at the same time knows this purpose as its own. This purpose is the rational freedom of spirit in all its possible forms. Hence what was a mystery at earlier stages is manifest. Christ knew his own history just as much as an objectively mediated necessity as he did as a subjective work of his own freedom. No incomprehensible fate to him defeated him, but just as little did he take his history to be the mere reproduction of a divine predetermination; rather he stated that he laid down his life of

himself, just as the Father had given him to have it in himself. From this moment on, the period of providence has become a power over the fatalistic forms of the conception of history.

Schelling could now have passed immediately from the system of practical philosophy to the development of aesthetic production, had he already treated the concept of teleology at the close of theoretical self-intuition, in the transition to the practical. But although he had already expounded the concept of organization there, he nevertheless contented himself, for this purpose, with the category of reciprocal action. And likewise, in practical philosophy, though he did speak a few times, almost involuntarily, of purposiveness, he otherwise contented himself with the expressions lawfulness and harmony. He conceived the matter thus: in history, the identity of the objective and subjective exists in an objective, i.e., unconscious, way. Schelling said very beautifully that every human life, in its history, is a proof of the existence of God — but this was supposed to hold only in itself, for if the period of providence should come, if God should be, then, according to Schelling, history would sublate itself. He therefore asked: how does self-consciousness arrive at intuiting itself as existing objectively?

## The Concept of Purposiveness

Answer: through the concept of purposiveness, for in it lies the identity of ideality and reality. Since Kant, in his Critique of Judgment, had already developed in detail the distinction between natural and aesthetic purposiveness, this was perhaps the reason why Schelling here contented himself with a brief deduction. Nature is purposive without having been purposively produced; consequently that activity must be the highest in which the identity of the conscious with the unconscious is posited in the ego together with consciousness of this identity. In this activity, therefore, not only the product but also the production itself is purposive, without thereby excluding the unconscious and unintentional. Here Schelling made a decisive advance over Fichte, who related the concept of the aesthetic only indirectly to art, and directly still to the opposition of pleasure and displeasure.

For artistic production proceeds from the contradiction of the drive that determines, in an original and immediate way, the essence of the subject as genius. Whereas Schelling above, as we saw, demanded genius itself as a virtue, here on p. 458 he wants it expressly restricted to artistic production as its condition. The unconscious in the artist is the productive power within him, which appears to his own reflection as something higher, surprising and delighting him with its gifts. He has no power over it but must submit to it (pati deum, inspiration, etc.), and therefore feels himself moved by the favor of being able to produce the beautiful. The painful feeling of contradiction that drives the artist to work resolves, through the creation of the product, into blissful serenity.

This depth of unintentional infinity that reveals itself in the art-work is genuine poetry, the inventive element. But to poetry, art—that is, practice, the diligence of technical training—must be added as the equally necessary virtuosity of the formal side, and Schelling rightly declares on p. 462 that a lack of invention can more readily be dispensed with in an artwork than a lack of all mechanical training.

Up to this point the deduction is excellent; Schelling united what Kant had said in the Critique of Judgment and Schiller in his treatises on art, to which the Schlegels' writings attached themselves, and it rests only on a misunderstanding when Schiller, in a letter to Goethe of 27 March 1801,

(see also Schwab's Life of Schiller, p. 678 f.) expresses himself as though transcendental idealism had not sufficiently recognized the dependence of artistic production on the unconscious in the artist. When Schiller says: "The unconscious united with the deliberate constitutes the poetic artist," this is in fact precisely Schelling's opinion. But Schelling went astray in another way. He found in art the reality of harmony, which for history and nature is supposed to be a mere presupposition. Art is, p. 460: "the only and eternal revelation there is, and the miracle which, even had it existed only once, would have to convince us of the absolute reality of that highest [principle]."

This deification of art drove him, on p. 473, to see the faculty of poetry, the imagination, and the ideal world of art as the highest potency of productive intuition. Works of art are, through beauty, for everyone, whereas those of science are only for the few. Where art already is, science only wishes to arrive. Artistic genius is beyond doubt, but in science much can also be gained through diligence. So, on p. 475, art is to be the organ and document of philosophy, while nature is to have for the artist the same meaning as for the philosopher: to be only the imperfect reflection of a world that exists not outside him but within him. "What we call nature is a poem locked in a secret, wondrous script. Yet could the riddle be unveiled, we would recognize in it the odyssey of the spirit, which, wondrously deceived, seeking itself, flees itself; for through the world of the senses there shines forth, as sense through words, only as through a half-transparent mist, the land of fantasy toward which we strive."

It must strike us as highly remarkable, now that we can also survey Schelling's later works, that at the close of his whole treatise he fell upon mythology as that production of spirit meant to form the middle link between poetry and science. For poetry, he says, brought forth the sciences, and into it, as the universal ocean, they must again flow back. He assures us, p. 477, that he had already had ready for several years a treatise on mythology which would further develop this thought and would soon appear. "But how a new mythology—which cannot be the invention

of an individual poet, but of a new generation representing as it were a single poet—can itself arise, is a problem whose solution can be expected only from the future destinies of the world and the further course of history."

Yet determining this "how" would have been precisely the task. Schelling could arrive at this paradox only by ignoring religion, whose relation to art on one hand and to science on the other he leaves entirely out of account. He could reach it only by overlooking the absoluteness of form, which is just as possible for science as for art—though this absoluteness is not to be measured by the popularity of immediately appealing intelligibility, as Schelling does, and hence he ascribes to science only a perfection that is forever to be striven for. Finally, Schelling arrived at that paradox only through a lack of criticism, by which he wanted poetry, as an organ for science, to accomplish what is denied to science into infinity and can only hover before it as a goal. Science, however, can realize its concept only if it strives to carry through the peculiarity of its form in the strictest manner. Only dialectically, not poetically, can it produce works of art in its own domain.

Had we not resolved to confine ourselves here entirely to Schelling and to undertake a faithful review of his development without looking right or left, we would have to go into the Romantic school here, which nourished, with all its strength, the confused notion of a new mythology, namely in Fr. Schlegel's Dialogue on Poetry and in the Athenaeum. One must recall here how the Klopstock school tried to set Nordic mythology, and the Lavater-Zinzendorf school a biblical mythology, against the ancient as vehicles of representation; recall how many Protestants at that time found their cult too sober and sought to enliven it by inventing a new mythology. Roman Catholicism at least had a manifold symbolism and an Olympus of saints, and now people imagined they could produce something similar for Protestantism. Since this was impossible, and even the polytheism of the modern cult of genius is far too meager a surrogate for the cult of spirit in spirit, naturally, as history has taught us, not

Just as the appearance of the System of Transcendental Idealism fell precisely at the midpoint between the old and new centuries, it also marks for Schelling a dividing line in the form of his production. The striving not only for systematic comprehensiveness but also for its execution down to particulars began from this point to disappear, and aphoristic outpouring came to the fore. The propaganda of the system began. Schelling published the:

Journal for Speculative Physics, 2 vols., 1800–1801, Jena and Leipzig, 8vo.

It is interesting to note that he restricted speculation to physics, because speculation in general, in his sense, was represented by other journals of the time, chiefly by the Schlegels' Athenaeum, whereas politics and religion still lay outside his concerns at that time and were then more or less adequately handled in the manner in which the Kantian school

pursued them. From the outset Schelling set his journal in opposition to the general Jena Literary Gazette edited by Schütz and Hufeland, which had already given a bad review of his philosophy of nature and, in order to make up for it, let a second bad one follow, as if two bad ones could make up for one good one. A critique of Schelling's writings by Steffens had been rejected [by that gazette], and Schelling now had it printed in his own journal with an:

Appendix, concerning two reviews of natural philosophy and the Jena General Literary Gazette. Vol. I, No. 1, pp. 50–99.

In it he attacked, in the most vehement and open manner, the mischief into which journal editorships often sink. Fichte had introduced the boldness of such attacks. Schelling indeed did not have Fichte's armored quality, who in his polemics created the finest rhetorical works of art and advanced upon his opponent in closed phalanxes; Schelling rather poured out his anger, his indignation, seizing now this, now that—but apart from this, he was captivating in the striking power of his language, in the abundance of correct insight. That appendix could still today be read with the greatest

profit by such editorships as cannot bring themselves to let a paper die at the right moment once the sickness of death has crept into it—a tact which Schelling always possessed. He fought against the quietist maxim of editorships not to get involved in disputes, for dispute is necessary and nothing other than the manifestation of progress and of interest in it. That it should be conducted with dignity is self-evident. He fought against the tendency toward a bad kind of impartiality, which is infinitely different from true impartiality, i.e., from the certainty of truth determined by objective grounds. He wanted nothing to do with the protection of mediocrity, which eked out its wretched existence through the vehicle of journalistic trumpeting; nothing to do with superstitious belief in authority, especially that of editors themselves. He scourged the attempted murder that editorships commit against significant but, to them, unwelcome productions by ignoring them; scourged the sinking of literary cliques into anecdote-hunting and gossip-mongering; showed the ridiculousness of the favorite excuse for sins of omission—lack of space—and, in scientific matters, wanted never again to hear the lazy phrase that one must leave judgment to time. P. 76: "What indeed is time without us and you, who move within it? To be sure, time will decide, but not time itself—rather human beings within it, who do not think, or will think, as you think." Above all, however, and with the greatest justice, he attacked the mischief of anonymity. How the public would be astonished to learn from what common, venal, ignorant, nameless scribblers reviews often originate! The editorship had assured him that one of the bad reviews of him came from a man who had created masterworks in mathematics! What a pity not to know him! P. 58: "This colossal mathematician may, like King Pyrrhus's elephant, suddenly stretch his trunk out at me from behind the curtain of anonymity, without expecting that I will be frightened by it any more than Fabricius was."

This mischief of anonymity is fortunately, with the sad exception of the political sphere, on the decline among us. Both the Berlin and the Halle Jahrbücher have made the duty of naming the author a rule, whereas at that time it was the exception.

... Schelling was inwardly permeated to the core with the proud, victorious presentiment of seeing bound to his works a revolution in science, a palingenesis of it. This presentiment blared forth from his polemical trumpet-blasts. The new century surged through his veins, and the Kantians, who had gradually turned their master's wisdom into folly, seemed to him worthy only of being trodden into the dust, where they belonged. Having overcome his disgust at having descended into this "primordial broth of vulgarity," his feeling, on returning from such an abyss of flatness and baseness, was not unlike that with which one reads, in Dante, the verses:

"And then we came forth, to see again the stars."

The scientific work with which Schelling made his appearance in his journal was a:

General Deduction of the Dynamic Process, or of the Categories of Physics. I.1, pp. 100–136; I.2, pp. 3–87.

This essay contains a repetition of the exposition that Schelling had given in the System of Transcendental Idealism concerning the connection between the various dimensions of space and the various moments of the physical process. He repeated that by "construction" he understood not merely a logical but a synthetic representation—one that had real forces in view, as he had earlier put it, one that represented the activity of nature. That Schelling wanted nothing to do with the logical formalism that separates truth from certainty and content from form was already quite good; but his belief that construction as such carried probative force was an error, for construction rested indeed on that form of disjunctive judgment which suspends the opposition of the particular in the identity of the universal—an identity that is neither positive nor negative but rather positive and negative, or, as Schelling expressed it, their indifference. But the determinations themselves, from which one proceeded and which turned out to be indispensable conditions for explaining anything, remained mere presuppositions, justified not in and of themselves but only by their success. Schelling argued quite correctly that a force, unless checked by another force, would spread formlessly in all directions, and that one must therefore set against the continuity of an original positive force the limitation of a negative one—though one always sublated in its negating, always led back from its finitizing into infinity again; but the concepts of force, being, continuity, passivity and activity, position and negation, finitude and infinity, process and progress, etc.—all these concepts he developed himself only in passing, only as transcendental schematism, and yet everything depended on them. This lack of critique of the dialectical categories became noticeable in his school in precisely the opposite way that skill in handling them was occasionally noticeable in the Hegelian school. Schellingians believed they had proved something when they spoke of "constructing" and schematized according to thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, and Hegelians believed they had proved something when they spoke of the "manifesting" of the concept and likewise schematized according to the moments of the universal, particular, and individual. In formalism, no school has grounds to reproach the other; each has driven it equally far, and even of the genuine method a caricature is possible.

The sole task of natural philosophy, Schelling held, was to construct matter. He did this here, as before, from a dynamic dualism, except that with the negative force he emphasized still more its character as one acting at a distance, because actio in distans is the most obvious refutation of merely mechanical causality in the finite form of pressure and impact—as he had said in the preface to the World-Soul: a force is present where it acts. According to him, matter now raises the longitudinal direction to the power of magnetism, the planar direction to that of electricity, and the direction into depth to that of chemical process; and just as the dimensions constitute determinations only of the one space, so too all these forces are only different manifestations, passing into one another, of a single force. In this thought, correct enough in itself, Schelling let himself be led astray by the then-fashionable triangulation of knowledge urged by Baader, representing magnetism—still fairly innocuous—as a vertical line, electricity as an angle, chemism as a triangle (Book 2, p. 79); a piece of playfulness which Hegel too still had to contend with, as, e.g., one of the divisions in his History of Philosophy shows. In terms of actual substantive

determination, we find no progress in Schelling. His certainty has intensified and expresses itself against the empiricists with prophetic pride; nor is it lacking in far-shining, surprising glimpses, but these are rare and quickly leap away from the subject matter; one hopes an investigation will begin—but it is already abandoned again. Thus, in Book 2, p. 53, occasioned by tourmaline, he touches on thermomagnetism, whose definite discovery is Seebeck's glory, but he passes quickly over it with remarks such as: "The force of a magnet seems already to be compatible only with ordinary degrees of temperature." So too he insists, p. 38, that light within the material is the ideal element. It is supposed to be matter without really being matter: "According to this view it becomes clear how, at one and the same point in space, the rays of countless stars can seem to be present without excluding one another, indeed how, under given conditions, even the impenetrable is penetrable to light; for the construing of construing is excluded by nothing. At the close of the essay Schelling cast retrospective glances over his whole system. It must strike us that he entirely forgets art, of which he had said that in it, united into a single flame, burns what otherwise eternally flees itself in life and action, and that it reveals to everyone what science, by its nature, can grant only to a few. Here, because only the physicist gets behind the deception of consciousness, he would like to call out to all humanity: "Come here to physics and know the truth!" — "When people

For in the essay on the dynamic process, §7, he had given the following determination: "If speculation ascends above that absolute union of opposed activities which we think in the concept of nature, we no longer have any object other than the absolute-identical, which for intuition is designated by mere zero, or the absolute lack of reality. But how, from this infinity, which for appearance is zero, something finite—i.e. real—could have arisen, is comprehensible only by letting that zero separate into its factors (1, -1), and by assuming this separation to be an infinite one." Knowing this is important, because we shall presently see how quickly Schelling sought to free himself from this negative determination of absolute identity. Incidentally, in his polemic against the polemic directed at him, he became quite aware that the chief cause of the skewed grasp of natural philosophy lay in this: that in the production of the intuition of the object, the one intuiting forgot to abstract from his own intuiting, and hence could not attain the pure object.

Against Eschenmayer's misgivings as to whether experience had yet advanced far enough to make a system of nature possible, he quite rightly objected that only natural philosophy itself

could judge how far experience had progressed, and that he himself would be eager to know how long this difficulty was still to last and by what signs one would in future recognize that the time for this science had come. Of a science that subsists through itself, one can never say it is not yet time to invent it, for it is always time to invent such a science. Equally correct is Schelling's defense against Eschenmayer's merely quantitative determination of qualitative differences—e.g., regarding the specific differences of matter as mere differences of degree in density. But we must not forget that Schelling himself, in the second book of his Ideas, in the theory of the chemical process set out there, had given too great a preponderance to the category of quantity and, with undeniable regard to Eschenmayer's dissertation, had set up as a principle of chemistry (Ideas, 2nd ed., p. 454): "All quality of bodies rests on the quantitative, gradual relation of their fundamental forces." Now Schelling sees quite clearly the inadequacy of such a theory and reproaches Eschenmayer, p. 132, "for having held it possible that in the relation of the original forces to one another there is a merely quantitative difference, determinable through the relative more or less of the one or the other force," and says instead, p. 133: "I attempt to construct the qualitative determinations of matter from a different relation of the two forces to one another than the one by which specific gravity is determined." Nevertheless, Schelling soon afterward asserted, in the Outline of his Total System, II.2, p. 42, that gravity contains the ground of its own being, that it can be called absolute identity, that it is mediately the ground of all reality and not only of the being but also of the persistence of all things, etc.

To this he himself still appealed, even in Berlin, as an authentic document. In the preliminary remarks he complains that he is driven to this publication earlier than he himself wished, by the present state of science; that the philosopher, as Fichte too experienced, is given so little time to develop; that the intrusive populace, having no notion of speculation, wants everywhere, with the blindest self-confidence, immediately either to agree or

Of the empiricists, however, he prophesies: "By far the greater part of them will for a while still try to kick against the pricks; then gradually take up the expressions, even perhaps the constructions, of natural philosophy as probable explanations, or bring them to light in the form of experiments; finally even perpetuate the whole of dynamic physics, as a not unreasonable hypothesis, in their textbooks." A prophecy which has indeed come true.

Schelling's purpose with this presentation was, then, to develop the two sides of his system, realism and idealism, in their connection—not one-sidedly on its own, as had happened until then—and thereby to put an end to the misunderstandings that were beginning to accumulate. With this undertaking he made a decisive, highly important advance, though by its very nature it could achieve the effect otherwise necessary and specifically intended only through its completeness, through its self-contained roundedness. Instead, however, Schelling in the exposition got only as far as the general concept of the organic, and excused its fragmentary character with a brief postscript in which, most unphilosophically, he even appealed to the feelings of his readers. These memorable words run, p. 126, as follows:

"Here, however, we must for this time break off our presentation. Time and circumstances did not allow us to continue it at once in a following issue; still less did the richness of the subject and the necessity of treating individual points more fully than we ourselves wished, allow us to give it in a yet more concentrated form. This does of course produce the disadvantage that those who wish to get to know and judge this system do not receive all the documents complete at once; but for those whose feeling does not tell them that they have already grasped the sense of the whole from this fragment (which is not impossible), this will only be a reason not to rush their judgment; those, however, whose feeling does not tell them this—and I believe this will be the case with the greater number of my readers—by now running ahead in their thoughts of my presentation, will only follow me all the more prepared when I lead them from one stage of organic nature to another, up to its highest manifestations of activity, from there to the construction of absolute indifference, or to that potency in which absolute identity is posited under wholly equal potencies; when I then invite them, from this point, to the construction of the ideal series, and likewise again, through the three potencies positive with respect to the ideal factor, as now through the three negative with respect to it, lead them to the construction of the absolute center of gravity, into which, as the two highest expressions of indifference, truth and beauty fall."

If we consider this presentation first with regard to its form, we should, given what Schelling said to Eschenmayer about the method he wished to make known as infallible, expect a new form of construction. Instead we find the well-known synthetic form with its definitions, propositions, and proofs, but above all with its scholia and remarks—the chief betrayers of the contingency left unpurged within it. Schelling has visibly—and he admits it himself—wished to set beside Spinoza's Ethics, as a counterpart, the most perfect idealism opposed to the most perfect realism, as he called Spinozism. If only it had been done with the same circumspection and precision, and not with this brusque, slipshod ease! The paragraphs run breathlessly one after another and cite each other just as diligently as happens in Spinoza, yet without thereby having any closer connection. The exposition, however, ought to have had a marked resting-point at §50. Schelling insists on continuity and hurries on. That is: up to this point runs the presentation of what one might call the metaphysics and logic of the Schellingian system, the exposition of the concept of absolute identity in the abstract; from there on, however, the presentation of nature begins.

One can well see that he is imitating Spinoza in his systematics. Substance is everything, and outside it is nothing, just as identity is everything. Substance is self-identical, as is identity. In its concept it is at once its own existence, in its existing its own concept, and needs nothing further in order to be conceived. Substance appears to finite understanding in the form of extension or of thought, a distinction that does not exist in it itself, because it is always and everywhere both the one and the other. The particular is for substance only apparently independent, for everything is determined through substance and is at bottom substance itself, exactly as with identity. As for the second part of the presentation, the philosophy of nature, little that is gratifying can be said of it. We have already had to note that the empirical exactitude that Schelling had cultivated in the Ideas and in the World-Soul now began increasingly to recede. In its place, the uncritical audacity of combination, intuitive fancifulness, grew ever greater. If we try to extract, from the vague reflections in which Schelling's dynamics is here presented anew, what can be regarded as progress, this comes down chiefly, p. 92, to Kielmeyer's discovery that the activity within the galvanic chain is equal to the difference in the degrees of affinity of the two bodies for oxygen, and to Steffens's idea of setting up a series of cohesion in inorganic bodies corresponding to magnetic polarity, and of regarding carbon and nitrogen as the separated representation of the poles of the magnet. Herr Steffens's "happy thoughts" are praised everywhere, pp. 51, 65, 66, 123 ff. To be sure, it is also said once, p. 52: "The complete construction of the series of cohesion, however, is made possible only by the following laws." Certainly the speed with which Schelling then assimilated everything newly emerging in science was in itself an excellent quality. If only he had not been quite so hasty and sanguine in exploiting what was new; if only he had not already, right at the beginning, always been at the end; if only he had not, to such a degree, not merely raised expectations but made promises and then failed to fulfill them. Since we have, after all, been obliged by Schelling himself to review the records of the process, we may note in passing, as curiosities, such works announced by Schelling but never delivered. In §106 he declares himself against Herschel's experiments to prove the warming power of the sun's rays and to produce a heat spectrum, and says, p. 79, that it

was enough, for present purposes, to assert that even Herschel's more recent experiments in no way endanger the doctrine of the unity of light, and rather prove something quite different from its being composite. "The detailed proof of this assertion will be given by a separate essay in the following issue." Incidentally, Schelling now declared himself, having earlier (World-Soul, p. 33) held a different opinion, with the greatest decisiveness for Goethe's theory of optics. P. 60: "Let us thank the gods that they have freed us, through the same genius to whom we owe so much else, from the Newtonian spectrum—yes, indeed, spectrum—of a composite light." The addendum to §106, p. 80, contains this theory most clearly: "Light is by its essence colorless, or rather, light is not at all determined in its essence by color. For light is only obscured; it is not even light that is colored, but only the image or the object."

From matter Schelling proceeded to gravity, from this to specific gravity, and thence to the difference of cohesion, so that the original formation of the material universe, §95, was now to be thought of as a process of cohesion. On p. 65 Schelling promises to give the proof in detail in future, and says: "In the same way as the earth, the planetary system too shows, toward the one side, the south pole, a relative decrease of cohesion, and toward the opposite, the northern side, a relative increase of cohesion. The whole of physical astronomy must proceed from the principle here established. The cause of the eccentricity of the orbits, the relations of density to masses and eccentricities, the cause and law of the inclination of the heavenly bodies, of axial rotation, of all meteorological and general changes of nature—e.g., of the deviation of the magnetic needle—the laws according to which the moons are formed and attached to the main planets, etc.—all these subjects find their common elucidation in the idea of representing the formation of the planetary system as a general process of cohesion." It was concluded that the series of heavenly bodies corresponds to that of earthly ones; concluded that all contact of bodies must produce a relative decrease and increase of cohesion, because every body can in itself be regarded as a magnet, in which, §73, the relatively greater coherence is on the negative side, the relatively lesser on the [positive] side; concluded that in the material universe, which in itself is to be regarded as one mass, as the total magnet, the empirical magnet constitutes the point of indifference; and concluded that, since according to the note to §76 iron is declared to be the empirical magnet, all bodies are potentially contained in iron. Light, meanwhile, was indeed declared, in contrast to gravity, to be absolute identity itself, but this, §96, "insofar as it is light," was supposed to be not force or ground of reality but only activity, and according to §94, p. 64, the dominion of light, and hence also of the dynamic process, was to be restricted to the realm of cohesion—a proposition, it is said, "which will soon show itself to be important."

The theory of the chemical process is repeated, with minor modifications, in its earlier form, and then, at §141, one jumps to the secundum existens—as the organic is called on p. 115—with the following borrowed proposition: "The opposed poles under which gravity is likewise posited as the form of existence of absolute identity are, with respect to the whole, plant and animal, and with respect to the individual, the two sexes." To this Schelling adds the naive remark, verging indeed on charlatanism: "The reader will take it in good part if, in order to reach our goal by the shortest path, we, as before, set up propositions—whose proof anyone can find for himself through his own reflection—as mere borrowed propositions, or even without proof. It goes without saying, incidentally, that a further discussion of the above assertion will still occur in what follows." One naturally asks who compels the philosopher to take the shortest path, if that excludes proof, since it is proving, not the assertoric positing of determinations in general, that is the philosophical business. If one wishes to rely on what anyone can find through his own reflection, then we fall, together with this unknown Mr. Anyone, into complete indeterminacy. It is tasteless to entrench every assertion, even the most trivial, behind a fortress of reasons, and only philosophical formalists indulge in the excess of wanting to prove afresh even the simplest, most intelligible, already-proven things. But it is something quite different to smuggle in the most important propositions, without proof, as self-evident. And this is what Schelling does. He calls the proposition here at issue a "borrowed proposition" [Lehnsatz]! From where, O Hermes, is it borrowed? From what science? For what I borrow must belong elsewhere and is used by me only temporarily. A fine borrowed proposition, without which not a step further can be taken, and which can be placed in no other science than precisely the one for which it is borrowed! Some shame at this superficiality does seem to have crept over Schelling after all. In the addendum to the borrowed proposition (or is a Lehnsatz actually supposed to be a liege-lord [Lehnsherr] who, in his addenda, calls up the levy of his vassals?), it becomes clear to him that the total product is the organism, because §136 asserted that, immediately through the positedness of the dynamic totality, the addition of light to the product is posited; and now, §142, it runs: "Absolute identity is the cause of the organism, immediately in that it posits A² and A=B (in plain German, light and gravity) as forms of its being, i.e., immediately in that it posits itself as existing under the form of both.—The proof is everything said so far."

Since every body (p. 104) is a monad, but each, though in itself a totality, is itself only a moment of the total totality; and since it further strives constantly to represent this identity, everything in nature is metamorphosis. In the organism, activity is directed not toward the preservation of substance as substance, but of substance as the form of existence of absolute identity. Since the organism is the total product of nature, it is concluded, §148, that inorganic nature as such does not exist. It is in itself organic. What we call inorganic is only that which has not been able to become organic, the residuum of organic metamorphosis. The inorganic is such only for the organism; potentially it is itself organized. It is the earth itself, become animal and plant, that we behold in [its] organizations. P. 121: "In general, however, one should consider that we do not at all concede the ordinary and hitherto prevailing conceptions of matter, since it must be seen from what has gone before that we assert an inner identity of all things and a potential presence of everything in everything, and thus regard even so-called dead matter only as a sleeping world of animals and plants, which, animated by the being of absolute identity, might arise again in some period whose course no experience has yet lived through." The earth is now designated as an organic magnet, with the animal as its positive and the plant as its negative pole; the former is also called iron, the latter water—namely, organic water—and the highest potentiation of the positive pole is found in the brain, particularly of the human being, that of the negative pole in the flower.

We have hereby reached the end of the philosophy of nature, but cannot fail to point out one more remarkable poetic turn, because it is too characteristic of Schelling. In §149 he sets up several propositions, in themselves quite correct, that the heavenly bodies are organs of the universal intuiting principle; that every heavenly body is in itself a totality, that it is an individual; that in its organisms it likewise individualizes itself into intuiting organs; and that the central body of a system posits all the individual bodies of that system within itself, identically, as their relatively universal intuiting organ. But now, on p. 124, he lets himself be led astray into the following proposition: "Since every heavenly body is a determinate individual, the character of each will also incline either more toward the masculine or toward the feminine, or, like the earth, which runs its course between Venus and Mars, unite both within itself in a more perfect indifference." On p. 66 the difference of cohesion had been declared the sole principle of the difference among the planetary series—now sexuality mixes in, and the earth is turned into a cosmic hermaphrodite. How aptly, indeed, did the ancients name our neighboring planets in advance for this hypothesis! But what becomes of poor Mercury? Is he male or female? But, joking aside, we have witnessed, in weighty books—e.g. by Wirth—the sad consequences of this physiological mythologizing of the planetary system. To be sure, Wirth wrote his book in prison, an excuse which, fortunately for Schelling, he cannot so fortunately invoke for his own paradoxes.

If you would be so good as to compare, say, p. 83, you will already find there Schelling's point of indifference as the highest thing in the inquiry; the objective and the subjective, I say here in plain words, must in the end, for the speculator, coincide once more. But the whole tendency of the treatise, this genuine child of my humor at the time, aims at bringing pure philosophy back to aesthetics, and everything in everything, finally, to feeling — called by Schelling 'intuition' — etc." These words alone prove sufficiently how little Bardili had understood Schelling, whose concern was in truth for something greater than to make man, as Bardili expressed it of himself, into "an animated piece of the universe."

Given what Schelling had already said in the note to the preliminary remark to the presentation of his system, and Hegel in the appendix to his Difference of the Fichtean and Schellingian Systems, about Reinhold, and the latter also about Bardili, nothing really new occurs in this dialogue from a speculative point of view. It does, however, furnish proof of how, here, on the field of philosophical polemic, Friedrich Schlegel's divine coarseness reached its maximum. Whatever of the kind has occurred among us since is only a shadow of the ironically contemptuous manner in which Reinhold and Bardili are here treated as blockheads, weaklings, and fools.

The second essay that Schelling contributed, 1803, vol. II, issue 3, pp. 35-50, was:

On Dante Considered Philosophically. The impulse for this came, as we have already seen above, from the Romantic school of the time, given that Schelling himself wrote poetry in terza rima. As a poet he also called himself, under the pseudonym, Bonaventura. Schelling showed much taste and grandeur of mind in his conception of Dante. He was quite right to maintain that the Divine Comedy belongs to no fixed genre, that it is epic, lyric, and dramatic, historical, didactic, and allegorical all at once, that it is a work which, in its construction as Inferno, Purgatorio, and Paradiso, must be taken not merely from a limited ecclesiastical standpoint but from a universal, archetypal one. He was, finally, right to mock, in the journal's notices section, Bouterwek's view of this sublime poem as a poetic travelogue. But his whim of fusing philosophy and poetry led him, alongside these excellent expositions, into the murkiest pronouncements about the mythology that Dante was supposed to have created out of science and by which he became the model, as the first, for modern poetry; it led him to pay homage to the false theory — recently warmed over again among us — of seeing the goal of poetry (p. 37) in the great epic, which, so far only proclaimed rhapsodically and in isolated appearances, would then emerge as a completed totality. The philosophical-theological worth of Dante was exaggerated by uncomprehending imitators of Schelling, and this tendency, not without danger for Protestantism, has persisted down to the most recent times. Dante turns with the majesty of a judge of the world against wicked popes, against the corruption of the clergy — but he is not thereby yet a Protestant. He remains a strict Catholic. He makes his orthodox profession of faith to the princely apostles of the church. Arriving at the vision of the Trinity, he does not omit Mary — just as Cornelius too, in painting the principal moments of Dante's Paradiso in the frescoes of the Villa Massimi, quite correctly represented the Trinity, in the Roman sense, as a quaternity. Even the fact that he has Virgil for a time as his guide is perfectly orthodox, because the Church held that Virgil belonged among the prophets of paganism who had given Messianic prophecies. The limbo of the fathers and of infants is the convenient expedient by which the Church could meet the demands of humaneness up to a certain point.

But we do not wish to lose ourselves in these details. We wish only to point out that Dante is by no means yet Luther for the Church, that Dante, great poet though he is, stands as a philosopher entirely on the standpoint of Scholasticism and therefore can help us no more in our speculation than Thomas Aquinas or Albertus Magnus or any of the other greater Scholastics, and that the indifference of forms as well as the mythologizing allegory by no means correspond to the highest demands of art. One does not honor Dante by imputing to him the faith of the Protestant reformers and the knowledge of the Protestant philosophers, but rather by grasping him in his greatness as belonging to the thirteenth century. Without this restriction, the study of Dante easily becomes dangerous for weak souls, imperceptibly drawing them down into Catholicizing tendencies. The process begins with aesthetic admiration and ends with conversion to the sole church of salvation.

But that Schelling could have had any essential share in the composition of the introduction is quite out of the question, if any judgment at all is possible about the difference in tone between two writers, and if one recalls how Schelling had already, shortly before, poured out everything he had on his heart concerning the concept of criticism in his invective against the Jena Literary Gazette.

The true fruit that Schelling drew, in the first instance, from his contact with Hegel was the striving for a greater determinacy and clarity of expression, noticeable in the writings he now published — which one can see quite clearly if one considers the additions he made to the second edition of the Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature, whose preface is dated 31 December 1802. Here one recognizes not only the advance of his education in general, but within it also a new element striving for precision and orderliness. Schelling's particular achievements in this period can be characterized thus: first, he subjected the principle of philosophy to a new presentation; second, he critically worked out the relation of philosophy to the particular sciences; third, from the more disciplined bearing he had attempted to give himself in these works, he fell back again into his old looseness and indeterminacy, though now with a tendency toward the most thoroughgoing exhaustiveness.

The first happened in Bruno, or On the Divine and Natural Principle of Things, Berlin 1802; the second in the Lectures on the Method of Academic Study, which he delivered at Jena in the summer of 1802 and which appeared in print at the beginning of 1803 with Cotta; the third with the New Journal for Speculative Physics, Tübingen 1802, which broke off with the third installment of the first volume.

In Bruno Schelling attempted a new form of philosophical presentation, the dialogue, and here too, in imitating Plato and Giordano Bruno, he showed the most enviable ease of assimilation. He knew perfectly how to adopt the solemn tone, to speak of philosophy as the public mystery whose esotericism lies in the nature of its content and which need not first be made into a mystery outwardly through artificial devices. The dialogue, however, is for philosophy only a subordinate transitional form toward the objective, dialectical presentation of systematic coherence. It is not, say, the lack of talent, the incapacity of the moderns to write dialogues, that has pushed this form back. Fichte, on the contrary, moved within it with ease, particularly in his patriotic dialogues, and in the dialogue on Reinhold we have seen how vividly Schelling could carry on a dialogue once he was permitted to make his presentation the reflection of a concrete reality — a possession in which the Greeks had so great a model before us for the written dialogue. Now compare the dramatic animation of that conversation on Reinhold with Bruno, and one will soon see how, at bottom, only one person delivers a lecture, whose individual determinations are affirmed by the others in the most varied turns of phrase. This eternal tautology soon produces the judgment that the form of dialogue is only an inconvenient, tedious addition to the actual treatise. In Plato we find, especially at the openings of the dialogues, a rich mimetic staging that depicts the occasional coming-about of the conversation, through which Schleiermacher's Christmas Eve too receives so great a stylistic charm. Nothing of the kind is found in Schelling. There are, in all, only two persons, one active and one passive, called at first Anselmo and Alexander, later Bruno and Lucian. The whole subtlety here consists only in devising euphemisms for the tautology of simple assent: "So we said; — undeniably; — it goes without saying; — so it is; — evidently; — so we shall conclude; this is clear; — so it truly is; correct; indeed;

must be;

certainly;

you are quite right; — necessarily; — it cannot be denied;

in every way; — granted; — it seems; correctly hit; — this I concede; so we assumed." If we remove this manifold mode of expressing affirmation, and the turn of phrase borrowed from Plato whereby the seemingly conversing parties say to one another "O best of men, excellent one," we abolish the whole dialogue.

Little is to be remarked on its speculative content, since it remains very general and, as Schelling himself indicates, is drawn in part from the excerpts that Jacobi had given, in the appendix to his Letters on the Doctrine of Spinoza, from Giordano Bruno's treatise On Cause, Principle and the One. On p. 221 Bruno is agreed with when he says: "To penetrate into the deepest secrets of nature, one must not tire of investigating the opposed and conflicting extreme ends of things; to find the point of union is not the greatest thing, but to develop from it also its opposite — this is the true and deepest secret of art."

Schelling quite rightly sets forth, at the opening of the dialogue, the distinction between the concept of the Idea as eternal being and the world of appear-

-ance, by determining the eternal — which here, following the precedent set in the presentation of the system, he also calls the rational — as the totality that is the unity of indifference and of the difference opposed to it within that indifference; hence he declares a beginning from unity alone, or from opposition alone, to be equally unphilosophical. This thought is developed further, with graceful eloquence, in the forms of the unity of the infinite and the finite, the universal and the particular, the possible and the actual, which are contained in the "sacred abyss" of the eternal. But if we look further to see what particular determinations Schelling gives regarding the realization of the Absolute, we find on pp. 93-117 a paraphrasing and poeticizing extract from Hegel's dissertation De Orbitis Planetarum, and Schelling himself says in the notes that, regarding this speculative treatment of Kepler's laws, he can appeal with conviction to the earlier efforts of a friend. Here one can observe very well how much Schelling is inclined, as soon as he has any sort of basis at all, immediately to indulge in brilliant combinations and to mix what is insightful and true with what is skewed and half-true. Thus he says, for example, on p. 99: "What now determines things for the merely rectilinear and the finite concept is the inorganic component; what gives them shape, however, or determines them for judgment and for the reception of the particular into the universal, is the organic; but that whereby they express the absolute unity of the universal and the particular is the rational." Here one must say one does not know where one stands, if the organic is not itself determined as the rational, and if rectilinearity is made a characteristic mark of the inorganic, since this can only be said of crystal formation — which, however, as the individualization of the material, already itself represents the beginning of organic nature-formation, which, as the productive unity of the infinite and the finite, the universal and the particular in the individual, corresponds to the concept of reason. Or is rectilinearity supposed to be a mere image? Then one would have to expect that, with the organic, the curve would be spoken of, which would even make much sense for the shape of plants and animals. Who knows! But we must mention that in this section of the dialogue occurs the explanation of its title, namely that light is supposed to be the divine, active, and gravity the natural, passive, maternal principle of things.

Further on there occurs the emphasis on the syllogism. On p. 166, to be sure, logic is still expressly defined as the science that subordinates reason to understanding, and Bruno asks: "What hope, then, of philosophy for him who seeks it in logic?"

Lucian: None.

Nonetheless, earlier, on pp. 158ff., the transition is made from the concept of possibility, actuality, and necessity to the concept of the syllogism, and on p. 159 it is declared: "The infinite form is now the categorical, the finite the hypothetical, while the disjunctive has most of the nature of the eternal. But in every syllogism, whatever the variation, the major premise in relation to the minor is always categorical, or infinite; the minor premise hypothetical and finite; the conclusion, however, disjunctive, uniting within itself both the one and the other." Whereupon Lucian exclaims, delighted:

"Admirable form of the understanding! What a delight it is to fathom your relations, and to recognize the same imprint of the eternal, from the scaffolding of corporeal things up to the very form of the syllogism! In contemplating you the researcher immerses himself, after recognizing in you the image of the most glorious and most blessed, etc."

In this recognition of the necessity of the syllogism, alongside that contempt, there is an evident contradiction, which is explained only by the fact that logic is at one point taken as a science of the understanding, and at another the form of the syllogism is intuited in its speculative significance. Schelling also rejects metaphysics, the cognition of the Absolute by the logical route in general; all who seek it by that route are condemned, on p. 167, "as not yet having greeted the threshold of philosophy." Some light on these contradictions is given us, perhaps, by recalling that the first lecture course Hegel opened at Jena had logic and metaphysics as its subject — indeed, that the second of the theses he defended on 27 August 1801 ran: Syllogismus est principium idealismi [the syllogism is the principle of idealism]. Now we know whence Schelling came to hit upon this elevation of the syllogism while nonetheless rejecting logic. Through Hegel he had heard of a logic other than the merely formal one, without, however, seeing clearly into it.

From here Schelling further considers the true mode of cognition of philosophy, to which idealism is opposed as realism, and realism as idealism, both being, in and for themselves, One, and in this unity God is intuited in nature, nature in God. Here roughly the same views are presented — though with priestly unction — on the history of philosophy, the succession of its systems, and the necessity of perfecting the form of knowledge (by which alone it is distinguished from mere dilettantism), as are also found in Hegel's introduction to the Difference of the Fichtean and Schellingian Systems. Here too Schelling cannot free himself from his inclination toward schematizing construction. Starting from the true thought that in all philosophies the one and same philosophy exists, as self-cognition of the one and same reason, at different stages, he loses himself, on p. 184, in the following hypothesis: Just as the earth's one center of gravity can nevertheless be viewed from four different sides, and the one primal substance presents itself through four metals, equally noble, equally inseparable, so too has that indivisible unity of reason expressed itself preeminently in four forms, which designate, as it were, the four regions of the world of philosophy; for to the western world seems to belong what ours have called materialism, to the Orient what

intellectualism, while in the south we may call it realism, in the north, idealism." Such schematisms help not at all, for where do we now place Greece? Under Orient or Occident, South or North? And has it not rather developed all four of those forms within itself? And if by the western world the Romance peoples are supposed to be understood, then a reflective person will surely recall that scholastic philosophy had its seat there for centuries, and was anything but materialism. Or if realism is supposed to belong to the south, one asks oneself whether Neoplatonism, which certainly flourished in southern locales, can be called realism.

If, in Bruno, Schelling presented his philosophy once more according to its most general determinations, but now in a new, dialogical form, he expounded it in the:

Lectures on the Method of Academic Study,

he delivered them in lecture form, with critical reference to the particular sciences, and stated himself that they thereby had something of an encyclopedic character. For these lectures, first delivered in 1802, published for the first time in 1803 and for a third time in 1830, we must above all render Schelling our most sincere and warmest thanks. Throughout a generation they have provided the most manifold and most beneficial stimulus, and even today they deserve to be read and taken to heart earnestly by every student. The disposition with which the student is to be filled for science is depicted more negatively, as an aristocratic elevation, but the contempt for baseness is expressed with a noble, inspiring grace. The beginning of such a critical review of university study had, in turn, been made by Kant with his Conflict of the Faculties, which Schelling indeed calls one-sided, yet is unable to say anything further definite against. The Schellingian treatise was then followed, on the occasion of the founding of the University of Berlin, by others from Schleiermacher, Steffens, and so on. Given the great importance that these Schellingian lectures have had as the finest flower of his academic activity, as the most popular presentation of his philosophy, we cannot excuse ourselves from going through them in particular, and in doing so we must also not forget the opposition to some of Schelling's whims and errors....

The first lecture deals with: the absolute concept of science. On this we need not be lengthy. Schelling sets forth how knowledge, in and for itself, is one, and how particular knowledge is such only through its connection with universal knowledge, primal knowledge. He infers from this the necessity of instructing the student, from the concept of knowledge itself, about his task, and how this instruction falls to philosophy. Finally he demonstrates the shallowness of those who want to know nothing of knowledge and falsely set action against it as a higher purpose, whereas knowledge and action have absolutely equal worth, and the call to action is often heard loudest from those with whom things are not going well as regards knowledge. Knowledge and action can be raised to harmony with one another only through the same absoluteness.

The second lecture treats: the scientific and moral vocation of the academies. With frankness, with warmth, with emphasis, Schelling here shows the independence of science from external considerations, because it must be an end in itself. He demands that the universities not merely hand down historical knowledge but themselves conduct themselves productively out of the idea of science, all the more so since even tradition often cannot be thought of as faithful without a re-invention of the original production. The academic teacher should be capable of freely producing his science, and not possess it as though it were someone else's property. Such teachers will never be lacking, since the teachers themselves receive their education at the universities: "One need only grant them intellectual freedom and not restrict them by considerations that have no application to the scientific relationship, and teachers will form themselves who can satisfy those demands and are in turn capable of forming others."

"One might ask whether it is at all fitting to make demands on academies, as it were in the name of science, since it is sufficiently well known and accepted that they are instruments of the state, which must be what the state determines them to be. — If it were now the state's intention that, with regard to the sciences, a certain moderation, restraint, and limitation to the customary and

the useful should be observed throughout, how could a progressive tendency and desire to develop their science according to ideas then be expected of the teachers?"

"It surely goes without saying that we together presuppose, and must presuppose, that the state wants to see in the academies genuinely scientific institutions, and that everything we assert with regard to them holds only under this condition."

And with respect to the students, Schelling says: "If the teachers themselves spread around them no spirit other than the genuine one, and if no considerations count except those of knowledge and its perfection; if the outbursts of vulgarity of unworthy men, who disgrace the calling of teacher, are not tolerated by the baseness of the given common state of affairs itself, then those students will of themselves disappear from the ranks of the studying youth who are capable of distinguishing themselves in no other way than through coarseness."

The fourth lecture treats the study of the pure sciences of reason, of mathematics, and of philosophy in general. This grouping must strike one as odd. Mathematics, insofar as it is not treated philosophically, is not a science of reason but a science of the understanding, i.e., there is reason within it, without which it would not be capable of being a science at all. But what characterizes it in the treatment of its content is abstract identity, the consistency of understanding-based thought. Now Schelling maintains that mathematics is, in the abstract, just as much the most perfect, most objective expression of reason itself as nature is in the concrete, and that all natural laws, as they resolve into pure laws of reason, find their corresponding forms also in mathematics. This is a superficial claim not further substantiated, for mathematics is on the one hand itself a natural science, but on the other hand, outside its own domain, it can only ever occur as one moment, namely that of quantitative and figurative determinacy — which is why one can speak decisively of a so-called application of mathematics to natural science only within mechanics, whereas in physics, where the dynamic of specific quality already asserts itself, mathematics recedes, and in organics no demonstration at all can be given of laws it might prescribe to life. Schelling would never have arrived at these vague pronouncements had he recognized the logical form of the Idea. The need for it, together at the same time with the lack of its concept, then elicits from him such rash, ambiguous statements. As we saw earlier from Schelling's contributions to Niethammer's journal, he once wanted to resolve all science into mathematics!

To be sure, Schelling again helps himself out of the obscure feeling of the sheer boldness of his claims by pouring vehement censure over present-day mathematics: in order to understand even the least of the essence of nature, mathematics itself would first have to go back again to its origin and grasp more generally the type of reason expressed in it; its forms are symbols, "for which those who possess them have lost the key, which, according to reliable traces and reports of the ancients, Euclid still possessed." If the forms of mathematics are supposed to be symbols, I do not know how this can be understood otherwise than in the Pythagorean sense. But the Pythagoreans resorted to such symbolism because in it they still possessed, in a latent way, the logical element. Once this had therefore emerged in its purity for itself, this mixing of the logical with the mathematical — both the arithmetical and the geometrical — had necessarily to fall away of its own accord, as can be seen nowhere more clearly than in the critique that the Aristotelian metaphysics gives of the Pythagorean and Platonic philosophy. But what traces and reports among the ancients these are supposed to be, according to which Euclid still possessed the key to those symbols, cannot be said, least of all that such traces could be reliable. Besides Euclid's Elements we still have enough other works of ancient mathematics left to convince us that between them and Euclid there existed the finest, genuinely Hellenic continuity of development, so that that key would presumably have to be attributed to them as well. Schelling assures us that philosophy, on the path now entered upon, will also furnish the means of deciphering and restoring that ancient science. This is a compliment he pays himself, but an empty one, since he has done not the slightest thing for mathematics as a science. Why he calls it an ancient science is also hard to say, for the sixth century before Christ truly does not belong to primordial times. Just as little can one see why, as he boldly asserts, geometry should correspond "strikingly" to realism, and analysis to idealism, in philosophy. Have we not, since Descartes, also had an analytic geometry — if anything at all is supposed to be said by such vague, thoughtless comparisons.

On philosophy as the science of the Ideas, or the eternal archetypes of things themselves, Schelling is quite brief. He demands for its study intellectual intuition, which cannot be given. "Whoever does not have it also does not understand what is said about it."

The fifth lecture contains a polemic against the usual objections to the study of philosophy. Schelling first recalls the shallowness of the chatter that philosophy is dangerous for state and church, and exclaims very truly: "What sort of state and what sort of religion must this be, to which philosophy can be dangerous? If this were really the case, the fault would have to lie with the alleged religion and the alleged state. Philosophy follows only its own inner grounds and can concern itself little with whether everything made by men agrees with it." Schelling holds, on the other hand, that elevating the common, limited understanding to the position of arbiter over the Ideas is indeed just as dangerous to the state as the deification of mere utility, through which love of life and of pleasure would be set at the top. In Germany especially, philosophy alongside religion is so extraordinarily important precisely because both are the only inner bond of the nation, which is increasingly disintegrating within itself.

But not only has the outcry about the danger of philosophy for state and religion been raised; the practitioners of various sciences too, disturbed in their narrow-mindedness by philosophy, have grown loud against it, because it distracts from the thorough sciences, presents them as dispensable, and so on. "It would indeed be excellent if the scholars of certain fields could also rise to the rank of privileged classes, and if it were established by the state that in no branch of knowledge should any progress or even transformation take place. Things have not yet gone that far, at least generally, and probably never will. There is no science which would in itself stand opposed to philosophy; rather all sciences are precisely through it and in it One." — Finally he also mentions the stale objections raised against the study of philosophy from the so-called change of its systems, as if in this were not revealed the living impulse of a science ever more advancing toward its completion. The reproach of dismissing philosophy as a mere matter of fashion, however, cannot really be seriously meant even by those who are wont to speak that way, for if they do not want to be entirely fashionable, neither do they want to be entirely old-fashioned, and if they can only snatch up here and there something, were it merely a word, of the newer or newest philosophy,

they nevertheless do not disdain to adorn themselves with it. "Were it really only a matter of fashion, as they pretend, and hence just as easy as exchanging one cut of clothing or one hat for another, to set up a system of medicine, of theology, etc., according to the newest principles, they would certainly not hesitate to do so."

The sixth lecture on the study of philosophy in particular is at bottom also still negative, insofar as it rejects logic and psychology as merely empirical, unphilosophical attempts. In rejecting logic it does occur to Schelling himself that perhaps a speculative treatment of the laws of thought might be possible, but he counters this by saying that it would then no longer be an absolute science but a particular potency within the general system of the sciences of reason. And if this were the case, what misfortune would that be? Would such a position not be the redemption of logic from the abstract separatism in which it exists as formal logic? Would it not, as a member of the whole organism of science, correspond all the more truly to its own concept? Is not every science, as science, absolute and of equal dignity with all the others, while in itself, as a particular, it is only relative in relation to the totality? Why should this not hold of logic? Or why should the concept of thought in and for itself have a less speculative, i.e. rational, determinacy than the concept of nature, or of will, or of art? Here Schelling struck upon the point which he indeed always touches but never subjects to thorough critique. In his confusion about it he therefore says again: "What can be practiced through instruction, though not really learned, from philosophy, is the artistic side of this science, or what one can generally call dialectic. Without dialectical art there is no scientific philosophy! Even its intention to present everything as One, and yet to express primordial knowledge in forms that originally belong to reflection, is proof of this. It is this relation of speculation to reflection on which all dialectic rests. — But precisely this principle of the antinomy of the absolute and of the merely finite forms, as well as the fact that in philosophy art and production can no more be separated than form and matter in poetry, proves that dialectic too has a side from which it cannot be learned." Schelling thus comes close to the concept of dialectic as it was taken up by Hegel as the positive result of Kant's critique of reason, but immediately falls back down into a merely subjective conception. Dialectic is supposed not really to be learned but rather practiced; it is supposed to require a productive capacity, hence a talent, an immediate gift. Being innate is of course the most convenient way of arriving at possession of a science, and in the fourteenth lecture it is taught that the philologist too must be born. Schelling thus treats the formal side of science exactly as he treats the postulate of intellectual intuition, and thereby becomes a teacher only of those who already know. The institutions of our states, to be sure, are organized in a different sense. Composing a poem is indeed required by examining boards only in China; for artistic production we here presuppose talent as a chance gift of nature, but for philosophy examinations are instituted. That is, the states conceive of it as just as teachable and learnable as any other science. The upshot in Schelling's case, then, is again that he does have thoughts, but not enough of them, and that he prefers the dazzling effect, the indeterminate excitement, of simple determinacy that engages the root of the matter, to the quiet, lasting effect — because the logic he had before him in the ordinary compendia was bad, a product of the most finite understanding; must there therefore be no better one, and did not what he called dialectic have to take up the content of common logic into itself in speculative transfiguration, as happened with Hegel?

The seventh lecture, according to its title, deals with certain external oppositions of philosophy, chiefly in the positive sciences. But what is actually discussed is rather the inner relations of philosophy to religion, morality, and poetry. It is noted that these all rest entirely on Ideas, but that dilettantism — which shuns seriousness in science and feels comfortable in formlessness — has contrived a disdain of philosophy in opposition to religion, morality, and poetry, precisely because philosophy is science. "It is not to be wondered at that in an age in which a certain dilettantism has spread over almost all subjects, even the most sacred could not escape it, and this kind of inability or unwillingness withdraws into religion in order to escape higher demands." Quite rightly Schelling says: "If they

want religion not to be attained through philosophy, then on the same ground they must also want religion not to be able to give philosophy, or to take its place." Schelling now develops out of the indifference of the real and the ideal the positive sciences as the real objectification of the Idea of primordial knowledge. He calls the sciences positive insofar as they exist for the state. He now constructs the following parallel:

⁠1) The point of indifference of the Absolute becomes objectively "the immediate science of the absolute and divine essence, and hence will be theology."

⁠2) The real side of the Absolute presents itself objectively in natural science, and since this is concentrated in the science of the organism, in medicine — as that in which alone natural science has a direct relation to the state, and hence is a positive science.

⁠3) The ideal side of philosophy becomes in general the science of history, and insofar as the most excellent work of the latter is the formation of the constitution of law, the science of law, or jurisprudence.

But where, then, does philosophy itself remain? For it, Schelling had once got it into his head that art is the most perfect organ of its presentation — to lead philosophy over into a new, universal mythology! That philosophy, to which Schelling himself, in the necessary dialectic, granted an artistic side, must be just as independent as art is in itself, disappeared in this murky mixing of the Idea in and for itself with philosophy as its concept and art as its sensuously beautiful appearance. Schelling said: "The true objectivity of philosophy in its totality is only art; there could therefore in any case be no philosophical faculty, but only a faculty of the arts. But the arts can never be an external power, and just as little can they be privileged or restricted by the state. There are thus only free associations for art; and this was also, at the older universities, the meaning of what is now called the philosophical faculty, which was called the Collegium Artium, its members being called artists. This difference of the philosophical faculty from the others has been preserved to this day in that it does not, like the others, create privileged masters (Doctores) bound also in duty to the state, but rather teachers (Magistri) of the free arts."

Following the order of rank vindicated for the faculties, Schelling begins the closer discussion of them with theology, and in the eighth lecture gives the so-famous historical construction of Christianity. It is, in fact, nothing other than a paraphrastic distortion of the thoughts that Hegel had expressed in the second section of his treatise on the relation of the philosophy of nature to philosophy. It had been said there that religion as such cannot be conceived without historical relation; that one can view the essence of paganism as the immediate divinity of the natural, as the absolute reception or imaging of the finite into the infinite; the essence of Christianity, by contrast, as the intuition of the natural in the divine, as the imaging of the infinite into the finite; and finally, that mysticism in Christianity constitutes the highest point of opposition to paganism, because in Christianity the esoteric religion is itself the public one and vice versa, whereas a great part of the representations in the mysteries of paganism were themselves of a mythical nature. The fundamental thought running through the treatise was then that paganism and Christianity are the two only possible forms of religious intuition, which precisely for that reason can sublate themselves into unity with one another and unite the pain of reconciliation with the serenity and beauty of the Greek religion: "then heaven is truly regained and the absolute gospel proclaimed."

Schelling exploited these thoughts, but in such a way that he makes the intuition of the infinite in the finite the character of paganism, and the intuition of the finite in the infinite that of Christianity. These formulas are worse than Hegel's, which specify the direction of religion more precisely. To see the unity of the finite and infinite is the deed of every religion, but the starting point makes a difference. Hegel places this starting point, for paganism, in the natural, which precisely for that reason became a symbol for the infinite; for Christianity, in the spirit, which therefore gazes "through nature as the infinite body of God" into its innermost depths and comports itself mystically therein. In the particular determinations Schelling did not deviate from Hegel, whereby, however, he put himself even more into contradiction with his own general determination of the opposition of religion. According to him, nature — the exoteric side of the cult, the permanence of the god-figures, symbolism as a subjective infinitizing of the finite — is supposed to constitute the concept of paganism;

while history, on the other hand — the esoteric, that is to say the nature-less side of the cult, the transitory character of the divine manifestation as fleeting appearance, and mysticism as a subjective symbolism, as an allegory of the infinite in the finite — is supposed to constitute the concept of Christianity. He therefore also brings back his distinction of a period of nature, of fate, and of providence, but says no more of the first than that he assigns it to the Greek world at the time of its most beautiful flowering, and then adds: with the falling-away from it, it reveals itself as fate, inasmuch as it enters into real conflict with freedom. That was the end of the ancient world, etc." We recall well that Schelling two years earlier, in the System of Transcendental Idealism, had determined the periods quite differently, namely as fate, nature, and providence, and that at that time the period of nature was supposed to begin with the downfall of the Roman Empire. Here too, as there, Christianity is supposed only to introduce, within history, the period of providence. It is also asserted that what the Greek religion had all at once, Christianity has in succession, although the time of the separation of appearances and, with it, of configuration has not yet

come." What this is actually supposed to mean is not really apparent. For in the first place, succession is by no means absent in the Greek religion; there exists in it a theogonic process as a progression from chaos through Kronos to the Olympian Zeus. And in the second place, Christianity expressly teaches the eternal unity of God with himself, so that the succession of successive revelation falls only on the side of the human consciousness that comes to know God. According to its own words it decisively rejects the doctrine of certain sects, such as the Montanists and others, who assume a succession in the generation of the persons of the deity and make Father, Son, and Spirit into successors of one another. But that the time of the separation of appearances has not yet come in Christianity is one of those lazy, pretentious phrases of Schelling's which, in the form of portentous profundity, say nothing whatever. If Schelling wanted to resolve the "all-at-once" into a succession, he would have had either to engage with the hypostases of the Trinity, or he would have had to take into account the configuration of Christianity as Byzantine-Slavic, Catholic-Romantic, and Protestant-German.

Christ is therefore construed by Schelling also only as the "summit and end of the old world of gods,"

## Construction of the World-Process

as an appearance that, though decreed from eternity, is transitory in time, "standing as the boundary of the two worlds," and promising the spirit as the ideal principle which leads the finite back to the infinite. Hegel, by contrast, said in that treatise much more correctly: "The seed of Christianity was the feeling of a rupture of the world with God; its direction was reconciliation with God, not through a raising of finitude to infinity, but through a becoming-finite of the infinite, through a becoming-human of God. Christianity set up this union, for the first moment of its appearance, as an object of faith. Faith is the inner certainty that anticipates infinity for itself" — not, as Schelling puts it, that leads the finite back to the infinite.

The ninth lecture is concerned with applying the historical construction of Christianity to the study of theology. Its characteristic feature is the polemic against the empirical-philological and psychologically-moralizing theology as a product of the age of Enlightenment, or rather of "clarification." Schelling asserts the concept of religion against its worship of the mere letter. In doing so he pays homage entirely to a speculative rationalism. He grasps the eternal becoming-human of God as the representation of the finite in the form of the Son of God; Christ is merely the historical, appearing summit of the becoming-human; as an individual he is a person entirely intelligible from the circumstances of that time; since God is eternally outside all time, absolutely nothing can be thought in the notion that God assumed human nature at a determinate moment of time. "The Christian missionaries who came to India believed they were announcing to the inhabitants something unheard of when they taught that the God of the Christians had become man. Those people were not astonished at this; they by no means disputed the incarnation of God in Christ, and found it merely strange that among the Christians what happens with them [the Indians] often and in constant repetition should have occurred only once. It cannot be denied that they had more understanding of their religion than the Christian missionaries had of theirs."

Schelling now declares philosophy to be the true organ of theology, and wants the idea of Christianity to be made the measure of the worth of the books in which it is expressed, since already in Paul Christianity had become something other than it was in the spirit of its founder; had interpretation been given free rein, we would already be so much further along in the appreciation of the earliest documents of Christianity, and would not now still be seeking so many detours and complications in so simple a matter; explaining away the miracles of the Bible by means of philological knowledge is just as pitiful an undertaking as the opposite one — proving the divinity of religion from these empirical, and moreover extremely meager, facts. In his opposition to so-called sound exegesis Schelling goes so far as not only to reproach Protestantism for its hair-splitting treatment of the biblical books, but, with a eulogy of Catholicism for having withheld the Bible from the people, even bursts into the following words: "One cannot help the thought of what an obstacle to its completion the so-called biblical books have been for it (Christianity), books which, in genuine religious content, cannot withstand comparison, even remotely, with so many others of earlier and later times, above all the Indian ones." One must admit that not even the worst freethinkers of English and French deism dared say that the Bible had been an obstacle to the completion of Christianity, still less that it was so deeply subordinate in genuine religious content to other religious writings. Schelling is so bold as to speak of writings of earlier and later times, the latter presumably meaning writings composed after the emergence of Christianity. Would that it had pleased him to add some more definite indication to his bold words. He does name, it is true, the Indian religious books, but in 1802 the Vedas were still much less known than they are now, and it was then a mere opinion that they contained an infinitely deep speculative content. Given the lack of facts on which to base even a somewhat objective judgment, especially in so important a matter, Schelling's rashness strikes one here as offensive. In general, in these lectures homage is paid to the Orient as the motherland of Ideas, to India as the homeland of the intellectual system; the Occident is said to have been in itself a more barren soil, which knew only how to give shape, through its letter, to the ideal principle of the Orient, and so on. Now whereas Hegel had said in his treatise that in Christianity the esoteric had itself become exoteric, public, Schelling turned this into an esoteric Christianity; whereas Hegel had drawn the conclusion, from the disintegration of the particular temporal forms of Christianity, that it itself, as the reconciliation of God with the world, would emerge all the more decisively as the true gospel, Schelling made out of this the "rebirth of esoteric Christianity" through speculation united with poetry and religion by the total suspension of empiricism and naturalism.

The tenth lecture, on the study of history and of jurisprudence, is only a faint echo of Hegel's treatise in the Critical Journal on natural law. More is said about historiography than about history itself; jurisprudence is scarcely discussed at all; concerning the concept of the state, some of Hegel's determinations are presented, not without confusion and deterioration, in meager outlines; Plato's Republic is praised as the only solution so far to the task of constructing the state out of Ideas, and it is asserted that "the true synthesis of the state cannot be further explained without the execution of, or reference to, an existing document." What one is supposed to imagine under this document is prudently

not explained; nothing can be thought by it. What one should have expected in this lecture would have been a discussion of the idea peculiar to Schelling, which he had also expressed in the historical construction of Christianity, that "there is no state of barbarism that does not derive from a perished culture." "I regard the state of culture entirely as the first state of the human race, and the first founding of states, of the sciences, of religion and of the arts as simultaneous, or rather as One, so that all this was not truly separate but existed in the most perfect interpenetration, as it will one day again be in ultimate completion." When we conceive the beginning of history, it lies in its concept that the particular forms of the realization of spirit do not yet exist. Spirit in itself is the universal, real possibility, and to that extent the unity of all its particularizations. But the event that constitutes its deed consists precisely in this: that it sublates this embryonic unity by positing its differences; thus states, arts, etc. arise. The moment of simultaneity of the various particular spheres does not exclude the succession of their formation; the political, religious, artistic, and

scientific progress are naturally interconnected in the one Spirit. But precisely because history is the process of formation of Spirit, the beginning cannot already have contained state, art, etc., as actualities of determinate existence in mutual interpenetration. That barbarism can arise from a perishing culture, and that it, as the reversion of civilization into naturalism and cynicism, is true barbarism, can be granted without hesitation. But when Schelling calls the simple condition of primordial humanity — which admittedly was not barbarism — a state of culture, this is too much, once "culture," as he uses it, is understood as more than the alteration, the sublation, of the primitive naturalness of spirit. The Bible has here the fullest right to regard the deedless vegetation of man's immediate harmony with himself as the beginning of history, and the beginnings of culture as lying beyond the paradisiacal cohabitation with nature. Schelling and many of his followers, notably Schubert, have further embellished the hypothesis of an original perfection by holding that all later culture preserves only fragments of the primitive one, and hence is supposed to be merely a re-attainment of a height of culture once already possessed. They — as still Martius in his travels in America — have been unwilling or unable to conceive anything that "savages," as we are accustomed to say, possess in the way of legal institutions, legal symbolism, artistic skill, and the like, except as a work of tradition, not as a deed of spontaneous spirit. For Roman Catholicism this backward-turned view of history is of course most advantageous, for the dogmas of the Tridentine council, the canons of the decretals, the mission of the Roman bishop to ecclesiastical supremacy, the antiquarian golden age of Christianity — all of this lies, for it, behind; its authorities are empirical and indirectly exclude the eternally self-identical authority of reason and its progressive critique. For this very reason, this conception of history is also so dangerous for politics, because it turns the further development of what exists really into a regression of what exists into what has been, and thereby afflicts the forward-striving present with the death of decay. Such a procedure is "historical" only in this anti-historical sense, that the perfectibility of history into infinity is denied and a finished world is already placed at its beginning. But it is not organic at all, whatever one may call it; rather it is simply disorganizing; the

return to already familiar forms only deceives, for a while, about the destructive effects of such a method, which really attempts the impossible. The Bible is entirely reasonable on this point too. It has states, arts, and religions arise only after the crisis that sublated man's original unity with himself, with the world, and with God.

The eleventh lecture claims to speak about natural science in general, but offers us an entirely new philosophy. The indescribable naivety with which Schelling, without further ado, without recalling the difference between these present determinations and earlier ones, expounds a new doctrine of Ideas, renders him essentially unaccountable for the metamorphosis. For up to that point he had defined the Absolute as the negative of all oppositions of positive and negative force, of the real and the ideal, of object and subject, of nature and history. The Absolute was distinguished from the real and the ideal in that it positively constituted the indifferent middle of this polar difference. In the constitutive document of the system, meant to be authentic, indifference as absolute identity had been called reason. Now, all at once, God steps forth. This name, one might object, is innocent; one would have had to understand Schelling very poorly up to that point not to know that the Absolute, the indifference, the identity, the reason, are meant to be the same thing he now calls God. This is correct. But the difference becomes great. Up to that point Schelling had defined the Absolute only logically, as the negation of some difference; even at the outset of these lectures he had explained the Absolute as the unity of the finite and the infinite. In now determining the Absolute as God, the thought of subjectivity, of personality, was thereby brought to life. The world, in its unity with God, had to be just as much distinguished from him. The transition from God to the world had necessarily to force itself upon him, and with it the difficulty had to become noticeable of thinking God as independent not at the world's expense, and the world as independent without infringing on God's freedom. At first Schelling, Platonizing, helped himself out by means of a world of Ideas, which he inserted between God and actuality in such a way that he made the Ideas into the souls, and things — a barbarous expression — into their bodies. This world of Ideas, first hinted at in the Bruno, has unquestionably not occurred to us in this way in Schelling up to now, and we will let him

## The Theosophical Turn

speak for himself as to how he introduced this theosophical turn into his philosophy. He said:

"In order to grasp nature as the universal birth of the Ideas, we must go back to the origin and meaning of these themselves. — The former lies in the eternal law of absoluteness: to be object to itself; for by virtue of this, God's producing is an imaging of the whole universality and essentiality into particular forms, whereby these, as particular, are yet at the same time universa, and are what the philosophers have called monads or Ideas."

"It is shown more fully in philosophy that the Ideas are the sole mediators through which particular things can be in God, and that according to this law there are as many universa as there are particular things, and yet, because of the identity of essence, in all of them only One universe. Although now the Ideas in God are pure and absolutely ideal, they are nevertheless not dead but living, the first organisms of divine self-intuition, which for that very reason share in all the properties of his essence, and, in their particular form, nonetheless partake of the undivided and absolute reality."

"By virtue of this communication they are, like God, productive, and act according to the same law and in the same manner, in that they form their essentiality into the particular and make it recognizable through individual and particular things — in themselves and for themselves without time, but from the standpoint of the individual things, and for these, in time. The Ideas relate as the souls of things, these as their bodies; the former are, in this relation, necessarily infinite, the latter finite."

Now we can once again explain this theosophical turn from nothing other than the need to distinguish the concept of the Idea from its real configuration. Instead of grasping it in its truth, Schelling fell into the plural of Ideas, which he presented in a thoroughly gnostic manner, i.e., in a mixing of thinking and fantasizing. If he thereby made progress in the consistency of his system — which must unconditionally be granted — he made it at the same time in a way through which he prepared for himself an even greater regression. The ideality of the logical as the absolute form was the rock on which he foundered. That which alone completes the necessity of the concept became for him the obscure, the incomprehensible. Ordinary logic, as we have seen, he despised, and rightly so, because in its

separation from the metaphysical meaning of its determinations, in the external mechanism of its rules, it stood too far beneath the concept of his Absolute. In the Bruno he came near, following Hegelian inspirations, to grasping the logical in a higher manner and freeing it from the finitude of mere understanding, but, as has been shown, he immediately sank back down to the most common concept of logic and declared it a hopeless undertaking to advance a single step in philosophy from it. One is often reluctant to admit that so great a philosopher as Schelling is found so inadequate in this domain; but it is so. Let one read in this eleventh lecture the distinction he makes between being and essence, and one will be astonished at how clumsy and vacuous, or rather how irrational, Schelling appears there. Since Hegel had already, since Michaelmas 1801, been lecturing on logic and metaphysics in Jena, we can of course account for this confusion externally: it is a digestive experiment with concepts half-grasped and taken over from Hegel. Schelling says: "If this — the finite — does not already, in itself and as finite, comprehend and express the whole infinite, and is itself this, considered only from the objective side, then the Idea cannot enter in as soul either, and essence does not appear in itself but through another, namely being. If, on the other hand, the finite as such bears the whole infinite formed within it, like the most perfect organism which is already in itself the whole Idea, then the essence of the thing also enters in as soul, as Idea, and reality dissolves again into ideality."

Of the particular determinations that Schelling gives in this lecture and the two following it on nature in general, on physics, chemistry, and medicine, it is unnecessary to cite anything, since we mostly encounter only reminiscences of his earlier views. The brilliant side of the lecture here, as everywhere in Schelling, is the polemical one. Diligent use is made of the content of Hegel's dissertation on the orbits of the planets. Mathematical natural science, as it stands, is rejected as an empty formalism in which nothing of a true science of nature is to be found; however, it is not stated how mathematics should actually relate to natural science. In medicine, Brown is praised, as Schelling always praises; he asserts, namely, that the concept of excitability must in turn be constructed, at a higher sphere of knowledge, out of higher concepts in just the way that Brown derives the various diseases from it.

Finally, the fourteenth lecture concerns the science of art in relation to academic study. This lecture is a wild jumble of propositions of Schelling's philosophy with opinions of the Schlegel school, then in vogue, about the essence of art. That the philosopher can understand the artist best is certainly to be granted; but the construction of art is supposed to be a worthy subject especially "of the Christian philosopher, whose particular business it is to gauge and present its universe." For it is first claimed that ancient art as a whole bears the character of the finite, just as Christian art bears that of the infinite. Such distinctions are as true as they are false. Anything can be made of them, and because of this looseness they are objectionable. Why should the Homeric epic, why should a Sophoclean tragedy, be called finite? I must ask whether the former corresponds to the concept of the epic, the latter to the concept of tragedy; if this is the case, then from the aesthetic standpoint the infinity

is realized in the work of art, or else the non-identity between the Idea and this determinate actuality would have to be demonstrable. Schelling, with his limiting diplomacy bent on evasions, therefore says that ancient art as a whole has the character of the finite; by this he means to slip away. What he actually wanted to say is plain enough to see. He wanted to express speculatively the opposition, discovered by Schiller, of the naive and the sentimental, and the character of the antique as indicated by Friedrich Schlegel in his investigations into the history of Greek poetry. So he arrived at that wretched designation, and so he came to declare the Christian philosopher especially qualified for the understanding of art — in which, again, there is this much truth, that we moderns naturally understand antiquity more correctly than antiquity was able to grasp itself. Otherwise it is not unimportant to note that the fateful expression "Christian philosopher," in the phase of our philosophy since Kant, is here used for the first time by a philosopher of himself, and Christianity was made a condition of true understanding. That Schelling assures us that such a construction of art as he has in mind is not to be compared with anything hitherto called aesthetics, theory of the beautiful

This dry assurance of the downfall of a previous form of knowledge, this prophesying of a new organization of it, has been the lever with which he set so many people in motion. He declared "the philosophy of art to be the necessary goal of the philosopher, who beholds in it, as in a magical and symbolic mirror, the inner essence of his science, etc." But he also preached "the intimate bond which unites art and religion," which is why the scientific cognition of art is also necessary for the religious person. He rebuked the administrators of the state harshly for being neither receptive to art nor possessing true knowledge of it; this, he said, was no small disgrace for them. Even if it could not be generally recognized, he concluded, that art is a necessary and integral part of a constitution of the state designed according to Ideas, at least antiquity should be a reminder of this, whose general festivals, immortalizing monuments, spectacles, as well as all actions of public life, were only different branches of one universal, objective, and living work of art.

One need only survey these topics to recognize at once that Schelling here repeats his familiar generalities. First, the triplicity of the finite, the infinite, and the eternal, set up in the Bruno, is presented as authoritative for cognition as well, with the strangest statements occurring, e.g., that representations are purely finite insofar as they are implanted in the soul through education. The second investigation naturally concerns intellectual intuition as the conditio sine qua non of philosophizing. The third deals with the indifference of form and essence and begins with the words: "Spinoza, whom

not misunderstanding or ignorance of particular concepts, but an absolute misapprehension of philosophy itself, stamps as a dogmatist — sets, etc." And who had stamped him as such? Schelling himself. In his Letters on Dogmatism and Criticism he had set Spinoza up as the most consummate dogmatist opposite Kant, and had seen in dogmatism and criticism the two only possible forms of philosophy. But in the meantime he had become acquainted with Hegel's critique of the Jacobian philosophy, and in it a new conception of Spinoza. He imitates Hegel's structuring of periods, he takes up Hegel's terminology into himself. It goes without saying, once again, that this would be only praiseworthy and entirely in order for a person eager for progress, were it not that Schelling, of late, has been pressing Hegel down to the level of an impotent schoolboy and acting as though the latter's recognition as a world-historical philosopher, like himself, were a wrong done to him and to science. For this reason one cannot avoid the reckoning of just what Schelling, for his part, may well owe to Hegel. It is especially the base manner in which he appropriated Hegel's philosophy, so far as he was able, that must come up for discussion here, little as this mercantile consideration otherwise belongs to criticism. Thus we find, as the construction of things in the Absolute in the second issue, an absolute method described which is supposed to be neither analytic nor synthetic. We recall that Schelling had earlier already wanted to make known the true method, but in the presentation of the system still retained the synthetic one and symbolized it in the schema of a straight line. Here, p. 13 ff., he now explains that the distinction between the analytic and synthetic method belongs fundamentally to arithmetic and geometry, but that philosophy has only One method, of which what has recently been called the synthetic is indeed the true, but the image drawn apart in reflection. But already in the Difference between the Fichtean and Schellingian systems, Hegel had said: "The method of the system, which is to be called neither synthetic nor analytic, presents itself most purely when it appears as a development of reason itself; which, etc." In the critique of the treatments of natural law he had then given a concrete example of

the one-sidedness of both methods and the necessity of their sublation in a higher one. Schelling brought forth again almost everything Hegel had said up to that point — including his view of skepticism, the history of philosophy, etc. — only more diffusely and polemically, so that he too might have said it and could cite himself. Also, straight-linearity was now supposed to be the schema only for matter as such; for its "sensitivity," as the relative imaging of essence into form, the reception of difference into indifference, the circular line was to be the absolute schema, II. 57. For the third potency, the relative imaging of indifference into difference, he had no schema left at all — that is, this whole business of construction was mere play.

In the development of Kepler's laws, p. 63, he finally names Hegel: "The disfigurement which these laws have suffered through the Newtonian theory of attraction, and through the attempt to derive them in a mechanical-mathematical way from contingent and empirical, arbitrarily assumed conditions, has been recognizably and sharply enough shown in Hegel's treatise De orbitis planetarum." He thus highlights the negative side of the treatise, the polemic against Newton, and continues: "The view of these laws, which I hold to be the absolute and purely speculative one, is presented in the dialogue on the divine and natural principle. Here it can only be a matter, partly of carrying out this investigation in general in a stricter form, partly of showing with more determinacy and clarity what may have remained indeterminate and obscure in that presentation." With this, as already shown, only his conceptual determination of the Absolute changed. In the lectures he had already called it God and spoken of the "birth of all things." Now he set up ideality as the fundamental determination of the Absolute. The ideal secondly determines itself within itself into the real, and the real as such is only the third moment. Previously he had merely neutralized the real and the ideal within the Absolute; now he displayed the process within it.

## The Ideal and the Real

but rather both the ideal and the real at once; only now he inserted between pure ideality and reality a middle term, a transition from the one to the other. The Absolute as ideal is first to determine itself within itself into the real. Thus, as the middle between the ideal and the real, it is form, the form-giving principle. Reality, as mediated through the Absolute, is now itself to bear the character of absoluteness — and it is here that Schelling first decisively departs from the ground of Spinozism on which he had until then stood, by distinguishing from the Absolute the universe as its counter-image. In Spinoza, extension as well as thought, as attributes, are an immediate moment of substance. So too, until then, nature and history had for Schelling been the immediate manifestation of the Absolute, which was nothing for itself apart from them. Now it mattered to him to see the difference as difference. The Absolute posits itself in the Other in such a way that this Other, insofar as it is posited, is necessary, but insofar as it, as something posited, determines itself within itself, is free. In order to express the independence of the world within itself in a truly striking way, Schelling called the act of the world's immanent self-determination its "falling away" from the Absolute. Whereas in the academic lectures he had still spoken of a communication of the Ideas to things, there was now no more talk of such an intermediate world between the Absolute and the world. The Absolute as productive form was itself the middle term. The break was there, and was pursued through all its consequences. This conception of the relation of the Absolute both to itself and to the world was, for Schelling, indisputably again an advance, for the Absolute only now became truly absolute, since it became free of the infinite progress of appearance; but the universe, too, only now became world, became the other Absolute, since it too became free from the Absolute as Absolute and received life and movement within itself — whereas until then the two series of potencies had always been mere presentations of the Absolute, i.e. had brought not themselves but only the Absolute to appearance within themselves. Schelling, to be sure, later greatly obscured this standpoint, when he elevated the notion of the falling-away into that of the dark ground, and wove the world and God back into one another.

One must grant Schelling that at that time he had a fully philosophical awareness of the figurative character of the expression "falling away." He ex-

-plicitly declared that one must not conceive the acts within the Absolute as a sequence, and that the falling-away must be thought as a timeless, eternal event. Nevertheless, in the further course of his exposition, he fell away from these correct determinations and let himself be seduced into all sorts of mythical inconsistencies. The lack of logical precision took its revenge here once again, and the imagination found too much nourishment in the notion of the Fall. The fall of the angels, Lucifer's part in it, the fall of the first humans into sin, the instability of one already outwardly baptized but not yet inwardly reborn, and similar notions can readily attach themselves here — as when, for instance, Schelling himself calls the understanding "fallen reason." One can also see, in the further deduction, the one-sidedness immediately emerging in that Schelling locates the falling-away in the sensuous. This stunting of the in-itself speculative concept of the heteron makes it incomprehensible how a falling-away could come about at all, how the sensuous could have the power for it — and the old mystics were more perceptive when they located the Fall first in created spirits, an insight that Schelling seems to have made up for later. Matter now becomes for him the element of separation, the principle of differentiation, and a wholly new conception of nature necessarily arises from this. The ancients, the Platonists especially, had already conceived matter as the non-existent, as the ungodly — a view that some Christian sects adopted and even carried out in practice to the crudest fleshly aberrations. Schelling now for the first time conceived matter as the negative of spirit, spirit as the negative of matter, whereas earlier he had always reveled only in the thought of their harmony. A dark shadow fell from here onto nature for him. Matter became the clouding, darkening malignancy, and we know to what monstrosities this opinion can proceed. Such things already occur among the Cartesians, in Arnold Geulincx, in Malebranche. But in our century the notion that nature, that matter, stands opposed to spirit with demonic seduction has been driven to its highest pitch. The Hellenic spirit departed from Schelling; he could no longer say, with Schiller's cheerful words:

And the sun of Homer, behold, it smiles

on us too!

Whereas in the academic lectures he had spoken quite generally of things to which the Ideas allotted souls, he now for the first time ventured upon a genesis of the subjective spirit — though only in the wholly abstract manner of letting the producing agency of the Ideas, as soul, sink itself into finitude, and then speaking, in Platonizing fashion, of the reproduction of the archetypes of the Ideas in the soul, which must work its way through the ambiguous intermediate being of matter to the intuition of light and break through the fetters of sensuousness. According to Schelling, the soul, in its selfhood as the highest potency of being-for-itself, bears at once the point of furthest distance from the divine center and, simultaneously, the turning point of return to it through the negation of selfhood. There are indeed true determinations contained in these turns of phrase — that cannot be denied — but on the one hand one can enjoy them in purer form, in their original shape, in Plato's Phaedo and elsewhere, and on the other hand it is precisely this form which, just as knowledge is in the process of forming itself, cancels it again and makes it waver. This is especially the case with the immortality of the soul. Schelling declares the wish for individual continuance to be a product of finitude, a misapprehension of the eternity of spirit, such an immortality being merely a

continued mortality. And yet he again speaks so enigmatically of the wanderings and future state of the soul, and again seems to make it depend so much on the soul's relation to the Ideas, that with him even less than with Hegel does one know where one stands, if one seeks certainty not from the context of the whole but from individual pronouncements. In thinking immortality as an already-present quality of spirit, Schelling is in full agreement with Hegel.

Schelling here repeats his view of history as a successively unfolding revelation of God, in which, however, he now distinguishes only two periods — one of humanity's greatest distance from its center, and another of the return to it. With great frankness he again puts forward his assurances about the beginning of history, that present-day humanity enjoyed the tutelage of higher natures, who scattered, in seed-form, everything of custom, religion, art, and science that still exists; only the original race possessed in unconscious splendor what the later race, scattered into individual colors and rays, links together with consciousness. These absurdities — having science in an unconscious manner, and falling from a state of completion into one of imperfection — have long passed for profundities, because narrow-minded theologians believed they could find in them a support for their orthodoxy. In their unreasoning zeal they failed to consider that neither the Bible nor the symbolic books of any Christian confession speak of such splendor in the arts and sciences. The Bible depicts Adam and Eve in such childlikeness that the Elohim themselves make clothes for them. Of the angels who later have dealings with the human race, moreover, there is no talk at all of especially intelligent and wise beings; rather these children of heaven lusted after the flesh of the daughters of men and begot children with them. The symbolic books do indeed contain the doctrine of justitia concreata, but contain no depictions of man's primitive state in its particulars; it was Cain, according to the Bible, who, fleeing to the land of Nod, first built cities, invented arts, and so on.

But the main thing Schelling is driving at only comes fully to light at the end, namely the assertion of the inseparability of philosophy from morality and religion — for what is supposed to represent the proof of this identity is very meager and constantly evades criticism through the poetry of its terminology. One main turn that occurs here — that the recognition of fate as the identity of freedom and necessity is the first step toward morality — is also found, incidentally, as Hegel's tenth thesis when he habilitated in Jena in 1801: Principium scientiae moralis est reverentia Fato habenda. Now, according to Schelling, religion can exist only in the form of mystery, because God stands to nature only in an indirect relation, while the state in turn is a second nature. Paganism and Christianity, he holds, have always belonged together; in the latter, the mysteries of the former have merely become exoteric, just as the Christian sacraments and many practices of Christianity are manifest imitations of those that had prevailed in the mysteries. Exoteric religion is always polytheism, esoteric religion monotheism. In the mysteries, mythology, as a symbolism of the Ideas, represents the origin and falling-away of the soul, the sacrifice of its selfhood, and its return to the Eternal. Those who recognize this history of the soul in and for itself, without any figurative veil — the autoptai, the philosophers — also recognize perfectly the essence of morality and the destiny of the human race, and must therefore be the heads of state; a conclusion that, given how much this little tract has borrowed from Plato, will not further surprise us. And this whole Platonism is also merely a murky and diffuse paraphrase of the beautiful conclusion of Hegel's often-mentioned treatise, in which the philosopher's liberation from finitude, the Socratic death, is finally portrayed as the soul's journey not only to Eleusis but also to the eternal Father, after Hades has been revealed to it as the place that withholds the eternal good, the ultimate knowledge. Even the king of the gods is unable to dissolve this unbreakable chain; but he permits the soul to rejoice in the lost good through the forms which the ray of eternal light, by the soul's mediation, wrests from the dark womb of the depths."

To rightly understand this polemical tract, one must bear in mind that so many relations, no longer at all present to us, are interwoven into the personal relationship of two philosophers — one of whom had originally regarded the other as his pupil, while the other now regarded this his former master as a robber of his ideas — that it is hard for us, who can only artificially transpose ourselves back into that past, to be fair. In particular we must not forget what kind of gossip in letters and anecdotal tale-bearing was practiced, especially at the smaller universities, above all once definite rivalries stood opposed to one another. The only point that Schelling discussed in a somewhat speculative manner was the concept of existence. Fichte in the Guide had distinguished being and knowledge such that he called knowledge the "there-being" (Dasein) of being. Schelling, against this, brought forward the concept of existence as that which forms the unity, the bond, of an essence as a One with itself as a Many. We thus see Schelling, even while combating Fichte, nevertheless arriving through him at a new category. His speculative thought in general always remained consistent with itself, never an empty, merely intellectual unity, but always a unity that distinguishes itself from itself and returns from the difference into itself as its ground. He had consistently, as a matter of principle, set himself against abstract dualism as well as abstract identity, and thereby earned great credit. But because he never worked through the abstract forms of the Idea in their interconnection, he wandered gradually around their circumference, elevating now this, now that triplicity onto the throne of absoluteness. The interest in observing him here is chiefly a phenomenological one — to see how the richness of the Idea forces him onward to ever new forms, and how, in this respect, the change in Schelling is not merely a formal alteration, but also reveals a further determination at work within it, one that holds him in its grip and compels him to complete his fate and finally to declare willing to be the essence of the Absolute. As long as he was concerned with the contemplation of nature, Spinozism sufficed him, and he translated the distinction between natura naturans and natura naturata into manifold forms, as positive and negative force, as production and product, and so forth. But since he had, through the system of transcendental idealism, passed over into the domain of spirit,

those forms of forces and processes no longer sufficed. First there appeared the wholly abstract reflective form of identity, difference, and their unity in totality; then that of the infinite, the finite, and their unity in the eternal; then that of essence, form, and their absolute merging into one; then that of the center, the peripheral point, its striving toward eccentricity, and its return into unity with the center; now that of the One, the Many, and their unity in existence. The One as a unit turns of itself into the Many, the Many of itself into its opposite, the unity of the many as a One that mutually excludes itself. The true is therefore not the One as a mere unit; not the Many summed merely into a unity as one; but the One as the copula of unity and multiplicity. The unity that just as much posits as it cancels the difference must itself be distinguished from the unity insofar as it forms the reflective opposite of difference or of the Many. Schelling said on p. 56: "Existence is the bond of an essence as a One with itself as a Many. But is there indeed an existence? The eternal answer to this question is God, for God is, and God is being itself. "We can express the bond essentially as the infinite love of itself (which in all things is the highest), as infinite delight in revealing itself — save that the essence of the Absolute is not to be thought as distinct from this delight, but as precisely this willing-of-itself. This very self-affirmation is, regardless of form, the intrinsically infinite, which therefore can never become finite into nothing. — The Absolute, however, is not merely a willing of itself, but a willing in an infinite manner, that is, in all forms, degrees, and potencies of reality. The imprint of this eternal and infinite self-willing is the world. But if in this imprint that is the world we look to what it has of the bond, and by which it is like it — the positive in it — and not to its inessential properties, then it is not different from the Absolute itself, but only the complete copula, spread out in progressive development."

But we must return once more to that "Exposition," because Schelling's later conduct toward Hegel obliges us not to leave his conduct toward Fichte out of account. Schelling states the most excellent principles regarding the distinction between the private and public affairs of a writer; he regulatively abhors the mingling of morality into criticism; he wishes to have nothing to do with personality, everything only with the matter itself. But we see here too how it goes with passion. It makes one act contrary to the finest theories, even while one is preaching them. The upshot of the whole polemic amounts to depicting Fichte as an idealess word-artist who, with the utmost self-satisfaction, wishes to force the public, through his tedious disquisitions, to understand his handful of thoughts, and who, tormenting himself around with base desires in moral anguish, avenges this torment — by denying and disavowing it in nature, and against his opponents through the most shameful slanders. Unable to refute them, he indeed does not shrink from robbing them besides, down to the most dishonorable plagiarism. Schelling, who had himself already several times cleaned out Augean stables and adequately depicted the crapulence of the literary rabble, forgets this so thoroughly in his passion, on p. 117, that he says in a note: "Into what recesses of base-mindedness, one might ask, must the man have looked, whose bitterness can draw such a picture of his opponents as that in the Characteristics?" This address is, stylistically, the most perfect thing that has flowed from Schelling's pen. One can read it again and again without ever tiring of it. Such beauty of form, however, is never possible without an adequate soundness of content. This, however, does not exclude the presence of errors and other deficiencies of insight. But we must guard against letting our insight into these spoil the enjoyment of what is genuinely excellent — as so easily happens with narrow-minded people, who graze on the self-feeling of their superiority in uttering blame, and who, to keep this titillation alive in themselves, generally prefer to blame rather than to praise. And just as little as we have let polemic rest in our critique, just as little do we wish to deny apology its due, and we gladly recognize this address as a genuine masterpiece. Its title, admittedly, is poorly chosen. It should read: Some Ideas on Nature and Art. For there is little talk of the plastic arts as such. Whoever expects to learn how architecture, sculpture, and painting relate to nature will be disappointed. Art is treated quite generally, and there are really three points that Schelling discusses:

⁠1) the relation of art to nature as an imitation of the latter by the former;

⁠2) the relation of the ideal to the characteristic;

⁠3) the sequence of stages of the severe, the graceful, and the soulful form in the development of nature and art.

These problems had been much debated since Lessing's Laocoön and Winckelmann's History of Art. Goethe, in the Propyläen, had sought to do much to correct these concepts, and in his 1805 biography of Winckelmann a certain conclusion of the whole movement had been reached, whose calm also pervades Schelling's treatise. Schelling determined the first relation such that not the dead reproduction of nature can be the essence of art, but rather the producing of the Idea that presents itself in nature. And likewise a merely mechanical equilibrium is by no means the ideal of art, but rather the measured taming of the life-filled urge — just as, in ethical matters, it is not the mere absence of passions that constitutes perfect character, but the wise mastery of desires and inclinations. Nature, like art, showed in its history precisely this progress from the shaping of the fermenting content into the severity of form, until at last, in perfect unity with it, the soul radiates through and through. In this section the most splendid depictions occur, for example:

"In nature and art, essence strives first for the realization, or presentation, of itself in the particular. Hence the greatest severity of form shows itself in the beginnings of both: for without limitation the limitless could not appear; were there no hardness, mildness could not be, and if unity is to become perceptible, this can happen only through particularity, separation, and conflict. At the beginning, therefore, the creative spirit appears wholly lost in form, inaccessible, closed off, and even in greatness still harsh. But the more it succeeds in uniting its whole fullness in a single creature, the more it gradually relaxes its severity, and once it has fully developed the form, so that it rests satisfied within it and grasps itself — it becomes, as it were, cheered, and begins to move in gentle lines. This is the state of the most beautiful ripeness and bloom, where the pure vessel stands complete, the nature-spirit becomes free of its bonds, and feels its kinship with the soul. As through a gentle dawn-glow rising over the whole form, the coming soul announces itself: it is not yet there, but everything prepares itself, through the quiet play of delicate movements, for its reception. The rigid outlines melt and soften into gentle ones. A lovely being, neither sensuous nor spiritual but ungraspable, spreads itself over the form, and nestles into every contour of every movement of the limbs. This — as said, ungraspable yet felt by all — is what the language of the Greeks designates by the name Charis, and ours as grace."

Toward the end of the treatise Schelling speaks a little of the difference between sculpture and painting, and wishes to give, from the history of Italian painting, an example of his theory of the developmental course of art, taking Winckelmann's distinction of the severe, the high, and the pleasing style as his model. According to his theory, Michelangelo was to have the character of severity, Correggio and, even more, Raphael that of grace, Guido Reni that of soul. Schelling here excuses himself by saying that, while this may not correspond exactly in chronology, it does so in substance; but in the fourth note he also wants to justify the matter chronologically, because although Michelangelo painted the Last Judgment in the Sistine Chapel only after Raphael's death, his style had already been born before him and alongside him. Schelling's theory is not wrong in that it separates the severe style from the graceful one. But it is wrong in that it wants to see soul, as he puts it, as a new — though not higher — stage of art. For instead of soul he ought to have recognized the pleasing/charming as the dissolution back down of the ideal beauty that had been attained. Schelling says quite correctly that art, at its summit, runs back through the earlier moments — naturally as mere moments of formation. But it is just as necessary that art, after reaching its summit, in the further progress that inaugurates the history of its decline, presents these same moments, but in an inverted modification. The simply sublime becomes the colossal; grace begins to become coquettish; the naive necessity of exhausting a subject becomes the calculation and accumulation of the various possibilities it offers to the understanding relation. And so Schelling's example was bound to prove inadequate as well.

Instead of Michelangelo he ought to have looked at the earlier painters Giotto, Orcagna, Cimabue, Perugino, Fiesole — and above all, if he wanted to name one all-encompassing name, Leonardo da Vinci, whom he presents as a transitional stage from Michelangelo to Correggio. With Vinci the striving for severity, for precision of form, came to a close. With him harshness softened in every direction. Raphael, however, whom Schelling also calls a philosopher and poet — which

Michelangelo, in the proper sense of the word, was in a higher degree — was not only graceful but also soulful; and, half in jest, one might cite as proof that he indeed painted, unsurpassed, the story of Psyche. Michelangelo, for his part, already went beyond the soft ideal forms; he already passed over into the athletic. Daring postures, fullness and power of the limbs, entanglement of groups, manifold contrast of figures — these attracted him. Correggio was therefore his true opposite, in whom, just as in Angelo greatness began to shade over into the colossal, grace began to shade over into a melting dissolution. Correggio loves, as precisely his most famous works — his Io, Antiope, Leda — show, unusual postures, but lovely, softly swelling, sweet forms, twilight-toned lighting. The reaction against the philosophy of nature had vented itself in a parody: Aphorisms on the Absolute. The suspicion that Aenesidemus Schulze had a hand in this satire — which some, in good faith, even wanted to introduce as a textbook of natural philosophy in the secondary schools — moved Schelling, at I, 1, p. 22, to refer him to Hegel's essay on skepticism; but it also seemed necessary to him to give, under the very same title, a genuine outline of his own system. He therefore wrote:

Aphorisms as an Introduction to the Philosophy of Nature. I, 1, pp. 1-88; 2, pp. 1-36; II, 1, pp. 121-158...

In it Schelling treated: of unity and allness; of reason as knowledge of the Absolute; of the incurability of rational knowledge; of the way in which unity is allness and allness is unity, and of the eternal non-being of the finite; of the qualitative differences in the universe (namely gravity and light); of the essence of nature, the reality of things, matter and motion; and finally of the infinity and freedom of nature in individuality and in the connection of things, or on the essence of gravity and light. — Even from this listing it is clear that here we encounter nothing but old acquaintances. Apart from the aphoristic form and the often oddly solemn diction, there is nothing new to learn here in substance. In his manner, Schelling again emphatically singles out one category, that of unity and allness, above the rest; toward the end he draws a great deal, often verbatim, from Spinoza, particularly for the concept of time, duration and motion; empiricism is spoken of as a religious act. In general, there prevails here an abuse of this expression and too great an indulgence with the predicate of divinity. What, finally, in the broadest sense, is not to be called divine! The philosopher, precisely for that reason, should not be too liberal with this noble word. Reason is declared to be indescribable. Schelling makes it easy for himself and leaves it to everyone to define it for themselves. I, 1, p. 12, §31: "Reason cannot be described to anyone: it must describe itself in everyone and through everyone." Against this, however, without any connection to this definition that defines nothing, it is further stated in §32: "Sense is divine in that it grasps the particular, yet each on its own, as if nothing existed outside it, like a world of its own. Unconsciously it beholds a present infinity; it thus beholds in each thing allness, but without dissolution back into unity. — Hence the unfathomableness in everything sensuous, the chaos, the confused fullness. Sense is to be regarded as equal to religion."

When one calmly analyzes such assurances, they dissolve into nothing. What is meant by saying that sense is to be regarded as equal to religion is hard to grasp and could only be brought to an apparent intelligibility through overly clever analogies. Schelling had earlier declared that he could only engage with those who actually had a system and adhered to method; now, on p. 7, he stated that it was a matter of indifference for philosophy whether it presented its enthusiasm lyrically, epically, or dramatically, in freer or more determinate form, so long as the material were only drawn from infinity." He himself, it seems, did not feel how disjointed these aphorisms were, how they floated off into the vague, for he said on p. 11: "§29. For a long time I have set up iron and bow before opponents and others, to see whether they could shoot through: what follows will show whether they have managed to bend the bow."

On the whole, the Introduction breathes an enormous self-regard. Not only are opponents thoroughly given a dressing-down, but friends and students too are rebuked with much wit and lordly rudeness back into the confines of their feeling of dependence. Schelling is especially averse to rhetorical embellishment. He wants a school, yes, but the way there were schools of poets. He promises his school the unifying Homer. He rebukes the mere imitators: "§26. Those whose consciousness most condemns them to being pupils cry loudest about the compulsion of the school, and self-seeking place-hunters of every kind plant themselves in natural philosophy just as the insolent suitors did in the house of Odysseus: no wonder if, at last, even impudent beggars — poorer in spirit than Irus was in wealth — challenge to a fistfight the very man from whose table they still keep devouring the scraps."

Strange, and indeed, through its vagueness, quite flat and vacuous, is how Schelling describes himself. P. 8, §19: "Of what do I boast? Of this one thing: that it was given to me to proclaim the divinity even of the individual, the possible equality of all knowledge without distinction of object, and thereby the infinity of philosophy."

The prophetic unction which Schelling cultivated in this journal was nevertheless bound, since it was devoted to medicine, always to contain a panegyric of nature — an ambition which led him into strange assertions and at times pressed him down below the standpoint he had already attained in the essay Philosophy and Religion. Thus he asserted on p. 9, §22, that religion and philosophy must be in harmony. Good. But then he affirmed that the religion of the philosopher has the color of nature; that it is the vigorous religion of one who descends with bold courage into the depths of nature, not the hermit-like religion of idle self-contemplation, and so on. Such generalities Schelling drove to the very edge of braggadocio, especially in the introduction to the Miscellanies, which contain a species of comparative physiology and zoology (after Oken), where he even says on p. 285: "For heaven's sake, do not speak of science, since you have scarcely scratched the surface. No one has yet had the heart to behold the truth exactly as it is."

This latter expression was hardly quite fitting, since under these "matters" nothing less than God, religion, and history must be understood. If we said that Schelling's address on the relation of the plastic arts to nature was the most beautiful thing he wrote, we must say that this essay was the most profound thing to which he ever rose. By his own account it was meant to carry further the ideal part of his philosophy. The concept of freedom, namely, is the one that at the same time has an absolutely central significance. It was high time, he said, that the opposition of necessity and freedom be considered as the innermost center of philosophy.

Schelling had to arrive at that definition, for in his wandering from category to category he had exhausted everything: the I, being, force, indifference, identity, the merging into one of the real and the ideal, art, religion, philosophy, reason, God, unity and allness, actuality — what still remained? Philosophy. The State.

But he had still presented the Absolute itself as the absolutely undetermined, out of which indeed all potencies proceed, but which is itself potency-less, since it can fall under no limitation, whereas potency, as a particularization, necessarily has other potencies opposed to it. Now he grasped the Absolute as will in such a way that this will, in itself undetermined, determines itself to what it wills to be. With this, Schelling tore himself entirely loose from Spinozism. According to Spinoza, God must act ex necessitate naturae; he cannot be other than he is. Schelling set, in place of necessity, freedom in God — the ability to determine his own being-thus for himself. But given his naive nature, he had to do this unmediated. He simply left his philosophy of nature lying there. He ignored himself and left it to his critics to deal with his shed snakeskin, to construct him, and to bring him into connection with himself. Non-derivation, that is, the postulating of the beginning, was once and for all his unmethodical method.

But for this essay, whose standpoint Schelling never subsequently went beyond, we must recall how he had solemnly promised, against Fichte, to study the visionaries he had overlooked not out of contempt but only out of blameworthy negligence — whose simplicity, unity with God, and wisdom he had already so eagerly praised. This promise he fulfilled. He studied Jakob Böhme. With him he shared the grasping of speculative cognition as a kind of immediate intuition, except that in Schelling the ecstatic moment fell away which showed itself initially in Böhme. Further, he shared with him the mixture of abstract and sensuous forms, a jumble of logical determinacy and fanciful embellishment. Further still, the striving to conceive the negative as a moment immanent in the Absolute itself, sublated by it as an eternal non-being, μὴ ὄν. Böhme is full of pregnant expressions for the negative — wrath, anger, harshness, torment, prickliness, roughness, discord, and so on. Without its counter-throw, according to him, the eternal One would never arrive at any impression. The will of the groundless, out of which everything originates, becomes sensible in its self-grasping only through the power of its counterpart, the many. Schelling absorbed this doctrine deeply into himself, and this unashamedness in recognizing dualism not as something alien and accidental to the Absolute, but as its own inherent malady, which it eternally overcomes within itself into harmony, is genuinely speculative — and it redounds to Schelling's credit that even in Berlin he did not hold back from it, but expounded it in his Satanology. If one wished to press the comparison with Böhme further, one might also mention the latter's polemical force, which he made his opponents feel so keenly — Pastor Richter, Herr Stifel, and so on. Schelling in his essay often moves entirely within Böhme's and Baader's terminology, but names only the latter, and otherwise almost only Leibniz.

The development of the concept of freedom led Schelling, if one extracts the main points from his rather disorderly presentation, to a determination of: 1, the essence of God; 2, creation; 3, the initial history and the opposition, coinciding with it, of the appearance of good and evil. On the first point, Schelling remained consistent with his earlier determinations in that he opposed to the predicateless One, the self-affirming, the negation of another force. The difference, however, was that he now posited the One as will. Formerly he had only ever spoken of the Absolute as the being free from all distinctions and limitations; now he also defined it positively as will, love, goodness. But now it would be incomprehensible how the One, the Absolute identical with itself, should arrive at the creation of a world unless there existed in it — in itself independent of it — its Other, which alone makes it possible for it to spread itself out into the multiplicity of distinctions.

The dark ground, nature in God, is not God himself, for he is the One, the will of the groundless; but the ground is in God the element conditioning his actualization. It is not a being dualistically opposed to God, which with specific heterogeneity relates itself against

to his own essence in an absolutely negative way. It is not a principle, only a condition presupposed by God himself, without which he could not be personal, for personality, according to Schelling, must always involve the union of an ideal principle with a real basis. Schelling expressly declares that he teaches no Manichaeism. The dark ground is not to be thought of as the cause of God's existence, but only as the basis of his existing. Schelling holds that being in itself and the activity of being — of seeing itself as existing — must be distinguished. One should not, on account of the expressions, reject Schelling's view outright, in the manner so often done, of expressing great astonishment at how a person could arrive at such strange assertions about a dark ground in God. Schelling, as throughout this essay, has also with this concept hit upon a real problem, namely that of the distinction of God from himself. He grasps this distinguishing, however, from the side on which it is, as the distinction, as otherness, something determinate in itself. In this determinacy he isolates it through the representation of a nature, of a ground — not merely a ground, but a dark ground. These designations arise in him out of a

## The Lack of Logical Sharpness

lack of logical rigor. Hence he must afterward also describe the negation of the negation — the seeing of the distinction as no distinction — in forms that belong to the imagination, and speak of struggle, of overcoming the ground, and the like.

Without the ground, the Absolute would remain within itself. Through it, it is stirred to go forth out of itself. It cannot be compelled, for its own most proper essence is willing; as being it is will; willing cannot be separated from its being, but whatever else may be said of it, every determination is subsumed under that of the will. The ground, however, arouses its longing to bring itself forth into birth. Schelling's metaphysical category here is actually the distinction of potentiality and actuality. Nature in God is the force reacting against his simple essence; the negation of this reaction is the essence of God stepping forth in act out of its concealment into itself: thus it is the self-revealing. Schelling here makes particular use of Böhme's designations for becoming sensible, for the counter-throw, and so on. God now, through actualization, becomes really existent. He sublates both his own essence and the essence of the ground in his manifestation, and, as creating, is the copula of the forces — whereby we may well recall that Schelling, against Fichte, had defined existence as the unity of unity and multiplicity, this unity as actuality, and actuality as God.

Creation is accordingly the constant negation of the negation of the reacting ground. It is therefore timeless in itself, but appearing in time and essentially historical, insofar as the divine universal will seeks to manifest itself ever more, the longer it goes, in the interpenetration of the dark ground. All deformity, ugliness, noxiousness, sickness, malice, irrationality are, in and for themselves, nothing in the eternal essence of God, but in the appearing world they constitute moments of development. The difference of the world from God is sublated in itself, and therefore it becomes so continually. The principle of negativity, to be sure, is again wrapped by Schelling in very murky forms. He opposes to the universal will the particular will of the dark ground and the individual will of the creature — though he also confuses these latter two, since it is hard to say, and even harder to see, how the dark ground should arrive at a will at all, insofar as its own peculiar determinacy is rather the merely being, not-willing negation of the essence of God as the primal will. Schelling speaks of a will of the ground, which is supposed to be something intermediate between consciousness and unconsciousness, the mere impulse of a becoming nature, and thus can only improperly be called will. In the individual, whose ensoulment is the deed of the primal will, abstraction from unity with the All becomes possible. This abstraction is, positively, the self-affirming selfhood, which, however, in excluding its essence from itself, necessarily sets itself in contradiction with itself and with everything that it is not.

Here Schelling becomes wholly mystical. He is still quite right to see in man the completion of nature, the equilibrium finally attained of all its forces. But he assumes that, through man's becoming evil, the bond of forces, as he says, fell into disorder. Indeed, he perceives in God himself a "source of sadness," and over all the works of nature he sees a "secret melancholy" spread out. He attributes the existing irrationality, the never-resolved remainder of unreason in everything existent, to the dark ground in God, insofar as its reaction against the shaping by the love of the divine universal will still always leaves a brand-mark of its resistance in everything creaturely. Yet he also, quite definitely, again presents sin as the cause of the negative forms and processes of nature. But this derivation of evil from sin is no derivation at all. It posits something incomprehensible, namely that existence, unconscious in itself, should be altered not only in man — for that is comprehensible — but also outside him, through an absolutely spiritual act. It naturally does not help to wrap this incomprehensibility in words which, like the term "magical," are only another formula for the same thing. According to Schelling's theory one must remain in doubt as to which irrationality of nature is to be derived from the struggle of God with the dark ground, and which from the crisis of the fall into sin; and to this question he would probably answer that this cannot be distinguished empirically at all. But what he would answer to the question of which residues in nature remain unresolved, which are such alogical incommensurabilities — what he would answer to that, especially as against his own earlier philosophy of nature, I do not dare to say. Philosophy can point to no such existences without falling into the conceptless sentimentality of which Fichte was guilty in his Vocation of Man, and which Hegel so ridiculed in the Critical Journal — that still hurricanes lay waste to gardens and forests, hail and frost devastate the fields, beasts devour each other and man, and so on.

In describing evil, Schelling made particular use of the image, so favored by Baader, of the peripheral point that wants to make itself the center, of the organ that wants to make itself the total organism. There is nothing to be said against such figuration, provided it attaches itself to a logically conducted investigation as a means of clarification. In Schelling, however, it takes up too much space. It crowds forward, with its retinue of sensuous representations, too early and too broadly. Some check on this is still provided by the reproduction of the Kantian doctrine of radical evil. Schelling wants to raise this same doctrine to its completion by regarding whether someone is good or evil as the consequence and continuation of an act that occurred outside all time, in the first creation. This act is supposed to be a deed of freedom. Judas, for example, according to this had to betray Christ, yet was not compelled to it. Schelling will hear nothing of a predestination to blessedness or damnation by God. Man is supposed to have determined himself to this in an eternal manner. Indeed, not only to this, but also to the manner of his corporealization. And not only to this, but also to the possibility of passing over from evil to good, of being able to become good out of evil through the help of divine transmutation.

Kant had conceived the intelligible ground of evil in man as a radical propensity to evil preceding every determinate evil deed, and had thereby opposed philanthropism, which asserted the goodness of man by nature. In this lies the entirely correct point that immediate existence, natural willing, is unsuited to the concept of spirit and must be sublated. Kant therefore, through his doctrine, gave the impetus to a deeper understanding of Christianity, which is grounded entirely on the presupposition that man, in his immediacy, is to be grasped as standing in contradiction with his true concept. Kant distinguished actual sin, imputable as a deed, from the general propensity to evil, and pronounced the beautiful words that every human being, before every evil deed, finds himself in the paradise of guiltlessness. Kant wanted to conceive of evil neither as the consequence of an

unfortunate accident, nor as mere weakness, as a mere defect — as the supranaturalists did in the former, holding fast to the history of the Fall, and as the Rousseauian neologists did in the latter. Schelling transformed this Kantian theory into a mythology, by regarding the pre-existence of the deed that decides man's fate as an act coinciding, through his will, with the genesis of his existence. The determination of willing according to its eternal, absolute significance is here indeed correct, but the form in which he presents it leads to the impossibility of having to think knowing and willing as at once unconscious and will-less. Before I even exist, I have already qualified my existence; before I even distinguish good from evil, I have already willed the one or the other! Schelling, amid these contradictions, does not consider at all that the actual condition of most human beings is a jumble of good and evil, by no means a fixed direction toward only the one or the other. Now if it were true, as he assumes, that everyone had already, in a time immemorial being, predetermined even his own rebirth, this would in the end amount to nothing other than a fatalism, whose predestination would be an even far more incomprehensible one than the Pauline-Augustinian.

It must give the appearance of schoolmasterish pedantry to reproach Schelling so often for his lack of logical clarity and order, and yet this reproach is unavoidable. He sees very well the errors of others and censures them with pointed sharpness. He touches here too on all the oppositions of indeterminism and determinism, of God's foreknowledge and forewilling, of pantheism and theism, and so on, but he does not develop them in their determinate antithesis so as to let the result of the self-negation of the contradiction emerge from them. He wants to proceed "conversationally," but only as it were, that is, he wants to discuss now this point, now that, without disposition. He himself says on p. 508 that only division — that is, science and dialectic — will be what "holds fast that system, more often present than we think, yet ever again escaped, hovering before all of us and still fully grasped by none, and will bring it to knowledge forever." He speaks excellently of an "unchastity and shamelessness in cognition, a kind of faunish lust that toys with everything, without earnestness and without love, to form or shape anything." And Schelling has certainly not lacked striving after ever higher perfection in presentation; nearly every book, every essay of his takes a new run-up. But the calm and endurance of consistent, thorough elaboration has indeed been lacking in him, and so he has wavered back and forth between the abstractions of scholasticism and the fancies of poetry. He therefore often sinks down to an entirely unphilosophical popularity. For instance, where he speaks of the self-activation of the good or evil principle being the consequence of the intelligible deed by which the essence and life of man is determined, he says on p. 473: "Hence in the man in whom that transmutation has not yet occurred, but in whom the good principle has also not entirely died out, the inner voice of his own better essence — better, that is, in relation to him as he now is — never ceases to summon him to it, just as, only through the actual and decisive turning-about, does he find peace in his own inner self and, as though only now the original idea had been satisfied, finds himself reconciled with his guardian spirit." Such expressions as "inner voice," "the dying-out of the good," "guardian spirit," and so on are too loose, too figurative for philosophy. It comes down to whether we find such things as a mere offshoot, or whether they are stated with a claim to speculative significance.

Let us go back and recall that Schelling had begun to call the Absolute first reason, then God, and to distinguish from God the world of ideas, but in the realization of the latter, the falling-away of the appearing world from God; let us further recall that Hegel had meanwhile, in 1807, in the Phenomenology of Spirit, determined spirit as the Absolute in such a way that it, as absolute substance, is at the same time absolute subject, and thus the unity of Spinozism and Fichteanism — then we cannot at all doubt that Schelling's essay on freedom actually has the concept of spirit as its task. Beyond the concept of spirit lies no higher concept; reason in it is predicate; it has reason. Of the I, of self-consciousness, of reason, of God, Schelling had already spoken; of spirit, not yet. Spirit, as absolute, is that which eternally externalizes itself out of itself into its otherness, and which, just as eternally, returns and has returned out of this otherness into itself, in that it posits not only negatively the distinction from itself, but also positively the re-unity of what is distinguished with itself. Schelling had indeed determined the Absolute as the identity of object and subject, but not as the identity of subject and substance. This is the true reason why, at the outset of his essay, Schelling gives a critique of Spinoza and Fichte and the declaration that he wishes to unite in his system both realism and idealism, neither of which had been able to arrive at a personal God.

Only thus can one explain the torments with which Schelling struggles to go beyond both Christianity and Hegel — who agree in determining the Absolute as spirit; only thus the remarks about subject, predicate and copula, in which the old profound logic is praised for its doctrine of antecedent and consequent; only thus the expression of the One will in place of subject, of the dark ground, of nature in place of substance, and the emphasis on the abstractum "freedom" instead of the concretum "spirit." Thus it is stated on p. 496: "Even spirit is not yet the highest; it is only the spirit or breath of love. But love is the highest. It is that which was, before ground and before the existent were as separated, but was not yet as love, but — how shall we designate it?"

This breaking-off of the description is highly characteristic here. On p. 500 it is stated: "Above spirit is the original groundlessness, which is no longer indifference, indifference, and yet not the identity of both principles, but the universal unity, equal toward everything and yet seized by nothing, the beneficence free from everything and yet working through everything, in one word, love, which is all in all."

Of reason, however, Schelling said, comparing it to the primum passivum of the mystics, on p. 509: "It is not activity, like spirit, not the absolute identity of the two principles of cognition, but indifference; the measure and, as it were, the universal locus of truth, the quiet abode in which original wisdom is received."

Schelling never came to terms with his own earlier determinations of these concepts. He refers a few times to his work Philosophy and Religion, and to his historical construction of Christianity in the academic lectures, but without noting the changes made, or rather that had occurred, in his doctrine. Thus, on pp. 459-61, the theory of history is again an entirely different one from that given in those lectures. Christ here is not the culmination of the ancient world of gods, but the appearance of the higher light of spirit, which had been in the world from the beginning, but, uncomprehended by the darkness working for itself,

Of the apostolic age, Schelling assumes a religious somnambulism to be its character.

The preface is again excellent in its tendency and brilliant in style. One must feel well disposed toward Schelling when one sees him attending to the interest of literature with such zeal and such circumspection. Eschenmayer wished to restore faith to precedence over speculative knowledge, protested against the unity of the divine with the human, against the assumption of the comprehensibility of God, against the conception of the earth as the center-point of the whole universe on which alone the history of spirit realizes itself. Schelling had, in the essay, displayed the most decided rationalism. He had said on p. 506: "We hold, with Lessing, that the development of revealed truths into truths of reason is absolutely necessary if the human race is to be given hope thereby. Heaven rests in every way upon hell, and this is a proposition that can be made evident to anyone. Heaven is the highest concord, hell the discord of forces. Living concord is discord overcome and subdued. If God is to live in a human being, the devil must die in him, just as, conversely, you say: in the human being who is without God, the devil dwells."

Schelling finished his rebuttal against Eschenmayer in April 1812. On December 13, 1811, he signed in Munich the preface to another polemical work which likewise elucidated, in many respects, the system set forth in the treatise on the essence of human freedom. For F. H. Jacobi had, in his work "On Divine Things and Their Revelation" (1811), accused Schelling's philosophy of abolishing the concept of a personal God, of destroying the distinction between physical and moral necessity, the distinction between good and evil, and of leaving nothing but nature behind. Against this Jacobi now preached faith in the one living and holy, yet incomprehensible, God, and in the dignity of human freedom and virtue, all the more ardently the less he himself had ever felt the need of a demonstrative, systematically articulated philosophy. This accusation against speculation is the most common one: as soon as philosophy deals with the idea, with the absolute, with substance, and so forth, representational thought loses its footing. It feels uneasy. It feels abandoned by the warmth of the God who stands opposite it as father, counselor, judge, and so on, and accuses philosophy of a vampirism that annihilates religion and puts the icy cold of the heartless concept in the place of love, of groundless trust, and so on.

This accusation against a philosophy, when made with rhetorical solemnity, with a full painting-out of all the possibilities that might conceivably attach to a real atheism and to the non-distinction of good and evil, rarely fails to produce a frightening impression on the mass of the public. One already sees civil society perishing, throne and altar tottering. The accused philosophy is proclaimed absolutely dangerous, and this predicate of destructive tendency, of undermining the most sacred things, is transferred to the philosophers themselves, as though they, in order to carry through their system, were quite deliberately aiming at this. Such an accusation is also often welcome for the reason that it provides a means of diverting the public's attention from the true, objective causes from which the lack of piety, the striving toward anarchy, religious indifferentism, and dissatisfaction in general not infrequently spring. One seeks to conceal these causes by accusing a philosophy of being their author.

However much one may make excuses for Jacobi, there remains in him the vehemence with which he attacked the philosophy of nature. And for my part he might still have been permitted his vehemence, had he only been scientific at the same time. But this was by no means the case. He fell, as Schelling says, into his genre larmoyant. He declaimed, but he did not prove. If we now consider that Schelling was in Bavarian service, and that Jacobi was president of the Academy of Sciences in Munich, we must grant the former that the accusation of atheism, of the destruction of morality, of naturalism, could well appear personally important enough to him to warrant answering it. The scientific critique of Jacobi had already been carried out before him by Hegel and Fr. Schlegel, and in this respect there often remained to him only a repetition of what these had already said. But his own position with regard to Jacobi's critique of him — or rather, view of him — he could and had to lay out for himself. In doing so he too became passionate to the point of harshness, indeed to the point of cruelty. An image which Hegel, in his work on the difference between Fichte's and Schelling's systems, had used against Reinhold — that his preliminary philosophizing, his beginning before the beginning, resembled a running-start to get across a ditch — this image was spun out at length by Schelling into an allegorical vision. Jacobi appeared on a plain; on a hill separated from it by a ditch the genuine philosophers were assembled. Jacobi wanted to cross over to them. But the fatal ditch! Speeches are made back and forth. The public takes a lively interest. Jacobi throws the waste paper of his writings into the ditch to fill it. He binds a cloth over his eyes, leaps up — and, taking it off again, finds himself on the very same spot. Schelling also delivers him a stern speech, on how character must preserve the philosopher throughout his life, how he must shrink from no exertion, no persecution, how indulgence of feeling, mental idleness, and lawlessness in thinking are incompatible with the seriousness of speculation, and ends by telling him that he is not only a sophist but also a sycophant. Jacobi tries to fetch out a few masks in order to appear to the public as the venerable and noble one. But they are broken and torn to pieces, whereupon he breaks out into lamentation over how he is misjudged and how his good intentions are misinterpreted — quite as we are accustomed to see from people who regard everything as permitted to themselves, who spare no one's person, but then, once their deeds become manifest and are not even rewarded quite according to merit,

fall into a frenzy and complain to God and the world of the supposed injustice done to them."

The title of this polemical work reads: J. W. J. Schelling's Monument to the Work "On Divine Things" of Herr Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi, and to the Accusation Made against Him Therein of a Deliberately Deceptive, Lie-Speaking Atheism. (Motto: "Ah, alas, sorrow! — matters have already come to this, that those who openly confess that they have no idea of God and in no way know God are not ashamed to accuse philosophers of atheism. — Ben. de Spinoza.") Tübingen, 1812. 8vo.

The historical section of the work portrays Jacobi as a worthless man. Reinhold had dismissed Schelling as a feeble mind, Fichte as a mere wordsmith; Jacobi was now supposed to be a sentimental, headless, and malicious babbler all at once. Whoever, in a scientific dispute, presumes so far as to touch upon the innermost being of his opponent — which he does not know, which he was not even capable of knowing, which only God knows — needs nothing further to brand himself than his own act, and whoever still believes in fame and honor, in a judging posterity, will infinitely

prefer to have been attacked in such a manner than to have attacked, even granting that — incredibly enough — the attacker should find means, in his own lifetime, to escape the verdict of the shame due to him."

Let us leave this part of the book, which foams over with the spray of irritation, and see what the second, scientific, section contains. Here we find, in fact, two important points explained: first, the concept of the aseity of God, and second, the concept of becoming in relation to that of being. The first point we today prefer to treat chiefly under the category of immanence and transcendence. Schelling called the standpoint of immanence naturalism, and that of transcendence theism. The former makes God the ground of the world, in such a way that the ground is constantly absorbed into that which it grounds, and thus does not distinguish itself, on its own account, from the grounded. The absolute is accordingly, as essence, identical with its appearance, or rather it is only being. Nature, as uncreated, is all in all; it then has absolutely no prius and no posterius. The human being emerges from it only as its own highest perfection, in which it begins its retrospective view of itself. Theism, on the other hand, makes God the cause of the world, in such a way that he himself remains constantly foreign to its existence. It exists, and is as it is, because he wills its existence and its being-thus. God is given, in relation to the world, the position — if I may put it so — of a merely policing supervision.

Schelling was entirely right to maintain that only the unity of naturalism and theism produces the true concept of God. God is both ground and cause of the world alike. He derives it just as much out of himself, making himself the matter of the universe, positing himself as the ground that sublates itself in appearance, while at the same time positing himself, above what is posited, as its prius and also as its superius. On the one side, in that he does not find the matter of the world ready-made and must therefore have it within himself and determine himself to it, he is ground; on the other side, in that it is he who determines himself to be passive in order to unfold his essence into creation, he is cause, because he distinguishes himself from what is created.

As already noted, this is entirely correct. If in our own day one speaks of an immanence without transcendence, that is entirely unspeculative.

And if, conversely, one exalts transcendence without immanence with all one's might, that is just as unspeculative. Both one-sidednesses contradict the true concept of God and hence also the true essence of Christianity, in which God is known simply as spirit, who does not withhold his essence from the world but on the contrary reveals it therein, while yet also distinguishing himself from the world as the one who is revealed. Hence Christianity grasps the God who has become human, in whom there is a real homoousia of divine and human nature, as one and the same with the eternally-becoming-human Logos. Immanence without transcendence is abstract, that is, it must in the end terminate in naturalism, as indeed we already hear a deification of nature proclaimed anew, and as we see, quite superfluously, the Mirabeau-style system of nature reprinted again in translations. And transcendence is equally abstract when it takes the world not as revelation but merely as a made thing of God. It then estranges God from the world, sets him back into an incomprehensible darkness, turns the world, instead of into a mirror, into an obscuring veil of God, and ends in the spiritlessness of serving God as mere power. And this too we can perceive today among those who, even when they speak of God as the spirit of truth and love, nevertheless, in contradiction to such speech, represent him only as the Lord and preach a terrorism of religion.

Now, in that Schelling posited the unity of ground and cause, of naturalism and theism, in God, there arose the further question of how the world itself was then to be conceived, inasmuch as an aseity cannot be ascribed to it. And in this respect too Schelling was entirely right. He held namely that it does not contradict the concept of God for him, in so far as he reveals himself, to proceed out of himself from the imperfect to the perfect, for the imperfect is precisely the perfect itself, only as becoming. The stages of becoming are necessary in order to let the fullness of the perfect emerge on every side. God is, he said, strength rather than goodness. If one wanted to say that God should become perfect starting from an imperfection that was not himself, that would indeed be absurd. But it is no contradiction if he, as the one who is in and for himself perfect, is in his appearance rather imperfect than perfect. Imperfection is then, relatively, perfection itself. Being without becoming would be death. But becoming contains not only being but also non-being as its moments.

Schelling was deficient only in that he did not express himself definitely enough to avoid feeding the reproach of pantheism so popular among those shallow toward speculative philosophy. He had indeed said that God, both as ground and as cause, must be thought in identity with, and in distinction from, the world. He had appealed to the old maxim: Deus se ipso prior sit necesse est [God must necessarily be prior to himself]. But that helped him nothing. His theory — that without assuming a negative principle, a nature, a dark ground in God, there could be no talk of a consciousness of God, and that without this kind of narrowing-down, the denial of a personal God would be a scientific honesty — had as its purpose to present the development of the world as a series of acts of God. He wanted to vindicate for God a real share in nature and history. This endeavor is, to be sure, incomprehensible to those who do not reflect at all on the modality of God's relation to the world, but who, even while speaking of God's omnipresence and omniscience, content themselves from the outset with the opinion that such reflection would in any case yield nothing —

with respect to which, as concerns themselves and their own thinking, they are perhaps only too right. Schelling did not want to content himself with the usual generalities, but made the mistake of bringing the development of the world and the development of God within it into a confused entanglement, in that he did not distinguish clearly enough between the process by which God exists in himself, further the process of creation, which is an absolute passing-over into difference, and the process of the world in itself, which is essentially its return into God. In his later years, in the Philosophy of Revelation and Mythology, in what he calls the cosmogonic and theogonic process, he made many efforts to bring greater clarity into these regions, but, so it seems, without overcoming a certain dependence of God on the world. The thought of a God subject to fate and to suffering had impressed itself too deeply upon him. In God's understanding there is, according to him, indeed a system, but God in and for himself is no system but life, and as such takes up into himself the pain of the negative. This is not what is erroneous in Schelling — to see the negative in God — but rather the manner in which he does so, in that he does not keep the world's distinction from God free within its unity with him.

We already noted, at the very beginning of this presentation, Schelling's habit of leaping from one subject to another. He displayed this peculiarity in the most remarkable way at his new appearance in 1815, in the short work:

On the Deities of Samothrace. Read aloud at the public session of the Bavarian Academy of Sciences on the King's name-day, October 12, 1815. Appendix to the Ages of the World. Stuttgart and Tübingen. 8vo.

For at the close of his treatise on freedom, Schelling had indicated a direction toward purely speculative research. He had said, p. 510: "We hold the greatest respect for the profundity of historical investigations, and believe we have shown that the almost universal opinion, that man gradually raised himself from the dullness of animal instinct up to reason, is not ours. We believe accordingly that the truth lies nearer to us, and

that for the problems which have become active in our time, we should seek the solution first within ourselves and on our own ground, before wandering off to such distant sources. The time of merely historical faith is past, once the possibility of immediate knowledge is given. We have a revelation older than any written one — nature. This contains prototypes which no human being has yet interpreted, while those of the written revelation have long since received their fulfillment and interpretation."

What else could one have expected after these lines than that Schelling would either, like Oken and others of his school, devote himself anew to the study of nature, or, like Hegel, create a metaphysics? And what did he do? Precisely the opposite. He lost himself not merely in the study of history, but of the most ancient history — not authentically remembered but only mythically hinted at. And naturally he immediately gave yet another quite different account of the highest task of philosophy, saying in that address, p. 41:

"The investigation of the past fills the greatest part of all scientific labor. Whether it is the oldest expeditions, deeds of war, and constitutions of peoples that are investigated, or the image

of vanished creations of rich nature is reconstructed from almost unrecognizable impressions, or the traces of the path which the earth followed in its development are sought out — always these investigations (these at any rate) are directed toward times of the past. But of all that is worthy of investigation, the most worthy remains that which once inwardly united human beings, in which thousands, and in part the best of their age, recognized the highest consecration of life."

According to the Lectures on the Method of Academic Study, the philosophy of art was to be the philosopher's goal, and from the address on the relation of the plastic arts to nature one might have supposed that Schelling was really working toward that goal. But now it was not so much religion itself but rather the mythology of religions that was to be the "most worthy" object for research. If Schelling had already turned his back on the actual study of nature at the end of the previous century, then his answer to Eschenmayer was his speculative swan song. Since that time he has no longer positively advanced philosophy, for what he calls positive philosophy is precisely not philosophy but the more or less ingenious interpretation

## The Representations of Religions

of given representations belonging to the religions. That in this activity a philosophical spirit can be shown is not to be doubted. But — it is not philosophy in the strict sense of the word, especially because it is conditioned each time by the particular measure of the interpreter's erudition. And the further back he goes from the developed stages of mythology's formation into its confused beginnings, the greater becomes the dependence on erudition, since the reports about them must be scantier and more contradictory, and often trivial points require a laborious criticism demanding many detours. With such investigations Schelling returned entirely to his youth, and one may now be in doubt as to which he now more properly is — the scholar or the philosopher. As philosopher I can, with regard to religion, derive its concept and, from that concept, the necessary forms of its appearance, e.g. pantheism, dualism, and monotheism; but in order, for example, to say anything definite about the Adonis myth, I would have to combine a great many particulars from many writers, compare the depictions of the Hebrew prophets with the laments of the Hellenic idyllists, undertake etymological researches, trace the spread of the Phoenician colonies,

and so forth. Such investigations Schelling, judging from the notes to that address, does indeed appear to have actually undertaken. Here, then, the verdict of the philologists and archaeologists also awaits him.

The address, which affects the preciosity of Johannes Müller's style and even says "Insul" instead of "Insel," appeared as an appendix to the Ages of the World, a book often promised by Schelling but never published, under which one may not incorrectly imagine a kind of philosophy of history. In a postscript, in which he noted that the address could also stand on its own, he said it was only a beginning and transition to several other works "whose intention is to bring the actual primal system of humanity, after scientific development, if possible by way of historical inquiry, after long obscuration, to light. For inseparable from history is science that has reached a certain point (?), and almost necessary is the transition of the one into the other. It is not by chance that the particular investigation of the Samothracian system precedes the more general inquiry; it was intended to lay this as the foundation, for, as if made to be the key to all the rest, the doctrine of the Cabiri stands out through its great age as well as through the clarity and simplicity of its outlines."

The outward impulse for this investigation Schelling received from Creuzer's Symbolik. On p. 79, in a note, he praises it because, through higher ideas combined with comprehensive erudition, it had broken the path toward a deeper understanding of the whole of mythology, and says: "In particular I consider it right to mention here what should really have been mentioned earlier, that Creuzer, through the light in which he placed the doctrine of Ceres and Proserpina, provided the first means for the view developed in the present treatise. He has, especially in IV, §39, shown irrefutably that Ceres is the first of beings, and this proposition — rightly understood, that is, the first being not, as with Creuzer, taken at the same time to be the highest, but rather as the sole underlying ground — is the foundation on which this system of explanation rests."

Schelling's procedure actually consisted in impressing the conceptions he had expressed, in the treatise on freedom and in the polemical work against Jacobi, concerning the absolute and its development, onto the mythological material supplied by Creuzer. The deities of Samothrace are only one example of this impression. These deities were constructed by Schelling in such a way

that he posited: 1) the non-ground, which strives to bring itself into existence — the divine longing to give birth to the world, the hunger for being; 2) the realization of this longing in nature, to be conceived as a kind of magic; 3) the spirit which again overcomes this magic; 4) the mediation of spirit with nature; and 5) the absolute, as the personal being that sums up and holds together within itself this whole process ascending from below to above — a being which not only links nature and spirit but is the demiurge free with respect to the world. In this way he arrived at the following system:

⁠1) Axieros = Demeter = Ceres = Isis.

⁠2) Axiokersa = Persephone = Proserpina (protogeneia) = Isis.

⁠3) Axiokersos = Dionysos = Liber = Osiris = Odin.

⁠4) Kasmilos = Hermes = Mercurius = Metatron (in the Kabbalah).

⁠5) Zeus.

These are five deities. Now, in that Schelling assumes fire to be their common element, in which they act, partly kindling it, partly appeasing it, he arrives at the conclusion that they are all, taken together, really Hephaestus. Hephaestus by himself is the all-shaping necessity

which forges for the gods their thrones within the world. Thus a sixth god would be born. A seventh, however, he brings out by making Dionysos himself the lord of the spirits of Hades. For Schelling must have seven gods, because he sees, in the seven planets and the days of the week, as he once hopes to show, the key to all systems of gods, op. cit., p. 26. Those deities work magically, but only together, which is why they are also called Cabiri, a word which, according to the Hebrew, is supposed to mean both an indivisible and a magical linking together (p. 110). The name of the Cabiri was therefore held in the highest honor in antiquity, and later, out of flattery, was even given to emperors. Schelling too, on p. 42, turns out a compliment for the Holy Alliance which conquered the infernal demon Napoleon: "And the finest application of the name would be permitted at the very moment it also recalls that Cabiric league by which the power of a truly typhonic realm — which threatened to end only in general demoralization — was first broken, and its last convulsions finally stifled."

Schelling later carried out this system in detail in his Philosophy of Mythology, insofar as it has become known. Of the arbitrariness with which he lets the potencies play in it, he here gives an example in the notes, p. 81: "Zeus relates to 1, 2, 3 again as 2 relates to 1, and conversely 2 relates to 1 exactly as 4 relates to 1, 2, 3. Zeus's number is always the fourth number. Besides this, however, Dionysos returns once more in a higher potency. Axiokersos is Dionysos in the lowest potency." With such superficial distinctions one can prove anything. In others Schelling sees this quite well, but in his own case he forgets it. To declare Dionysos, Osiris, and Odin identical on account of general similarities is merely an experiment in combination, not yet knowledge. Or to declare the Etruscan Camillus, the Samothracian Kasmilos, Aaron (Moses's brother), and the Semitic Kadmi-El, qui deum praecedit, immediately identical, and then, guided by Eisenmenger's newly discovered Judaism (1715), to proceed as far as the Kabbalistic angel Metatron and Melchizedek, are as yet only analogies, for whose objective truth Bochart's Hierozoicon, St. Croix, Vossius, and the like are no particular authorities. Thus Schelling himself speaks quite excellently about the false use of etymology; but are his own etymologies — connecting the Nephilim with the Nibelungs, the theurgists with dwarves, the Indian Maya with magic, the Kobaloi/Kobaroi with kobolds and Cabiri — any happier? Given Schelling's earlier statements, one would have expected a careful consideration of Indian material. But he understands Sanskrit as little as Creuzer does. So the Kabbalah is praised while the obscurity of Egypt is lamented. P. 109: "At present nothing is spoken of except the language and wisdom of India; but the Hebrew language and writings, foremost among them the Old Testament, in which the roots of the doctrine, and even of the language, of all the ancient religious systems are clearly recognizable down to particulars, lie unexplored." A fine compliment for the theologians! Schelling also wishes that these "most venerable monuments" might pass out of the hands of theologians into those of pure historical researchers. Schelling's wish to see a Kabbalist engage with his ideas was granted him in Molitor and his work on Tradition.

It is an entirely groundless assumption on Schelling's part to want to raise the Samothracian mysteries to the status of the primal system of humanity. The reports about them are so scanty and ambiguous that for this very reason one can make anything of them, turn them any way one likes. But with the same method of analogy applied to a presupposed scheme, as Schelling does, one could just as well make the mysteries of Mithras or the Eleusinian mysteries the oldest of all. It should still be mentioned, however, that Schelling, instructed by St. Croix, now (p. 28) determines the concept of the mysteries to mean that they were not opposed, in content, to the popular religion, but rather contained only a deeper grasp of the popular religion, chiefly, as he thinks, in the respect of showing that Dionysos is also Hades. Indeed, on p. 97 he even calls the separation of the Jewish people from other peoples a mystery. The mysteries are supposed to relate to the cult of the popular religion just as the esoteric exposition of philosophy relates to the exoteric. Against all objections that one might raise to Schelling in particular, he would probably meet them in two ways: first, by means of the doctrine of potencies — so inconvenient for any comfortable philosophizing — asserting that something is the same thing, only now at a lower, now at a higher stage, by which he can immediately fuse Apollo, Pallas, and so on with his constitutive deities; and second, by the proposition that every deity can appear partly as male and female, partly in the doubling of mother and daughter, father and son,

For the rest, if it is a matter of hunger, then surely Kronos, who devours his own offspring, is far hungrier than Demeter, and would also fit far better with other myths on which Schelling reflects, such as Zurvan Akarana among the Persians.

Again, at a public session of the Royal Academy of Sciences on March 28, 1832, Schelling delivered an address:

On Faraday's Latest Discovery.

This was the only word he spoke about natural science since the Jahrbücher der Medicin. In it he recounted the progress of the discoveries of Galvani, Volta, Davy, and Oersted, supported by "luck and chance," and recalled, p. 10, how "some Germans," under the name of the dynamic process, had long ago discovered a priori the connection of magnetism with electricity and galvanism, which was now also empirically confirmed by Faraday's all-decisive discovery. In the notes he set forth in still greater detail his own relation to these investigations —

Otherwise it should be noted of this address that it again shows a thoroughly rationalist Schelling, who on p. 31 even sets himself against the "priestly" persecutions of science. Schelling is, as he also showed in his academy addresses on Schleiermacher, Talleyrand, and Döllinger, an excellent writer of obituaries, and so we cannot leave unmentioned the rhetorically fine, though otherwise — were they true — for the Germans shameful words which he wove into this address, p. 22, about Goethe, as though without him the German people were nothing:

"Of the Germans in general it can be said, depending on one's disposition, either as praise or as blame, that they always fail far sooner on the side of understanding and judgment than on the side of will and disposition. And so one might maintain that, at least with regard to Germany, the true calamity of the age is to be sought far less in a deep moral perversity, as one is fond of presupposing, than in a widely spread fantasy-mongering, unfortunately favored from many sides, which infects everything, falsifies everything, and, by leaving nothing reliable or firm remaining, necessarily spreads a feeling of universal insecurity."

"Under such circumstances, men of grand experience, of unshakably sound reason, and of a purity of will raised above all doubt, act, by their mere existence alone, in a strengthening and preserving way. In such a time,

it is not German literature alone but Germany itself that suffers the most painful loss it could suffer. The man withdraws from it who, amid all inner and outer confusions, towered like a mighty pillar, on which many steadied themselves, like a beacon that illuminated every path of the spirit; who, by his very nature an enemy of all anarchy and lawlessness, always wished to owe the dominion he exercised over minds solely to truth and to the measure found within himself; in whose mind, and, I may add, in whose heart, Germany was certain to find, for everything by which it was stirred in art or science, in poetry or in life, the judgment of paternal wisdom, a final reconciling decision. Germany was not orphaned, not impoverished; amid all its weakness and inner disruption it was great, rich, and mighty in spirit, so long as — Goethe

lived."

The very nature of Schelling, always beginning his story anew, compels us, in following him, likewise to proceed by leaps rather than by an immanently unfolding continuity. Such a chain of qualitatively distinct new beginnings does not, however, exclude all connection among them. Schelling has not become another in himself; his becoming-other is only his fulfillment. This cannot be thought apart from a reaction against the further development of philosophy through Hegel; Schelling himself had to express his difference from it. This is not at all to be held against him — only the manner in which he did it has caused so much stir. Hegel had died in the autumn of 1831. As late as September 27, 1829, he had written to Daub (Collected Works XVII, 500) that he had spent five days with Schelling in Karlsbad in old cordial friendship. Hegel had never allowed anything against Schelling himself to be printed; rather, while acknowledging and combating his philosophy at once, he had always personally observed the greatest delicacy. Still less, however, had Schelling expressed himself against Hegel, however quick he otherwise was, against Reinhold, Fichte, Jacobi, and others, to defend his rights. Meanwhile, what happened, it seems, was unexpected for Schelling: that Hegel's fame had reached

his own, and that Hegel's activity, from Berlin, had called into life a numerous school. At first, after Hegel's death, the general expectation was that his school would quickly dissolve through its own internal division, once it lacked the cohesion given by the energetic personality of the master. But the opposite happened. The school developed further, and the publication of Hegel's complete works, especially his lectures, created for him a larger, a new public outside the academic one which he had chiefly had until then.

A Frenchman, Cousin, had had contact with Hegel first in Heidelberg, then in Berlin, but had been even more captivated by Schelling in Munich, whose lighter eloquence appealed to him more than Hegel's more ponderous deliberateness. Out of high esteem he dedicated to both his edition of Proclus's Commentary on the Parmenides. In 1833 he published a second edition of his Fragments philosophiques, in whose preface he narrated the history of his philosophical development, visibly striving to vindicate some independence for his eclecticism. It was here too that he expressed himself on his relation to Hegel and Schelling, in such a way that, while he paid the latter unconditional

recognition, he greatly restricted, by all sorts of qualifications, the recognition given to the former. He presented himself as the herald of Hegel's fame in France, who had "prophesied" him to his countrymen and, on returning, had told them of Hegel: "Gentlemen, I have found a man of genius." Of these Fragments Schelling now gave an excerpt in the Bayerische Annalen, 1833, no. 135, Blatt für Literatur XC. A pupil and friend of Schelling's, the professor of philosophy at the Lyceum in Dillingen, Hubert Beckers, translated Cousin's preface and asked Schelling to place his review of the Fragments in front of it. Schelling agreed, but at the same time expanded and altered the review, so that it appeared as a 28-page preface to a 62-page pamphlet:

Victor Cousin on French and German Philosophy. Translated from the French by Hubert Beckers. Together with a critical preface by His Excellency Privy Councillor von Schelling. Stuttgart and Tübingen, 1834. 8vo.

In it Schelling goes through French eclecticism, partly in its psychological, partly in its metaphysical aspects. But this is a secondary matter. The main matter is a polemic against Hegel, whose

## Schelling's School and the French

philosophy and school. From the outset the French are praised for their exemplary light style of writing, for their clarity, and also, toward the end, for the fact that, amid all upheavals, the deepest and most thorough studies retain their worth among them. But at every moment the reflection passes over to Hegel's philosophy and smuggles in the manifesto against it that has since been so often repeated. We shall take this up as the core of the preface.

P. XIII: "That philosophy which has most definitely, in recent times, been reproached with its agreement with Spinozism, had, in its infinite subject-object — that is, in the absolute subject which by its nature objectifies itself (becomes object), but emerges victoriously again out of every objectivity (finitude) and withdraws only into a higher potency of subjectivity, until, after exhausting its whole possibility (of becoming objective), it remains standing as the subject victorious over everything — in this, then, that philosophy did indeed possess a principle of necessary progression."

Here Schelling permits himself a fiction. He had originally determined the identity of subject and object not at all as absolute subject, but as indifference. Only in the Lectures on the Method of Academic Study did he call it God, and from this insinuation onward he made such rapid progress with personification that by 1809 he was left with nothing but will as the absolute, whereas in 1801, in the exposition of his system, only reason as the infinite was spoken of, and subjectivity itself was only a moment of it. But even taking the matter as he presents it here, that pantheistic moment emerges in it of which we have already had to speak repeatedly, and whose importance we fully acknowledge, as against the superficiality of ordinary deism. The Schellingian system accordingly posits:

⁠1) the absolute in itself;

⁠2) the absolute in its objectification, which is a process containing a progression of ever more freely becoming subjectivity, until it no longer has any new objectification to see and sublate as a negation of itself, and, after this total exhaustion of its possibility;

⁠3) the absolute has become, or rather will become, in and for itself.

Here the absolute occurs twice as absolute: first, as actual, which is all possibility; second, as actual, in and out of which,

and for which, all possibility immanent to it is actualized, in which it exists no longer merely as potency but as actus. Between this twofold actuality falls the broken realization, the actualization itself, and this is meant to be thought not as the self-shaping appearance of the absolute, but rather as the absolute's own development — a conception by which Schelling falls precisely into Patripassianism or Montanism. The theological opposition to Schelling used earlier to express this by saying that a becoming God contradicts the concept of God and makes religion — as unconditional trust in a spirit eternally identical with itself — impossible. Progression here is emanatory evolution, kabbalistic in character.

"...But if the purely rational, the merely not-not-to-be-thought, is pure subject, then that subject which, in the assumed manner, progresses in ever-increasing degree away from every objectivity — the subject under this determination is no longer the mere not-not-to-be-thought, purely rational, but this very determination was an empirical determination forced upon this philosophy by a living apprehension of actuality, or by the necessity of securing for itself a means of progression." Schelling here distinguishes, then, two kinds of subject: the logical and the empirical.

"This empirical element, one who came later — whom nature seemed to have predestined, as it were instinctively, to a new Wolffianism for our time — did away with, by putting in the place of the living, actual, to which the earlier philosophy had attributed the property of passing over into its opposite (the object) and returning out of this back into itself, the logical concept, to which he then, by the strangest fiction or hypostatization, ascribed a similar necessary self-movement."

That "one who came later" is evidently meant to be Hegel. Science has nothing to do with friendship, but the manner of expressing oneself certainly has something to do with friendship. Hegel had earlier been acknowledged by Schelling as fully his equal; he had appealed to his works as those of his friend; he had published a journal together with him; he had left his major works uncriticized, thus apparently approving them. And now he coldly calls him "one who came later." Why not simply name him? But not only the affected coldness is striking, but also the epithet "a new Wolffianism." In the first

place I must say that Wolff, however much Hegel derides him for his rage for demonstration, deserves neither to be dishonored nor despised, given his great merit as universal teacher of the Germans; second, no comparison can be drawn between Hegel and Wolff, since the original productivity of the former and the dependence of the latter on Leibniz lie too far apart, and mere scatteredness alone does not yet make a Leibniz. Schelling too, following Herder, has taken it upon himself to gladly put himself in Leibniz's place. What Schelling calls Wolffianism in Hegel is the scientific, systematic character. Schelling has never worked through a science. It has always only been the general with which he has occupied himself. But it is only in the particular and individual that this must prove itself. This breadth of the concrete extension of philosophy, combined with depth, is Hegel's greatness. Hinrichs, in the Berliner Jahrbücher, reproached Schelling with being the one left behind and fallen back. Schelling's pupils declared this to be slander, arrogance, and so on. To the objection as to why Schelling, who otherwise never hesitated with polemics, expressed himself only after Hegel's death, they answered that Schelling indeed could not have known that Hegel would die so soon (Hubert Beckers, on K. Fr. Göschel's Attempt at a Proof of Personal Immortality from the Standpoint of Hegel's Doctrine, Hamburg 1836, p. 3). But if Schelling wanted to speak on so important a matter, it should indeed not have happened in fleeting words, in a merely incidental manner. This mode of proceeding remains blameworthy.

Hegel, according to Schelling, is supposed to have put the logical concept in the place of the living, the actual. But what, then, is this so-beloved "living, actual"? Does Schelling perhaps imagine that, for Hegel, the logical concept is the causal principle of concrete reality? Does he mean that the generation of the universe is, by Hegel, entrusted to the abstract forms of the concept — one knows not how or where — rather than to concrete spirit, which alone thinks the concept? Schelling has entirely ignored:

⁠1) that the concept of the concept in Hegel is not the psychological abstraction of formal logic — which is why his mockery of Hegel for ascribing to it, by hypostatization, a self-movement is only proof that he has not understood Hegel at all, and that at the words "logical" and "concept" he has always thought only of the dead universality of the understanding so emphatically combated by Hegel himself. Why then could Schelling, in Bruno, distinguish the speculative inference from the formal one, why could he, in the treatise on freedom, praise the "old profound" logic and, against Jacobi, so emphatically stress the necessity of the understanding in the sciences?

⁠2) Hegel's Logic is by no means only logic, but at the same time metaphysics. On this important point Schelling says nothing at all. The concept of the concept, as it constitutes the ordinary content of logic, is here only one moment. Hegelian logic is the science of the idea as the absolute form immanent in all concrete formations. How else could Hegel have said that it contains the presentation of God as he is to be thought in his pure essence before the creation of nature and of a world of finite spirits — because indeed, as soon as one abstracts from nature and man, only logical-metaphysical determinations remain for the absolute. When Hegel's opponents appeal, against his logic, to the Aristotelian one, they do not know, or overlook, that Aristotle in his logic distinguishes quite well between the formal syllogism and the scientific one, which as form is identical with content. Aristotle himself very definitely carries his Analytics over into metaphysics, by tracing proof and definition back to first principles; and he institutes, in the Analytics — and not only there, but even more in the Hermeneutics — investigations into first causes; the actual, the possible, and the necessary occupy him at great length, especially on account of contrary and contradictory judgments. And what, after all, are Schelling's own potencies other than metaphysical categories? 3) The self-movement of the concept is, concretely, what both Schelling and Hegel call process. Naturally, in pure thinking nothing sensuous is to be thought, and the expression "movement" is to be taken ideally. Insofar as the concept is thought for itself in the abstract element of thinking, its self-movement too is thought only as such — unless one holds thinking as pure thinking to be, as seems to be the case with Schelling, an inferior existence compared to nature. Mere being is just as abstract as mere thinking; for that very reason neither is worse than the other. But thinking as thinking, in its necessity, is also process. It is not I who bring about the progression from one determination to another; rather this is a connection existing in and for itself, which must indeed be called ideal self-movement, because one concept, e.g. that of cause, of itself determines itself further to the concept of effect, this in turn of itself to that of reaction, and so on. I, the thinker, do not make this connection here; rather it makes itself, and to find this connection — free of us as thinkers — in its objective necessity is the goal of all science. Since, then, the concept of the idea has being and thinking immanent to it, Hegel needs no hypostases, of the sort in which Schelling indeed moves about with his cosmogonic and theogonic potencies. What actually applies to himself he here again reproaches to another.

"The latter (self-movement) was entirely his own invention, admired, as is fitting, by feeble minds — as was also the fact that this very concept, at its beginning, was determined as pure being."

The "feeble minds" and their admiration we shall leave aside as Schelling's private amusement; but what is really at stake with Hegel's "pure being" has remained unclear to him, especially judging by the note to p. XVIII. Schelling, when thinking of being, always thinks of the existent, and argues thus: some "what" must first exist before I can abstract being from it. For him, being always carries the sense of the substantial, haplōs einai as Aristotle says, or the Platonic ontōs on. Instead of beginning with the identity of being and thinking, he posits an unthinkable-before being. Hegel took the trouble to develop all the definitions of being as abstract being; being, becoming, determinate being, reality, quality, essence, existence, appearance, substance, objectivity — all these determinations are presented by him in their interconnection as moments of the organism of the idea in its logical form, whereas Schelling, as we have seen, always drew upon these categories only singly, according to whatever he happened to need at the moment. The absolutely true being, according to Hegel, is absolute spirit — of which Schelling again says not a word, and for which the abstraction of undetermined being in fact carries no more weight than being the most superficial, lowest — not the highest — of its determinations. Schelling has always turned the absolute itself, even under the form of will — the most concrete form he reached — into undetermined being, into indifference, yet was forced by the presupposition to let all differences emerge from it as potentiations of itself, up to the point where it would have exhausted, in the development of nature and history, the possibilities contained within it as the in-itself-existing unity of its differences. In the former form, before the differences, it is the undifferentiated prius; in the latter, after their overcoming, it is the superius that gathers together in itself all differences as posited into unity. What chiefly occupied Schelling was always the middle term, the progress and process of the potencies. In Hegel, this middle term is the free appearance of the absolute, whereby it is conditioned neither in its in-itself nor in its in-and-for-itself, but rather, as the negative unity of itself and of its difference — a difference posited by it just as much as sublated — it is the real root and ideal blossom of everything.

Concerning the nature of the beginning in Hegel, Schelling is therefore entirely in the dark. If the beginning of philosophizing is meant, this, according to Hegel, is to be sought in the concept of consciousness, and is possible for the empirical subject only by a resolve to it. If the beginning of the system is meant, this is the concept of the idea in its logical form, because this is the simplest form, possible entirely within the element of thinking itself. Mens notior corpore. But if, finally, the beginning is meant in the sense of the absolute principle of the existence of the real, then it is absolute spirit, in whose essence the concept of itself as idea, its existing as Logos, is only a moment, not the concrete totality. These distinctions Schelling has left unregarded, and in particular he has never dialectically examined the ambiguity that the beginning must have — of being something seen yet presupposing nothing — but has represented it only mystically, as longing, hunger, and the like. He continues:

"He had to retain the principle of movement, for without such a principle there was no getting anywhere, but he changed its subject. This subject was, as I have said, the logical concept. Because, then, it was this that allegedly moved, he called the movement a dialectical one; and because, in the earlier system, progression was in this sense indeed not dialectical, this system — to which he owed the principle of method, that is, the very possibility of making a system in his own way, entirely alone — had, according to him, no method at all: the simplest way of appropriating to oneself that system's most peculiar invention."

To begin here from the back end: it is not in accordance with the truth that Hegel denied all method to the Schellingian system. Schelling surely cannot blame Hegel's polemic against flat schematizing, which he himself was not innocent of either. Hegel constantly acknowledged Schelling to have a method, namely construction, as Schelling himself called it. But he declared himself against holding this method, since it does not yet exclude the arbitrariness of the subject, to be the highest and sole true method. For that he held only his own method of manifestation, of presenting the self-formation and self-dissolution of concepts. That Schelling, since the aphorisms in the Jahrbücher der Medicin, in fact proceeded without any method at all, "conversationally" as he himself put it, was not Hegel's fault. That earlier method of construction, however, as he practiced it before the mischief of self-appointed followers made it distasteful to him himself — followers who, as he himself mocked, let their hurdy-gurdy sound "in every marketplace" — was never disputed to Schelling by Hegel or any of his pupils, Hinrichs, Gabler, Michelet, Bayrhofer, and so on. That Hegel appropriated the method to himself by denying it to Schelling is therefore untrue; true, however, is that the proof of the self-justifying concept, which refutes its own one-sidedness — manifestation — is something other than mere assertoric-categorical positing, opposing, and combining. That Hegel changed the subject of

progression while retaining the principle has, I think, already been sufficiently refuted above. Hegel indeed also had a higher principle, the concept of absolute spirit, without which he would have had to leave the higher conception of the idea in its logical form by the wayside. Through this he arrived at freedom, at rest, at clarity, whereas in Schelling the culbute [overturning] of the universe threatens to break in at every moment, and the uncanniness of the struggle of the world-begetting God with the satanic principle spreads within itself that peculiar shudder which the ancients called the terror of Pan.

"Meanwhile the logical self-movement of the concept (and what a concept!) held out, as was to be foreseen, only so long as the system proceeded within the merely logical; as soon as it has to take the difficult step into actuality, the thread of dialectical movement breaks off entirely; a second hypothesis becomes necessary, namely that it occurs to the idea, one does not know why — unless it is to interrupt the boredom of its merely logical being — to let itself fall apart into its moments, whereby nature is supposed to arise. The first presupposition of this allegedly presuppositionless philosophy, that the pure logical concept as

such has the property or nature of turning of itself (for the subjectivity of the philosophizing thinker was supposed to be entirely excluded) into its opposite — of, as it were, overturning itself — in order then to strike back again into itself; something one can think of a living, actual thing, but which, with regard to the mere concept, one can neither think nor imagine, but only assert. The breaking-off of the idea, that is, of the completed concept, from itself, was a second fiction, for this transition (to nature) is no longer a dialectical one but another kind, for which it would be hard to find a name, and for which there is no category in a purely rational system, and for which even the inventor himself has no category in his own system."

This passage has been the one most gladly cited and paraphrased by those who invoked Schelling's authority against Hegel's philosophy. They point to nature and ask scornfully whether such an existence could really owe its origin to the thin logical concept. Naturally this question is at once answered with a resounding: Impossible! And yet in doing so they only prove their own ignorance and philosophical crudeness. According to Hegel, the concept which, in systematic development, results as the last, and hence as the total result,

is that which, as the highest thinkable, also constitutes the real prius of all the others — of finite spirit, of nature, and of the concept of the idea in itself. This is the concept of absolute spirit. For, according to Hegel, the determination which dialectically results from a preceding one is always the one that contains the ground of that which, though mediating for cognition, precedes it.

The logical idea sublates itself in nature, nature in spirit.

Schelling had a presentiment of this progression, which exists as an absolute process wholly independent of time, when he expressed himself popularly by saying that, in existence, the imperfect precedes the perfect.

But according to Hegel, spirit does not arise out of nature, nor nature out of the logical idea.

This too Schelling had a presentiment of, when he said that the imperfect must be thought as the perfect's own presupposition.

According to Hegel, the logical idea — being as thinking — and nature — thinking as being — form the opposition which sublates itself in spirit, as the subject that thinks itself as being and realizes its thinking,

and whose principle is accordingly spirit — what Aristotle would call the hou heneka. Transitions between these three basic forms do not exist, if by that is meant that some intermediate entity is still to be posited between them. Here, namely where the concept of creation enters, there is indeed a break present, which Aristotle would probably call metabolē. The idea's being-other — an expression Hegel shares with Plato and Aristotle — can indeed only be rightly understood if one proceeds to the end of the system and arrives at the recognition that the subject which must be thought as immanent to the logical idea is absolute spirit.

But insofar as this spirit is thought as not relating to nature and history, it is indeed the pure idea. The word "pure," so much insisted upon by Schelling, means here nothing other than abstract. The abstract is here no reproach, for it designates the logical only in its relation to the non-logical. The logical, taken for itself, has just as good a claim to the meaning of the concrete. But the concrete could not exist at all without possessing the logical as a moment. In itself it is accordingly always logical, and it is called non-logical only

in relation to the logical as such. The God without nature and history is not the concrete, living God, but only the abstract God — or, to put it more clearly for Schelling, the merely potential God, who actualizes himself in that he freely releases being-other out of himself. Schelling thus already knows how to speak of magic, of the world's falling-away, of the negative in God — but what is creation other than the willed transition into its opposite? Of thinking into being? Creation is no becoming, for we do not call the growing of a plant, and the like, creation. Why do we, rightly, admire that passage of the Old Testament scripture: "God said: Let there be light! And there was light"? Here there is nothing more standing between God and the light. But Schelling here wants to have gnostic intermediate beings. Here he lets his potencies appear, which God, as the One in All, does indeed, as their lord, tame and compel into harmony, but which nevertheless also pursue their own nature and often fall into tensions quite dangerous for them, out of which Schelling helps them, not speculatively, but only mythically and mystically, by means of a deus ex machina. Hegel, in his concept of creation, agrees entirely with that of Jewish and Christian monotheism,

whereas Schelling, who inserts between the God existing in himself and the world the dark ground, a nature in God, a potency of transition as such, the world of ideas, the constructing line, or whatever else, has, despite all assurances to the contrary, always found himself at variance with ecclesiastical orthodoxy — this word taken in its good sense — and has inclined toward the heretical sects and their cosmogonies.

When Schelling finally wants to make Hegel ridiculous by saying that the concept gets bored and that there exists no category at all for its passing-over, then, in regard to "the concept," what we already once had to reproach is again entirely forgotten: that Hegel's Logic is not only logic as the science of the rules of thinking, but just as much ontology. Hegel's Logic ends as the doctrine of ideas; the concept of objectivity and its forms, the concept of life, of the true and the good, are set forth in it. Whoever knows this will no longer find the transition from the logical to nature so ridiculous. A special category for it is not possible for the very reason that the passing-over into the opposite occurs throughout all the moments of the system — just as Schelling himself, earlier, before he began to philosophize "conversationally," quite analogously practiced this well enough in construction, by means of the antithesis opposed to the thesis. The I has no middle term between itself and the not-I; between expansive and coercive force there is no middle term, and so on. Rather they are identical only through absolute opposition. Negative identity is itself the principle of opposition, on which Schelling himself, under the expression of the "copula," could once speak so beautifully.

Hegel called the logical idea the system of pure reason. If I am to cite from Schelling's collected writings a passage relevant here, it is I, 509, where he speaks of reason, in the mystics' expression, as the primum passivum in God. The emphasis on rationality in the Hegelian system can, seriously meant, only be the highest praise for it, since a philosophy not paying homage to reason is no philosophy at all, and Schelling himself once placed reason at the summit of his philosophy. But one already tastes only too well here that, through the term "rationalism," he wants to stamp the Hegelian system with the mark of that period of the Enlightenment in which "rationalism" all too often meant the abstractions of mere understanding. By this he wants to characterize Hegel as shallow, as unchristian, as negative, indeed even as destructive — just as he indeed, in Berlin, actually designated his own philosophy in its earlier form as the negative one and Hegel as its completer. But now he has become positive, compact, solid, conservative. Throne and altar can rely on him. He will halt the "shipwreck" of ideas in Germany. The words "vitality," "actuality," "experience" sound so human, so pleasing! What indeed is the concept, with its rational necessity, against them!

"This attempt, with concepts of an already far-developed philosophy of nature (such a philosophy had been worked on since Descartes), to go back to the standpoint of scholasticism, and to begin metaphysics with a purely rational concept excluding everything empirical — although even this concept was not found or correctly recognized, and the empirical, rejected at the front door, was reintroduced through the back door of the idea's becoming-other or becoming-unfaithful to itself — this episode, then, in the history of modern philosophy, if it has not served to develop philosophy further, has at least served to show anew that it is impossible to get at actuality with the purely rational."

Schelling, if only you had spared your wit for other matters! Is a philosophy that defines the idea as the unity of the concept and its reality supposed to exclude everything empirical from itself? Is its first concept — the concept of being — supposed to exclude it? If being is not even permitted to be a predicate of the empirical, what then is one to hope for from it? Is Hegel, who worked out a complete philosophy of nature and of spirit, supposed to exclude the empirical from himself? Is Hegel, who wrote in his Phenomenology of 1807 the immortal section "Observation of Reason," supposed to exclude the empirical from himself? Is Hegel, who developed in such detail in his Logic the inferences of totality, of induction and analogy, on which all empirical science rests, supposed to exclude the empirical from his system? And what is supposed to be meant by "getting at actuality with the rational"? Is the rational, then, an instrument, like a net, in which one captures the colorful butterfly of actuality? Is rationality, then, not immanent to the concrete? But is not the rational, in itself, just as much something actual as that which, in distinction from it, is so called? We indeed say that reason, insofar as it constitutes the necessary form of all true existence,

is, in isolation by itself, abstract; but in this its transcendence it has the same dignity as nature and spirit, which are unthinkable without reason. Ah, why must one preach reason to a philosopher!

Now, when Schelling asserts that the philosophy of nature has been worked on since Descartes, that was presumably a momentary forgetting that here Bacon of Verulam holds historical precedence.
