### Dritter Abschnitt. Der meteorologische Proces.

#### §. 432.

#### C. Der elementarische Proceß der Erde

#### §. 433.

#### I. Der Luftproceß

#### §. 434.

#### §. 435.

#### §. 436.

Since the earth is a sphere and the sun's rays therefore strike obliquely at the poles and perpendicularly at the equator, there results from this a difference in air temperature which, on each hemisphere from the pole to the equator, horizontally presents the same gradation from a minimum to a maximum of warming that occurs vertically from the ground to the limits of the atmospheric envelope. This difference is further determined in particular by the fact that the character and configuration of the ground conditions reduce the general differences of the temperature zones to more constant measures. In detail, however, from the generality of the temperature zones and the particularity of the isotherms there arises the mobile yet lawful play of the winds.

#### 1) Die Temperaturgürtel — 1. The Temperature Zones

#### §. 437.

The temperature of our earth's atmospheric envelope is, in general, from the pole to the equator on each half of the same, spread out into different degrees which encircle it in ring-like fashion. The minimum of temperature prevails at the poles; the maximum at the equator; between both extremes a mean temperature is produced.

#### a) Die Polarzone — a) The Polar Zone

#### §. 438.

The polar zone is the coldest. At the South Pole as at the North Pole the same low degree of temperature prevails, which causes all liquid to solidify into ice and stunts all organic life. The southern half of the earth, in which the water mass predominates, is, however, even further icy than the North Polar zone, in which the landmass consolidates into a broad continuum. The polar zone divides itself within itself according to the double relation toward the pole and away from the pole. The former is the properly arctic zone; the latter, turned toward the milder temperature, is the subarctic. However, only in the continental hemisphere is this transition more decisive, while in the pelagic hemisphere it necessarily becomes very blurred at the level of the sea.

#### b) Die Aequatorialzone — b) The Equatorial Zone

#### §. 439.

The opposite of the polar zone is the equatorial zone, in which the heat of the perpendicularly radiating sun prevails, eliciting the most luxuriant life of the plant and animal world, insofar as the height of the temperature can unite with the corresponding abundance of water, but otherwise killing this very life in scorching sultriness. The equatorial zone is in itself only one, with a double transition toward both poles. The middle of the earth's surface is the properly tropical zone, which moderates itself as subtropical toward the north as toward the south.

#### c) Die gemäßigte Zone — c) The Temperate Zone

#### §. 440.

Between both zones, between the grimness of frost and the fury of heat, unfolds the belt of temperate temperature, in which organic life develops, though not with such surging vital force as in the tropical world, yet more evenly. This zone likewise has a double transition, but not merely a simple one, as the tropical zone, but one that is itself again internally differentiated: namely, toward the polar zone the cold-warm, and toward the equatorial zone the warm-cold. According to abstract distribution, this zone itself exists again in duplicate: once on the southern, and again on the northern half of the earth. It is therefore the most manifold in every respect. However, in the comparison of the more southern temperate zone with the more northern one, the latter appears, because it encloses the maximum of continental area within itself, as the truly temperate zone, while the former realizes itself only very incompletely.

#### 14) Die Isothermen — 14. The Isotherms

#### §. 441.

In the zonal series of temperature, the character and configuration of the ground produces a particular difference, a certain equality of the measure of warmth at different places. This equality appears in the form of curves that wind through the zones, and which we call isochimenes below the freezing point and isotherms above it — the equality of winter and summer warmth.

#### 3) Der Wind — 3. The Wind

#### §. 442.

The general temperature zones as well as the isotherms are the preconditions for the movement of the gaseous envelope of the earth. This movement, which encompasses infinitely many degrees, from the gentle murmur of the zephyr to the roar of the hurricane, imitates, as it were as an ethereal projection, the nature of the ground lying beneath it, whose chemical composition, density, shape, height, moisture or dryness, and color modify heat production everywhere. Air is heavy. The denser it is, the heavier it is. The heavier layer of air presses upon the thinner, and therefore lighter, layer; but the thinner layer, expanding in its purity, presses equally upon the heavier. The different layers of air with their different temperatures and their different weights are therefore in mutual tension and through this in constant motion. This motion, identical with the incessant transition of the denser and thinner layers, of those sinking to the ground and those rising to the heights, is the wind. The minimum of air warming occurs at both poles, the maximum in the tropical zone. The warmed air layers of the tropical lands — the so-called courant ascendant — therefore rise as lighter, in order to flow off southward and northward over the belt of the temperate zone toward both poles, from which the cold-heavy air creeps or storms forward, merging with the equatorial current in the most manifold conflicts. However, since the earth has a rotation from west to east which mechanically acts upon the movement of the air layers, and at the same time, in the diurnal period of the earth with the advance of the sun, the air layers are successively warmed from east to west and thereby also altered in their movement, there arises, instead of a direct transition from south to north, a turning of the air currents in which the northeast trade wind is deflected to the southwest and the southwest trade wind to the northwest current. Between both trade wind directions, in the region of the calms and variable winds, there properly prevails the seemingly capricious indifference of the dual system of mechanical air movement, which corresponds symmetrically on the continental and pelagic hemispheres of the earth. With this difference of the northern and southern hemispheres there is naturally also a modification of the whole process, a modification which is further altered by the contrast of land and sea, which produces the daily alternation of the regular coastal winds, by mountains, and so forth.

#### II. Der Wasserproceß — II. The Water Process

#### §. 443.

The liquid envelope of the globe consists of oxygen and hydrogen gas. It exists: 1) as the sea; 2) in the form of moist precipitation; 3) in the transition to the generation of free electrical fire as thunderstorms.

#### 1) Das Meer — 1. The Sea

#### §. 444.

The water of the earth covers about two-thirds of its surface. In this enormous continuity we call it the sea. It stands in the same relation to the sun as the elastic envelope of the earth and therefore presents quite analogous phenomena. The warm equatorial current (the rotation current of the Pacific and Atlantic Oceans, the Guinea current, the current of Mexico, Florida and Bahama) is deflected by the cold polar current — the one from the Gulf of Mexico, for example, reaching as far as Ireland and the Scandinavian coasts, where therefore tropical plants drift ashore, warm air keeps the temperature mild, and in winter even thunderstorms discharge. These great relationships are extraordinarily modified in concreto by the rotation of the earth from west to east, by the daily successive warming of the water surface from east to west, by the ebb and flow of tides, by the winds, and by the ground-form, both the submarine and the mass elevation projecting above the level of the sea, so that, for example, the temperature and velocity of ocean currents become most various. The velocity of an ocean current can, moreover, be so rapid that beside its arrow-swift wave the sea lies in smooth calm. — What the sea is for the whole earth, the springs and the rivers and lakes arising from them are for individual parts of the same.

#### 2) Der Niederschlag — 2. Precipitation

#### §. 445.

Now, since water evaporates through warmth, there arises in the air, simultaneously with its movement, a new process. The water dissolved in gaseous form is at first so identical with the air that it does not become visible at all. However, since in the air the different layers are of unequal temperature, the diaphaneity of the air becomes clouded during the transition of the water vapor from a warmer layer into a cooler one, and the water is precipitated as water. It transforms itself back from its elastic form into the form of liquid drops. If these drops are microscopically small, the mist hovering in the air is fog; if such little vesicles gather into globules that sink to the ground, they become rain; if such drops are driven from warmer layers again through colder ones, they crystallize and form snow, sleet, and hail. From the eternal restlessness of this process develops the picturesque spectacle of cloud formation, which, identical with the movement of the air in general, changes horizontally from the poles to the equator and vertically from the earth's surface to the uppermost region of air in absolute multiformity. And yet this infinite creative urge is also governed by a simple type, which indeed permits the most countless transitions of its basic forms, but directs every concrete formation in its arising or passing to one or another of the same. Settling immediately on the ground as fog, the precipitation becomes the gray layer cloud (stratus); in the middle region, delimiting itself into isolated, freely floating masses, it becomes the black cumulus cloud; finally, in the highest regions, fraying apart, fanning out into light, flocculent strips, extending itself into so-called weather-trees, it becomes the white feather cloud (cirrus).

#### 3) Das Gewitter — 3. The Thunderstorm

#### §. 446.

With the change of temperature, which again is identical with the change of air movement, which again is identical with the change of precipitation, an electrical tension of the air layers is generated through friction. In the middle between the upper and lower air regions, in the domain of the powerful cumulus cloud as the cloud par excellence, this tension is strongest, because here precisely all those changes undergo their crisis. Storm, rain, and lightning are only the different sides of the same mighty process: the thunderstorm. The violence of the struggle of the meteorological opposites can press it down to the ground as well as let it pass over the summit of mountains many thousand feet high. It depends on the particular circumstances whether it forms irregularly, even if in a certain regularity of number for a given region, or regularly at a certain time of day, as in the tropics and in enclosed valleys with mountain lakes. The northern lights are a rainless thunderstorm in the upper air region, which lacks intensity of opposition, so that it does not reach the explosion of the air burning electrically as actual lightning, but only an extensively broad scintillation, for which reason it has also been called the magnetic storm. But it is not an equalization of magnetism, but rather, as a meteoric phenomenon, of the electricity of the air. The energy of the elemental powers can go so far in their collision that they even produce solid bodies, the atmospherilites or meteorites, in that the substances dissolved in the air as gas are concreted into solid masses by the electrical combustion process, which then crash down accompanied by mighty detonations. The substance of such atmospherilites — iron, cobalt, manganese, silicon, and the like — is quite the same that we also find in rainwater.

#### III. Der Feuerproceß — III. The Fire Process

#### §. 447.

For the air, nitrogen gas is the decisive element; for water, hydrogen gas; for fire, oxygen gas. Yet not only as fire in the form of flame, but also as oxidation of substances is oxygen active. The earth consists of a mixture of the most diverse substances, which already as such, through the simple quality of their difference, enter into dynamic tension with one another. Through their exposed surface they stand in the liveliest interaction with the air and water process. In the fissures, caves, and vaults of the earth, gases are therefore constantly liberated. The earth's fire perpetually fans itself anew, especially on combustible substances such as sulfur and the like. Water, seeping into the interior of the earth and coming into contact with the fire-gas, expands as steam. In accordance with the nature of gas, it rolls about in constant restlessness, greedy for continual expansion, licks at all minerals, sucks them out, metamorphoses them, specifies itself as carbonaceous, sulfurous, and so forth. The pliant gas creeps through all pores, steals through all cracks, breaks through confining walls, and lifts the most enormous masses to the greatest heights, so that precisely the greatest mountains and their uttermost peaks belong to the youngest formations of the earth.

#### §. 448.

If it remains at a simple uplift, the ordinary mountain arises. But if the glowing steam breaks through the enclosure, if it thunderously forces an exit upward, then the tip of the cone is lifted off and the crater mouth is opened, through which the gases now escape on all sides, discharging lightning, scattering ash and stones, igniting flames, pouring out melted stones and metals in lava streams. The movements of the still enclosed, still burrowing and twitching vapors in the depths appear in the now horizontal, now vertical, now whirling oscillations of the earthquakes, which usually subside temporarily after a violent volcanic eruption.

#### §. 449.

In the present period of the earth, earthquakes have therefore become, as it were, systematized through the relatively constant channels of the earth's interior and their exits in the volcanoes. Each volcano forms in its surroundings a tremor-circle inherent to it as a center, insofar as the lesser plutonic phenomena of the same — solfataras, mud eruptions, and the like — are connected with its activity. The configuration of the earth's fire process shows us two interlocking series of volcanoes: the insular series from Unalaska to New Zealand, the continental series running from south to north in America, and between both, in Iceland, Sicily, and so forth, the sporadic central volcanoes. Through the earth's fire, the earth's crust is not only mechanically torn apart but also constantly crumbled in continual oxidation and deoxidation, and thereby opened up to fertility for vegetation, for organic life in general. The inequality of the horizontal and vertical direction, which are thrown into confusion by the volcanic powers, makes possible the flowing of waters, their fall. Australia shows us how greatly river formation falls behind without the contribution of volcanic formations. Its rivers drag themselves about in pool-like and bankless fashion on the ground, whose topsoil rests unmediated upon the primordial rock.

## Dritte Abtheilung. Das Leben. Organik — Third Division. Life. Organics

### Einleitung — Introduction

#### §. 450.

The merely mechanical movement of matter, as well as the play of its qualitatively different forces, is sublated in the self-determination of life, which has in them indeed the condition of its external emergence into existence, but not the principle of its being. The character, shape, and climatic determination of the ground promotes or hinders the development of life, but does not produce it. Life lives only from itself. One may not, to be sure, abstractly oppose the mechanical and dynamic (or physical) nature as dead or inorganic to the living as the organic, but must rather conceive both as one whole, which only in life attains the form of complete subjectivity, which lays itself out into its own differences in order to take them back again into its unity and to generate them ever anew. But the qualitative difference of the living from the so-called inorganic is the automorphism that articulates itself through immanent virtuality. Not in indeterminately bounded masses, not in indeterminately extended processes does life exist, but only in individuals which articulate themselves within themselves and, with such inner articulation, simultaneously close themselves off outwardly as an appearing form.

We transfer the concept and thus also the expression of the organic to everything systematic, indeed to everything ordered in general; within nature, however, only the living is to be regarded as the truly organic.

#### §. 451.

The organic individual therefore cannot appear before its mechanical and dynamic presuppositions have entered into existence, because it has in them the external condition of its subsistence. It does not, however, arise from them, as if the dead could produce the living. In this way, what is called equivocal generation is impossible and is not merely an ambiguity but an absurdity. Yet in the inorganic, the relation to the organic is already co-posited, as that in which it has its wherefore and to which it submits itself as a basis. The organic does not, however, therefore proceed from the inorganic as its cause and cannot be comprehended from something other, only from itself. From the ether as the universal cosmic menstruum the rotating gas-rings form themselves; from these the individual globes; from these their individual formations. In this respect one can say that the turgor vitae must have been contained in the still indifferent primordial matter of the ether just as well as nitrogen, carbon, gold, sulfur, and so forth. In the blood of the individual, nature rises again to the indifferentiation of matter, but to an indifference which, as concrete and living, is the opposite of the abstract unity of the original ether. The original generation of a particular organic being is therefore always bound to the epoch in which a star first develops the conditions for its existence. If, on the other hand, a star has reached the completion of its generic production, then with this moment actual original generation ceases for it, because nature can now maintain the individual through germinal generation epigenetically as genus, and epigenesis, however, is the more direct and secure path. Original generation as such can never become an object of sensory perception, but neither can germinal generation be grasped in its actuality. What appears to the eye as the punctum saliens, as the mother cell, as the vesicle, is always already the result of generation as an act that is absolutely ideal in its reality. In plants, in infusoria, in fish and batrachians, nature seems to profane the secret of generation and to publicly display the nuptial bed. Yet even if we have the frog eggs here and the frog semen there, dab the eggs with this and now see them transform into living subjects, we shall absolutely not perceive the how of this transformation, because it is precisely the lightning-flash of the idealism of nature. The privilege of generation we cannot wrest from nature, and we are therefore also not in a position to produce organisms by artificial means through chemical processes, let alone a homunculus.

#### §. 452.

For the earth, the human being was the immanent purpose of its formative catastrophes. With the original generation of the human being, the original generation of plants and animals consequently ceased and passed over into germinal generation and species variation. The earth could never generate any organisms other than those identical with its position in the solar system and with its meteorological process. The type of the petrified plants and animals is not different from that of those now growing and living. They are likewise palms, pines, pachyderms, lizards, and so forth. If certain animal forms, such as the Plesiosaurus, Ichthyosaurus, Pterodactyl, and the like, seem particularly striking to us, we not only comprehend their necessity for the conditions of the solid, liquid, and elastic elements of that time, but also find in present-day organics living analogies of strange form-mixtures, such as the paradox of the platypus, the flying membranes, and so forth. It goes without saying, moreover, that if our planet has the epoch of its original generation already behind it, it cannot be concluded from this that original generation may not still be possible as a progressive one for the universe, for perhaps newly forming stars. It is only meant to be noted that original generation is not a heterogeneous one but one arising homogeneously from the essence of a star.

#### §. 453.

For the justification of a repeated original generation, infusorial and parasitic organisms have been especially adduced. Microscopy has, however, demonstrated the illusions into which one had thereby fallen. The smallest organisms, the proletarians of nature, are protected through their innumerability and indifference to the absolute extinction of their genus, since the individual can indeed be destroyed so easily. Moreover, little makes a truly decided impression upon the inherently feeble; it behaves passively and through such yielding enduringly. Plant germs and animal infusoria equally endure a high degree of heat as of cold. Scarlet runner beans, found in Herculaneum destroyed by a glowing rain of ash, sprouted afresh after eighteen hundred years. Parasitic animals can also exist outside the organism on which they are usually found; they do not belong to the concept of the organism whose life they exploit. The tapeworm no more than the itch mite belongs to the constitutive moments of the human organism. Parasitic animals do not arise from the organisms in which they are found, even if they reproduce within them as their host body; originally they are colonists. That such animals, especially entozoa, could not bring their eggs through the tissues of plants and animals is correct, because the tissues have no pores and only absorption takes place in them. But in diseases of the tissues, in injuries to them, the parasites penetrate. Or they bore through the tissues and cause inflammations. Many, especially the so-called family or nurse animals, e.g. salps, undergo transformations. Within the same organism they appear in its different organs as different ones. Or they migrate from one organism to another and attain a different stage of development in each. Indeed, we see them pass from fish into birds. The multitude of the smallest plants and animals goes to infinity, because the life of the individuals is short and exposed to destruction on all sides. Precisely this multiplication, increasing to the enormous, proves that nature is not inclined to let repeated original generation occur for them, but rather is endeavoring to secure the existence of the genus through mass. Even if millions of spores and pollen perish, even if millions of already living individuals die, the remnant of a single germ and individual suffices, especially in asexual generation, to immediately call millions back into existence again. Scarcely born, such a lowly being already exteriorizes itself again in new births, so that one can say: As it is born today, so it shall give birth today — ah, what short bowels this brood has!

To illustrate the enormous fertility of the lower organisms, one has taken the example of a higher one and calculated that from a single mother field mouse — since she can give birth every six weeks and her young in such a short time themselves become capable of reproduction — 23,000 specimens can descend in a single summer. The higher organism can endure, in its strength and self-sufficiency, to a certain degree the existence of parasitic plants and animals upon itself as a nourishing body. But if they multiply in too great a number or grow too large, they trouble the individual from which they feed, like the internal worms of the cercariae and the Distomum volvens in the eye chamber of the dolphin, where they occur by the hundreds, like the ascarids in the rectum, like the taenia in the intestines of the chyle and milk preparation of the human being. But if one makes the distinction that for the lower and smaller animals the generatio originaria may still continue, while for the higher and larger ones it has already been extinguished, such a comparative is too indeterminate. The actual boundary would have to be drawn and thus it would have to be settled whether cryptogams, infusoria, and mollusks can arise through repeated original generation, but phanerogams and arthropods no longer. Only the crystal is an undoubted repeated original generation, for it is an individual which, as individual, does not arise through the liquid matter, only from it, and as unity only through itself. But the crystal is only dead life.

#### §. 454.

But is original generation to be conceived only as a single one in one specimen, or as a manifold one in different specimens? To prove the assumption of one original specimen, one has appealed to the migration of plants and animals. Plants have gradually spread further, as for example the sweet flag has advanced from the shores of the Caspian Sea to those of the Baltic. Animals, especially birds, and even in their excrement, have carried plants about, have fertilized cliffs and islands in the middle of the sea. Through the waves of continental rivers, not only, but also through the currents of the sea, plant germs have been conveyed to other regions, other shores. Intentionally, the human being has spread cultivated and ornamental plants over the earth's surface: as coffee was brought from Arabia to America, as tobacco and the potato were brought from America to the Old World, as the dahlia came to Europe only at the beginning of this century and is now already at home in all gardens. Finally, the obliging breezes have carried the germinating spores from place to place. In the case of animals, their locomotive mobility explains their occurrence at the most diverse points of the earth even more easily. Larger animals, originally colonized, have often expanded en masse as wild animals, as for example the horse, which the Europeans first brought to America, has now gone wild there in the great grass plains. The human being has also spread smaller animals, unintentionally, like the shipworm and the rat; intentionally, like the silkworm.

All these facts are unconditionally to be admitted, yet they do not suffice for the primitive origin of organic beings. This origin cannot have been bound to one point and one specimen, but must, in the creative epoch of the earth, have occurred simultaneously at various places or at various times everywhere where the type of soil and the meteorological process provided the necessary inorganic presuppositions for their subsistence. For we find, in the horizontal as in the vertical projection of the earth's surface, with the same character of soil and climate, the same plants. From the palm region on, we can observe in both dimensions everywhere, through the evergreen broadleaf trees, through the trees with changing foliage, through the conifers, dwarf birches and shrubs, down to the lichens and mosses, the same sequence. The alpine rose, which smiles at us at the boundary of eternal snow, cannot so constantly have been brought by animals or humans to the mountain peaks rising solitary into the ocean of air. Were plants not originally lawful autochthons, then nature would have to deviate at some point from that order, whether of longitude or of altitude. But the vegetation of the two arctic zones, separated from each other by the entirely different flora of the tropics, and the Chimborazo of America, the Peak of Tenerife, Adam's Peak of Ceylon, and so forth, persistently show us the progression of vegetation changing with the degrees of temperature. — The same holds for animals, even if it is correct that they can more easily extend themselves into wider circles and that these circles, as more recent investigations of the tiger, for example, have proved, are larger than was formerly assumed. In this consideration, one must not be troubled by the pedantic separation of species according to one-sided characteristics — which should be logical, but which misjudges the true nature of the concept and even more so of the idea — as some natural historians love to do and through which they so greatly impede a clear overview. Nature cares about its fundamental types, which are finite and determinate, whereas it pours itself out into the infinite and indeterminate with varieties. Here it modifies the type according to the conditions it finds, with patient, adaptable, supremely manifold elasticity, while remaining the same in the essential differences. Mutatis mutandis, the animal nature therefore also remains the same in the different locales, insofar as they are equal or similar in soil composition, ground-form, and climate. In the tropical zone of the New World we find apes, great cats, large scaled lizards, and so forth, just as in the tropical zone of the Old World. Only the abstract uniformity is lacking. On the western ridge of the Cordilleras, the ruminant animal, which in the sandy plains and grass steppes of Africa and Asia has become the camel, has appeared as the llama; and in Brazil, the tusked pachyderm, which we admire in the African and Indian elephant, has appeared as the tapir.

#### §. 455.

Life really exists only as something living. Already matter in general exists only in masses, and force requires a substrate. Life therefore posits itself: 1) as a single being, as an individual; 2) it repeats the process of its individualization through overcoming its presupposed inorganic nature; 3) it goes beyond itself as a single being to the preservation of its genus as the universality immanent in it in itself. The first process, the morphological, closes the individual within itself and outwardly; the second, the biological, constantly renews it in its form, as life sublates the change of substance into its ideal unity and thereby proves itself as the power over inorganic existence; the third, the sexual, also again produces the individual, but as a genuinely independently existing other, to generate which is therefore also the highest deed of the individual, for which reason it also, on lower levels of organization, often exhausts itself entirely therein, and sexual desire drags it into destruction.

#### I. Der Gestaltungsproceß — I. The Process of Formation

#### §. 456.

The positing of the individual as a totality closed in upon itself, which automorphically delimits itself from within — this is the first and most necessary act of life. Because the organic individual is an indivisible, self-forming whole, the concept of the mineral as crystal also falls into this category, for the crystal generates itself from the mother liquor without mechanical juxtaposition entirely by itself. It gives itself its form from within: these faces, angles, colors. It is the flickering-up of life that immediately dies again; the first attempt at life that is, as it were, still expiated with death. That the crystal shows no special organs is no reason not to recognize it as an organism, even if as the simplest one. After all, there are enough plants and animals that also show only a most simple structure, being nothing but a cylinder, a cone, or a sphere. The fundamental shape of the organism is a spheroidal one, a vesicle that in an apparently indifferent manner contains a definite opposition, which it develops through its specific virtuality in the form of cell tissues. Each cell, with its parent nucleus and its enclosure, is a relative individual, a diminutive repetition of the whole organism; yet it is not an atom, as if the total organism were merely an aggregate mechanically composed of a multiplicity of cells. Rather, it is the ideal type of the organic individual that pervades the cell formation everywhere and, reaching beyond it from within, guides its formation into longitudinal and transverse fibers and the intergrowth of these into tissues of determinate shape, size, and color. There exists no moment in the whole organism in which it would not be sovereign as a productive subject. The self-formation of the organic individual differentiates itself: 1) according to the abstract ground-plan of the whole; 2) according to the particular proportions of the form; 3) according to the manner in which life brings itself to appearance in the form.

#### 1) Der Grundriß der Gestalt — 1. The Ground-Plan of the Form

#### §. 457.

The ground-plan of the form is either flat-faced or curved-faced, or mixed of straight and curved surfaces.

a) The abstract beginning of the formation of the organic individual is the rectilinear, or more correctly the flat-faced, for the straight line and surface are abstract, partly because they always follow the identical direction, and partly because they in themselves run to infinity and therefore, in order to be terminated, can only break off. This form is that of the mineral.

b) The opposite form is the curvilinear, or more correctly the curved-faced, for the curved line and surface not only changes its direction at every moment but is also able, precisely thereby, to return into itself in the most manifold windings. But as nature proceeds to the evolution of the curve, it also sublates the straight line within itself. It is not so abstract as to oppose to the flat-faced surface a pure curve-facedness. However, it initially combines the straight and curved line only in such a way that it winds the curve as a spiral around a vertically standing axis and makes cylindrical shafts the bearers of cone and sphere forms. This form is the fundamental one of the plant.

c) The fusion of straight-facedness and curve-facedness, such that in countless combinations and gradations the straight and the curved pass into one another, is the highest form of organic formation: the form of the animal. The further the organization advances, the more the individual conceals the abstract linearity of its framework behind softly swelling and subsiding forms, which blend wonderfully in spherical, cylindrical, and conical roundings. The infinite charm that the human form offers to contemplation lies precisely in the fact that in it every one-sidedness of form is annihilated, that every moment of the whole form indeed shows a decided character, but in this decisiveness never pays homage to a merely intellectual purity; rather, in every form it strives to see all the others as well. No animal achieves this harmony. The human head is a sphere and yet also not; the upper arm is a cylinder and yet also not; the spine is a straight line and yet also not, and so forth.

#### 2) Das Maasverhältniß der Gestalt — 2. The Proportions of the Form

#### §. 458.

In its particular proportions, the form of the organic individual is either regular or symmetrical or harmonious.

a) The regular formation is that in which the surfaces and angles enclosing the individual correspond as equal ones under the same axes. The irregular formation is the inequality of the axes, angles, and surfaces which, in their correspondence, should be congruent to one another. Regularity is the essence of the mineral proportions, and crystallography is merely applied stereometry.

b) The symmetrical formation is that in which the parts that are in themselves equal face each other in reversed order. Within symmetry, regularity continues as the form of its special structures, but through their reversal the equality is reflected in itself. Asymmetry consists partly in the absence of the correlate double member that is presupposed in itself, and partly in the merely regular repetition of the same. Symmetry is the essence of the vegetable proportions, in that a spiral winds around a vertically standing axis, whose nodes repeat themselves in an arithmetic progression with the exponent two, and indeed in such a way that the fundamental number peculiar to a plant reappears in all its particular parts. On the vertical axis there again stand horizontal axes — in themselves the same formation as the former, but reversed. The leaf of many plants divides so that one half of it has exactly the same formation as the other, yet each half has the same schema turned toward the left and toward the right side, and this inversion is precisely symmetry. In the crystal form, the twin crystals constitute the foreshadowing of this form.

c) The harmonious formation is that in which the symmetrical parts relate to one unequal to them in such a way that they form a unity with it. Here a greater manifoldness arises, a higher tension, for not only do the symmetrical parts relate through themselves to one another, but they also relate to the part unequal to them, which for its part relates to both. — Disharmony is the contradiction of formation when the inequality of the parts does not equalize itself again. Harmony is the essence of the animal proportions, in that the organs of movement, of theoretical and practical assimilation, and of nutrition — thus fins, wings, feet, eyes, ears, teeth, breasts, and so forth — form themselves symmetrically, but at the same time relate to a third element as their unity, which in the vertebrates finally encloses its principal mass in a special housing. The higher the animal stands, the more it develops the difference between outer and inner form, to the point where the inner, with the local positioning of the vital organs, in contrast to the symmetry of the extremities, even proceeds to disharmony — for example, the pericardium, stomach sac, and spleen lie on one side, while the other shows no correlate formations. Nature does not shrink from appearing irregular, asymmetrical, and disharmonious inwardly, and outwardly clumsy, strange, indeed ugly, for the sake of securing the economy of life, which is all that matters to it. It must be prepared to sacrifice the enchantment of beauty to the energy of life.

#### 3) Die Erscheinung des Lebens in der Gestalt — 3. The Appearance of Life in the Form

#### §. 459.

The ground-plan of the form and the measure of its particular moments are conditioned by life itself. The interior — that is, the actually existing vitality, the soul — is the in-and-for-itself prior of the exterior, and the classification of organic forms according to individual externalities as distinguishing characteristics has therefore rightly had to yield to the physiological one. The feeding apparatus, the kind of covering, the locomotive apparatus are only consequences of the psychic standpoint of an animal.

a) At the lowest level of the organic, there exists only a pure externality, because life, as it begins, immediately sublates itself again. The mineral individual closes itself off rigidly within itself without developing any further process within.

b) The vegetable individual constantly brings forth not only itself but also other individuals. Yet it is only metamorphosis, in that it reshapes its simple basic form, the leaf, through unfolding and infolding in the most manifold ways, and is indifferent to its relative productions.

c) Only the animal individual works toward protecting the organs that actually generate life against lethal injury as much as possible by relocating them into the interior of the form and surrounding them with a protective covering. The plant still displays all its processes and organs openly; the animal differentiates itself into an outer and inner side, establishes in the latter the hearth of life, entrusts the former with the organs of movement, and yet governs its whole periphery as a central interiority.

#### II. Der Lebensproces — II. The Life Process

#### §. 460.

The first deed of life is therefore to posit itself as living and to give itself form; the second, commencing simultaneously with the first, is the striving of the individual to preserve itself as a living individual. So long as it lives, it is a continuous process, a constant forming, dissolving, and reforming of all its moments. As a perennial change of substance, it incessantly alters its material existence, but all change also again changes nothing in it insofar as it remains in essence and form the same that it was from the beginning. The organic individual proves its vitality through the overcoming of the inorganic world, which it takes into itself and raises to unity with its life. Immediately, it is indeed itself the inorganic counterpart from which it draws sustenance, but without the intervention of inorganic nature it would also soon consume itself. Its organs presuppose the inorganic as the means of their stimulation; the inorganic is already co-posited in the organic as its passive side, but must also confront it as an other, so that it may sublate the semblance of this otherness. The living individual is always stimulated only by that with which it is already joined as by a pre-established harmony, and everything that is not already immanently its own in essence it lets pass with indifference. Through its individuality it has a specific need of the inorganic, feels its lack as a deficiency of itself, yet instinctively directs itself toward it with the certainty of overcoming it and appropriating it. The external seizure of the material is, in assimilation, a mechanical act; the dissolution of the inorganic in the organic does not occur without the chemical process; but the actual act of appropriation is, like the act of generation, an absolutely ideal one, a transformation of the material through the simple power of life. We call this transformation infection — the contagion of the inorganic with the soul of the organic, through which it is transubstantiated into its quality.

#### §. 461.

a) The mineral has no further life process than that of its formation in general; it has only affinity. Homogeneous minerals, situated in neighborly proximity, or in a homogeneous menstruum, grow more easily and beautifully. Once closed off in its form, the mineral falls prey to the general mechanical and chemical powers.

b) The plant, however, already possesses real vitality. The circulation of sap is not merely a mechanical-chemical pumping apparatus, but is produced by the plant's formative drive, with whose cessation it also immediately stagnates. Since, however, the plant cannot change its location and is therefore absolutely passive in its outward process, it must content itself with the material that the favor or disfavor of the elemental process happens to supply. For this reason, its intussusception is also one that is universally distributed within it and uninterrupted.

c) The animal, finally, is not merely alive, does not merely have a complete life process, but also appears to itself in its states. As an ideal unity reaching through all its processes, subsuming them all under itself as their unity, it is central subjectivity; reflecting the difference of its unity and their differences in itself as unity, it has feeling. This self-sufficiency expresses itself in the process of its life not only through the fact that it can interrupt its intussusception, but also through the fact that it excretes as dead everything that it cannot transform from the ingested inorganic material into its own vitality, and divests itself of it as a torturous burden. As it forms a mouth for itself, so also an anus. The animal therefore has not merely, like the plant in general, vitality; it also has sensibility — that is, return from all its processes into its ideality existing as self-feeling. — That the reproduction of the animal has its organs in the intestines, the irritability in the muscles, and the sensibility in the nerves is indeed correct; in the use of these expressions, however, it must not be forgotten that they are inadequate insofar as these so-called systems also dialectically pass into one another.

#### §. 462.

The process of life can be hindered in the most manifold ways from without and within. The living being can, at all stages of life and from all organs and their activity, fall ill. This possibility, as one inseparable from the concept of life, is necessary — but not its actuality. The oscillation of the individual, by which during its life it sometimes promotes one side of its organism above the others — for example, already behaves unequally during sleeping and waking — is not illness. The restless transformation of the organism within itself, the change of its states, is not illness. Illness arises only when the dialectic of the life process destroys itself. The harmony of the activity of the particular organs both with themselves and with the general vitality is health, which insofar exists only as a perpetually triumphant struggle over the ever-possible illness. Illness is therefore the contradiction of the activity partly of the particular organs with themselves and thereby with the universality of life, partly of the universality of life with itself and thereby also with the individual organs. But a single organ is not only the stomach, the lung, the heart, and so forth, but each cell. The illness is therefore neither merely a parasitic formation, a false individual within the true one, nor a mere relapse of life to a stage already overcome within it — as if scrofula were the larva, rickets the mollusk, dropsy the egg, scurvy the latent egg, and so forth (which has also been called retrogressive metamorphosis) — but rather illness is the estrangement of the living from the life process. Every moment, illness can begin from every point of the organism, but so long as this indeterminate possibility is always taken back again, the organism is precisely still healthy and maintains itself in equality with itself.

#### III. Der Gattungsproceß — III. The Genus Process

#### §. 463.

The life process of the individual manifests its particularity, how it relates to earth, water, air, and light. But the individual is in itself universality; it is its genus, and the genus has concrete existence only in its individuals. Outside of them it exists only as their concept, as the universale ante rem. And yet the single individual is not the genus absolutely, but only one specimen of the same, in which its essence comes to appearance only one-sidedly, not absolutely. The individual perishes in this contradiction. The history of its development runs through the following periods: 1) the individual period, in which the individual secures its self-sufficiency in order to equip itself for the act of generation; 2) the generic period, in which the individual goes beyond its isolation and continues the genus in another individual mediated through its deed; 3) the universal period, in which the individual withdraws from the tension of the generic difference back into itself, but only in order to sublate its isolation within itself — that is, to die. After the genus-act, nature, as it were, discharges it from its service, abandons it to itself, and gradually coffins it up.

#### 1) Die individuelle Periode — 1. The Individual Period

#### §. 464.

Originally, the individual, in forming itself and preserving itself, is absolutely egoistic. It must first consolidate itself in its own vitality before it can proceed to exteriorize itself for the preservation of the genus. Until sexual maturity, it therefore behaves progressively in this period of self-strengthening. During this time it can even develop organs that it later loses again once it has reached a still higher stage of life. Worms, for example, often show in their primordial state adhesive organs which, once they have used them to settle in a location, they lose again, like the barnacles and balanids. Antennae and eyes die away in some animals as soon as they are no longer needed. Insects originally often have a multitude of feet, which they lose and reduce to a few as soon as their wings are developed. In the vertebrates, the notochord disappears as soon as the vertebral bones develop. The batrachians lose their tails as soon as the hind feet have developed, and so forth. Nature lets such organs that have become superfluous, which function only provisionally, only for a certain epoch, partly drop off per dissolutionem, partly be absorbed per solutionem — the former especially in plants and invertebrates, the latter in vertebrates. Because of the disappearance of organs, this process has been called retrogressive metamorphosis. It is easy to see, however, that it has an essentially progressive meaning, because the losses only occur when the individual has reached that higher perfection which it developed behind the seeming framework of the lower form pushed forward for protection. Once the former is finished, it throws off the latter.

#### §. 465.

The individual self-sufficiency of the individual momentarily and more or less periodically always returns to the beginning of its existence. It differentiates its activity into one in which it stands in active rapport with its inorganic nature and assimilates it, and one in which it occupies itself only with itself, refreshes itself and renews itself after its initial self-formation, sinking back into its embryonic state and burying itself in its abstract being-in-itself. The higher an organism stands, the more decidedly this alternation comes to appearance in it. This periodic self-confirmation through the return to the primitive automorphism of the individual is sleep, which even plants do not lack; also molting, reskinning, antler-changing, and the like belong here.

#### 2) Die generische Periode — 2. The Generic Period

#### §. 466.

When the individual as such has reached the maximum of the realization of its peculiarity, it has thereby become capable of going beyond itself, beyond the relation to itself, and of really exteriorizing itself to the universality that is already immanent in it as its determinacy. At this stage it no longer relates to an inorganic nature that it overcomes within itself, constantly rekindling the selfhood of its life through such victory. Rather, it relates to life itself, and indeed to its own life as its other-being, which it is irresistibly driven to seek and in which to actually sublate its individuality, because animal universality is only real universality when the individual, as self-feeling, ceases to be individual for itself. Through the genus process, the individual reaches itself in the other individual. It enjoys itself in the other as genus. It fills itself in its singularity with the life of its universality, gives itself up as individual, and in the same moment receives itself back as genus. Hence the all-forgetting desire of the living to become intoxicated in the goblet of this bliss; hence, however, also the phenomenon that plant blossoms and lower animals die soon after mating. In the production of the new individual they have exhausted themselves, have swung themselves beyond their individual life, and, since the genus in its continued existence is secured through their deed, they can perish. The crystal has as yet no genus process, because it does not need one and can form itself from the chemical menstruum always and everywhere. The plant already has a genus process up to even such individuation of the sexes that they can be distributed among different specimens. The animal, finally, presents to us the most manifold forms of generation. Lower animals, like the polyps, sprout out into new individuals, or, like the globe-animals, pour out from a thin-membraned, albumin-filled bladder new globes that quickly repeat the same process. Other animals are hermaphrodites that fertilize themselves or each other. In the higher animals, from the insects on, the sexes separate; generation becomes more sparing in the number of individuals but can repeat itself all the more frequently.

#### 3) Die universelle. Periode — 3. The Universal Period

#### §. 467.

When the organic individual has reached in mating the highest realization of its natural possibility, its actual life as progressive is completed. A process of de-formation now sets in, more rapidly or more slowly, which ends with the extinction of individuality in death. Vitality, after it has lived itself out, breaks off its existence, and the de-souled individual then falls prey to dissolution by the elemental powers. This return of the individual saturated with the experience of its universality into its singularity — the wilting, wasting away, lignification, stunting, ossification, dying off — is no illness, no degeneration, but the normal process of the destruction of individuality that is necessary for nature. Death is necessary for it because it posits individuality as appearance of the genus. The genus process, in the momentary intoxication and in the generating of a new individual, sublates the individual only relatively to the genus; death, on the other hand, proves the absoluteness of the genus.

#### §. 468.

From the difference of its processes, the division of the concept of organic nature results:

1. The first of its processes is that of self-formation; as a particular function, it appears in the geological organism;

2. The second of its processes is that of self-preservation; it already includes self-formation within itself and appears as a particular function in the vegetable organism;

3. The third of its processes is that of self-feeling, which takes self-formation and self-preservation into itself, and of which self-movement is only the consequence; it appears as a particular function in the animal organism.

Thus the sciences arise: 1) Geology (Mineralogy), 2) Botany (Phytology), 3) Zoology. The name Biology should be restricted to the general concepts treated in this introduction.

### Erster Abschnitt. Der geologische Organismus — First Section. The Geological Organism

#### §. 469.

The first, hence lowest level of organic nature has as its principle self-formation, which is still as indifferent outwardly as it is to itself. It differentiates itself as mineralogical, geognostic, and geographical.

1. Mineralogical self-formation is the generation of the abstract, material individuals with which the planetary mass originally concluded its cosmic process of origin, so that the planet now appears as an aggregate of minerals, the result of cosmic chemistry.

2. The mineral individual is therefore everywhere equal to itself in its topical diversity. Its isolation is sublated in the strata in which homogeneous and heterogeneous geological formations are deposited as masses. These strata also make up only an aggregate, but in their succession have a definite progression from older to younger formations, which are at the same time differentiated in their chemical quality and together form the geognostic organism.

3. The surface of the geological strata contains, through character, form, and climate, the ground for the development of plants and animals. The strata come together in their horizontal and vertical juxtaposition to form general systems, which indeed in themselves also constitute only an aggregate of strata, yet outwardly delimit themselves in a distinctive way. They also rise above the surface of the planet's water envelope into its air envelope and in their articulation constitute the geographic organism of the earth, the plastic expression of its physiognomy.

#### A. Das Mineral — A. The Mineral

#### §. 470.

The concept of mineral individuation excludes from itself: 1) the general chemical elements; 2) the burned or decayed plant remains dug up as coal, brown coal, peat, or amber; 3) the remains of primordial animals occurring as petrifactions, as shell limestone, and as deposits of the siliceous shells of infusoria, often in enormous masses. All these fossils belong in principle to the higher levels of the organism, while the principle of mineral individuation is restricted to the self-formation of matter.

#### §. 471.

The mineral offers only an anatomy and morphology, no physiology. Its self-formation is the counter-struggle against the mechanism of gravity and against the dissolving dialectic of dynamic-chemical interaction.

1. At the first level it appears as one that still confines form to the separation of matter and its cohesion. The form is still held back by general gravity and hindered in its plasticity. This level of amorphism is constituted by the metals.

2. At the second level, the energy of self-formation presents itself through decided crystallization in the closed hardness of stone formation.

3. The third level, finally, contains individuals that emerge as crystalline formations from the neutralization of basic and acidic elements and therefore on the one hand point back to metallicity, on the other to lithism. These formations are the saline ones, which as the youngest and highest product of mineral individuation already have a direct relationship to the nutrition of the plant and animal world.

#### I. Das Erz — I. The Ore

#### §. 472.

The ore is nature's first attempt to arrive, from the unity and universality of matter, at an individuation existing for itself, which, as an initial one, contains less a complete formation than rather only an intimation of it in granules, little stalks, and flakes. It does not arise, however, through atomistic aggregation of an external juxtaposition, but through an imperfect crystallization. The atomistic hypothesis is already refuted by the emergence of all particular form from the liquid. The metal seeks to wrest itself from general gravitation through individual centralization and its own weight, as well as from the attack of physical powers through the firmness of cohesion of its molecular parts. Specific gravity and density, in connection with the otherwise still very indifferent color, constitute the principal determinations of the metallic mineral. The metals, very accessible to the dynamic processes of heat, magnetism, and electricity and the preeminent conductors of these, are still closed to light and reflect it with a distinctive luster. Within itself the metal differentiates itself as the light, the base, and the noble.

#### §. 473.

1. The light or alkaline metals are those which in their looseness and volatility possess so little self-sufficiency that they only ever occur in combination with other substances, namely: a) the alkalis, potassium and sodium; b) the alkaline earths, calcium, barium, and strontium; c) the earths, magnesium and aluminum.

#### §. 474.

2. The base metals are heavier and already appear in isolation. They show a progression from the greatest malleability through an indifferent phlegmatic softness to selfish brittleness. In color, the distinctions-less white and the blackish and bluish gray predominate, and only the red in copper makes an exception. The base metals divide into three related groups: a) iron, manganese, cobalt, nickel, and copper; b) bismuth, lead, tin, and zinc; c) chromium, antimony, and arsenic.

#### §. 475.

3. The noble metals are the heaviest and at the same time those which combine the greatest contraction of cohesion with the utmost expansibility. a) Mercury repeats at this level the peculiarity of the light metals in easily amalgamating with other metals. Its dynamic excitability is so great that it becomes liquid at elevated temperature and solidifies only at a very low one. b) Silver is the higher reproduction of the base metals. Brittle, dirty-white platinum is indeed specifically somewhat heavier than gold, but fundamentally only a paradox of silver pretending to occupy the highest level of metallicity. This belongs c) only to gold, which as the heaviest metal is at the same time the most ductile and, with the exception of chlorine, invulnerable to all chemical powers. In it, metallicity reaches complete saturation and therefore smiles upon us from it with the warm gleam of a pure, lovely yellow.

#### II. Per Stein — II. The Stone

#### §. 476.

In gold, nature indeed arrives at the most intensive self-sufficiency of specific matter, but outwardly it remains formless. Self-formation appears as truly self-enclosed only with perfect crystallization, to which the character of the substance becomes indifferent and for which everything depends on formal determinacy. A mineral individual that closes itself off around one or more equal or unequal axes with determinate angles, corners, and symmetrical surfaces is a crystal. The relative amorphism of the metal is therefore entirely sublated in the crystalline stone formation. Isomorphism and dimorphism can even occur: isomorphism when two chemically different substances crystallize in the same schema; dimorphism when the same chemical substance crystallizes in different forms. With the idealism of form, the oryctognostic individual maintains its self-relation against general gravity and proceeds from the night-side of the earth's womb to the day-side. The stones are less accessible to dynamic excitation through heat, magnetism, and electricity; they are insulators. But once excited, they hold the process more stubbornly through their formal being-in-itself. Finally, while the metal is opaque, the stone develops light refraction and, on the highest levels of its formation, the most beautiful colors. The stones differentiate themselves: 1) according to their form, 2) according to their cohesion, and 3) according to their chemical quality.

#### §. 477.

1. Crystal formation begins with the fixation of a core shape, whose primitive type continues evenly in the secondary extensions that are added layer by layer as leaves or lamellae. If the genesis of the crystal is favored by perfectly homogeneous liquid, by temperature, and by spatial conditions, it can attain great size and beautiful form. Most crystallizations, however, are carried out by nature only imperfectly in the turba rerum; we call the fully-faced forms holohedric, the others hemihedric. From within, one can conceive the structure of the crystal as circumscribed by an axial cross, according to which six main systems can be distinguished for stereometric comprehension: the regular or tesseral; the two-and-one-axial hexagonal or pyramidal; the three-and-one-axial hexagonal; the one-and-one-axial rhombic; the two-and-one-membered monoclinohedric; and the one-and-one-membered triclinohedric. From these forms as the fundamental ones, the remaining ones are then derived.

#### §. 478.

In the external plasticity of the form, the following are distinguished: a) the triangular surface formation of the pyramid, which progresses to the dodecahedral bipyramidal formation; b) the quadrangular surface formation of the cube, the parallelepiped, and the finally polygonal prism, which progresses to the hexahedral columnar form; the rhomboidal shape forms only a modification of this group; c) the combination of both basic forms in such a way that a middle, square-faced member is bounded pyramidally at its sectional surfaces — a shape that perfects itself in the hexagonal dodecahedron. Such a type is now, however, modified to infinity with the concrete formation. Calcium carbonate, for example, belonging to the latter type, occurs in approximately 700 modifications. In the greatest manifoldness, however, the self-formation of mineral nature always remains rectilinear, and only through truncation and chamfering of the corners does the crystal, in polyhedral formations, gain an approximation to the spherical. — In twin crystals, the simple individualization proceeds to a symmetrical doubling. The form of the grouping of crystals in fissures or veins is partly in rows, partly circular and cylindrical, partly star-shaped.

#### §. 479.

2. The crystal is hard precisely because it delimits itself decidedly outward and therefore also possesses in its molecular parts a determinate cohesion, which, however, according to the quality of the stones, shows a different degree of density, so that they form a hardness scale from the softest substance to the hardest, in which the harder substance is always able to scratch the softer, but not the latter the former. Thus from bottom to top follow: talc, gypsum (rock salt), calcite, fluorite, apatite, feldspar, quartz, topaz, corundum, and diamond.

#### §. 480.

3. That a crystal has precisely this and no other stereometric form and precisely this and no other cohesion is ultimately founded in the quality of its substance and in the temperature during the process of crystallization. The difference of the stone minerals, with regard to chemical individualization, must be conceived as the progressive elevation of the principle of individuality over gravity, so that the metal oxide, sulfur, and silicon unfold as the particular stages of this telluric self-realization.

a) The metal oxide presents the metal in a determinate crystal form. Because of the manifoldness of the acids, because of their countless combinations, and because of the great diversity of degrees of oxidation, this group is infinitely rich. If one compares the metal oxides, however, with respect to their magnitude, calcium from the alkaline earths, aluminum from the earths, and iron from the base metals emerge as the most significant. From the oxide a) of calcium are formed: fluorite, anhydrite, gypsum, calcium carbonate, and aragonite, with numerous varieties; b) from the oxide of aluminum: corundum, spinel (ruby), zeolite, clay, feldspar, garnet, and mica; c) from that of iron, finally: red iron ore, brown iron ore, iron pyrite, siderite, green earth, chrome iron, and magnetite.

b) What one usually calls earth are only indistinct metal oxides, poor synsomatia, which yet still show a certain form to the microscope — for example, clay. The first stone formation, however, that appears without a metallic base is sulfur, an octahedron that shows a beautiful bright yellow color. In its formal self-sufficiency it is, however, still very volatile, has in its easy combustibility and fusibility still something metallic, and willingly combines with all metals to form crystallizations that we call pyrites or sulfurates.

c) From this sympathy, finally, silicon frees itself and triumphs in the transparency of its crystallization over the nocturnal depth of gravity. It is the true stone. From silicon originate the actual scleroliths, which should be called precious stones not only according to cosmetic but also according to mineralogical estimation, such as rock crystal, amethyst, quartz, chalcedony, prase, jasper, agate, opal, and others. In the formation of the diamond, nature already goes beyond the mineral ground insofar as it uses pure carbon for this purpose — the substrate of vegetable formation. Coal, the residuum of the burned plant, is absolutely opposed to stone, for it is light, porous, and eagerly absorbs other substances into itself. And yet nature succeeds in producing from this the firmest, smoothest, most transparent stone with the strongest light refraction — which it, because it is such a perfected individual, seemingly secure in its indestructibility, scatters in rubble stone and gravel on the surface of the earth, and at whose edges it bends away from rectilinearity into an imperceptible gentle curvature.

#### III. Das Salz — III. The Salt

#### §. 481.

The unity of metal and stone formation is the salt. If in the metal, form falls behind the immediate determinacy of the substance, and if conversely in the stone, the quality of the substance disappears against the determinacy of form and texture, as the diamond proves to the highest degree, then in the salt the chemical composition is just as important as the crystallization. It has a moist luster, is white, is gray, is black, like most metals, but also plays, like the precious stones, into the more vivid colors. Since the salt arises through the equalization of the opposition of bases and acids as the perfectly neutral product, it is not rigidly holding fast to itself, but easily pulverizable, and soluble not through fire but through water. In ordinary occurrence it seldom completes its crystallization, but appears fibrous, radiate, hair-like, in granules, flakes, and efflorescences.

#### §. 482.

The salt now repeats all previous levels within itself and thereby also factually proves its higher position. Thus there are: a) metal salts, among which especially the sodium formations of saltpeter, the alum salts, the iron and copper vitriols, and the lead salts stand out; b) stone salts, namely the sulfur salts and the so-called amphid salts formed through silicic acid in combination with lime and clay; salt and silicon fuse together into transparent glass, a perfect stone formation; c) the actual salt, the haloid salt, also called rock salt or table salt, is the chloride salt, which appears both in the solid state in mighty deposits and in the liquid state in the sea, and is of the greatest importance for the existence of the vegetable and especially of the animal world. With it, then, the mineral points teleologically toward a higher level of organization.

#### B. Die Schich t — B. The Stratum

#### §. 483.

The individual minerals aggregate into larger conglomerates that attempt to close themselves off as geognostic individuals. We call such aggregate-individuals strata (rock types). Distinguished in them are: 1) their aggregate state; 2) their external form; 3) their stratification.

#### I. Per Aggregatzustand — I. The Aggregate State

#### §. 484.

In relation to itself, a stratum is: 1) conditioned by the individual form of the stone composing it, which consists: a) either linearly in a schistose or b) spherically in a granular texture, or c) is porphyritic when various substances are interspersed in a mass. Amygdaloid with its often large vesicular spaces can also be counted among the porphyry.

2. In relation to the qualitative character of the stone and the form associated with it, the stratum consists: a) either of one kind, uniformly of the same stone throughout, or b) heterogeneously — partly mixed, as in granite, partly blended, as in fused stones of different rocks; or the stratum is c) chaotically composed of more or less various fragments — partly so that a certain unity still prevails, as when a cement, as in oolite, cements the minerals together; partly so that in rubble stone and gravel a loose jumble of the most heterogeneous minerals exists.

3. But because the strata not only press upon each other mechanically as a juxtaposition but also act upon one another dynamically through their specific determinacy, they can also change chemically, as in schistification, calcification, silicification, and so forth — which has been called metamorphism.

#### II. Die Gestalt der Schicht — II. The Form of the Stratum

#### §. 485.

The form of the stratum in itself is flat, saddle-shaped, trough-shaped, shield-shaped, mantle-shaped, or hump-shaped. In its position, however, it is horizontal, vertical, or inclined.

#### III. Die Lagerung — III. The Stratification

#### §. 486.

The stratification of the strata is founded upon the original development of our planet. This was probably first a gaseous nebular star. The nebular star became a glowing liquid sphere. The sphere separated within itself a solid core from a sea and an atmosphere of high temperature. Thus the earth had already attained a certain form. Yet a great, violent revolution altered it. The gases enclosed beneath the solidifying and cooling earth's crust uplifted and broke through it. Long fissures yawned open; mountains raised their peaks; the glowing vapors melted the already crystallized minerals into plutonic masses; great rock masses traversed the horizontal strata in a vertical direction and often covered the youngest formations with the oldest. The water in its basins was pressed upon and often had to seek new outlets, washing away earth, rolling away stones, bursting rock barriers. Fire and water have therefore an equally great share in the formation and stratification of the strata.

#### §. 487.

1. The original products of fire are granite, syenite, porphyry, melaphyre, greenstone, and serpentine. From different perspectives they are called plutonic, massive, crystalline, abnormal, petrifaction-free, endogenous, pyrogenetic. 2. The products of water, the sediments, are greywacke, dead-lying rock, breccia, variegated sandstone, shell limestone, keuper, Jura limestone with lias and oolite, clay, gypsum, freestone, chalk, gravel, alluvial stone, and soil. From different perspectives they are called neptunian, stratified, crystalline, normal, petrifaction-bearing, exogenous, hydrogenetic.

The coal deposits between shale and primitive limestone, or between limestone and sandstone, as well as the molasse — that is, brown coal mixed with sand and clay — and peat, are as little purely geognostic formations as the petrified infusorian deposits and coral banks. The petrifications themselves, however, can in their occurrence only be regarded as a guide for determining the age of the strata; otherwise they must be incorporated as integrating moments into the system of vegetable and animal organization.

#### §. 488.

3. When the crystalline massive rocks, slowly pushed forth from the long gaping fissures of the primordial earth in a soft state and slowly uplifted by the giant force of steam, constitute the first system of geognostic stratification; and the stratified sedimentary formations, with their great quantity of carbonic acid in limestone, of siliceous earth in sandstone, of chlorine in the saline rocks, are to be regarded as the second system; then the third is that of the volcanic formations. This system is generated through the still continuing glow of the earth's fire, which melts the most diverse metals and stones into a fluid mass that, elastically uplifted, with its vertically ascending current breaks through the core rock no less than through the normal deposits and vein rocks, and, when it is granted to reach the surface of the earth, pours itself out on it, crystallizing and vitrifying in all directions.

The volcanic rock, as the mediated unity of all mineral products, contains them all partly dissolved within itself, partly it merely envelops them within itself, imitating the porphyry, and also does not lack the vesicular spaces that particularly characterize the amygdaloid. That in the particular volcanic mass formations, because of the contingency of their origin, now iron, now feldspar, now augite, now labradorite, and so forth predominates, and thereby a specific peculiarity is given to them as trap, tuff, dolomite, dolerite, phonolite, basalt, and so forth — this is natural.

#### §. 489.

The volcanic rock forms itself: a) into formless mass; b) into a bell-shaped cone; c) into a polyhedral column. The first form is that of lava in general, as it wells out of narrow fissures or from craters; the second is that of the trachyte domes, which constitute the mantle of the central volcanoes; the third is that of the actual basalts, which occur not only in horizontal stratification but also vertically erected. This latter form, often in beautiful hexagonal columns, even forms walls, which it again covers with a beam-roof. In it, the mineral organism reaches its final, boldest individuality.

#### §. 490.

The volcanic, dark-colored, heavy, and yet both mechanically easily pulverizable and chemically readily dissolvable by acids rock prepares the best soil for vegetation through its decomposition; the volcanic fissures likewise maintain a dynamic connection between the earth's interior and exterior, sublating the deadness of the geological ground and the rigidity of the rocks through the shaking and tearing of the earth's surface, and promoting the mixing of the individual minerals into fertile earths. Australia shows us how greatly river formation lags behind without the contribution of volcanic formations. Its rivers drag themselves about in pool-like and bankless fashion on the ground, whose topsoil rests unmediated upon the primordial rock.

#### C. Die Gestalt der Erdoberfläche — C. The Form of the Earth's Surface

#### §. 491.

The individual mineral is sublated in the stratum; the stratum, however, in its stratification is only a moment of the great articulation of the earth's crust as a whole. This articulation is in its essential features the result of that process of original self-formation of our planet belonging to the past. It may at first seem that pure contingency was dominant in the wild struggle of that titanic period of elemental powers. But the deeper penetration of geographical relationships manifests in them too an adorable order of reason, which is all the more interesting here because it could with apparent justice not have been expected. Geography is essentially natural science, for its task consists in demonstrating how the opposites of the solid and liquid, the horizontal and vertical, the core form and the coastal margin, the flora and fauna, equalize one another in a consequent dialectical sequence up to the harmonious totality. Treated as a scientific monograph, geography indeed makes it possible to extend its boundaries into the indeterminate. On the one side it can, since the earth is a planet, go back as far as astronomy; on the other, since the human being inhabits the whole earth and transforms it through culture, it can pass not only into ethnography but also into history. The political geography that reshapes itself with every great war and peace treaty is merely a variable moment of history. But even if the monograph may reach forward and backward, the systematic treatment of science may not allow itself such encyclopedic expansions, through which geography swells into the science of sciences. The astronomical determination of the earth falls, for geography, into the absolute mechanics of the heavens; the physical into dynamics, particularly into the concept of the meteorological process; but anthropology and history belong essentially to the concept of spirit, which prepares for itself in nature the presupposition and means of its appearance.

The formation of the earth's surface conditions the spirit and contains, because it exists in itself for it, the symbolic prefiguration of its world-historical course, which advances from the potamic formation of the great river valleys of the Asian Orient through the people-connecting Mediterranean thalassa across the Atlantic Ocean to the new Western world, where on the Californian peninsula the European, the Negro, the Indian redskin, and the Chinese unite, where morning and evening meet from opposite directions on the shores of the Pacific Ocean, and the eventual indifferentiation of all races must produce a new human type. The universal commerce of the ocean mediates in the peoples the consciousness of their human unity, in which they are only members. Geography as such is immediately natural science and has initially to omit all consideration of history entirely. The relation of history to geography, with all its consequences — including especially the alteration of nature through human activity — falls as much into the science of history as the gradual reception of the existence, form, and character of the lands into human consciousness — that is, the history of travel.

The division of geography can therefore contain only the description of the elements of the earth's surface formation, the landscape profiles, and the individual plasticity of the particular continents and countries.

#### I. Die Factoren der Erdoberflächengestaltung — I. The Factors of Earth-Surface Formation

#### §. 492.

The form of the earth's surface is in its principal moments the work of our star's development from its cometary and lunar epoch to its planetary one, in which it matured for the generation of life and for the solar solicitation of the same. This form, essentially still present, has as its factors on the one hand the solid geological element, on the other the liquid element of the water process. In and from them unfolds organic life, which clothes and crowns their forms with the manifoldness of plant adornment and swarms through them with the teeming animal world.

#### 1) Der orographische Factor — 1. The Orographic Factor

#### §. 493.

The form of the solid element in its abstraction is on the one hand the spherical plain, on the other the radius of the earth projecting as a secant above the level of its circumference. We regard the first form as the horizontal projection, although it is evident that no plain of the earth, since it is a sphere, can be perfectly level. The second form, the vertical projection, is the generally conical elevation of the earth's mass, which can be either a pointed or a truncated cone, and which we call, at a greater distance from sea level, a mountain; at a slight height, a hill; or, with a general depression, a slope.

#### §. 494.

The mountain can: a) stand quite abstractly alone in the plain as a hump form, such as the Petersberg near Halle; or it can b) join with other mountains into a mountain system — a) partly in a simple linear chain, like the ridge of the Kjölen Mountains and the Apennines; partly b) in a parallel chain, like the giant mountains of Burma, Siam, and Cambodia, or the Oregon Mountains; partly c) in circular form or grouping, whether the elevation forms a simple ring mountain around a plain, or whether from a central point, star-shaped radiations develop in a half or full circle. The continuous continuum of the elevated mass constitutes the crest or ridge height as the mountain range; the peaks projecting above it form the summit height of the mountains, according to which, for example, the summit height of the Pyrenees forms the ridge height of the Swiss Alps, the summit height of these the ridge height of the Andes, and the summit height of these the crest height of the Himalaya chain, in the proportion of 1, 1, 2, and 2½. Finally, however, the mountain can also c) repeat the plain within itself and then becomes a plateau, a high plain. If its summit itself is cut off by a plain, it thereby becomes a table mountain. — In mountainous country, the manifoldness of peak-splitting surpasses the continuity of the basic mass; in tableland, the monotonous continuum of mass surpasses peak formation, and the cleft mountain form often presents itself before it as the rampart of the border mountains.

#### §. 495.

With the form of terrain elevation, the depression is identical, for it is only the reverse side of the former. Through elevation there arises: a) in general, the contrast of lowland and highland; b) in particular, valley formation, which either forms a longitudinal valley with parallel, now open, now one-sidedly, now doubly closed mountain chains; or as a breakthrough of a mountain range, a transverse valley; or as a wreath-like enclosure of a depression, a basin valley; c) in detail, the valley can, through the fact that as a main valley it branches laterally into side valleys, acquire a very complex formation that in concreto goes into infinite manifoldness.

#### §. 496.

The unity of the horizontal and vertical projection is the scale of horizontal planes set upon an inclined plane, which we call an escarpment insofar as the horizontal character predominates, and a terrace insofar as the vertical character predominates. As the completed equalization of these forms, it becomes a terraced landscape.

#### 2) Der hydrographische Factor — 2. The Hydrographic Factor

#### §. 497.

The stereographic projection, through its elevations, depressions, and hollows, generally contains the prefiguration of the form that the configuration of the liquid element can assume. This element appears in relative rest or motion: in rest through the closure of a basin bounded on all sides — it becomes a lake; in motion through the free, boundless sea, which covers over two-thirds of the earth's surface. The lake is set in motion only by the air from without; the sea, on the other hand, has a general current from east to west produced by the planet's rotation, which immediately, as it is thrown back from the east coast of the continents, produces a partial counter-current from west to east. In particular, the sea has the daily movement of ebb and flow produced by the attraction of the moon and the sun, which becomes visible chiefly at the shores. In detail, however, it has on its surface an infinite manifoldness of currents and counter-currents, which develop through the difference of the submarine earth-surface form, through the difference of the warm tropical and cold polar currents, and through the difference of atmospheric movements.

#### §. 498.

The unity of the closure of the lake formation and the mobility of the ocean current is river formation. The channel of the river is in general prefigured by the ground form, but in particular developed by its own activity according to the force of its gradient and the water quantity it gathers. The river in its one-sided form is either the mountain torrent plunging down from a highland in cataracts, or it creeps from a hill plateau with flat banks through the plain. The complete river, the terraced-landscape river, unites in its upper, middle, and lower course all contrasts. In the alpine country it springs forth as a wild torrent, forms mountain lakes on the mountain massif, and breaks through the rocky barriers that oppose its entry into the plain with thundering cataracts. Now its middle course begins, often in meandering serpentines, sometimes distinguished by bifurcations. The lower course often characterizes its beginning through the rapids with which the river of a complete terraced landscape enters from the high plain into the lowland and in it, in contrast to the lakes of the upper course, develops islands. The exit of the mouth into a depressed basin produces the liman formation; if, however, the river disperses with periodic floods in the lowland, divides itself into several arms, and washes up a swampy, fertile foreland between their mouths, it produces the delta formation. If the river reaches no other river, no lake, and no sea, but loses itself in the sand, it is a steppe river; if the distance from the river's source to its mouth in the sea is short, it becomes a coastal river; if it is long, as with the rivers of the terraced landscapes, it becomes a continental stream. — On the floor of the valley formation, every river develops through the reception of brooks and tributaries a more or less manifold network.

#### 3) Der organographische Factor — 3. The Organographic Factor

#### §. 499.

The organographic factor is here a partial anticipation, insofar as it actually constitutes a moment of the particular forms of organic nature, and the presentation of the latter can conclude with the description of their distribution over the earth's surface — with a geography of minerals, plants, and animals. Yet that anticipation is justified by the fact that the formation of the earth's surface is completed through the higher organic nature, especially through the vegetable. Of the minerals there can be no actual geography, only a topography, since they, as indifferent to their locality, possess a terrestrial ubiquity. Plants, on the other hand, are thoroughly dependent in their spread and distribution on the character and form of the soil, on the dryness and moisture of the climate, on the temperature and light. According to the difference of temperature, the plant zones of the cold, temperate, and hot zones can to some extent be distinguished from one another in the horizontal projection. The arctic zone is characterized by mosses, lichens, alpine herbs, alpine roses, birches, and partly by conifers; the temperate zone by conifers, by summer-green deciduous trees, and partly by evergreen deciduous trees; the tropical zone by evergreen deciduous trees, by myrtaceae and figs, palms and bananas. This same sequence is shown by the immediate vertical elevation from sea level to the snow line. — A third line extends between the snow line and the pole as a step-scale, or rather the snow line forms a line that falls under the equator into the snow-capped peaks of the mountains and at the pole into the pole itself. In the concrete appearance, this sequence is naturally most manifoldly varied by the meteorological process, especially by the relation of the sea to the mainland, and the type itself of the same plant families individualizes itself differently in different continents. The individual unity of a particular landscape plant-region, which also presents itself aesthetically as a harmonious totality, we call a flora. From the mosses and saxifrages to the escallonias and calceolarias, almost thirty such distinctive circles can be distinguished, which in themselves again enclose a multitude of smaller local systems. Toward the north, the cryptogams are more prominent; toward the south, the phanerogams. In that direction, plants spread more in social form; in this, they individualize themselves more to individual self-sufficiency.

The animal is in its distribution dependent not only on the same conditions as the plant but also on the plant itself. Nevertheless, the boundaries of its range are more indeterminate, because it possesses locomotion. What great spaces does the elephant, the buffalo, the chamois, the tiger encompass! How far does the flight of birds extend! Where no plant can survive, animals still live, like the Protococcus nivalis even on the snow, and infusoria in the air itself. Some animals migrate with the change of seasons — especially fish, to spawn; birds for warmth and food; reindeer, camel, buffalo, antelope, and horse herds for pasture. Still others migrate sporadically, like locusts, mice, termites, and wood pigeons. The animal world therefore cannot be divided, like the plant world, into temperature zones, kingdoms, and local faunas. In general, the oceanic fauna is everywhere more uniform, the continental more manifold. In the latter, especially in the higher animals, phenomena develop that are peculiar only to individual regions. Among these characteristic animals belong, for example, the platypus and kangaroo of Australia; the giraffe, hippopotamus, and gnu of Africa; the two-humped camel, the elephant, and the tailless ape of Asia; the sloths, armadillos, and pangolins of South America; the bison of North America. Other animal species, however, such as the dog, the horse, the cattle, the pig, the sheep, and the rat — these companion animals of the human being — show themselves capable of an almost universal distribution.

#### II. Das landschaftliche Profil — II. The Landscape Profile

#### §. 500.

The orographic, hydrographic, and organographic factors come together in the earth's surface formation as relative wholes that we call landscapes. The condition of the landscape is: 1) the particular formal relation of the solid element to the liquid; 2) the core form of a land. These conditions, which constitute the outline of the whole, are sublated: 3) in the aesthetic character of the landscape.

#### §. 501.

1. Through the particular relation of the solid element to the liquid, a delimitation develops of the former as well as of the latter, which conditions the external articulation of a land, its general habitus. The solid element, namely, rising from the liquid into the elastic, forms itself as an island. All appearing earth surface is fundamentally insular. But one can distinguish an elevation of land above the sea in which the development of the coastal margin exceeds the size of the area enclosed by it, and one in which the size of the area absolutely surpasses that of the coastal margin. The former form is the insular par excellence, the latter the continental; the unity of both forms is the peninsular — that is, the half-island formation connected with a continent. — The liquid element forms itself either as a bay or gulf in the curves and serrations of the coastal margin; or it becomes a thalassa, an inland sea, enclosed on all sides, except for narrow passages, by land; or finally it becomes the free ocean, which has only whole continents for its boundaries, like the Indian Ocean between Asia and Africa, the Atlantic between Europe and America, the Pacific between America and Asia. The most articulated, absolutely unique sea as a thalassic one is the one surrounded by Asia, Africa, and Europe — the Mediterranean, into which, in the transition from the temperate zone to the tropical, four peninsulas with large coastal islands and many island groups extend, into which great continental rivers discharge, which reaches deep to the northeast through the Dardanelles into the Black Sea and opens itself to the west through the Strait of Gibraltar to the most current-rich and most articulated ocean.

#### §. 502.

2. A land receives its particular spatial configuration through its simple planimetric delimitation, its so-called core form, which can be rectilinear, curvilinear, or composed of straight and curved lines. This holds not only for land bounded by water — as New Holland presents a trapezoid, Borneo a circle, East India a triangle, the Iberian Peninsula a rectangle, and so forth — but also for enclaves, that is, land bounded by land, as are especially the oases.

#### §. 503.

3. The mutual delimitation of the solid and liquid elements and the geometric core form are sublated in the aesthetic character of the landscape. The landscape, a local system of spatial forms, unites the orographic, hydrographic, and organographic elements within those abstract contours into a distinctive physiognomy, which has either a monotonous, contrasting, or harmonious character.

a) The landscape is monotonous when in it one natural form as a maximum makes the other forms present disappear against it. Depending on whether the earth, the water, or the vegetation subordinates other forms to itself with one-sided dominion, it becomes: a) a flat, hill, or mountain landscape; or b) a lake, sea, or river landscape; or c) a grass, forest, or fruit landscape.

b) In the contrast landscape, one element enters into opposition with another, one form with another: a) the solid element with the liquid, like the rocks of the Norwegian coast with the sea; b) the barren land with the plant-covered ground, like the sand of the Libyan desert with the palm oasis of Jupiter Ammon; c) one form of the same element in contrast with another, like the plain against the mountain, or the waterfall plunging into a lake, or a tall-trunked forest in a grassland, and the like.

c) The harmonious landscape arises through the fact that it fuses unity and contrast into a new unity, by adding to the contrast yet another form that contrasts with both members of the antithesis, so that they now equally relate to it and in it elevate themselves to a new, higher charm. Thus, for example, it is evident that the wave of the Neapolitan bay and the luxuriant vegetation of its amphitheatrical shore first receive their full beauty through the column of smoke of Vesuvius towering above them. We call the harmonious landscape in its richest endowment preeminently the romantic — preeminently, for we also call contrast landscapes romantic.

#### §. 504.

Each of these forms can in its more particular determination be sublime, graceful, or beautiful. Or it can also be the opposite — common, repulsive, or ugly. And each of these moments can be composed by nature in a high, middle, or low style, each of them with each set in the most manifold interaction, rising to the most bizarre humor.

#### §. 505.

The stable elements of the landscape receive through sunlight a mobile charm. Light mediates in infinitely manifold transitions the change of seasons and times of day, and accordingly the spring, summer, autumn, and winter landscape is distinguished, as well as their analogue, the morning, midday, evening, and night landscape, which the latter can receive a peculiar enchantment through moonlight. These differences also present themselves in the individualization of the zones. The arctic landscapes are night and winter landscapes; the tropical landscapes are midday and summer landscapes; the temperate landscapes are morning and spring and evening and autumn landscapes. But at the same time these differences of zone also coincide with the difference of the aesthetic physiognomy of the landscape, for the arctic region is monotonous and desolate; the tropical rich in contrasts, colossal in its forms, magnificent in its coloring; while the landscape of the temperate zone strives for harmony of elements, equalization of contrasts, rounding of forms, and softening of colors.

#### III. Die individuelle Plastik der Erdoberfläche — III. The Individual Plasticity of the Earth's Surface

#### §. 506.

The ground form, the water, the plants and animal world, the external figuration and delimitation, as well as the distinctive landscape endowment, constitute the general morphology of the earth's physiognomy. But the earth's surface develops these to the determinacy of individual systems, whose maximum we designate with the expression "continent." We see how the pelagic element of the globe predominates on the southern half, the continental on the northern; we see how the land tapers pyramidally toward the south, extends northward as a broad continuum, and how an eastern and western hemisphere are distinguished. The question is now whether, within these general differences, the telluric structure shows a gradation of formation that from form to form, through constant integration of the lower into the higher, unfolds an ever greater perfection. This question must be answered through the attempt to find the relation of the given forms in their sequence — an attempt that must initially be made quite independently of history. Afterward, history will justify the reason of the structure of the earth's surface. In this structure, the eastern hemisphere appears as the one in which the essential forms of the earth's configuration emerge in relative isolation with characteristic expressiveness; the western hemisphere, however, as the one in which those forms bind themselves to a unitary concentration and mutual approximation. This difference is the decisive one for the earth's physiognomy.

#### 1) Die Osthemisphäre — 1. The Eastern Hemisphere

#### §. 507.

The simple basic form of the appearing earth's surface is, as demonstrated above, conditioned by the interrelation of the solid and liquid elements, and accordingly is the insular, continental, or peninsular. In the eastern hemisphere these forms emerge with sharp individualization, as the insular type presents itself in the Oceanian island-world, the continental in Africa, and the peninsular in Asia and Europe. In the landscape physiognomy, the Oceanian island-world appears monotonous, Africa contrasting, Asia with Europe harmonious.

#### a) Die Insularformation — a) The Insular Formation

#### §. 508.

The insular form, insofar as the development of the coastal margin absolutely surpasses that of the interior, is the most abstract. It begins with the chaotic fragmentation of the countless small islands in the groups of the Pacific Ocean, then proceeds in New Zealand and New Guinea to greater extent, until in New Holland it attains even a continental character. New Holland is the South Polar, monotonous continent. Its core form is the least articulated: that of a trapezoid parallelogram. Except for Spencer Gulf to the south and the Gulf of Carpentaria to the north, its coastal margin is smooth-lined. Apart from moderate mountain heights, especially toward the east side, it appears to be a flatland filled with swamps and stagnating rivers that cannot carve a deeper channel in the clay soil lying unmediated upon the hard primordial rock.

#### b) Die Continental formation — b) The Continental Formation

#### §. 509.

The abstractly continental form is that in which the central area absolutely predominates over the periphery and the insular moment appears only in a few coastal islands. This form Africa has — the tropical continent, the continent of abstract duality. Its core form is a triangle whose east and west sides are oceanic and whose north side is Mediterranean. It is: a) a flat coastal land, bounded by moderately high mountain ranges from which short-course terrace rivers spring; b) an interior enclosed by these mountains, which falls into the contrast of a southern highland and a northern, mostly desert lowland, from whose interior the lake-like Quorra River, reminiscent of New Holland's river formation, works its way westward in a long course; c) toward the northeast, a terraced landscape in which it attempts to break through its monotonous duality. It throws up an alpine mountain range and unites two rivers, the White Nile and the Blue Nile, into a third, the Nile, which it dispatches into the Mediterranean. Yet even here the duality continues insofar as the Nile remains almost always confined between rocks and, without a proper middle course, develops only a very short lower course and a rich delta formation upon entering the plain. Between the high mountain range and the flatland, the middle link of a gently sloping highland descending into hills is lacking here.

#### c) Die Peninsular formation — c) The Peninsular Formation

#### §. 510.

In the insular formation, New Holland is the foreshadowing of the absolutely continental; in Africa, the terraced land of Abyssinia, Nubia, and Egypt is the foreshadowing of the next higher form, which in its manifoldness at the same time repeats the earlier forms within itself. This higher form is the peninsular one of Asia and Europe, which geologically constitute only one great whole, Europe being nothing other than the western peninsula of the entire continent. That Europe possesses in the Urals a certain separation from Asia has only the same significance as the eastern and southern peninsulas are also separated by mountain ranges. Geography must not here be misled by a historical convention. But because this continent stands higher, it also repeats the lower forms presupposed to it within itself, in that: a) easterly and southeasterly a great island-world attaches itself to it — the Aleutians, the Japanese Islands, larger coastal islands, the Philippines, the Moluccas, and the Sunda Islands, which in Borneo even repeat the continental type of New Holland; b) northwesterly a continental massif rises, which falls into the contrast of a northern lowland and a southern highland, yet with the difference from Africa that the former is swampy, rich in lakes and rivers, and as flatland extends directly into the sea; the latter, however, is partly salt steppe toward the west, partly in the plateau of the Gobi toward the east an actual sandy desert, and partly between the Kunlun, the Nan Shan, and the Himalaya mountains in Tibet a grating-like furrowed tableland in which deep valleys, lakes, and icy mountain peaks alternate; c) the peninsular form decisive as characteristic for this continent, the advance of earth-formation, develops easterly, southerly, and westerly from the edge of the inner high mountain wall.
