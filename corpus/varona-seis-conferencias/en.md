## IDEAS OF MLLE. DE SCUDÉRY

### ON THE EDUCATION OF WOMEN <sup>(1)</sup>

Ladies and Gentlemen:

A fter thanking you for your kind and flattering welcome, it falls to me, before anything else, to declare that I do not intend to deliver a speech here, but only to entertain you for a few short moments with a simple lecture; for my purposes aspire to no more, nor do my powers reach any further.

Whatever the vicissitudes of this society may be, it will always have as its foremost aim the culture of woman: that is why it has seemed to me a fitting subject to speak to you of a celebrated woman writer

(1) Lecture delivered at the *Nuevo Liceo de la Habana*, on December 9, 1882.

[p. 6]

who concerned herself by preference with the problems that bear on woman in the home and in the world, and who was perhaps the first in modern times to proclaim the need to think seriously about her education.

Antiquity did not even suspect the existence of such problems. Woman, confined to the Greek gynaeceum, cared only to keep her industrious hands busy and gave her attention solely to directing the labors of her serving-women. “Go back to your rooms, and attend to your own tasks, your spindle and your loom; bid your maidservants do their work: debate is the business of men.” These are the words that old Homer puts in the mouth of young Telemachus as he rebukes his mother Penelope. The women who went out into the public square adorned their minds, it is true, as they might adorn their bodies, the better to heighten the incentive of their venal charms. The Roman matron, first among the slaves of the master of the house, lived under a régime no less narrow, in domestic and in civil life, and her greatest glory was to win the renown assured her by that famous epitaph: *Domi mansit, lanam fecit* [She kept the house, she spun the wool]. When the women of Rome broke the fetters of their hard and long servitude, it was to weary the world with their scandals.

[p. 7]

The great revolution, at once political, social, and religious, which overthrew the ancient world and opened a new career to humanity, the advent of Christianity, began to modify substantially the role of women in society. Its worship did not make them equal with man, but it gave them a much more prominent place than the pagan rites had done; and its dogmas above all, ceasing to speak only to the eyes and the intelligence in order to move the heart with a gentle sway, made them partake in an immediate and constant way in the most intimate life, the life of the feelings and affections of their brothers and husbands. If the apostles kindled with the word and the apologists fired with the pen, the female neophytes insinuated themselves gently by their intercourse and, according to the fine old expression, edified by example. Christianity had no propagandists comparable to women; to them was owed the conversion of entire nations: hence it has been possible to say, with a certain irony, that one of the conditions necessary for bringing about some of those great changes was a devout woman at court.

Furthermore, the spirit of the Germanic tribes, so quickly Christianized, was very favorable to the participation of women in [p. 8] social life. Thus, during the Middle Ages, there filtered through the compact meshes of that political organization, which seemed an immense net of iron, gentler customs, owing to the presence of ladies amid the jousts and tournaments, amid the din of the hunts, and even amid the clamor and martial preparations of the camps. For the first time and, strange to say, during the unbridled empire of force, there was voluntarily accepted in the world, as a moral notion, respect for the weak; it is true that here the weak bore arms of such temper that the finest suits of Milanese armor could not withstand them: beauty and grace. Those queens of loveliness took possession, in their courts of love, of the long sovereignty which they have maintained in the salons almost down to our own times, surrounding themselves, as ladies of honor, with all the fine arts, and joining to the charms of sympathy between the two sexes the incentives of a pleasant and festive intercourse, which afforded the weary and adventurous paladins hours of delight and of true leisure. From Normandy to Provence and Castile, and from Tuscany to Hungary, the lute of the trouvères and troubadours resounds only with the gentility of the ladies of the castles, whom they glorify as [p. 9] goddesses, setting them in a world of enchantments and marvels.

When Greek culture revealed itself to this society of brilliant ladies and of subtle, enamored poets, accustomed to distilling their feelings and refining their delights, far from undermining the empire it found established, it became, by a curious transformation, its most effective auxiliary. The Platonic doctrines on love came to be poured into the mold of chivalry: there are Greek philosophers who exclude woman from the realms of true love, and their commentators of the Renaissance so interpret their theories that they can lay them as an offering at the feet of the idol, of woman deified. Gallantry disdains the alliance, until then so fruitful, with the gay science, and takes pleasure only in the company and friendship of philosophy. Love, which had been the inexhaustible theme of songs and poems, becomes an exercise of discourse, a thesis for dialogues and discussions, and even an argument for interminable treatises. Woman, mistress of the senses, makes her triumphal entry into the world of the mind. The radical change begun by Christianity and continued by chivalry culminates in the Renaissance. Woman is the chief ornament of the courts; her modesty and [p. 10] decorum suffer nothing from direct contact with strangers; her feelings may now manifest themselves with greater freedom and without risk of indiscreet censure or fear of her own discredit. Tommasina Spinola, the beautiful Genoese, enamored of King Louis XII, can ask him without embarrassment to choose her as the lady of his thoughts. Later the tender and unfortunate Gaspara Stampa dedicates the collection of her rhymes to her fickle lover, to see whether, all those proofs of her passion and tenderness being gathered together, they might succeed in making an impression upon the heart of the faithless one; and all Italy resounds with applause. Everything is permitted, provided it be veiled in the finery of wit.

It is truly a strange and brilliant spectacle that Renaissance Italy presents. Each of its little courts becomes a focus from which the most exquisite civility and the most refined culture radiate; and in each of them there shine some of those exceptional women who held in chains, by their beauty and the ornaments of their intelligence, the princes of power and the princes of art. Vittoria Colonna, wife of a great captain and beloved of an illustrious artist, handles the classics and rivals Petrarch. Veronica Gambara, as illustrious by lineage as by talent, was most learned in Greek and [p. 11] Latin letters, and at the same time gave models to the Tuscan tongue. And so with many others. About them were grouped the most florid wits and even the most learned professors, and in those academies the language was polished, letters were enriched, and social intercourse gained in gentility, discretion, and suavity. That was the dawn of courtliness. When the magnificent Giuliano de’ Medici wishes to present the model of the perfect lady, he endows her with all the qualities necessary to shine at court and to excel in conversation. He wished her, according to Castiglione, to have *knowledge of letters, of music, of painting*; but only that her company might be the more exquisite and agreeable. He wished her virtuous, certainly, and also gentle, but above all affable and discreet in every kind of talk. This portrays for us that civilization and the part that women played in it.

Those small but sumptuous courts of Florence, Ferrara, and Urbino spread throughout western Europe the influence of their arts, their letters, and also their customs. The two great Latin nations of the age, Spain and France, although they occupied large portions of Italian territory as conquerors, recognized the superiority of its civilization and gladly sought to appropriate [p. 12] its exquisite culture. Italian ideas and tastes penetrated, then, into both; but with respect to the social role of woman, the influence of the Renaissance had a different fortune in each. There were learned women in Spain, who familiarized themselves with every sort of study, grave or merely ornamental; ladies of singular renown for their endowments of body and of mind shone in the palace of the kings; but none, outside that privileged precinct, succeeded in gathering about her the cultivators of the arts and letters, or in forming any of those sorts of free academies that have been called in France *salons*. The spirit of the Castilians, which was beginning to set the tone for the nation, obeyed too much the goad of jealousy, and the old habits of seclusion and reserve were too deeply rooted for Italian liberty to be easily acclimatized among Spanish ladies. Thus it is that when a Doña María de Zayas takes up the pen in defense of her sex, she seems rather to wish to excuse her boldness in launching the fruits of her wit upon the public than to attempt to combat the prejudices and errors of her contemporaries. And when Enríquez de Guzmán feels herself drawn, by her longing for knowledge, to frequent the lecture halls, she begins by concealing her condition and sex beneath masculine garb.

[p. 13]

Not so in France. With the doctrines of the Renaissance there came in many of the usages it had authorized. The focus of Italianism was the celebrated Hôtel de Rambouillet, where illustrious ladies held sway by their discretion, talent, and graces. Its first mistress was Italian, and endowed with all the charms of her compatriots. Her daughter, the Marquise de Rambouillet, followed her mother’s traditions scrupulously, and with her begins the uninterrupted predominance of French women in the good tone of society. Her salon was the arbiter of good and bad taste in conversation, in eloquence, in poetry, and in the theater. If Marino reigned there, there Voiture and Balzac debated, and through it there passed on their way to immortality Corneille and Bossuet. In this salon, surrounded and admired by these great wits, we are going to find the writer of whose ideas I propose to give you some account: Madeleine de Scudéry.

Few of those names that have been repeated day after day with love and enthusiasm have afterward been so forgotten as this one; so that it will not surprise me if it scarcely awakens an echo in your memory. And yet few women have reigned over a wider and more select circle of admirers, and not by the charms [p. 14] physical—Mlle. de Scudéry was not beautiful—but by the perennial attraction of a cheerful character that was naturally allied with the most perfect distinction and composure, by the salt of her ever fresh and animated conversation, and above all by her most discreet wit, so apt and disposed to the observation and analysis of human characters and passions. Few women writers, too, have enjoyed so great and so prolonged a popularity: the appearance of her works was regarded as a literary event, and they were read with delight by more than one generation. Friend of the great Condé, who took such an interest in the battles she described that he gave her lessons in strategy; pensioned by Louis XIV, who wished to offer to her merit the consecration of royal munificence; and crowned by the French Academy, which inaugurated with her the prize for eloquence; she received in her lifetime all the homage to which talent can aspire, and was forgotten, completely forgotten, not long after her death. Forty years later Voltaire recalled her only for a few agreeable verses.

Posterity, nevertheless, wishes to make amends for this unjust disdain. In our century the forgotten habituée of the Hôtel de Rambouillet has found champions of such worth that their defense alone suffices [p. 15] to establish her merit: Victor Cousin, the gallant scholar, who forgets philosophical systems to become the courtly historian of the illustrious ladies of the seventeenth century, as Brantôme had been of those of the sixteenth; Sainte-Beuve, the coldest and most perspicacious of critics, and therefore a decisive vote; Saint-Marc Girardin, who has known how to follow with such sympathetic attention the development of the manifestations of passion in all literatures; lastly, and only yesterday, Mlle. Chateauminois, who has perhaps woven for her her finest crown.

These are the ones who have taught us to dispense with, in those old volumes, the *préciosité* that now and then overloads her diction, the mannerism that comes to encumber the style, the anachronism in the ideas and sentiments of those Tituses and Scaevolas, those Tullias and Plotinas; to discover in that conventional Rome the France of the seventeenth century, and in the author of *Cyrus* and *Clélie* a shrewd and judicious observer of the customs of her time, interested in all the social problems that were already taking shape, capable of resolving them with good sense and energy.

It has been said, gentlemen, and on indisputable authority, that *Clélie* is the manual of good society in the seventeenth century, and this explains to us her [p. 16] extraordinary vogue. She who frequented and adorned the best circles of an age and of a court so polished had to be a perfect lady and very apt to teach good manners and good language. But without our undervaluing such fine endowments, and even sincerely believing that nothing has contributed so much to polish modern manners as the more frequent intercourse of men with ladies, by the elements of reserve and delicacy that it introduces into manners, into ceremonies, and into all social relations, I am inclined to confess that what most attracts and moves me in the writings of this singular woman is the tact with which she touches on the grave moral and social questions that concern the condition of her sex in modern times. She forgets nothing, neglects nothing. She studies the young woman of marriageable age, draws up a whole plan of life for her who accepts celibacy, and describes in the most beautiful and pathetic colors the fair and respectable old age of the aged woman worthy of the crown of years. Marriage, as is natural, preoccupies her in a preferential way, and it is remarkable to find in her pen the theses that have attained such vogue in later times. There is one of her characters who defends the rights of woman outraged or *disregarded* in pleas so ardent that they would arouse [p. 17] the envy of a modern propagandist of free love. But this serves only to heighten by contrast the interesting picture she presents of the mutual rights and duties of spouses; and if formulas sufficed to resolve so intricate a matter, it might well be asserted that Mlle. de Scudéry has given humanity the most excellent matrimonial formula. “The husband,” she said, “must be neither the tyrant nor the slave of his wife.” A commentary on this phrase might suggest a book that could be entitled *On Perfect Married Couples*.

And there is yet more to admire in this moralist disguised as a *précieuse*. She has not been dazzled by the brilliance of those salons in which there shine a Catherine de Vivonne, a Mme. de Sévigné, a Countess de La Fayette; nor has she been blinded by the smoke of the incense burned in honor of the new deities. She has seen woman turned into an idol for her outward beauty, and into an agreeable or learned pastime when she has possessed a lively mind or a cultivated intelligence. But, though raised upon the shield of the knight and idealized by the songs and sonnets of the courtier, she has not yet received the offering most worthy of a rational being, of the companion of man: esteem and respect. Mlle. de Scudéry has seen many princesses [p. 18] revered for their rank, many beauties flattered for their physical attractions, without any improvement thereby in the opinion that those courtiers and flatterers keep in their inmost hearts of woman as woman. Mlle. de Scudéry perceives that all this worship is external, inasmuch as it is directed only to external qualities. Hence she loses no opportunity to ridicule the education given in her time to persons of her sex, their manner of living remote from the things of the mind, their ignorance even in the most elementary studies, and the frivolity and coarse appetites that are its consequence. In these matters she has all the ardor of a reformer. I am going to prove it to you with passages from her own works. You will see that she does not soften the truths or spare the censures, even upon those who are the very object of her intelligent solicitude.

“Most women,” she says, “think that they ought to know nothing but that they are beautiful, nor to learn more than the art of dressing well.”

A reformer so exacting and so authoritative as Scudéry was might think himself entitled to repeat those words today. In another place she adds:

“There are women who can talk of nothing but [p. 19] fashions, and for whom all *gallantry* consists in eating well the sweetmeats offered them, and in eating them while talking nonsense.”

The writer here employs the term *gallantry* in the sense of the age, as the art of conducting oneself well and making oneself well liked in society, which today we call *courtesy*. But in order that women may turn insupportable chatter into interesting and attractive conversation, it is not enough that they frequent the court: what are formed by this are women “who say nothing but trifles in very *resounding* words.” Mlle. de Scudéry wants more radical and better-adapted remedies, and haughtily raises her banner on behalf of the rights of her sex to a complete education.

“Our lot is beauty,” says one of her heroines, “whence it follows that we should be spared the trouble of learning the sciences and the arts, that ignorance is no defect in us, since, if one is fairly agreeable and possesses a middling intelligence and much modesty, there is already enough for a perfect woman. Yet I am persuaded that women are capable of all the great virtues, and even that they have more sagacity than most men. Indeed: if one observes carefully men and women [p. 20] in the places where their education is almost equal, as in the country, you will find that women show more perspicacity than men; and it is therefore necessary to confess that nature has not treated us women worse than men.”

Whatever explanation be accepted, the fact is undeniable and proves that Mlle. de Scudéry knew how to see beyond the surface. As she has not concealed from her companions their faults, neither will she conceal those of the chief culprits. Therefore she writes:

“The rarest thing is that a woman, who can decorously dance only five or six years of her life, should employ ten or twelve in learning with great constancy what she ought to practice only five or six; and that this same person, who is obliged to have discernment and judgment until death, and to speak until she draws her last breath, is taught absolutely nothing whereby to learn to speak in a more agreeable manner, or to conduct herself with greater tact. If we attend to the manner in which many ladies pass their lives, one would say that they have been forbidden to reason and to have good sense, and that they are in the world only to sleep, grow fat, and say foolish things.”

I should like to impart to all of them her spirit and her [p. 21] noble nonconformity; hence she adds, in an outburst of indignation: “Very culpable are women in letting themselves be treated as slaves condemned to live preoccupied only with trifles.”

I fancy you must already be impatient to learn the program of reforms of this strenuous censor; but her program is contained in her criticisms and in another formula equally luminous and highly suggestive. She saw the remedy for that state of things in “a very spiritual and very solid education (*très éthérée et très solide*).”

It is certainly not a plan of studies, nor was this what I had had the honor of offering you. But if we disentangle its meaning in the light of the author’s own principles, it will give us all that even today we can desire for a serious reform of the education of young women. A very solid education, because woman is a sensitive and intelligent being, placed in the midst of a world whose phenomena affect her by modifying her existence, and she must know them in order to seek in them aid and strength, and to avoid the causes of harm and destruction, so that she may not go blindly where there is so much light, and may not substitute for knowledge unfounded terror or foolish confidence, which are two forms of [p. 22] superstition. Since she must live, and life is a struggle, let her take the best arms, those of science. A very solid education, because woman is a moral being whose feelings, desires, and passions exert a constant influence on the life of societies, which they can alter and disturb, as they can preserve and strengthen; because her role is preponderant in the home, where characters are tempered, from whose conflict or concord the prosperity or ruin of states afterward results. She who acts so directly upon the heart must know the inner man; she who leads the child by the hand must know how to show him his way. Since she moralizes or corrupts, teach her morality, theoretical morality and the fruitful morality of history. A very solid education, because woman is a member of society, and when by exception she lives for herself alone, she must know the rights that protect her, as she knows the duties she practices, and there is no reason for you to leave her defenseless when she is left in isolation. Since she is a juridical person, open to her the secrets of the law, let her know the organization and the mechanism of the social body of which she forms part and to whose development she contributes.

But at the same time Mlle. de Scudéry wished a very spiritual education. Has not [p. 23] woman a sovereign intelligence? Then people it with great and noble ideas, and she will know how to make them fruitful. Has she not an exquisite sensibility and a rich fancy? Then direct and exalt them, that she may delight in art, but not in art exclusively, and bring into her domain what will always be the patrimony of women artists: supreme elegance, grace, delicacy, which does not exclude depth, nor novelty, nor daring. A very solid and very spiritual education, so that faculties now in lamentable disequilibrium may develop harmoniously; so that woman, thanks to the judicious cultivation of what is natural and peculiar to her, may complete man and be his companion, and, if need be—how many times it is!—his best support on the rough and gloomy journey. For support is not only that which prevents the falls of the body, but also, and far more, that which makes impossible the prevarications of the spirit, the abdications of reason, the moral falls, infinitely more painful because they are irreparable. Woman thus educated, woman capable of raising herself to this noble role, without ceasing to be the magnet of the senses, will be the captivator of her husband’s intelligence, refreshment for his heart, a constant impulse that will lead him ever upright and serene along the path of honor and [p. 24] virtue. Happy will she be in her home, and happy the generations that shall issue from it, schooled and ready for the great labor of life!

The criticisms of Mlle. de Scudéry, and the ideas she set down on this most grave problem, were not wholly sterile, although for many reasons they may be considered premature.

That extraordinary woman who shared the bed of a humorous poet, poor and perhaps misunderstood, and that of the most powerful monarch of his time, Mme. de Maintenon, wished to put into practice at Saint-Cyr something of what her one-time friend had indicated; and Fénelon, the future mentor of princes, inaugurated his brilliant career as a writer by systematizing many of her ideas in the admirable *Traité de l’éducation des filles*. But these high examples must not delude us. The general way of feeling in her age and in the next is represented by the opinion of the Abbé Fleury, who taught without scruple that study corrupts the heart of women without improving their intelligence. As if there could be a solvent of the mind comparable to ignorance, and as if putting a bandage over one’s eyes were the best means of hitting upon the right road!

The efforts of the reformers were, [p. 25] therefore, destined to encounter almost invincible resistance and to be lost without appreciable results. It must be admitted, moreover, that the dominant spirit in them largely sterilized their generous ardor, because they could not conceive woman in the world except as displaying herself in the salons. At Saint-Cyr they prepared, first, ladies for the court, and then gentlewomen for the palaces and castles. They were flowers cultivated expressly for the hothouses. This confined their aspirations and their projects of reform within a very narrow mold.

In order that the men of our age might acquire the costly experience that the ancients lacked, it has been necessary that the wind of revolutions be unleashed upon the world and strew with ruins Europe and America, astonished and overwhelmed. The tremendous series of catastrophes which so many centuries of monstrous errors and of countless and nameless iniquities prepared and made inevitable, and which begins with the French Revolution, has made tremble to its foundations the old social edifice in which the men of our races had taken shelter for so many generations; and, at the same time that it overthrew thrones and scattered dynasties, it sowed germs of dissolution in [p. 26] the most deeply rooted customs and in the most ancient beliefs, threatening to undermine and perhaps destroy even the most venerable institutions. The sociologist cannot conceal the facts from himself, however painful they may be. The very constitution of the family is undergoing grave modifications among highly civilized peoples, and one must reckon with the influence of preaching and of example. All those commotions and their consequences have presented to us, if not in a new light, in a different aspect the social role of woman. Political convulsions have flung her into the public square; we have seen her salons converted into clubs and her pens placed at the service of the ideas that impassion our age; the continual wars, bloody as none others, have left innumerable widows and orphans alone and thrown upon their own resources, obliging them to compete for life in the midst of this maelstrom of present-day civilization; the early relaxation of family bonds has emancipated in certain countries not a few young women; and all this has brought women into the industries, the professions, the scientific careers reserved until recently to men. There has been seen what is perhaps, what will doubtless always be, an exception; but what may exist, what does exist, the [p. 27] woman alone, a social individual, a member of the State. Since this has happened so frequently that it has come to fix attention, it was no longer possible, without imminent risk to the individual and to society, to postpone any longer the reform of the education of women. Our age has inscribed this problem among those that most urgently demand a solution, and on every side there spring up projects, studies, plans that recall the celebrated formula that I have repeated to you here this evening: An education for women, solid and spiritual, or, in other words, professional and intellectual.

Is not the moment, therefore, opportune for recalling the judicious, discreet, and wise woman who proclaimed it two centuries ago? We owe her much, we who witness with joy the dawn of this great, necessary, and well-understood emancipation; much do we owe her these posthumous honors, this grateful remembrance; much do we owe her this reparation for the unjust oblivion to which she has so long been condemned. The seed she entrusted to an apparently ungrateful soil sprouts today, grown into a vigorous tree that promises us abundant fruit.

May this beautiful example serve as a consolation to many. Oh yes! All you who bear in your breast an ideal of liberty and justice, and feel yourselves disheartened [p. 28] by the setbacks of the present hour, by the adversity of circumstances, more powerful than any human will; take comfort, considering that society needs, in order to subsist, to realize right, which is as it were its natural level, the one that all the silent labor that agitates it seeks, and the one that, at last, it finds. Years pass, centuries too, in which the beautiful ideal is a chimera: you have already seen it in the case I have presented to you; but that idea, scattered to the wind of contradictions, provided it be just, have no fear that it will be lost. It will become incarnate in its day in a system, in a law, in an institution; it will mark a new course for humanity and change the face of the world. Long may be its eclipse: long also will be its noon. So the dweller at the extremities of our globe, plunged in a night that is eternal to his anxieties and terrors, fears that the light will never again come to greet his eyes, sees the darkness prolonged without end, and when he is already fainting, there rises before his astonished sight the resplendent sun, and rises so full of glory and majesty, so luminous in its long course, that man, rejoicing and rapt, believes then that night shall never return.

## THE SOCIAL IMPORTANCE OF ART <sup>(1)</sup>

Ladies and Gentlemen:

N ever as at this moment have I so deplored not feeling myself equal to the situation in which circumstances have placed me, because never had so brilliant and at the same time so imposing a spectacle presented itself to my eyes; and on no occasion has it seemed to me so difficult, for my slender powers, to hold in a worthy manner the attention of an audience as distinguished as that which honors me tonight by disposing itself to hear me. For I have no personal titles to recommend me to

(1) Address delivered at the Teatro de Payret, on January 21, 1883.

[p. 30]

your benevolence, allow me to trust in your gallant courtesy, and at the same time in the importance of the theme that I propose to elucidate before you, since by no means can I aspire to captivate you with the novelty or the brilliance of my words.

I shall not need to exert myself to justify the choice of my subject, for it follows, as simply as it does naturally, from the very act that is being performed at this moment, thanks above all to your valued support. It has sufficed that there should sound the voice of a father to whom adverse fortune has shown the fair future of a daughter placed in jeopardy, a daughter on the point of realizing the most flattering hopes, now about to tread the threshold of the world of art, where applause and renown await her, presages of a useful and glorious life; it has sufficed that he should call upon you, for the generous people of Havana to hasten forward to extend a firm hand to one who wavers, and to smooth for the young artist the road to glory. This movement of so fair a spontaneity, this as it were instinctive warning that a work not only beneficent but fruitful was about to be accomplished, well deserves to be analyzed, for it will not thereby lose the seal of nobility that disinterestedness stamps upon human acts; rather will it acquire the irresistible [p. 31] force that is given to impulses by the full knowledge that they conform to the dictates of reason. That is why I have resolved to set forth to you, with the brevity that the circumstances require, how much it matters, and why it matters, to peoples to protect the arts.

Faced with the feverish and urgent activity of which our century gives incessant proofs; deafened by the deep and monotonous roar of the immense factories; dazzled by the marvels that science applied to industry has produced, the science that has given wings to articulate sound and has lighted our nights with a light more intense than that of the very stars; some minds, more sensitive than perspicacious and farseeing, lamented the abandonment of the arts and foretold the irreparable decline of every artistic manifestation. The century of steam and electricity was not to be the century of painting and poetry; the age that acclaims as its benefactors the Fultons and the Edisons was not to consecrate with its applause the inspiration of a Virgil, the genius of a Michelangelo. And the reason is that, in their loving cult of art, they had come to idealize it in such a way that they considered it as something extrinsic to human nature; as something essentially divine, wholly incompatible with the [p. 32] other fortifying activities that make up our being; whereby they failed to recognize, without suspecting it, the greatest, the true excellence, the grand and legitimate value of art as an emotional and expressive element in the life of man, and therefore as an element of communication and sympathy in the life of societies. The human individual neither lives nor can live in isolation, in the sole converse of his mind with itself, attentive only to the throbbings of his heart, in the selfish contemplation of the luminous ideal of his conscience. When he feels himself possessed by a generous emotion; when some beautiful spectacle strikes upon his spirit; when he kindles before the heroism of valor or of duty, he needs, with an imperious necessity, to transmit the feeling that masters and stirs him to his equals in sensibility, in intelligence, in moral dignity; and for this gesture is little, and language poor and obscure, and he needs to animate his figures that they may speak to the eyes, and to give color to objects that they may attract and dazzle the sight, and to put into sound so great a part of his soul that it may seize irresistibly upon all souls, and purify them, and raise them to the level of his own feeling. Thus all that is great, all that is noble, pure, and beautiful in the world and in humanity finds enduring form and expression [p. 33] pathetic in the sculptor’s group, in the colorist’s canvas, in the musician’s scale, or in the poet’s lyre. Thus art, which completes and universalizes language, gathers, preserves, and transmits the most select of all that man observes, thinks, and feels: the flower of the culture of an age, the most exquisite of the affections of a people or of a race. The intense life that it imparts to its works vibrates and spreads across the generations, calling forth by its powerful evocation not merely the men, the societies, the institutions that passed and vanished, but the perishable and transient feeling that possessed them for a moment in the succession of the ages. Thus there lives for an instant and throbs within us a spirit that is not our spirit, and our soul is enriched with affections that are not its own, nor those of its country and its time. Would you have the cold and skeptical analyst of our own days share in the ardors of ascetic mysticism, that he may appreciate and understand them? No description, no discourse could accomplish it with so much rapidity and force as a simple painting, the *Monk in Prayer* of Zurbarán. Upon a mass of intense shadow, which scarcely lets one guess the deep arcades of the cloister, there stands out, bathed in a ray of light, a kneeling and motionless man. Between his clenched hands is seen a [p. 34] yellowed skull; but his eyes, veiled in the half-shadow cast by the hood of the coarse frock, are not fixed upon it; they look on high, they look into the shadow, with an expression of such sweet vagueness, of such rapture, that we feel him absorbed in the contemplation of an inextinguishable light. His hands cling to the earth, to death; they grasp the nothingness of ephemeral man; but before his sight the heavenly felicities hover, and his half-opened lips let escape no moans of grief, no sobs of agony, but the fervent hymn of the soul in ecstasy before the splendors of eternal glory.

Would you pass from the life of the cloister, with its faintings and its raptures, to the life of the public square and the council, with the animation of traffic and the tumult of popular enthusiasm? Would you penetrate that municipal spirit which has so aggrandized the small states of the North Sea coast? Do not shake the dust from the archives, nor linger to leaf through the cold pages of old narratives. Seek out some canvas of the Flemish masters. Let us choose Rembrandt’s *Night Watch*. It is wholly full of figures, men in every costume and attitude, arms of every kind, and merriment on every face. One slings over his shoulder a heavy arquebus, [p. 35] another brandishes his pike, another leans upon his halberd; the captain is about to draw his broad sword, and the standard-bearer raises above the group the banner of the city. The whole subject of the picture is reduced to the sortie of a municipal guard on a festival day; but so great is the animation of these figures, so much satisfaction is seen in these burghers at their civic duties, that it costs no effort whatever to recognize in them the sinew and the spirit of those modest municipalities that knew how to vanquish the most seasoned captains, to repel the invasions of the most powerful monarchs, and to strike terror into the formidable armadas that ruled the seas.

And the artist has no need to breathe those airs of liberty: art breaks the gag that the suspicion of tyrants places upon the mouth of peoples. Rightly has it been said of absolute monarchy in France that it was a despotism tempered by satirical songs. In vain does one seek to draw an impenetrable veil over the intimate life of the despot, and to hide from the avid curiosity of his subjects—who understand that there is a mortal where they are meant to see a demigod—the piteous tragedies that are wont to have for their stage the magnificent chambers of royal palaces. One [p. 36] among them all presents itself at this moment to my memory. Upon it has been condensed all the darkness of history: so willed the unyielding will of its principal actor. A prince in the flower of his years, heir to the vastest monarchy of Christendom, shackled in his father’s very palace, dies a strange and mysterious death, and a few months later so does the young queen who had once been destined to share his marriage bed. A profound silence follows throughout the nation upon the stupor produced by the terrible coincidence: the somber figure of Philip was there to impose it; but in the end there is one who breaks it: not many years pass before the tragic event unfolds before the astonished eyes of the public, to give to posterity the key to the enigma. Who has dared so much? An artist, a poet; and the instrument has been a work of art, a drama: Lope de Vega in *Punishment Without Revenge*.

Thus, when all is silent and opinion seems drowsing, and the pen falters in the writer’s hand, and even the philosopher in his retreat doubts whether it will be lawful for him to hear the oracles of the intelligence; since the human spirit cannot succumb, since there is something that throbs and strives for life even in the faintness of an entire people, there will always be a more sensitive soul in which [p. 37] may be gathered all the emotions of those dark and glacial epochs, and who will fling them forth into the world in a groan or in a sarcasm. Michelangelo carves his *Night*, to whom her sleep of stone is pleasing *mentri che il danno e la vergagna dura* [so long as the harm and the shame endure]; Goya draws his Dantesque visions amid the darkness with which superstition surrounds a society likewise lethargic. Recall the state of Spain in his time: the generous efforts of those learned and liberal men who surrounded Charles III had not sufficed to free the people from the terrors nurtured in its own conscience; the Inquisition, though a mere shadow, still rose like a fearsome phantom that reigns in the darkness; the Expurgatory Index rose proudly, a landmark set up by ignorance on the threshold of the temple of fanaticism. Recall it, and you will understand all the daring of the protest, of the bloody mockery with which the artist breaks openly with the murky prejudices of his contemporaries. I know nothing in the history of art, apart from some scenes of the *Inferno* in the *Divine Comedy*, more energetic, in its sublime horror, than that drawing in which Goya paints a corpse half buried. His livid and distorted face reveals the last convulsions of the agony; it seems that the last [p. 38] throbs of life, for there still flutter about him, like horrible nightmares, the last images, the grotesque figures that represent the errors, the crimes perhaps, of his existence, and among which there stands out the fleshless hand that holds the inflexible balance. But something more terrible, more horrible than all this, he has found in the bosom of the grave; for, rising by a convulsive effort, and propped upon his left elbow, he writes with his right, like a revelation that bursts terrible from the tomb, this fateful word: *Nothing!*

With such vigorous means of expression at its service, art must not limit itself to revealing to us the intense emotion that dominates an individual, even though that emotion be produced by the social medium in which he moves: much wider is its sphere of action, and more important its social function. If you isolate the most sublime artist, even though you do not succeed in blinding altogether the source of his inspirations, since it springs from the innermost part of his being, you inevitably mutilate his genius and sterilize the best part of his work. The artist does not create for himself: he creates to transmit his passion, to seek other worshipers of his ideal; he produces for his fellows and needs his conceptions to be refined, to be embellished [p. 39] and even transformed, reflected and refracted in thousands of hearts and consciences. He gives the key that the rest are to repeat in concord; and when he succeeds, as true artists always do, his own passion comes to be the passion of all who surround him, his aspiration that of an entire people. Behold how every great collective feeling, all national beliefs, are concentrated in the soul of the artist in order to radiate thence in such a form as to inflame the most lukewarm, the most apathetic. Thus have the great artistic manifestations been formed, which serve to keep feeling alive and to transmit it. There you have the example in national anthems: not otherwise can be appreciated the significance of the epic or legendary poems that in the various countries the generations have handed on one to another. What does that epic of the *Fox* come to be, which is born in the center of Europe, when the Middle Ages were already advanced, spreads through France and Germany, passes to England, and reaches our own times with such vitality that it succeeds in inspiring one of the most eminent of modern poets? Nothing other than the representation, in rhymed words, of the constant preoccupation of that whole epoch in those nations; of the tenacious struggle of the interests and principles that agitate [p. 40] and divide them; of the duel to the death engaged between intelligence and force; a happy allegory in which the people discovered without effort the vindication of the popular conscience, which begins to set its rights as a barrier against the growing invasion of the monopolies of the feudal lord and of the courtly nobility.

Societies are organisms that, the more coherent they are, the better resist the surrounding and adverse forces, and you see already what a powerful means of provoking and maintaining cohesion among men a rich artistic production is. Those who learn to feel, in the same way learn at once to love one another, for there is no communion that draws together and unifies more than that of feeling. Wherever you find the same works equally esteemed, studied, and exalted, do not look whether there are fictitious divisions, whether there are frontiers that separate: there are men whose thoughts communicate, there is a people. Italy, divided into pieces by the foreigner, sold to the highest bidder by her natural princes, her secular discords fomented by the clumsy policy of the Catholic Church, an object of profound grief to her sons and of pity or disdain to strangers, subsists, remains one in the heart of the Italians, who repeat in unison the sorrowful [p. 41] tercets of Dante or the inflamed octaves of Tasso, adore at once the Madonnas of Raphael, and raise their prayers together to the measure of the melodies of Rossini. These were the glorious forerunners of Italian unity.

I said before that art multiplies a hundredfold the great power of language: that is why its influence is not limited to one race, nor halted before mountains, nor dies where the boiling waves of the sea begin. The artistic production of a people, once it attains the characters of excellence and duration, radiates around it, as from a luminous focus, and carries to those who surround it, or in any way enter into contact with it, prolific germs of culture, of instruction, and of stimulus. Let us not speak of the peoples who cultivate friendly relations among themselves through the commerce of products and of ideas; let us speak of those who seem most separated, of those who are divided by opposing interests or by flagrant injustices: of the state of war, in short. There is no spectacle more frequent in history than that of a conquered people imposing upon its conqueror its civilization and its arts. Recall that long combat of two peoples, that struggle of centuries between the Christians and the Spanish Muslims. Everything divided them: faith, race, customs, political interests, the [p. 42] state of ideas; and yet, gentlemen, the splendid culture that had made Andalusia the wonder of Europe is so reflected upon the hard warriors of the North, so infiltrates the ideas of those peoples alternately slaves and masters, that not only do the voice and the doctrine of the Muslim sages and artists resound in the court of the Castilian kings; not only does the people driven back at the end of eight centuries to the burning sands of Africa bequeath to the people of the reconquest the portentous monuments it had strewn along its path, that it may learn and produce a new architectural style; but it leaves the indelible seal of its spirit upon the words that the conquering nation involuntarily takes from it, to designate the objects of the grand art that its embittered enemies had transmitted to it.

What wonder, then, that that great destroyer of human works, indefatigable time, should also be vanquished by the duration of the works of art? Only through them does the mortal succeed in seeing realized in some fashion the most deceptive perhaps, but also the most ardent and tenacious of his illusions: immortality. Through them, which often keep the most precious deposit: the soul of a people. What did we [p. 43] know of those colossal empires of the East, of which there had reached our ears only, re-echoing from century to century, the immense rumor of their dreadful fall? Strange fables, ludicrous legends that the Greek historians had gathered out of mere and childish curiosity. But no sooner does the bold science of our century stir the rubble and the ruins buried under the dust of so many ages, than from among the broken obelisks, between the massive columns, from the somber depths of the hypogea, the spirit of antiquity rises, animates the mural paintings, illumines the attentive face of the sphinxes, shakes from its secular lethargy the entire pantheon of monster gods, and reveals to us through the voice of art the lost secret of those remote civilizations.

And without going back so far in the course of time, if we carefully examine the stock of our notions and concepts, if we wish to trace the origin of many of our customs, of our predilections, of the very taste with which we assay the most refined productions of the aesthetic sense, we shall have the plainest demonstration of all that lives with immortal life in humanity solely through the ministry of art. This civilization of which so much [p. 44] we proclaim, this culture of which we are so proud, are penetrated by a spirit that is not ours nor that of our immediate predecessors: the spirit of two peoples sovereign in intelligence and in sensibility, whose luminous trace neither the cataclysms of nature nor the revolutions of men have sufficed to efface from the world: Greece and Rome. For do you know, gentlemen, how the Renaissance imposed itself and triumphed? By substituting one system of teaching for another, overthrowing scholasticism, which was logic, and putting in its place the humanities. And these are nothing other than the study, the interpretation, and the admiration of all that in the sphere of art and literature the Athenians and the Latins had left stamped with the seal of their superior taste; the most complete triumph of the artistic beauty created by peoples and races already extinct over the men of other and very different ages. For four centuries Europe has again frequented the Porch and the Academy, the schools of Athens and Argos, the hippodrome of Olympia, the Roman forum, and the Alexandrian museum. And the result has been the marvelous fecundation of all that humanity had elaborated, in the Middle Ages, by virtue of the genius of the ancients: the most exquisite flower of the whole culture of the West.

[p. 45]

You see now, gentlemen, how art, the artistic manifestation of the great upheavals and of the permanent sentiments of the human being, is so effective a means of communication among men, so refines and invigorates their aspirations and points a direction and an object for their activities, that it draws tight the social bonds, contributes in the highest degree to the aggrandizement of peoples, increases their power and their influence, serves as vehicle for their most useful and most beautiful ideas, for their inventions and their systems, and thus assures them the gratitude and the admiration of posterity. And so it stands manifest, without further effort, how much it matters to peoples to protect the arts.

But I cannot consider my theme exhausted without first establishing a necessary distinction. There are two ways of making effective that support, that aid which powerfully encourages the artist and places him in a condition to produce with ease: official protection and the protection that I shall call *popular*, because it springs spontaneously from the very bowels of the people, when it is sufficiently enlightened and cultivated to appreciate the treasure that it defends and secures. I shall not deny all efficacy to the first—as the partisans of certain economic schools are wont to do—especially in the form in which the [p. 46] modern States practice it, and for countries placed in certain conditions; but I shall maintain that it runs into such drawbacks that at every step it is falsified and adulterated. It has been said that all the works the State undertakes turn out more costly: all the more must they be so in matters that have to be entrusted almost exclusively to individual taste, and in which it is always rightly to be feared that pomp will be sought, as a tribute to one’s own vanity, rather than the real utility and necessity to which attention must be given in all public expenditures. Moreover, how is the door to be closed against bastard influences, which favor mediocrities, in order to open it only to distinguished merit? How much incense has not been burned in praise of the munificence of monarchs! How many grandiose monuments has not their creative breath drawn out of nothing! But, if we examine many of these constructions, they appear to us as empty of meaning or as out of proportion to their object as those immense masses that still dominate the course of the broad Nile, destined only to enclose the handful of dust to which a mortal is reduced. What have the historians not said of the generosity of Louis XIV, who, not content with pensioning the artists, the poets, and the savants of his nation, sent presents and assigned [p. 47] life annuities to foreigners! But in the royal roll how few illustrious names! how many obscure names! It is plain enough: the monarch knew that Richelieu had splendidly rewarded the foreign men of letters who had written in his praise, and in this way was beforehand in soliciting such praises, sure, as he was, says Voltaire with his characteristic ingenuousness, of deserving them. Quality did not enter into his calculations. From every quarter, indeed, a concert of praises arose in honor of the French Maecenas, and with this gilded dust the royal libertine blinded the eyes of his people, that they might not fix them too closely upon his royal scandals.

More equitable, in that it is bestowed upon real merit, upon the merit that makes its way by its own worth or by its persevering efforts; more lasting, because it does not depend upon the taste or the fickle caprice of a sovereign or a minister; more fruitful, because it always takes the road of the true needs of the age and of the country, is the other form of protection, the popular. When there is awakened or maintained in the whole body of citizens a fondness for the fine arts, and all seek and encourage their productions and share in their spirit, and there is well-being, esteem, and applause for the artist; when there flourishes and spreads a good [p. 48] taste, which penetrates even into the intimacy of the home and is reflected in the decoration of houses, in customs, in ceremonies; when there is artistic life in a people, then it is that art shows itself luxuriant and vigorous, then that it produces its truly immortal works. All the civil architecture of the Christian peoples was born in this way. The town halls of Brussels and of Louvain vie in grandeur and magnificence with the superb mansions of the Capets or the Bourbons, and eclipse them by what they signify and represent. In proportion as public liberties are affirmed and extended in the Low Countries, and the people, enriched by industry and the useful arts, turn their attention to the things of the spirit, the flowering of the fine arts reaches its apogee. Bruges, emporium of commerce and wealth in the north, is a focus of artistic culture, and rivals the queen of the arts in the south: free Florence. Antwerp and Amsterdam see architecture shine in splendor, and endow the world with a school of painters whose fame the marvels of Italian painting have not sufficed to tarnish. In Spain, while the ancient capitals preserve vitality sufficient to resist the stifling centralization of the court, there are [p. 49] as many other centers of artistic movement; and the school of Seville in painting, and that of Valencia in dramatic poetry, precede those of Madrid, send them their masters, and give them their models.

There is, moreover, another aspect of the problem that must not be neglected: the moral aspect. Collective protection, and therefore anonymous, better defends the personality of the artist against the risks that are inevitable when the protection comes from the powerful; it raises his condition and secures his independence, which is absolutely necessary if spontaneous and sincere works are to be produced. The eminent poet who perfected the Tuscan tongue in an imperishable monument, the author of the *Jerusalem*, never passed beyond being a domestic of Alfonso d’Este: the great, the inimitable Molière was no more than one player the more in the company that delighted Louis XIV in his leisure hours. Today the artist lives by his works and his genius, and what he receives from the public he returns to it a hundredfold. Even those who were most disfavored and once even despised, the actors, are now considered everywhere on a level with the other citizens, with no consideration alien to their conduct and personal qualities limiting the sphere of their relations in society. This example alone would suffice [p. 50] to recommend and to make preferable a form of protection that is as effective as the official and is exempt from its risks. All peoples that come to acquire that consciousness of themselves, without which there may be larger or smaller groupings of men but not societies truly organized; that collective consciousness which makes them feel in unison and be impassioned for the same ideals and rejoice in the same joys and grieve at the same misfortunes and catastrophes; all peoples that attain this height will always regard it as one of their most pleasing duties, and will hold it one of their most glorious titles of honor, to present to strangers a copious artistic production, favored and encouraged by their intelligent generosity.

It would be easy for me now to prove that our age, which may be distinguished among all those of history as the one in which nations have concerned themselves most with themselves, have become most clearly aware of what they are and of what they can do, and in which their different elements have come to see themselves fused in that form of consciousness of the whole of which I have just spoken, possessing, in short, the notion and the feeling of solidarity; is precisely the one that has produced the greatest artistic riches and that [p. 51] has rewarded more liberally those who have gathered them. It would be easy for me to show how this century which bores through mountains to open a way for the swift locomotive, and unites the oceans, and breaks the impassable barrier of the polar ice, seeking a wider field for its activity and more space for the need of work that goads it; is the same century that raises Ristori and Nilsson upon pedestals, draws Fortuny from obscurity and surrounds him with glory, burns all the incense of its praises in honor of Verdi, endows Dumas with a royal fortune, and celebrates in his lifetime the apotheosis of Victor Hugo.

I might also point out to you how, as common interests have united a greater number of men across frontiers and have brought a greater number of peoples closer together, leveling the walls of age-old hatreds and of tenacious suspicions, it has become necessary that there should be extended and perfected the art that best satisfies the new and pressing demands of universal communication: the divine art that most gently moves the human heart, because it epitomizes, translates, expresses, in a language understood by all, whatever trembles and vibrates in the most sympathetic fibers of our soul, alike in the smiling hour of glorious hopes and in [p. 52] the somber moments of despair and faintness. You all know already that I allude to music, the favorite art of our times. But an eloquent voice is making ready to trace before you that beautiful picture, with a coloring to which I certainly cannot aspire, and it falls to me only not to delay you further in those much-desired moments.

Let it be permitted me, however, to remark that we ought not to part, after having spoken of so much grandeur and magnificence and having glimpsed something of the splendid world of art in its glorious career across the ages; after having tried to sketch what that lofty sphere of the life of individuals and of peoples signifies and is worth, without fixing a loving glance upon our Cuba. Not to draw vainglorious parallels, but to recall that, notwithstanding having lived so long forgotten and obscured, in spite of the slow and prolonged gestation of her culture, as soon as she has entered into communication with the sympathetic current of modern ideas and has been able to make herself heard in the concert of civilized peoples, she has sought to crown herself with the laurels of letters and of the arts. And the world has not denied them to her. Some of her artists have gathered applause and renown, love and esteem, in the [p. 53] very centers in which the fine arts now shine with purest brilliance; the voice of her distinguished poets has made cultivated Europe turn its eyes toward our shores lost amid the mists of the immense sea; and if at this moment I do not enumerate their names for you, it is because I should fear to offend those who still live, and because the admiration that our hearts cherish for them has no need of this testimony. For something more than to send them barren praises have I recalled them to you now: that you may pause to consider that, although the courage and the effort have been great, we cannot yet call the result great. The duty that falls upon us all of favoring our artistic life springs spontaneously from this consideration, true however painful it be to a certain degree.

Cuba, richly endowed by nature, pampered until yesterday by fortune, if she has been famous in the world, has owed it chiefly to the splendor of her riches. Although some of her illustrious sons have shone gloriously in the eyes of strangers, they have always been regarded as extraordinary and happy exceptions. Today, tremendous internal upheavals and external events no less grave have made us descend from a [p. 54] pedestal that was so little solid; and, without our forgetting the useful arts and the tasks of industry that make firm the foundation of every social edifice, a new and ample road opens before us to recover the lost rank and to sustain the brilliance of our name. Heirs of a sumptuous art, in constant communication with the cultured world, with excellent natural gifts, the Cubans may well acquire for their country the aureole of intellectual and artistic culture. I believe I have shown you how much it matters to peoples to acquire it. Therefore I address myself to all: to as many as here have greeted the first light, to as many as receive sustenance from this generous land, to as many as feel themselves united to her by any sacred tie, to as many as make up this young and vigorous people; to all I address myself, saying to them: honor, protect the arts; favor the intellectual life; assure your children the enjoyment of the pleasures that do not stain the body nor contaminate the spirit; open new springs of imperishable riches for beautiful Cuba, so that this beloved country may not come forward ashamed, may not present herself empty-handed at the magnificent banquet, at the royal feast that the great, the resplendent civilization of our century offers to all peoples.

## CERVANTES <sup>(1)</sup>

Ladies and Gentlemen:

A mong the diverse feelings that give tone to our mind, few are so powerful, or so capable of inflaming the fancy and moving the will, as admiration. When the beautiful or the grand occupies our sight and subdues us, there follows, as it were, an inward reaction of our powers that expands our spirit, lifts it out of its level, and seems to raise it to the height of the object contemplated. And since in man there is no sensation, nor image nor affection, that does not in one way or another

[p. 56]

turn into action, or at least into a tendency toward action, the need to imitate, to realize, so to speak, the resemblance, acquires in such cases an irresistible energy, which makes of it a happy instrument of personal education and of progress. We strive to appropriate, to possess that loveliness; we would give our souls the temper of that grandeur. Behold here explained the commanding influence of superior men over their contemporaries, and the indelible mark left upon humanity by those who tread the radiant summits of heroism or of genius. For this reason no religion has bound men together more durably than the fervent love, the worship, we might say, of great men. Even today, after so long a succession of centuries, we try, moved by respect, to penetrate the dense darkness that surrounds the life of Homer, and we halt, awestruck, before the dauntless stoicism of Marcus Brutus. Wherever, in whatever age, a man has possessed in an eminent degree any of the qualities that ennoble our species, there posterity has gone to seek him, to hail him as a forerunner and acclaim him as an example.

The study of those illustrious lives, the appreciation of the deeds and the works of those men [p. 57] of eminence, has been a favorite labor of human curiosity, which has wished to discover the prime motive, the powerful spring that impelled them throughout their whole career to rise and excel, their character, in short, product at once of all the forces of their spirit, exponent of all their activities, bond of union of their acts, objective expression of their personality, whereby they are distinguished and set apart from the uniform and colorless multitude of their fellows. To study a man’s life is nothing other than to bring a character to light. But even the most obscure, insignificant, and monotonous life would be an indeterminate problem, an unconnected succession of scenes without appreciable meaning, if along with the man and his deeds we did not attentively consider his habitual society, his most frequent occupations, the great and small events of which he is witness or in which he is actor; in a word: the medium in which he develops, which shapes him and urges him to action. And if the study of these factors, by their very complexity, is always difficult and laborious, what will it be when we deal with one of those individuals so richly endowed that they succeed in reflecting in their clear and vast intelligence the inextricable web of human life, whose faintest threads they follow and know, as if they were present at [p. 58] the invisible loom on which they are knotted; so exquisitely sensitive that in their hearts there echo alike the great and the small joys, the brief sorrows and the tremendous griefs of humanity, like those sympathetic strings ready to vibrate to all sounds; so powerfully expressive that they have an accent, an image, a formula, for every passion, for every aspect of life, for every problem of society; and who, after concentrating in themselves all the throbbings, all the ideas, all the dreams and the aspirations of a country or an age, know how to fix them in imperishable forms, in order to give them back to the humanity that inspired them, as its image beautified and perfected? The primordial data of human nature are then combined in a manner so various with the results of historical circumstances, that all the lights of erudition and all the sagacity of criticism are scarcely enough to allow us to trace an outline that will not afterward prove a capricious offspring of the fancy. We must reconstitute the times and the society in which the personage flourished, in order to place him in his natural medium, to contemplate him in his true light, and to be able to appreciate him at his genuine worth and in all his significance. Consider now whether these difficulties are not [p. 59] greater for me, who cannot count upon such effective aids, and who must nevertheless undertake so vast an enterprise, in deference to the wishes of the distinguished persons who direct this society, pressed besides by the form and the limits of an oration; and prepare yourselves, I beg you, to hear me with benevolence.

The *Liceo* devotes this evening to commemorating the anniversary of the death of Cervantes: the object of my address must therefore be to study this eminent writer in relation to the political and social state of his country in his time, in order to try to discover in his deeds and works his character, and to follow the development and witness the full flowering of his powerful intelligence. If my faculties were equal to my subject, I could with certainty presume that I should awaken your interest and captivate your attention, making you see the happy harmony that reigns between the work and the life we are about to examine, by virtue of which, if the productions of the artist are interesting, the actions of the man are no less so, to the point that we find a hero where we had expected only to admire a genius.

Cervantes was born at the moment when the heroic age of Spain seemed to reach its culmination, about the middle of the sixteenth century, when the emperor Charles V did not yet give [p. 60] any sign of weariness, and the rumor of his arms and the splendor of his glory filled the world. The scene that presented itself to his eyes in the dawn of his youth, in the fair age of easy admiration and of fervent and active enthusiasm, was capable of producing complete dazzlement in the most experienced eyes. Everything about him displayed itself full of life and luxuriance. The kingdoms, gathered under the scepter of Philip the Second, vied with one another in the various spheres of social activity, each according to its situation, its population, and its customs, all contributing to the inner prosperity of Spain. From the Northwest to the South, following the coast throughout its length, and then penetrating to the very center of the country, if the products varied, the unremitting labor of man did not slacken in wresting from the earth its most prized yields: the fruit of the Cantabrian country and the grain of both Castiles were as famous as the marvels of the orchard of Valencia and of the vega of Granada, where plants of all climates flourished in delightful consort, and where cultivation, grown too narrow by now in the depths of the valleys, climbed boldly up to the very summits of the Alpujarras. Extremadura sustained countless herds, and Asturias, Navarre, and the Basque region were likewise covered [p. 61] with pastures and flocks. Diverse and rich industries increased the credit of the nation. The morocco leathers of Córdoba, the cloths of Segovia, the damasks of Talavera, the tissues of Toledo, the silks and brocades of Seville, had no peer in Europe; commerce between the several kingdoms and with foreign lands was at its height; when the fairs of Medina del Campo were spoken of, men reckoned only by thousands of millions; Barcelona gave the law of commerce as far as the most remote markets of the ports of the Levant; but above them all rose Seville, converted, without hyperbole, into the emporium of the commerce of the world, tributary to which were Flanders no less than Italy, and to which flowed the treasures of the East Indies and the inexhaustible riches of both Americas.

If the prosperity was so great, the power was no less. The dominions of the King of Spain stretched over all the Earth: men of the most diverse races, of the strangest tongues, obeyed his scepter; in Europe he was lord of the fairest part of Italy and of the industrious and opulent Low Countries; in the very center of the West, like a watchtower between France and Germany, he possessed the Franche-Comté; a seasoned army, a formidable fleet, great generals by sea and land, and diplomats [p. 62] shrewd at every court, assured his preponderance in the affairs of the world. The Spanish nation beheld itself at the summit of grandeur, and was as if possessed by the vertigo of the heights. From the monarch, the cold and suspicious Philip, down to the obscure adventurer with no other patrimony than his sword, all had an unshakable faith in the power and the fortune of Spain, and believed her resources unlimited, all enterprises possible, even the most fantastic dreams attainable. The real and the imaginary mingled in their minds in equal proportions, producing before their eyes the strangest and most brilliant mirages.

This was the moral atmosphere that Cervantes breathed in his youth, impetuous, ardent, eager to know the world, to enrich his intelligence and to exercise his activity: an immense horizon opened before him, offering to his mind golden prospects, still lost in the distances of the future, but glimpsed and already enjoyed in the promises of his rich imagination. He loved letters, the nourishment of lofty spirits stirred by a wholesome curiosity, and he saw them flourish in his country with unwonted luxuriance. Castilian prose acquired, in the pen of the Mendozas, the Granadas, and the Leóns, the characters of amplitude, grandiloquence, and [p. 63] majesty that constitute its chief ornament; poetry was transformed, following in the footsteps of Boscán and Garcilaso, and sharing in the abundance and harmony of the Tuscan meters; the study of languages and of the humanities, of the sciences and of philosophy, was held in great esteem and assured renown and social importance; the universities could scarcely contain the multitude of students who thronged the lecture halls, and it was necessary to found places of study for Spaniards in the principal university centers abroad; his own native city, the celebrated Complutum, disputed with Salamanca the primacy of intellectual life, and in the halls of its famous colleges more than eleven thousand young men were gallantly preparing to win renown and honors, submitting for long years to the severe discipline of study. Cervantes was not counted among their number: the resources of his parents were too slender; but he shared in their enthusiasm, he had to supply by his application and his efforts the means that fortune denied him, and even to cultivate his intelligence he had to separate himself from the generality of his companions and taste the bitter aftertaste of isolation. But what did that matter, in that early hour, to the youth eager to taste at any cost the fruit he had dreamed of as exquisite? Cervantes wished to study, and he studied as he could.

[p. 64]

Moreover, his condition as a nobleman imposed upon him, as a patriotic obligation, the service of arms, and his natural impetuosity made it desirable to him. There was then no place on earth where Spain was not contending, either to secure her dominion or to extend it, and daring navigators had just opened to her the empire of the sea. From the most remote parts of the globe there came to the ears of the Spaniards an incessant rumor of arms, and there passed from mouth to mouth the tales of encounters and battles with innumerable multitudes, of conquests of immense empires and of treasures beyond reckoning, of exploits of fable made real and tangible. To tread the mainland or the islands of the Ocean Sea, to unsheathe the sword and win a lordship like a kingdom, must have seemed as easy to conceive as to accomplish to so many young hidalgos without a roof, though with a known ancestral seat; to so many younger sons, reared in abundance and destined to little less than misery. Cervantes too felt the impulses that draw men to the active life, and the fascination of glorious perils. To unite the cultivation of letters with the trade of war was neither new nor unprofitable in that age: so that his double vocation must not have embarrassed Cervantes much at the outset.

[p. 65]

The first, his literary inclinations, were without doubt what led him to accept a position that in those times implied no personal discredit, and that assured to wits of little or no means the leisure necessary for their studies. He entered, as a familiar, the household of a dignitary of the Roman church, and in his company passed to the land of his artist’s dreams: to Italy. The splendors of the Renaissance still lit the peninsula; still, in spite of the clerical reaction, of the rigorism of narrow minds and the indifference of accommodating ones, readier to follow the currents of the favor of the great than the dictates of their conscience, the beautiful teachings of the Ficinos and of the Pico della Mirandolas fired privileged intellects, and the beauty wrought by the divine hands of a Leonardo, a Michelangelo, or a Raphael bore immortal witness to its excellence. Cervantes could have known Giordano Bruno, who has been called *the knight-errant of philosophy*, and could have heard Torquato Tasso, posthumous and sublime child of that grand wedding of ancient art with the modern spirit. Those illustrious representatives of a proscribed ideal inspired in him one of the feelings most natural to youthful enthusiasm: the worship of a glorious past.

[p. 66]

His gaze widened over new and splendid horizons; he heard talk of beauty, of valor, of virtue, of happiness, in a new language, and he formed that very lofty conception of art which fitted him for his role as a literary reformer; he came to know a new interpretation of the classical world, he saw a different society, which also called itself *its heiress*, and he was able to draw fruitful comparisons, and to acquire that very lofty notion of life which prepared him for his role as censor of manners and as philosopher.

Tranquil observation and speculation held him for some time in their golden nets; but neither could his character accommodate itself to the situation he had accepted, nor were circumstances favorable to retaining in the contemplative life an impetuous youth in the first ferment of his young manhood. Cervantes had not been born to be a courtier, as he himself says in that unadorned language in which he is wont to utter the bitterest truths; he had shame and could not flatter. The atmosphere of a palace was bound to seem too rarefied to a noble mind, enamored of chivalrous sincerity and of justice unadulterated by favor or interest. At the same time, all about him was life and movement, nothing was spoken of [p. 67] but heroic designs, of grandiose enterprises, and all Italy resounded with naval preparations and the din of arms. A new and imminent peril threatened the nations of the Mediterranean shore, and behind them Christendom and its glorious civilization. Islam, conquered so many times, had gained new impetus by the transfusion of vigorous blood: under the leadership of the Turks it had established itself firmly in the extreme east of Europe, was contesting victoriously, inch by inch, the north of Africa, peopled with its daring corsairs the Adriatic no less than the Tyrrhenian, menaced Malta, seized Cyprus, and threatened at one and the same time the Empire, Italy, and Spain. It became necessary to attempt a supreme effort; the Latin peoples prepared themselves with resolution and enthusiasm, and the Catholic king was called upon to lead the enterprise. Cervantes, drawn by the glory of the undertaking and by the renown of the commander, the celebrated Don John of Austria, hastens to take his place beneath his standards and takes part in adorning—he, a humble and obscure soldier—the famous day of Lepanto. He lay prostrate with fever in his bed at the break of the dreaded day of battle; but at the first shots, deaf to every other voice than that of his high-spirited sense of honor, he hastens to the thickest of the fray, from which [p. 68] they were at last to carry him, badly wounded and maimed forever in the left hand.

This seems the prologue to a life that is to run its course amid noisy applause and to arrive at the lawful possession of honors and fortune; but it is only the first scene of that interminable series of catastrophes and misfortunes that make up the whole web of this extraordinary man’s existence. He dreamed, no doubt, from his poor pallet, of the glorious career that his own valor and his merits had just opened to him, and he awoke in a dark dungeon in Algiers.

The reward and the advancement he hoped for were suddenly changed for him into the captive’s chain and the horrors of servitude among the enemies of his faith and of his country. Long and painful was his slavery; but from the trial his heroic soul came forth once for all assayed and proven. In Cervantes, the flowering of the loftiest virtues was a preparation for, and a warning of, the maturity of his excellent genius. He bore pain, not merely as a Stoic who takes pride in despising it, but as one who knows that it is a natural accident in a human life and that it is more beautiful to turn it into a motive of perfection for oneself and of teaching and example for others; therefore he lived among the Christians who [p. 69] shared his lot as a brother and missionary. He never accepted the yoke, and tried to break it on every occasion, now alone, now in company, heedless of danger, but wishing to take upon himself the whole responsibility. Each time that one of his rash plans was discovered, he stoutly cleared his accomplices of all guilt and presented himself undaunted as the sole one deserving punishment. He never forgot, in so anguished a situation, the peril of his country, and, laden with irons, he conceived the project of destroying that nest of pirates, freeing in a single day all the captives, and delivering the security of Spain from a permanent menace. If he did not accomplish so great an enterprise, it was not through neglect or lukewarmness on his part: he had everything ready; but there failed him the succor he expected from his government. And yet, in the midst of these struggles, of this perpetual yearning, of such harsh labors and with such risks of torture and death, he preserved so entire a possession of his mind that he did not neglect the cultivation of his spirit; and when he could not encourage his companions with promises of liberty, he at least consoled them with his verses, written in the native tongue, to speak to them of their country, so near in reality and so distant for their powerless efforts. Thus, amid the fatigues [p. 70] of military life as well as the toils of captivity, he remained faithful to his first vocation, and went on aspiring to shine in the luminous pleiad of wits equally renowned at that time for the pen and for the sword, to occupy a place beside the Rufos and the Artiedas, the Aldanas and the Ercillas.

When at last, after countless bitternesses, he regains his liberty and joyfully greets his native soil, this is his greatest concern. He wished to continue his services as a soldier, and to write, for those who had the duty of hearing and understanding it, all that his personal experience and his patriotic foresight had taught him. He had lived whole years amid the natural enemies of the Christian name and of Spanish power, he had been able to measure their strength and appraise their designs, he had been victim and witness of their daring raids, he had seen them make ready under the shelter of their well-defended harbors, and fall suddenly, like swift vultures, upon the defenseless coasts of Sicily or Naples, of Valencia or Andalusia, spread terror through fields and cities, and return rich with booty and with slaves taken in their very homes, in the very dominions of the most powerful king in Europe. He knew moreover that Tripoli and Tunis, and Algiers and Oran, were nothing but the advanced posts [p. 71] of Islam, the vanguard of a shapeless and immense army that was swarming on the frontiers of the Christian world, impatient to leap them and hurl itself impetuously upon its conquest. He recalled the glorious past of his country, her preponderant part in the tenacious struggle of the two civilizations, of the two creeds, and it seemed to him that Spain had not yet finished her gigantic task, that she ought to unite all her forces to dispatch the Mohammedan colossus, thus to acquire once for all the supremacy among the European nations, and to realize splendidly the apocalyptic dream of Campanella. How far he was from suspecting the spectacle that was about to present itself to his saddened eyes, and from believing how different were the courses then followed by the intention and the designs of those who conducted the nation! At its head, a hard, selfish, suspicious man, slow to deliberate and to resolve, and possessed at the same time of the most unbridled ambition; a statesman who had set himself to govern his peoples by means of expedients; a politician who sought to dominate Europe by force of intrigue. Around him and under his hand, instruments of easy use, not counselors zealous for the public good, not ministers attentive to the greatness of the State. Beneath, peoples ill at ease, dazzled still by certain heroic [p. 72] enterprises, but beginning to perceive that they bought them at the cost of their immunities and of their liberty. The treasures amassed by the labor and the efforts of the Spaniards were squandered in serving personal or dynastic hatreds, and the ever-empty Treasury was a perpetual menace to the fortunes of private persons. The valor and constancy of the soldier, acquired in tenacious and bloody wars against the enemies of Christendom, now served to harass and oppress Christian nations; and under the senseless pretext of setting a dike against the Reformation, if not to stifle it, the great, the portentous forces of the Spanish monarch were wasted in tyrannizing most cruelly over his own subjects, and in fomenting rebellion and anarchy among strangers. Cervantes, wholly given up to his chivalrous ideal, saw only the East; Philip, intent upon his plans of domination, kept his gaze fixed upon the North.

The soldier-writer had no means of making himself heard by the sovereign, isolated and inaccessible: once already he had tried, and in vain. He then wished to speak so loud that at least the echoes of his voice might reach the walls of the palace, and he addressed himself to the public. The theater presented itself to him naturally as the means of easiest and promptest publicity, and he devoted [p. 73] to the stage all the abundance and fervor of his vein, stirred by patriotism. With what generous ardor he undertook his singular crusade! Time and again he sets before the eyes of his countrymen the near, imminent danger; he describes to them the wretched life of the bagnios and the torments of captivity; he paints for them the risks, the seductions of a life in intimate contact with the enemies of their faith and of their customs; he speaks to them of the Ottoman power, of its enterprises, of its covetous hopes, of the possible allies even on Spanish soil: always the same plaintive or terrible note, always the same evocation of the feelings that seemed to slumber in the breasts of the conquerors of Granada and Tunis. It is hardly necessary to say that his voice was lost amid the tumult of other enterprises: the patriot was ignored, and if the artist won days of glory, that glory was ephemeral enough to do no more than embitter the rest of his existence. There was then appearing in the East of the Spanish stage the sun that was to light it most resplendently, and there came to dispute its empire with Cervantes a poet who seemed born, as writer and as man, to be his constant contradictor, as he was his perfect antithesis. I refer to Lope de Vega.

[p. 74]

Seldom will two men of such exceptional faculties have measured themselves so closely. Both were essentially artists, and looked at, studied, and represented the world to themselves with the eyes of such. There was no picturesque aspect, nor typical form, nor striking character, nor peculiar passion, nor conflict of affections or beliefs in that many-sided society in which they lived, that did not impress and move them, that did not fertilize their inspiration so as to make it bear fruit later. For them human nature held no secrets; they scrutinized it and laid it bare in its smallness and in its sublimity, in its deformity and in its beauty. They possessed a science that the wise have pursued with longing, and that discloses itself, reveals itself spontaneously to poets: that of the life of the soul, whose slow evolution and sudden metamorphoses they afterward knew how to paint with such exquisiteness, with such truth. They knew the secret of the eternal sphinx, man, and held in their hands the marvelous power of wiping the sarcastic smile from his lips and of drawing tears from his eyes. But how different a use they made of their genius! Those men, equal in intelligence, differed entirely in their feelings. Lope, a spirit of light, a heart of mire, did not know the generous passions that exalt [p. 75] and sublime our species, except when he created them artificially in the souls of his characters; he touched misery without being hurt by it, he saw close at hand the sumptuous vices and put himself in their service, he flattered all the passions of his age, he temporized with all the aberrations of his contemporaries, and he wished and managed to conceal beneath the excessive splendor of a false glory a life defamed by every baseness. Cervantes lived full of the most sublime aspirations: he loved with fervor the beautiful ideal that his fancy had created, and preferred all the tortures of the spirit, misunderstanding, estrangement, solitude, oblivion, rather than stain and prostitute it; he wept over all the vices that he stigmatized, he flinched before no prejudice, he respected no fanaticism, and found himself at last unknown and almost a stranger among men by dint of feeling himself so penetrated with humanity! For this reason, though both painted masterfully the world that surrounded them, their works have had so different a fate. Lope, indifferent and ironical, traced his pictures intent upon the immediate effect, submitting to fashion, to the caprice of the day; for this reason they preserve only a historical value. Cervantes, moved and indignant, drew his figures in order to inspire in them some spark of life, and that, in the dress and the gestures and the language of his [p. 76] age might feel and act as men, censuring and teaching; for that reason they acquired a lasting value.

Cervantes and Lope met, understood one another, and did not love one another. The struggle came, but the forces were unequal. The field of the first encounter was the stage; and Lope, though inferior, very much inferior in invention, in spite of his ostentatious abundance, surpassed his rival in practical knowledge of the theater and its resources, in facility of composition, and in his inexhaustible vein. He proposed nothing but to divert and captivate the public, brought no thesis to defend, and gave the stage a new play in twenty-four hours. Cervantes was vanquished. He was still young, young enough not to bear this defeat with calm, and he resolved to abandon letters and condemn himself to silence. The eagle was renouncing the empire of the air. But he had also ungirded his sword, and it was necessary to live. With no other protection than his merit—so great was the ingenuousness of his magnanimous heart!—and no other recommendations than his services, he set himself to petition, and obtained, after a minor commission for the provisioning of the fleets, the post of collector of the excise taxes. His distinguished services, his notorious misfortunes, his virtues and his talent did not [p. 77] avail him, all together, as much as caprice or intrigue might avail the first fortunate upstart. Instead of the company of learned men and conversation with distinguished wits; instead of literary occupations and the life of the mind; instead of the renown and influence, or at the least the respect, that his works had won, his daily employment was to be litigating with rustics and defending himself against the snares of the shrewd, assessing small holdings and seizing herds, living among constables and being exposed to their vexations, serving the treasury and running the risk of losing in its service his repose and his honor. There begins then that long and obscure pilgrimage of Cervantes across Spain, which was to be the sorrowful school out of which his experience was to emerge so enriched, and in which his genius was to reach full maturity. A piteous spectacle, in truth, that of Cervantes, unrecognized and humiliated, engaged in a dumb and tenacious struggle against poverty and adversity, lost in the midst of the busy and indifferent multitude, exposed one day or the next to vanish forever, swept by the human tide down to the bottom of the immense abyss in which are ceaselessly buried the sterile and idle men and generations who leave behind them no trace whatever of their passing!

[p. 78]

But his fortitude was great! He went on undaunted, seeing everything, observing everything, and accumulating a treasure of lessons for his mind where others would have found only grounds for prostration and degradation. He was then able to study truly the social state of his country, and he felt his love for it grow stronger, because he saw that he still had great and noble services to render it. With a firm hand he lifted the splendid mantle in which that brilliant society was clad, and was able to contemplate the deformities it concealed. A despotic and tenacious will had wished to take the place of all wills and to turn the nation into an enormous, inert machine subject to a single impulse. The result had been that, with every outward appearance of health and robustness, the social body wasted away day after day, its members were numbed, and an expert eye might have discovered in more than one place signs of impending ulceration. The monarch had humbled the nobility without thereby raising the people, and the magnates went off to consume their fortunes in idle splendor, to no profit whatever for the defense or the government of the State; the higher clergy saw growing and swelling in the shadow a power that pretended to wield, as it did, the [p. 79] spiritual weapons, but which was only one more instrument in the hands of Philip to terrify and annul every authority that was not his own; the magistracy had lost all its prestige through the scandalous sale of offices, and was the byword of the people for its corruption and venality; the municipal spirit, that most firm foundation on which the greatness of the nation had been built, had disappeared, silently undermined or stifled by despotic rule, leaving in its place enmity and rivalries between town and town, the quarrels and hatreds of family against family. The commons, the great mass of the people, through the diversity and mixture of races, through aristocratic and religious prejudices, through poverty that spread unchecked, and the idleness that accompanied and fed it, was in ceaseless ferment, growing ever more infected with all the vices that ruin States. Hidalgos, Old Christians, Judaizers, Moriscos, and gypsies, now separate, now mingled, were leaving at the bottom of that society a sediment in a state of corruption, the dregs and refuse of all classes, from which rose deleterious miasmas that poisoned everything; and the poet-knight saw with dismay the contagion spread and seize even the most reserved and leading families, [p. 80] and a multitude of youths who ought to have served their country with their arm or with their wit, going to swell the shapeless and innumerable army of ruffians and bullies, of swaggerers and gamblers, which had risen to peaceable possession of the most populous cities, with the full knowledge and sufferance of justice. Morals, though excessively rigid in outward bearing, were singularly licentious when seen close at hand: woman, tyrannized over without regard, took her revenge by paying in the coin of wantonness and dishonor; and in men personal daring and contempt of danger were qualities enough to gild the most ungoverned passions and even the gravest outrages; a strange mixture of culture and ignorance, of refinement and violence, one may say, gave tone and color to that society. Religion had turned into a meaningless ritualism, content with outward practices so as not to fall foul of the Holy Office, but which did not hinder the persistence of the most grotesque superstitions; and when some exalted or vehement mind cried out for a reform, all it could produce was a body of absurd rules, fit only either to crush character or to render human pride more unsubmissive and intractable, compressed in the inmost [p. 81] recess of the moral being by the fear of every kind of public humiliation and baseness. Everything seemed to conspire to break down and abase the spirit of a people so enamored of personal dignity, so jealous of the nation’s honor. And yet, since it was dangerous to speak the truth; since every censure might be turned into a crime against the State; since those most concerned to know the facts were either deceived or were accomplices in the deceit, and since Spanish arms still won showy victories and the fleets of the Indies still arrived laden with riches, and new discoveries and new conquests seemed to extend the dominions of the King of Spain; the people, unable to discern the symptoms of decline and ruin amid the displays of so much grandeur and prosperity, remained besotted with the deceptive certainty of its invincible might. In its conceptions, in its projects, in its desires, everything was out of proportion; upon a real foundation a thousand wild chimeras were built; and hence the permanent exaggeration of all feelings, accepted as a mark of greatness of soul; bombast as the standard of the sublimity of ideas, and violence as the sure sign of strength.

Before so pitiful a picture, in vain did Cervantes seek to restrain the impulses of his [p. 82] patriotic heart and bid his pen remain still. He had to write, but he could no longer write in the same way. Indignation, which goads and kindles lofty spirits in the face of the miseries and meannesses of human life, had been poured into the new mold in which his misfortunes confined him, and by a transformation as natural as it was imperceptible, in copying the types he met upon his way, in sketching the far vaster picture that his fancy, made fertile by observation, now embraced, in pronouncing his terrible censures, in setting satire and example side by side, there was revealed for the first time in all its force the humorist, who weeps and laughs at one and the same time, who laments and roars, because he touches and assays and measures, and in a single profound glance takes in the paltry reality and the most beautiful ideal that might and ought to replace it; while to his torment he understands that so noble an aspiration is held captive in the fetters of an incurable, an invincible impotence.

Laden with years and misfortunes, rich in the warnings and lessons of a most turbulent life, and more than ever eager to disclose his fears and give the public his counsels, Cervantes returns to the profession he thought to abandon [p. 83] once and for all, and sends in succession to the presses the immortal works in which he had enclosed all his heart and his profound knowledge of the world. The first part of *Don Quixote*, and then the *Exemplary Novels*, the *Journey to Parnassus*, and the *Comedies and Interludes*, appear, proclaiming that the applauded author of *La Galatea* and *La Numancia* was still alive, and producing the most diverse effects upon the reading public and among the literary censors. Cervantes was not content, in those books, with ridiculing and vexing whatever seemed to him worthy of censure in manners and in letters: he presented himself at once as an independent man who thinks and writes without licenses from partial academies or privileges from sycophantic friends; he did not shelter behind famous names, nor barter false flatteries for self-interested applause; to all he said plainly what his heart dictated, and he claimed that the public should esteem him at his just price, without any need for the critics of the day to appraise him. No more was needed to be received as an enemy in the by no means peaceful republic of letters: all the factions were stirred, all the parties united, mockeries, epigrams, and invectives rained upon the rash author, and even the thundering king of that Olympus, Lope, deigned to hurl [p. 84] his thunderbolts to bring him down. His familiars and partisans distinguished themselves above all by their ferocious rancor, and among them was forged, and from them came, the last and perhaps the most painful blow that was to wound the so sorely wounded soul of Cervantes. In spite of the hostility of the learned, *Don Quixote* had won from the first days a most rapid popularity, its editions were repeated, and the promised continuation was awaited with eagerness. It was known for certain that Cervantes was writing it, perhaps finishing it; when there suddenly appears a second part of *Don Quixote* written by another author, who hid his name under a pseudonym. At first sight it seemed to be merely a matter of usurping from the legitimate author the possible profits, as if a counterfeit product were being covered with a reputable label; but in reality the purpose was far more infamous. They took the two marvelous types created by Cervantes, the general design of the work in which he had made them move so naturally, in short its very grandeur of conception, not merely to tarnish and dim it, not only to bastardize the generous thought that had dictated his book and to present its spirit as in a grotesque caricature, but his very invention was exploited to write [p. 85] against him the bloodiest satire, to load him, without disguise, with abuse, and to denounce him to the wrath of all who might feel themselves scourged by his indignant censures, making thus—refined perversity!—his own glory serve as an instrument of his ignominy. The *Don Quixote* of the false Avellaneda is not merely a bad book: it was, in the full extent of the term, a villainy.

Deep was the wound and terrible the injury; but the years and misfortunes had already deadened in Cervantes the choleric impetuosity of his earlier days, and the constant contemplation of social misery and human smallness had inspired in him a more resigned philosophy, which allied itself marvelously with that fund of ironic joviality peculiar to his character. He parried and returned the blow, but in what fashion! He published his second part, and like Jupiter rising to confound the presumption of Apollo, a single step sufficed him to leave behind, at an immeasurable, an infinite distance, his paltry competitor. He gathered up their insults and blotted them out with his contempt; he took the measure of the pygmies who dared wound him, and forgave them loftily and disdainfully. He discharged against them, as if in play, a few jocoserious phrases, and delivered them to the laughter of the public and the mockery of [p. 86] posterity. In his breast there was no room for the sting of tenacious hatreds; thus the disposition of his spirit, in this last part of his life, discloses once more its superior temper. His profound melancholy, as it recognizes causes of imponderable grandeur, does not disdain to consider the festive or laughable aspects of the thousand trifles with which men puff themselves up; and for that reason he seasons even his severest lessons with most discreet salt. But even then faith in his ideals does not abandon him—the product perhaps of his purified religiosity—and instead of moaning in despair, he resigns himself, and writes his last pages in a spirit of amazing, unalterable serenity.

And how worthy of admiration he must seem to us when we consider that nothing had been left standing of the glorious castle that his youthful illusions had raised! He was going down the slope of life burdened with the weight of undeserved disillusionments, reckoning his epochs by his misfortunes; and wherever he turned his eyes he found only grounds for sorrow or for shame. If the soldier and the poet saw their hopes frustrated, what had become of those of the patriot, of the reformer? His nation, his homeland, so prosperous, so great, to what pass had it come?

[p. 87]

Whither had they led it? Tyranny and fanaticism were beginning to reap the fruits of their senseless and tenacious labor. The expulsion of the Moriscos had turned the most fertile regions of Andalusia and Valencia into uninhabited wastes; the outrages and usurpations of the Inquisition, placed at the service of a constantly aggressive foreign policy, had driven foreign ships away from Spanish ports, and already the English fleets had learned the road to the Indies; the intolerance of the governors and the vices of the people were depopulating the manufacturing centers, provoking a disproportionate emigration that left industry in its death throes. In politics, the suspicious and petty despotism of Philip II, far from being a principle of cohesion among his diverse peoples, had been a ferment of dissolution. Holland irretrievably lost, an example and temptation to the rest of the Low Countries; the Italian provinces irritated and discontented; the will of the Portuguese estranged, a sure symptom of the approaching separation; Catalonia aggressive and restless, Biscay threatening, Andalusia uneasy… Everything was menace, everything announced the danger of a tremendous and frightful dislocation. And in the midst of all this, the third Philip, who had not inherited even [p. 88] the willful and inflexible perseverance of his father, inherited his dreams of universal domination, a fatal legacy transmitted by the representatives of that ill-starred dynasty to the ruin of Spain, which saw its greatness destroyed by the very men who should have increased it, without even the personal glory of the monarchs wrapping in a deceptive splendor the abyss toward which they were leading their peoples.

Though in another form, all the fears of the patriotic statesman had taken shape, had been realized. Cervantes saw it and saw his insignificance and his impotence, and might have believed that he had squandered his life, had he not had so clear a consciousness that, in one field of the many he had traversed, he had left a work, had raised a monument, and that the artist and his creation would survive all the vicissitudes of fortune, all the catastrophes of time. He contemplated then for the last time life, now without secrets for his searching eyes, and turned his face to behold without fear that the shadows of eternal silence and eternal solitude were drawing near him, were already enveloping him.

Silence and solitude reigned around his tomb, which not even the curiosity of his contemporaries marked; but, as he had foreseen and [p. 89] foretold, they did not reach, could not reach his work or his name. In the midst of such great public upheavals, in that society in complete ebullition, at the mighty breath of the new ideas that were about to stir the world, there grew and gathered strength a genius capable of contemplating without dizziness the thunderous whirlwind of human affairs, and of discovering, through the boiling and many-colored surface, the motionless and permanent depths down which they plunge and are hurled; and who knew how to reveal to men, in immortal signs, the luminous vision of his spirit. However rapid the strokes with which I have recalled to you the life of Cervantes, I well believe they have allowed you to follow the development of his intelligence, the different phases of his character, as his successive productions reveal them to us, until we see his genius culminate in the book in which he concentrated, as in a focus of inextinguishable light, all that he had seen and felt, the world that his eyes had looked upon and the one that he had fashioned in his mind: *Don Quixote*. If now we pause a moment before this gigantic conception, perhaps we shall find ourselves with the data necessary to appreciate its extraordinary complexity and to assay its extraordinary value.

The personal elements that, knowingly or [p. 90] unknowingly on the artist’s part, always enter into the composition of any work, are revealed to us without difficulty. Remember the generous ardors that moved the youthful and impassioned soul of the poet who fought at Lepanto and conspired in Algiers, of the enthusiastic reformer who set up his professor’s chair upon the stage and wished to lead by other paths the destinies of a people; remember the bitter fruit of such noble and continued efforts; remember his continual yearning for beauty and glory, and his continual falling, prostrated by deformed and paltry reality, and you will see how a spark of the spirit of the knight-errant of Alcalá animates the typical figure of the demented and generous hidalgo of La Mancha! The impersonal elements, those that the author had to take from the social medium he inhabited, need not be dug out: they are manifest on every page. In *Don Quixote* there unfolds the most faithful picture of the Spain contemporary with Cervantes, presented with such coloring and relief that it would be impossible to ask more of the art of perspective. But it is not only a pictorial effect that the admirable painter wished to produce, that he produced: we hear men discourse, and we seem to hear them think, and we see the men of that remote age moved by passion; and thanks to the marvelous art, to the power of reproduction of the [p. 91] incomparable artist, we witness their life and share in it. Great would the praise of the book be already with this; but I have in reality pointed out only a minimal part of its merit. It is much to produce a work that reveals and communicates one’s own thought and emotion; much more to transfer in signs the life of an entire people; but the *summum* [summit] of art consists in somehow posing the human problem and interesting in its resolution men of all times and all countries; in leaving the narrow ranks of a national art or literature and going to occupy a prominent place in the pantheon of the geniuses who belong to universal art or literature. To this height Cervantes has attained, and it is easy to show it. He knows his people marvelously, and paints them; he is a man of his age, and studies it; he writes his native tongue with all the ease of genius, lavishes idioms with full hands, does not stop at incorrectnesses, and yet, today as then, in English, in Russian, as in Castilian, his immortal work is delight and instruction and wonder to all men, by the mere fact of their being men. And this is because he owed to the permanent illumination of genius so thorough a knowledge of the human soul that he could take it apart and lay bare its most [p. 92] hidden springs, with the same assurance as the artificer who takes apart a mechanism of his own invention. Let us seek no farther than in ourselves the eternal originals of his two marvelous figures; here they are at the bottom of every conscience, ever struggling and ever united, ceaselessly contradicting and ceaselessly agreeing, seeing at once the double aspect of things and deceiving themselves at once, seduced by desire. The admirable dialogues of the knight and the squire have resounded, with a voice more or less low, in every heart, for there has always been some beautiful chimera that at some time and in some form seduces even the man of coldest and most positivist temperament; and at some time and in some manner bare experience has laid its glacial hand upon the temples of the most ardent pursuer of imagined beauty. The adventures of Don Quixote and Sancho are a transparent symbol of human life, solicited with equal sway by the generous passions, which tend toward the good of humanity,—self-denial, sacrifice, heroism,—and by the selfish passions, which assure the preservation of the individual—wary caution, interest in gain, the prudence that is not afraid to be timorous;—without its being possible, in the one case or the other, to escape being the victim of that impersonal and tremendous power [p. 93] that plays with the destiny of men and of their most grandiose works. For observe that on no occasion does his selfishness save Sancho from the failures to which his valorous unselfishness drags Don Quixote. Each deceives himself from his own exclusive point of view, and at his own cost the terrible irony of reality triumphs over both. And let not Cervantes be accused of pessimism: he gave back to the world the lessons he had received from the world, and placed deeper within his work the higher teaching that his spirit dictated to him. If you would find it, seek it in that admirable scene with which the work truly ends, when, Don Quixote having been thrown to the ground by the Knight of the White Moon, who sets his lance to his breast and offers to spare his life if he confesses the error in which he has lived, if he renounces the ideal of his existence, and declares that Dulcinea is not the fairest lady in the world, the indomitable knight answers him: “Dulcinea del Toboso is the fairest woman in the world and I the most unfortunate knight on earth, and it is not right that my weakness should defraud this truth. Thrust, knight, with your lance, and take my life, since you have taken my honor.” What is brutal force to master the spirit? What matters it to fall vanquished if one strives [p. 94] for the truth, adored in the sanctuary of conscience? There is no blow, nor reverse, nor pain, nor threat, nor certainty of death, that can impose a conviction on the thought, which rises free and resplendent from the field of defeat, and affirms and proclaims its right to hold as good and beautiful and holy what it contemplates and reveres as such.

Thus it is that the spirit is tempered and prepared for the great struggles of life. This has been the painful labor of all those who have touched close at hand and scrutinized human miseries in order to seek in our own forces, cast down by corruption, by pleasures, or by social injustices, the elements of renewal and health. You, generous and indignant censors of the errors of man, Aristophanes, Juvenal, Dante, Rabelais, Quevedo, Swift, and you, Cervantes; you have not stirred up the social dregs, nor laid bare our cancerous ulcers, to take savage delight in so terrible a spectacle, but to wash them with burning tears and to apply with firm hand the cautery that could heal them; you have scourged indolent or sleeping peoples to warn them of the imminent danger; you have called to the one post worthy of man, to the very thick of the fray, [p. 95] for, if it is not given to all to win the victory, it is the duty of all to contend for it and to show themselves worthy of deserving it. Your voice has not been lost without an echo: it resounds from century to century in the heart of all men who aspire to better their condition; it is repeated by the generations that pass the torch of enthusiasm from hand to hand as they go forth to conquer a more perfect state; and in this way your works shine amid the darkness of the horizon like beacons that guide us, that call us with gentle and unfailing light toward the promised land, where man is one day to dwell in peace, respecting right and practicing justice. Meanwhile, O forerunners! your names resound with eternal praise upon their lips, and altars are raised to your memory in their heart and in their conscience.

## VICTOR HUGO

### AS SATIRICAL POET <sup>(1)</sup>

Ladies and Gentlemen:

N othing less than these significant tokens of benevolence is needed by me to master somewhat the fear that seizes me on finding myself in this tribune and in your presence. There assail me at once the memory of the eloquent voices that have resounded from here, to the profit and delight of the select audience that this favored Society always draws, and the conviction of my own inadequacy; for, accustomed to solitary study and to communication in writing, not on every occasion

[p. 98]

does speech obey me readily. How could I flatter myself that it would show itself less rebellious today, when I am certain I shall not succeed in clothing it in the finery demanded by the presence of so many distinguished ladies as have come to honor me this evening? I should think I had achieved much if I managed even to give my phrases the warmth of the emotion that animates me, and to set upon my assertions the seal of the sincerity with which I submit them to your benevolent criticism.

Moreover, even without forgetting for a moment the character of this purely literary festival, it is not in my power to withdraw entirely from the influence of this place, where so many times the energetic voice of the tribune has been raised, defender of trampled right, stern judge of the blunders or the iniquities of power grown overweening, and where so many times the thunder of popular applause has lent to the orator’s word the solemn dignity of the protests of public opinion against the outrages of force. It seemed to me that here, where the gravest problems of the present and the future of a people have been treated with such loftiness and fire, any subject whatever must appear colorless; and I should have found myself singularly perplexed in the choice of a theme, if [p. 99] the distinguished orators who have preceded me in this tribune on a similar occasion had not established, with happy judgment, a kind of tradition that has come to mark out for me the course I am about to follow. In imitation of them, I have wished to seek a subject which, without taking me out of the sphere of art, to which these evenings are devoted, would allow me to touch upon those matters more immediately connected with the ideas, the designs, and the passions whose conflict constitutes the dramatic interest of the life of societies, and to consider them in the form in which they present themselves today to claim the attention of the human mind. Nothing could be more fitting for this purpose than to choose in our age one of those men who, by the superiority of their genius and the vigor of their activity, have succeeded in embracing and illuminating the arduous questions that preoccupy and stir humanity, and who have been prominent actors in the great struggles of the century; seeking in his work, or in some part of it, the materials for a study that may disclose to us his character, the key to every personality, and explain to us the secret of his action and his influence. I have fixed upon Victor Hugo, the great poet and great patriot, whose name is by itself a program; and I have chosen the period of his life and the part of his work that best reveal his heart.

[p. 100]

I consider it rash, and in the highest degree rash, to formulate absolute judgments; nevertheless, I venture to assert that Victor Hugo is the greatest poet of the century. I do not forget, in saying this, that he has been the chief and standard-bearer of a school, nor that he has been personally involved, sustaining it with his propaganda and invigorating it with his example, in the noisiest and most embattled contest that the annals of literature record. Far from it: when I see him rise above the prejudices of faction and sect, and survive in glory the triumph, always ephemeral, of a doctrine, which is as much as to say of a partial and limited way of considering multiform and limitless reality; when I see him withstand the whole torrent of adverse criticism, victor over the caprices of taste, and go on producing, without apparent weariness, marvelous works for successive generations of readers; even without yet entering upon the appraisal of his intrinsic qualities, I cannot doubt that the mighty breath of great inspirations has lifted his breast and that the divine afflatus of sovereign conceptions has fanned his brow; that he has felt as an artist and produced as a genius. For in the midst of this eternal ebb and flow of things and of ideas, it is not [p. 101] always the victor of the moment who has the last word; and only those who have been truly worthy of victory obtain the supreme sanction, that of an impartial and dispassionate posterity. And it is impossible not to recognize that his long and glorious old age has allowed the eminent poet to hear that voice of the future, which resounds propitiously for so few.

But I do not ask that you content yourselves with this favorable testimony of the facts: to other, more decisive considerations I shall entrust the confirmation of my assertion. If we ask ourselves, “What is it that characterizes our age?” no one will hesitate to answer, “The greater proximity in which men find themselves to one another, and in consequence the greater intensity of individual and social life.” The printing press established the universal republic of minds, but steam is accomplishing a far grander work: the universal federation of hearts. The railways are everywhere leveling the frontiers; propeller-driven ships suppress the immense sea and unite remote continents. The communication of products is followed by that of ideas, and this by the sharing of the same affections, of the same feelings and passions. How vast is the intellectual horizon of the man of our day! [p. 102] how rich his emotional scale! what a sum of collective consciousness is revealed by the daily struggles, the periodic upheavals of contemporary peoples! Thus we see how individuals change and are transformed before our eyes and within a few years; how nations evolve and vary in a few decades. The changes, the revolutions in tastes, in systems, in beliefs, which formerly took centuries to be accomplished, humanity now accomplishes from one generation to the next.

Let us confine ourselves to the sphere of literary ideas and production. Centuries did Christian literature take to develop and flourish: centuries the classical reaction, the Renaissance, to yield all its fruits. Our own century dawns, and an unaccustomed stirring of minds announces, first in Germany, later and with more fire in France, that the canons to which literary composition conformed were to be wholly overturned; the old forms are denounced as narrow, the ancient rules mocked as puerile, the traditional subjects discarded as worn out; gravity and decorum in style are branded as senile pomposity; the harmonious simplicity of plans bespeaks intellectual myopia; the graduated proportion of the parts betrays timidity and poverty of mental vigor; the [p. 103] careful choice of themes, sterility and manifest deficiency of inspiration. Provided that life teems and ideas seethe and images shine luminous and many-colored and verse resounds musical, everything is accepted, and fancy lords it without check over the entire domain of art. It seemed that a new sap was coursing through the veins of the old peoples of Europe and that a breath of youth was refreshing their temples. Beholding the ardor with which an entire generation of young geniuses hurled itself along the new paths, casting into the arena, one after another, immortal works, and how the contagion spread even to the more modest and less brilliant literatures on the vast stage of the civilized world, who would not have believed he was witnessing the advent of the definitive art of an entire epoch? But wait a little: look further on: not thirty years pass, and already the taste for form begins to reappear; the phrase is again sought out and the idea chiseled in its verbal manifestation; there arises a poetry as polished and chastened as that of the Renaissance, though richer in ideas and feelings; and the Parnassians announce the first defeat of romanticism in France itself, its most formidable bulwark. In Germany the humorists, disciples of Heine, and the singers [p. 104] of philosophical poetry and of patriotic poetry; in England the psychological poets, with Browning at their head; in France the naturalists; have just made the public lose its taste for the romantic, and even before these schools or tendencies achieve lasting popularity, and for greater surprise and contrast, the Englishman Swinburne appears, followed by the Pre-Raphaelites, brandishing a standard even more radical and revolutionary than that of romanticism was.

What results must this variety in the manner of conceiving and externalizing, this diversity in the very subjects of the poets, have produced in the aesthetic capacity of contemporaries? We have all heard every tone that the human lyre can produce, we have discovered unsuspected resources in language, rhythm and rhyme have produced marvels, the word has in reality been made flesh; the deepest emotions as well as the subtlest, the most permanent affections as well as the most fleeting, all the meanderings of the heart, all the shades of individual character, have been analyzed and expressed with a masterly touch; all the personages of history have been evoked, peoples and civilizations have been exhumed; we have asked of the men of every age the key to their thoughts and have imitated their language, the secret [p. 105] of their feelings, and have reproduced their art. Thanks to our literature we have not merely considered events, as from a commanding height, nor have we attended the drama of the passions as spectators: we have traversed the field of history, living in each epoch, we have been actors in its thousand tragic scenes, and in our breasts all the sorrows of humanity have re-echoed. We have lived more and better than the men of any other age.

And tell me now, of all the singular artists who have accomplished this singular work, truly unique, which is the one who has gathered from the contemplation of the world and of man more emotions, has animated more ideas, has poetized more aspects of reality, has given more luminous expression to his creations, has moved his fellows in a more varied, a more profound, and a more lasting manner? Is it not he who has run through all the strings of the poetic lyre, and, alternately tender or sublime, full of grace or full of majesty, pathetic or indignant, plaintive or thundering, has placed before our eyes, by the astonishing magic of his genius, men of all times and of all countries, has made us understand the ideas that have died, has made us decipher [p. 106] the symbols that have lost their luster, be imbued with the beliefs that have forever been eclipsed, and at the same time has better embodied in us all the conceptions of our age, the new feelings that make us new men, agitating all the problems of the present hour, and elevating them, by the incomparable effort of his generous fancy, to the regions of art, where every concept is purified and ennobled, where every notion becomes light and life, and gathers new strength and acts more energetically upon a greater number of intelligences and hearts? Is he not the singer of *Les Contemplations* and *Les Orientales*, is he not the troubadour of *Les Chansons des rues et des bois*, is he not the author of *Les Burgraves*, of *Notre-Dame de Paris*, and of *Les Misérables*, is he not the Juvenal of *Les Châtiments* and of *Le Livre satirique*, is he not the sublime epic poet of *La Légende des siècles*, is he not Victor Hugo?

Let us give all that is due to the age of transition which we have reached and to the so complex social milieu in which the poet has flourished; let us consider all that is excessive, vicious, and superfluous in the marvelous production of that gigantic talent; let us not forget that in many of his pictures there is more dazzling invention than scrupulous historical truth; let us not deny that his style [p. 107] often falls into the mannered and the purely conventional; moreover, let us say frankly that his poetic ideal, his conception of art, though magnificent and grandiose, limits him, and therefore, up to a point, diminishes him, because it looks only, and as its supreme end, to the display of force, to the externalization of teeming and energetic life, to the continual aspiration toward the colossal and the superhuman. For all that, so great is his genius, so vivid and pure of all vile admixture his inspiration, so lofty his sentiments, so noble his notion of man and of humanity, so rich and profound his intuition of Nature, that he has succeeded in projecting upon his contemporaries a torrent of divine poetry, never before or since surpassed. This age of intense life, of exquisite and at times morbid consciousness, acclaims in him its poet, him who has revealed to it better and in lovelier language the secret of its spirit and of its aspirations.

I am certain that your enlightened attention would not fail me if I were to dwell now upon a study of the entire work of the distinguished author; but the limits of my task do not permit it. It suffices for my purpose to choose, as I have already indicated, that part which allows us better to understand the poet’s character, and which contains, besides, a loftier lesson.

[p. 108]

Criticism had already acclaimed Victor Hugo as an unsurpassable lyric poet when he suddenly revealed a new aspect of his eminently subjective genius: a new muse spoke through his inspired lips, and the century had its Juvenal, as eloquent as the ancient and far more generous, because he does not disguise his steel-tipped darts by raging against the dead, but makes the target of his thundering censures the living and the powerful. The poet lays down the heroic trumpet, forgets the lyre, and with an iron hand cracks the lacerating lash of satire.

Perhaps for some, wedded to the old scholastic poetics that establishes genres and gives some pre-eminence over others, the point of view from which I am about to consider the work of Victor Hugo will be an unfavorable one, since the genre I have just mentioned seems to them an inferior one. The human soul is too complex, and the vicissitudes of the drama in which it takes part, at once as actress and as spectator, are too intricate, for the manifestation of its feelings by means of the art of the word to be easily shut up within classifications that become titles of preference. However much certain schools may claim it, the beautiful is not the sole matter of art: the tragic phases of life, the risible aspects of the individual [p. 109] human being, the very deformities of our physical or moral nature, attract with absorbing interest the attention of the artist and of his public. Malignity has been as fecund a muse as enthusiasm and admiration. But, without probing this interesting problem at once of aesthetics and of morals, do we not see at every step that good humor predisposes one to set in relief the defects and the mere incongruities of character, and to turn normal traits into elements of caricature? do we not notice how another feeling, already austere, the desire to correct, leads us to accentuate the imperfections of the moral being, the disorders of social organizations, and to turn them into vices worthy of reprobation and censure? And, above all, in the face of the truly bitter evils that infest the spirit and make of the world a gloomy field of infamous struggles and of iniquitous triumphs; in the face of the crawling intrigue that usurps the rewards of virtue; in the face of the vile hypocrisy that supplants merit; in the face of the shameless perjury that tramples honor; in the face of unbridled force that chains and gags right; in the face of the obdurate wickedness that strips justice of her sacred attributes and profanes them by displaying them upon its own stigmatized shoulders; does not indignation rise up, which purifies and even redeems, with its [p. 110] thundering anathemas, with its deep voice that terrifies like lightning, with its angry countenance that paralyzes and arrests the heart in criminal breasts? Well, all these elements compose satirical poetry, and there are epochs so decadent and corrupt that no other string can vibrate on the lyre of the poet who abominates the present and prophesies the future. Of these elements, the noblest, the most capable of the sublime movements and of the grandiloquence of genius, indignation, is the one that has inspired the book on which I am about to fix my attention. Its historical antecedents show that nothing in its production was conventional; that it was the spontaneous fruit of a most mournful period and of a great and noble heart: it is well that we keep them in mind.

On December 20, 1848, Prince Louis Napoleon Bonaparte, elected President of the Republic, ascended the tribune of the French National Assembly, wearing upon his breast the badge of the grand cross of the Legion of Honor. There he pronounced this oath:

“In the presence of God, and before the French people represented by the National Assembly, I swear to remain faithful to the democratic Republic, one and indivisible, and to fulfill all the duties that the Constitution imposes upon me.”

[p. 111]

The President of the Assembly replied: “We take God and men to witness the oath that has just been taken.” The destinies of the French people were left in the hands of the representative of Napoleonic ideas; the French Republic was at the mercy of an imperial prince, with no defense but the feeble barrier of a political code, the work of mistrust and improvidence. Many noble spirits withdrew to their homes saddened, beset by the memory of the oaths of the First Consul.

Nevertheless, the acts and above all the words of the new President had for their sole object to calm those misgivings, to dispel those fears. In his first speech, in that same session, he declares that he will fulfill his duties as a man of honor, and that he will treat as enemies of the country those who attempt to change the institutions by illegal means. Months later, in an address delivered at Ham, in the very place where he had suffered a long captivity, he condemns his past conduct as an attack upon the regular government, and shows himself resolved to respect the institutions of the country. Almost two years later he indignantly rejects the idea attributed to him of changing the Constitution, because the title he most covets [p. 112] is that of an honorable man, and he recognizes nothing higher than duty. In November of the year 1850 he recalls with all solemnity, in his message, that he is bound by his oath, that it is not permitted him even to desire the revision of the fundamental charter. All around him, his partisans, his friends, give the nation the same assurances: there are those who go so far as to call him *the most honorable man in the Republic*.

But the 2nd of December, 1851, arrives, and Paris, on awakening, finds itself militarily occupied by a considerable army, and learns with stupor that the palace of the representatives of the people has been violated, the most illustrious generals of France and its most prominent politicians imprisoned, the National Assembly dissolved. The author of so great a felony was Louis Napoleon Bonaparte, President of the Republic. The comedy of the oath, the farce of honor, the carnival mummery of duty, had not lasted even three years. The conspiracy had been hatched in the supreme councils of the State; the crime had been growing in the shadow of the guardian of right; rebellion had seized the sword of the law; treason had been betrothed beforehand to impunity.

But the outrage was as yet a small one: the usurpation [p. 113] needed its baptism of generous and innocent blood. Scarcely recovered from the surprise, the people attempt resistance; some representatives who have managed to escape the henchmen sent out in search of them, some citizens possessed by patriotic indignation, rush into the streets, acclaiming the trampled law and the prostituted Republic; barricades begin to rise, and through all the reaches of the great capital resound the din of musketry and the hoarse, grave boom of the cannon. It is the hour of the heroes of conviction and the martyrs of duty. One of those heroes was Victor Hugo. A representative of the people, and having escaped the first fury of the conspirators, he tries to organize resistance; he goes through the most public places, harangues the multitude, and sets the example of resisting, shielded by right, the infamous pressure of force. The terrible vicissitudes of the lugubrious drama whose stage was the populous metropolis passed all before his eyes. Thirty thousand French soldiers, traitors to their faith and their flag, were shooting and cannonading twelve hundred citizens armed in defense of the Constitution and the law: in one place, a woman who was carrying arms to the patriots was seized and executed on the spot; in another a councilor-general was shot; farther [p. 114] off a house occupied by three hundred citizens was taken at the point of the bayonet, and those who took it killed all who offered resistance; in other places those who fought were thrown into the river; General Herbillon had prisoners under twenty years of age flogged; along the principal boulevards the cannon thundered, making the houses its target; and at the first barricades fell mortally wounded the deputy Baudin, who had opposed as his only arms to that mercenary soldiery his tricolor sash and the fundamental code of France, which they were tearing with their bayonets dyed in French blood. When all hope had been lost, when all resistance was now useless, those who did not go to expiate their heroism in the hulks, the prisons, or the penal colonies, sought or found their safety in exile.

The English island of Jersey gave asylum to the great poet, who was included in the first list of proscription. The world was about to witness a strange spectacle. Amid clouds of incense the usurper advanced to occupy a throne for which perjury, perfidy, and assassination had served as footstool. France, after the first convulsions and the first threats of resistance, submitted almost without a murmur, [p. 115] to the Caesar improvised in the barracks. Europe looked on with curiosity, but without indignation. Time, docile accomplice of all the violences of insolence and injustice, was preparing to bring them the supreme sanction of accomplished facts. Only one man was making ready for the struggle and forging in his mind, inflamed by patriotic hatred, the thunderbolts with which to strike the colossus full in the face. The poet was about to take up the voice of scorned right, to stand up for the sanctity of the oath despised, to defend the law reduced to shreds, to pour out all the gall of contempt upon the cowardly despot and his hired assassins, to scourge them by turns with insult, mockery, and sarcasm. He would not be an Ovid weeping over his sorrows on the inhospitable shores of Pontus, but a new Ezekiel, set upon a sublime watchtower, reviling the abominable crimes of the new Salem and foretelling the desolation and total ruin of the wicked. It was not the book of the *Tristia* that was to come from his pen steeped in tears and blood, but the book of *Les Châtiments*.

Never has a more genuine passion been expressed with greater truth; never has an event so terrible inspired a more pathetic poetry, nor had the grandiose wrath of a man representing right and justice [p. 116] ever succeeded in turning thus into images of fire, into luminous, somber pictures, into magnificent phrases of indignant eloquence that are to be engraved like indelible stigmata upon the pale brows of those culprits clad in purple. So profound in this unique work is the interpenetration of the aesthetic feeling and the moral feeling that it almost resists analysis. Into the soul of the poet the soul of his people seems to have been transfused, and his voice takes on all the inflections, all the accents, from the tone of the simple song that seems a stifled sob, to that of the sublime imprecation in which elegy weeps desolate and satire thunders furious. To paint all the scenes of horror that have composed the lugubrious drama of treason and usurpation, his style acquires an unlimited flexibility, and moans and curses and laughs sarcastically, and at times seems to lull death to sleep, and at times is lit up with the prophetic vision of tremendous catastrophes. The enterprise is titanic, for it is a matter of shaking, stirring, and raising up a prostrate and fainting people; but the strength is a Titan’s. He speaks to all France, and cites and summons all of it to witness the terrible execution; he strikes the perjurer implacably, harries him without truce or rest, nails him to the very [p. 117] top of the pillory, to evoke around him the bloody images of all his victims, to bring to his ear the sorrowful cries and the atrocious blasphemies of the widows, the orphans, the galley-slaves, and the proscribed; to rouse against him all the pure objects of nature and all the noble feelings of the soul; to leave him alone upon his infamous height and consecrate him to contempt; he then leashes the vile pack of his accomplices, the traitors who sold him their swords, the cowards who in exchange for favors delivered him their votes, the unworthy who auctioned off their conscience to him, and tears the braid from the generals, strips the ermine mantles from the magistrates, strips the linen tunics from the bishops, and one by one flings them into the fetid pool of their vileness and prostitution; nor does he spare the timid who hid, the prudent who waited, the weak who acquiesced, the innumerable rabble that does not aid the resistance and that afterwards forms the plastic mass of those who consent: all he clothes in the sanbenito of their selfishness, and declares them the authors of their own ignominy, deserving of their infamy and worthy of bearing it.

The sincerity of the emotion is so great in this [p. 118] work of such lofty inspiration that there is not a single discordant note; and the unity of feeling is so complete that all those the poet expresses, despite being so many and so various, blend harmoniously, to show throughout it one predominant, which is like the key of the book and the inexhaustible source of its inspiration: the full confidence of the patriot that the triumph of wickedness is ephemeral and that the reign of justice will be restored.

Among so many memories as he evokes, among so many images as he creates, among so many symbols as he animates, the image of Joshua appears before his eyes, and in the siege of Jericho he finds a sublime allegory that expresses his whole thought. When the Judge of Israel, at the head of his people, began to encircle the city to the sound of his trumpets, the King burst out laughing, and as the circuits of the strange army around the strong walls succeeded one another, the inhabitants of Jericho kept coming to watch and mock the singular spectacle: first came the children, who spat upon the ark and imitated with their hands at their mouths the fateful sound of the clarions; then the women, spinning tranquilly at their distaffs, and hurling stones at the besiegers; then the invalids, who flung insults and sarcasms at them; lastly the [p. 119] prudent elders, who laughed as merrily as their King. But in the midst of such confidence and revelry the turn came for the seventh circuit, and the walls of Jericho fell. “Sound, sound forever, clarions of thought,” says the poet; and his robust verse resounds throughout all France as a prophecy of sure fulfillment.

While the exile thus wept or imprecated year after year, what was happening in his homeland? Ah! The insensible demonstration of facts, to use Sainte-Beuve’s felicitous phrase, seemed bent on testifying against him: the country had passed from stupor to indifference; the group of the strong in spirit, of the upright in will, was growing ever smaller; right, liberty, all the consecrations that the law owes to human personality, had disappeared; but agriculture prospered, industry flourished, commerce widened its circle of action; the great conceptions of the mind, the noble aspirations of feeling, were stifled in that rarefied atmosphere, but social activity took other channels, and the inner prosperity of the nation increased day by day, so that all peoples wished to secure in it a place of preference for trade. Paris itself was being transformed: [p. 120] the indefatigable pickax of the engineer and the architect was razing whole districts, and a magic wand seemed to improvise in their place beautiful parks, magnificent avenues, sumptuous monuments. Before such a display of wealth credit could not remain insensible, and that of France was unlimited. These prestigious appearances served it no less abroad: its voice was heard with respect in the councils of the world, its vote was eagerly sought in international conflicts, and after the wars of the Crimea and of Italy its preponderance seemed decisively established among the powers of the first rank. The new Caesar could feel satisfied: time had effaced those stains of blood; success had changed the names of things; usurpation was legitimacy; the people that had given its suffrages had not merely elected according to its will, but had elected to its own profit. Once more fortune surrendered, dazzled, to audacity.

The bitterest oppositions, after so many years of prosperity, began to falter or kept silent. But there were some who remained inflexible. Needless to say, Victor Hugo was among them. To each amnesty he answered energetically: “No;” and when the very voice of friendship came to solicit his acquiescence, [p. 121] he opened the book of *Les Châtiments*, and repeated: “If only ten remain, I shall be the tenth; if only one is left, I shall be that one.” Thus he magnificently set the seal of truth upon the splendid work of his imagination; thus he proved that the constant commerce of his superior soul with all the great ideals of humanity—love, honor, right, justice—so gloriously sung in his work, had given him in exchange true freedom, leaving him inaccessible to self-interest, to fear, and to weakness, the authors of all prevarication. The artist had sanctified the man, and the eminent poet showed the world once more that if we stand face to face with a great mystery, with a profound night, the human spirit, there is a great clarity that suddenly bathes it: duty. Where all saw darkness, the poet discovered an inextinguishable light, and there the visions of his mind took body, were realized, and his verses issued from his lips, burning and luminous like prophecies.

Louis Napoleon had too much faith in his audacity, and in the decisive action of victory over the inertia of the greater number, not to receive with mocking smiles the lightning flashes of the holy wrath of a proscribed man. He was far from suspecting that some day the flashing verses of the poet would besiege him, like the Furies [p. 122] of vengeance that pursued the parricide Orestes even within the very sanctuary of the gods. But the years passed, and the ground began to tremble beneath his feet; intoxicated by prosperity and blinded by the smoke of incense, he plunged heedlessly into the abyss that was to bury him. Must I relate to you his fall, when it has appalled us all? The great politician who guided France had gagged opinion, had denied the field to ideas, had divorced himself from the public spirit, and stood alone with his courtiers and flatterers; the great diplomat who guided Europe had not perceived the subterranean labor of his enemies, knew nothing of the subtle net in which he was enmeshed, and found himself without allies; the great administrator who had made treasures spring from French soil had no provisions, no equipment, no armaments, no defenses, and found himself with soldiers but without armies; the great warrior, who had inherited the genius of the first Napoleon, threatens out of season, breaks forth without consulting his forces, leaves his capital threatening to invade the hostile country, advances, retreats, wavers, and scarcely does he find himself face to face with the enemy than he lets fall at the feet of the invader the flag and the sword of France.

The tremendous prophecy of the poet was fulfilled, [p. 123] confirming the apparent paradox of Aristotle: poetry had said more truth than history. Victor Hugo, full of sorrow and of glory, returned to his country after eighteen years to witness sadly the triumph of his work. From amid the ruins of imperial France the vibrant voice of the inspired bard rose up, showing that the foundations of iniquity are crumbling, and that in the world of men only what rests on justice is permanent and stable.

Rarely has genius been placed in the service of a more generous idea: therefore, among so many masterpieces as the great French lyric poet has produced, none possesses so permanent a value; and if we consider only the genre to which it belongs, we may say that it is the supreme effort and the highest production of satirical poetry. Marvelous passages, even whole pictures, are to be found in Dante, inspired by the muse of patriotic vengeance; more than one popular poet in the Middle Ages has stirred the iron hearts of the great and petty tyrants of the age with his daring sirventes; but before Victor Hugo only Juvenal had raised satire to such dignity, and yet the Latin poet is inferior to the French poet. Both embrace in their work the aberrations, the weaknesses, [p. 124] the vices, and the crimes of an entire society; the picture is equally vast in both, perhaps somewhat greater in Juvenal; the language of imprecation and invective is equally rich in the two poets, more sententious in the Latin, more brilliant in the French; the tone is wholly adequate to the idea; art in both shows itself powerful and vigorous; but Juvenal is the expression of a sorrow without hope, and his very despair makes him in a certain way disinterested in the sad spectacle that he contemplates and reproduces, and since he cannot correct, he loses no opportunity to moralize: he is a philosopher who draws the conclusions of an experience, even though that experience be the corruption and rottenness of a world. Victor Hugo does not lose for a moment his faith in the realization of the end he pursues, in the restoration of right, in the punishment of wickedness, of violence, and of cynicism; he is indignant at each blow, at each disappointment; his heart bleeds with every wound that the homeland receives; he shudders at all the infamies that stain and degrade it; but he trusts and answers with a noble and magnificent song, which is at once terrible to the wicked and consoling and fortifying to the innocent victims and to the partisans of reason and of right; he is always a poet inspired by a [p. 125] divine ideal, which shows him humanity regenerated in the lustral waters of love and equity.

The years may pass and tastes change; from the poet’s crown there may fall leaves that are still green today: the book of *Les Châtiments* will not die. Wherever there are men crushed by the weight of social injustices and peoples subdued by the brutal burden of force; wherever the man of upright heart sees crime rise up triumphant, crooked in its ways, and nations witness the long eclipses that law and morality may suffer, the verses of the exiled poet, of the patriot faithful to the cause of the vanquished, will be repeated from mouth to mouth to bathe hearts in fortitude. They will teach all the oppressed the hidden but irresistible force of right; the final triumph of good over evil, of intelligence over passion, of liberty over despotism; and they will make them repeat his sublime invocation: “Sound, sound forever, clarions of thought, and the walls of iniquity, the citadels of injustice, will crumble at last, by their own weight, into the abyss!”

## TWO THEORIES OF LOVE <sup>(1)</sup>

### (PLATO AND MICHELET)

Ladies and Gentlemen:

W hen one of the most illustrious thinkers of our times, the founder of critical philosophy, taught that we must never treat humanity, in our own person or in the persons of others, as a means, but always as an end, the dogma of human dignity had found its word, and its incarnation began. The true guarantee of social relations stood demonstrated. The principle that exalts and dignifies them acquired its concise and luminous formula: respect. What other could

[p. 128]

be more acceptable to modern man, to the citizen of the new societies that establish as their basis equality, supreme guardian of all justice?

For my part I consider none superior in the sphere of civil relations, and its import is so great that even the most intimate ones, so long as they bear its seal, will be purified and sublimated without losing thereby their character. Called upon to have the honor of addressing you at this philanthropic festival, that is to say, an eminently social one, I thought that the most fitting way to fulfill my difficult charge was to try to make you see how those powerful sentiments that draw man out of himself to make him suffer or rejoice with another’s pain and pleasure, the sympathetic sentiments comprised under the more universal and more beautiful name of *love*, take on a new and more splendid form, are refined and ennobled, when respect tempers and directs them; and above all, how they then fulfill more worthily and fully their most lofty destiny in the founding, the preservation, and the growth of societies. You see that I do not intend to develop here a sentimental thesis, nor have I sought a theme lending itself to poetic embellishments and episodes: a serious problem, the gravest perhaps of those that engage the legislator [p. 129] and the moralist, is the one I am about to pose before you; for I would not venture to solicit your attention for any less.

Even thus limited, the subject would be too vast, and for my unaided powers far too difficult. I have wished, therefore, to circumscribe my point of view still further, and to seek aid and light in the conceptions of two sovereign intellects who, in very different epochs and in very different ways, have considered and elucidated the same theme. Among the many that might claim my attention, I have fixed upon, in order to set them against each other, two theories of love, which will allow me to penetrate the spirit of the two most brilliant epochs in the life of humanity: Hellenic antiquity and our contemporary times; and to demonstrate at the same time the superiority of the latter, illumined and fecundated by the great principle of mutual respect among all who associate for the common ends of existence.

These two theories will be the ancient and celebrated one of Plato and that contained in the luminous pages of a modern book that is in every hand and deserves to remain there: *L’Amour*, by Michelet.

All that the Hellenic people, so enamored of physical beauty and of the graces of the spirit, had [p. 130] felt and understood regarding this lively and powerful sentiment which draws together and merges into a higher unity individuals who are dispersed, attenuating antagonisms and effacing differences, finds a new exposition and a more perfect form in the pen of the disciple of Socrates. Enclosing his teachings, as is his custom, in a dialogue among several speakers, he proceeds to set forth, in keeping with the more or less clearly symbolic role he assigns them, the diverse aspects under which he successively considers love, so that none may escape his consideration, and, the analysis exhausted, the reader may recompose the synthesis most adequate to his impressions. Plato doubtless possesses his conclusions; but also, as is his wont, he takes no care to state them, limiting himself to suggesting them. Once more he proceeds rather as artist than as master. It will therefore be necessary for us to hear each of his speakers, in order to know the whole thought of the philosopher. But first I must indicate to you the occasion on which he brings them together. The tragic poet Agathon has won a triumph on the stage and celebrates it with a banquet, to be enlivened by music, song, and above all by animated conversation and even philosophical disquisitions. When the hour has come, one of the [p. 131] guests proposes to put aside frivolous entertainments and to devote the evening to the praise of love, to be pronounced by those present in turn. Plato transcribes six of their speeches, and of all of them I shall offer you a brief summary. The first to speak is Phaedrus, who represents youth given to serious occupations, to the laborious preparation for an active life: he praises love because it is the most ancient of the gods, since neither the poets nor the authors of theogonies have been able to determine its origin, but above all for the manly virtues that it infuses into the breast of its adepts, because it teaches them heroism and self-sacrifice: an army composed of lovers, he affirms, would be invincible, and the city that possessed it would subjugate the earth. Pausanias, who symbolizes reflective and benevolent old age, accepts this praise, but distinguishes two kinds of love: that inspired by low and vulgar appetites, which follows after Venus Polymnia, and that which seeks the higher objects, the noblest and most exquisite in Nature as the end toward which it tends, which follows the celestial Venus, the Venus Urania. He concludes by indicating that only among men is true love possible, which leads to the exchange of mutual services, with a view to the gradual perfecting of lover and beloved. The physician [p. 132] Eryximachus, science that universalizes, rises to more general considerations: he establishes that love is the harmony of opposites, and that its empire embraces Nature. Following him along this path, to complete his doctrine, Aristophanes, who here represents the humorous sense, lays down that in love like seeks like; and he confirms it with an ingenious and strange myth, according to which human beings were at first double, but, separated later into two portions by Jupiter, who wished to punish their pride, there has remained to them from their primitive and perfect state an insatiable desire to find that lost half, by whose union alone they will succeed in realizing anew the integrity of their being; so that love is the constant tendency toward unity. Agathon, who personifies poetry, speaks in marvelous language and in grandiloquent style of the nature and the benefits of Love, the youngest, the most beautiful, the most tender, the most delicate, the most just, the strongest, and the most skillful of the gods. The youngest, because he seeks youths by preference; the most beautiful, since he goes about lost in pursuit of beauty; the most tender, inasmuch as he inspires every gentle affection; the most delicate, since he fixes his seat in the heart of mortals; the most just, because [p. 133] he abominates all violence; the strongest, because he dominates all beings; the most skillful, since he has been the first artificer and master of human inventions. Through him men have drawn near and united, social life has been polished, and humanity has attained its full flowering. The panegyric has reached its end. This is the moment Plato chooses to give the word to Socrates, on whose lips he always places the most beautiful and profound lessons of his wisdom. The philosopher begins by toning down whatever may have been exaggerated in those praises. Love is not a god, for were he one, he would possess all perfections and desire none, whereas it is of his essence to feel an incessant appetite for everything perfect. He is a being intermediate between the gods and men, son of abundance and need, wherefore he lacks everything and aspires to everything, serving as a goad to mortals to attain the beautiful and the good. He constantly desires, but desires in order to possess, and possesses in order to produce. His object is fecundation in body and mind, with a view to the elevation of beings, to perfection, to indefinite progress, to immortality. In his eternal ascent, he begins by fixing upon a beautiful body, but this is only his initiation: from this he passes to consider and love [p. 134] corporeal beauty in the abstract. Thus prepared and disposed, love rises to the beauty of a soul, which fecundates it and makes it capable of the contemplation of spiritual beauty, of absolute beauty, in which the supreme good and complete truth are fused. In these sublime regions the mind is possessed by an inextinguishable ardor for the possession of knowledge, for the exercise of virtue, and for the communication of the infinite beauty that it contemplates and comprehends. The man who, inspired by this exalted love, possesses the highest science, the science of the beautiful, partakes of all the virtues and engenders them in others; his life is truly happy: he becomes on Earth the living image of the gods.

Here ends the expository part of the dialogue. The author, with exquisite art, has been setting before our sight the sentiment he analyzes under manifold aspects; its heroic side, its moral, cosmological, social, poetic, and metaphysical sides appear successively bathed in light. He seems to leave the subject exhausted: it is not possible to withdraw from the charm of his limpid and luminous style, nor to fail to admire the serene majesty of his thought, ever more elevated, ever more distant from earthly things. And yet, this marvelous work does not satisfy the [p. 135] modern reader, who discovers throughout it an irremediable void. One might call it one of those magnificent amphorae, a prodigy of ceramics, on which the brush of a Nicosthenes or of a Euphronios has delicately traced the most beautiful conceptions of his art. All in it is enchantment to the sight: you touch it, it gives you a false sound; you draw near to look into it, it has no bottom. In this admirable dialogue, devoted to love, woman is missing.

And this is not to be wondered at, because, given the constitution of those societies, their customs and their ideas, it was bound to turn out so naturally. Those marvelous artists and subtle philosophers adored force and wholly ignored the notion of equality: every weak being was an inferior being, and therefore oppressed. Woe to the vanquished races! Woe to woman, humiliated by nature and by law! In the pride of their incontestable superiority they came to regard the slavery of native peoples and the perpetual tutelage of woman as a form of protection; hence, as a benefit. To raise and equalize through respect the human individual, in homage to his superior nature, susceptible of generation and reform, would have been for them a chimerical and absurd idea. The fallen man lay prostrate when he was not annihilated on the spot. For the Hellenes [p. 136] woman was radically inferior, in body and mind. How was she to be the preferred object of that sublime sentiment, capable only of linking and drawing close superior souls? This explains to us the secondary role of woman in Greek literature, and that almost complete absence of love as we moderns conceive it, which obliged Fontenelle to say that the ancients did not possess the science of the heart. Let us open their epic poets, their tragedians, even their lyric poets: in all, the same deficiency. Let us recall the tenderest episode contained in the works of Homer, the meeting of Hector and Andromache at the Scaean gates: the young wife advances, anguished, followed by the nurse who carries in her arms the infant Astyanax; the hero smiles at the sight of his son, and is moved as he asks the king of the gods to infuse him with valor and grant him greater renown than his father’s. But when his thought turns to the fair Andromache, it is only to picture to himself, with pride and sadness, that perhaps she who has been his companion, and a princess among the inhabitants of Ilium, will become a slave in the dwellings of an Argive and weave with her delicate hands the cloth that is to clothe a victorious soldier. In the theater of Aeschylus love does not figure, and when Sophocles presents his most beautiful [p. 137] and delicate feminine types, like Electra or Antigone, he animates them with the affections of filial piety or maternal tenderness, carried at times to the point of sacrifice, never with the overmastering passion that for us Juliet or Portia personify. And so true is this that even in Euripides, the revolutionary among the Greek tragedians, Achilles, detained by Clytemnestra, reproaches her with these words: “Let me go, for it would not be seemly for me to stop and converse with women.”

One would have to come down to the writers of the decadence, to minds cultivated in other ideas, amid other customs and in different countries, nourished on other doctrines, like Theocritus among the poets or Plutarch among the philosophers, to find anything of the modern sentiment. And so continuously and profoundly are opinions being modified; so greatly does the role of woman change with the course of the centuries and social innovations, that to be able to follow her through the manifestations of mental life would be to show you the most curious transformation, revealing how at the threshold of modern times the mystic Platonism of Dante symbolizes love in a woman; that Beatrice, copy of all gentleness and beauty, who cannot be contaminated by passing through the [p. 138] infernal regions, but who accompanies the poet in spirit in his pilgrimage through the city of eternal woe, until she receives him on his coming out of Purgatory, to open to him the ascent to the mansions where dwell the uncreated light and eternal felicity. You would see also the poetic Platonism of the Petrarchists and the sentimental gallantry of the troubadours deifying beauty, and the courtly gallantry of the Renaissance raising altars to wit and to feminine graces, and the naturalistic sentimentalism of the Revolution and its precursors leading woman to the temple of Nature to proclaim her for the first time the equal of man, priestess also of the great universal cult.

But we cannot linger so long, and we must come to our own times, where the reaction in favor of woman has in reality culminated, to consider the other book of which I propose to speak to you and which is specially devoted to her. Its subject, as you know, is love, and its first and last words are dedicated to woman. This suffices to prove to us how completely the point of view has changed. Love recovers in a certain way its natural proportions, but on the other hand its social importance grows to such a degree that there is no problem among all those presented by the [p. 139] extraordinary complexity of modern life that is not bound up with it, demonstrating its overruling influence in all that concerns the intercourse and communion of men. Without the family there exist neither the commune nor the city, and these are the nucleus of the State. The sentiment that creates and maintains the first is transformed, but subsists in essence to create and maintain the higher and more comprehensive units. If the waters that unite their volume to form a broad river spring from pure and crystalline fountains, crystalline and pure they will continue in their long course. Purify the home through love, and you will have purified public customs, which will in due time give you full testimony of the highest civic virtues. Like family, like citizen: in the gentle warmth of the home are refined the souls of the strong men who save the homeland in the forum and on the field of battle.

Michelet, the historian-poet, who with so loving and penetrating a gaze has sounded the life of ages past, has seen with clearest intuition where lies the danger of the feverish, restless, almost exclusively public existence of the man of our days; he has seen him swept along by the whirlwind of business or of pleasures, of speculation or of politics, and ever more distant from the point of departure, from [p. 140] the family, which is calm and repose, the rejuvenating spring, the true fountain of life and activity. Therefore he wrote not an idyll but a book of social reform. The cornerstone of the family is woman, who reigns and prolongs her reign through love. He asked science for its opinion of her, and science has presented him with a being sacred by her functions, more than all others lovable for her very weakness; a profound mystery of nature, before which we must pause with religious respect. Woman comes to her husband tender, weak, uncultivated. It falls to him to strengthen her, without drying up the springs of her exquisite sensibility; it falls to him to instruct her in the science of life, without bruising the flower of her virginal intelligence. She will repay him in treasures of love for his care and his respect; and in the midst of the tumultuous combats of existence, when the danger waxes fiercest and the shadows of the gloomy horizon thicken most, he will be able to turn his gaze securely backward, and will see shining amid the darkness the loving light that his vigilant companion keeps ever burning, a saving beacon in all storms. There is the harbor: sure shelter, new strength. In the constant communion of a virile thought, which fecundates, and an inexperienced intelligence, eager to receive the prolific pollen [p. 141] of every luminous notion, love must find eternal incentives; and the true and spontaneous union of two beings so diverse in the qualities of body and of spirit must form that higher unity which the philosopher contemplated and the moralist has fervently evoked. The man will have contributed on his part strength, activity, the costly experience of life: the woman will have brought in exchange tenderness, industriousness, and that spirit of sacrifice which is her most excellent dowry, the inestimable privilege by which she so often succeeds in saving the dearest treasures of her heart when all seemed lost in the shipwreck. She will receive his spirit in pledge and will build it a secure dwelling in her heart; he will go out into the world invested with his double personality, doubly strong and doubly responsible. Love, the bond that links the family, is afterward the solidarity that links fellow citizens; the focus whose light gave life to the children radiates about the home and extends to the city, to the homeland; which is no longer merely the earth, the fecund nurse, but the communion of spirits that understand and feel and believe and aspire in unison. Love, regenerated and sanctified at its most secret source, springs forth and runs and widens into a copious flow of lustral waters that regenerate and purify society [p. 142] in its entirety. The holy concord of two beings beneath one roof promises abundant fruits of concord to the peoples.

It is true that the undertaking of reform is not a small one, nor does the arduous labor it requires present itself to us as easy. The errors of so many centuries weigh upon our shoulders and hamper our steps. The very nature of the feminine character, delicate and timid, perspicacious and wary, complicates the problem. How is the husband to carry out that special education of his wife which his condition as a man imposes upon him, a master in the sad but necessary science of life? What is he to teach her? And how is he to teach her? Will he reveal to her, without any initiation, the recondite mysteries of Nature and of society? Will he condemn her eternally to the role of neophyte? It would not be possible for me to interpret here, at the length the subject requires, either the philosopher’s thought or my own. Allow me to condense them in a symbol, for which I beg your indulgence. There exists a marvelous work of ancient sculpture that we all know, whether cast in bronze, or modeled in plaster, or reproduced by the engraver: it is the Venus of Cnidus. The artist has stripped her of every garment and she rises in magnificent nakedness; but so serene her brow, [p. 143] so radiant her gaze, that to the rapt sight of him who contemplates her she appears clothed in grace and modesty. The goddess rises from the waves, naked and pure. Thus it is that I understand the artist, enamored of his work, whom we ought to find in the expert husband, must treat the intelligence of his wife. He will tear away without fear all the veils in which ignorance, prejudice, and fanaticism have wrapped it; he will tear them away without fear, but not without respect; so that, in the hours of the soul’s effusions as in the moments of trial, it may be given him in his turn to behold it naked and pure.

Here I might conclude, gentlemen, if a problem did not confront me which I must not fail to broach. To what point can we ourselves profit by these lofty and luminous teachings? A modern people—who doubts that for us the question is posed in the same terms and with the same imperious necessity? Ah! for us the terms are not the same, and the necessity is far more pressing. A modern people we are, by the age we have reached, and, to superficial eyes, by the external characters of our customs, of our ideas, of our culture, in a word. What do we lack? The advances of modern industry place in our hands powerful levers [p. 144] wherewith to remove the obstacles that the rebellious forces of Nature set against man, to improve his products and make our life easier and more abundant; the protective institutions of cultured peoples guarantee to the citizen among us life, labor, dignity; our social relations bear the stamp of civility and elegance; in splendid festivals we pay tribute to the arts that beautify life; in learned academies we discourse eloquently upon the important and obscure problems that trouble the intelligence of the child of the century… Prestigious appearances! Let us not be deluded, trusting in such smiling mirages. Let us fear, as we fear death, the fata morgana of our fancy. Let us but look a little more closely, and sad reality will present itself to us unadorned, and we shall see that here the modern is the outer varnish: at bottom we have an ancient society, built upon the same foundations as those that left so memorable an example to the world by their fall: the slavery of a race and the contempt of woman. It is true that in few places is a more ostentatious external cult rendered to the fair sex; I do not mean to deny your gallantry. Woman is the queen of our soirées, of our drawing-rooms, she enslaves our senses [p. 145] and wins from us the most fervent applause, the most complete praises, with her wit, her grace, and her beauty. But it is equally true that in few places do men profess a more mediocre esteem for the qualities that give woman’s nature its true worth; in few is her company less sought, and she less consulted on serious matters, unless it be sometimes to fill a legal or courtesy formality. To the law that leaves her unprotected is joined the empire of custom, which regards her as radically weak and frivolous. Is the fault that of the Cuban woman? No; as mothers, their inexhaustible tenderness sanctifies them; as wives, their boundless self-denial sublimes them; as patriots, their fortitude of soul will some day furnish the fairest pages of our history. The fault lies with the environment in which they have not lived, but vegetated; with the baneful disequilibrium in which their faculties have been left for want of cultivation; with the implacable supremacy that his strength has given to man, exercised without curb or discretion from the earliest age over so many weak and abject beings, accustomed to bend without so much as a murmur at the frowning of the black brows of the omnipotent lord. It is not in the gloomy school of servitude that man will learn to regard as his equal, to respect as his companion, [p. 146] woman, weaker by Nature, defenseless before the law, treated as an enemy by opinion. For this reason among us the separation and the misunderstanding between the sexes bring consequences infinitely more dangerous than in the countries where Michelet preached his wise reform. The spectacle of our youth, ever more frivolous and ever farther from the path to which public misfortunes are calling it, could not be more eloquent. It is time to think seriously of the remedy for what I consider the most desperate of our ills. The edifice our fathers founded threatens fatally to fall in ruin. It is time to prepare the new foundations, to think of the future constructions, to trace the work of the days to come. If we do not educate woman that she may be the coadjutor in the arduous labor of our social life; if we do not educate the young in respect and love for woman, who is to be their partner in the task of existence, let us dismiss from our breast its sweetest guest, hope… But no: let us not close our ear so early to its promises; let us hope that the lessons of the wisdom that radiates in happier regions will not find us blind, and that the teachings of our own costly experience will not find us indifferent.

[p. 147]

We shall doubtless teach our children how to raise that edifice, far more solid, able to withstand all the commotions of the earth and all the gusts of the wind, and beneath whose hospitable roof a true people may gather, united in a spirit of love, to practice the great social virtues: the charity that lifts up the weak, the respect that makes us all equals. Then, truly, may the generations that succeed us join their voice to the sacred hymn of labor that the laborious peoples of the Earth raise to the heights, and ask their share in the common wage of equal esteem among men, of brotherhood among peoples, of full and true justice among nations. When you shall do this, O compatriots! then and only then will you be worthy of so much light as you bear in your intelligence, of so much fire as burns in your hearts.

## EMERSON <sup>(1)</sup>

Ladies and Gentlemen:

T he eminent man of whom I am to speak to you tonight, having to address the Manchester Athenaeum on the occasion of a literary solemnity, at a moment when a tremendous commercial crisis was scourging that very rich district, began by congratulating his audience because neither the grave preoccupations of the present nor the terrible threats of the future had succeeded in cooling their fondness for the exercises of the mind, or in distracting them from attending that festival of the intelligence. Nothing could be more natural than this remembrance,

[p. 150]

when, in my turn, I can contemplate with intimate satisfaction that, amid far greater anxieties, more imminent perils, and in the face of far more irreparable calamities, you too come, in numerous concourse and with a happy disposition of mind, to forget for some hours the anguish of the frightful situation that overwhelms us, seeking solace for the understanding and strength for the will in the commerce of letters or in the cultivation of the arts. A most profitable gymnastic this proves to be for the mind, because it discovers to us inaccessible regions, where, assuredly, the commotions that wound and shatter the body do not reach, and where it is always lawful for us to go in search of refuge and refreshment in the sad hours of weariness or discouragement, so often the forerunners of gloomy, inert despair. Happy I, if I succeed in furthering your purpose, presenting for your consideration a theme worthy of attracting you and capable of leaving engraved on your memory some fruitful idea or some generous principle.

With a perspicacious and not seldom profound gaze, various orators have surveyed from this place the annals of humanity, seeking in the varied vicissitudes of the drama of history scenes and personages that might profitably [p. 151] stir curiosity or treasure up well-assayed instruction. But, for reasons I need not stop to weigh, it has almost always been old Europe that has been presented to us as the matter of study, whenever we have sought to recall how man has lived or how are developed the slow and complex evolutions that result in the progress of his culture, the successive stages of civilization. Today I propose to direct my gaze nearer. If Europe is and will always be our great teacher in the traditions of the past, fecund germ of all the grandeurs of the future, America is, and will be ever more, the great laboratory where the present tries out with the ancient elements the new experiments that are to rejuvenate, if not regenerate, humanity. And if we, by race, language, and political ties, are a European people, by geographical situation, by social organization, by the influences we ceaselessly receive and the aspirations we constantly cherish, we are an American people. The affairs of America have, therefore, for us an immediate and permanent interest; and an illustrious American, whose literary and philosophical role in the New World will be the theme of my lecture, is going to allow me on this occasion to tell you something of what so greatly concerns us.

[p. 152]

Although social transformations all appear, seen immediately, as the work of man, they may at once be separated into two groups with sufficiently distinct characters, and which seem to succeed one another, though it is not yet possible to determine their order of succession. Changes, apparently small, introduce noiselessly into the social aggregate active elements of modification; they pursue their invisible labor without truce, and with the passing of time show suddenly to the eye of the attentive observer a colossal work, where the ancient blocks of stone have changed their places and whose structure preserves signs of the ancient arrangement only in its exterior. At other times violent commotions threaten to overthrow everything existing, upset and confound it all, and when they calm their fury, present an immense field of fragments and rubble that compel to labor and facilitate reconstruction. To the first class belongs the discovery of America in the fifteenth century; and not because its importance was wholly hidden from the learned men of the time, but because they were very far from suspecting that those voyages made with so little apparatus, and those first settlements of handfuls of men on wholly unknown shores, were, as time went on, [p. 153] to change the face of Europe, to carry the policy of its states along new channels, and to give a new aspect to the civilization of the world. Industry, commerce, wealth, culture, which for so many centuries had been the patrimony of the peoples bordering the Mediterranean, begin to migrate farther west and to establish their seat on the fortunate coasts of the Atlantic; European civilization passes, according to Carl Ritter’s classification, from the thalassic period to the oceanic period, and it seems to multiply its strength a hundredfold as the space it embraces and dominates grows. What the great lake enclosed between Europe and Africa had been, the immense sea that separates two hemispheres was now to be; and the daring ships that began to furrow it in all directions, in exchange for the germs of new life they brought to the new world, returned laden with countless treasures that increased the vigor and power of the old. The civilization of the Christian peoples, in the very moments when it grows and is strengthened by the transfusion of the purest sap of antiquity, finds before it an unlimited field in which to extend, grandiose enterprises to undertake, unknown experiments to try: the detachment of numerous bodies of citizens, [p. 154] the occupation or the conquest of peoples foreign by race, by customs, by manner of living and governing themselves; the extension of the State to distant lands and across seas remote and scarcely known, emigration and colonization, in short, in proportions and form hitherto without example. Each group of soldiers, adventurers, or pilgrims is to become a nucleus of active population, where the vitality of its components begins to be tested, to preserve their properties and acquire those that the new adaptation required. They spread over the immense continent, isolated at first, feebly united afterward, cohering at length and displaying openly the work of their slow labor of proliferation and organization. European culture has been grafted upon the New World, begins to flower, and already announces its approaching luxuriance.

Setting aside facts that are to a certain point secondary, we may assert that the civilization brought to America by the European settlers assumes two wholly different forms, corresponding to the two distinct peoples to whom the greater part of the work of colonization is due: the Spanish form and the Anglo-Saxon. As we are more familiar with the characters of the first, it is of the second [p. 155] that I am to treat briefly, because it bears thus upon the end and plan of my address.

When the pilgrims of the *Mayflower*, when the *Fathers* landed on the unknown soil of America, which offered to their eyes nothing but the impenetrability of its age-old forests, they took heart again, grouping around them the wife and the children who shared their lot, seeing within their reach the tools of labor that wrests from the earth the sustenance of the body, and lifting to heaven the book that served them to put their souls in free communication with the god they worshiped and whose doctrines were the nourishment of their spirit. Not the longing for adventure, nor the thirst for riches, nor the fervor of propaganda impelled their steps: the yearning for liberty, the thirst for repose, the need of reconstituting a home, where they might pursue, exempt from the obstacles of old prejudices and of new fanaticisms, the traditional life of the homeland left behind but not abandoned, where they might perpetuate the customs of the good English people, where they might exercise the rights of the Saxon man, guided them. A new home in the new land; another, vaster field for the vigorous activity of robust natures, and beside the sacred domestic fire, as in the communal gathering of all the colonists, the most [p. 156] lofty spirit of sociability inspiring everything; equality establishing its code of mutual rights and duties, liberty effectively sheltering each workman of the future people, ennobling and fecundating his labor. The never-extinguished ferment of Germanic individualism finds now abundant nourishment in the exceptional circumstances of those colonies; the spirit of brotherhood, imbibed from the Gospel and purified by persecutions, fuses it into a higher conception of the social state; the uninterrupted exercise of the rights inherent in the condition of the Englishman soon finds the forms in which that concept is to acquire real life; and thus there filter into customs, into the formation of the family, into the organization of the commune, into the constitution of the churches, and even into the institutions regulating the whole mechanism of the State, the germs that one day are to bear as fruit the grandiose work, scarcely announced by the ancient world, only sketched in the modern, and now in full flower, of American democracy.

Full civil equality harmonizing all individual antagonisms, identity of political functions clearing the way for the most various capacities, the diverse spheres [p. 157] of the State revolving with extreme amplitude in their respective orbits, the Constitution, most sacred palladium of national life, venerated in every heart, defended by all the citizens, consecrated and immortalized by public respect, the will, the supreme will of a free people impelling even in its smallest details the complicated machine of so many regional governments joined together to form the central government of the Republic; and all this in the face of the still visible residues of the old feudal organization of the European states, of the ever-lively pretensions of ancient privileges, of the separation and strife of the social classes, of the multiplication and encumbrance of all the governmental wheels, of the capital centralization for the benefit of one part of the country or of a group of individuals, of the caprice, the interests, or the ideas of a few, arbiters of the nation’s future. What wonder, then, if to the eyes of aged Europe the never-imagined spectacle of this splendid foundation, the work wholly of liberty, presented itself as something radically unstable, when not altogether outside the laws common to societies capable of harmoniously fulfilling their destinies! Seeing all creeds coexist in peace and the propaganda of all [p. 158] systems respected; looking with astonishment upon the experiments, contradicted by no one, to carry into practice the most singular theories; observing the novel spectacle of a constant accession of heterogeneous elements, of men come from every country, and not to upset but to consolidate the existing order of things; contemplating, in short, for the first time, man in the full use of his activity, without artificial restraints, without alien impulsions, in the entire integrity of his independence, in the midst of his fellow-associates and facing the public organization, and perceiving that he succeeded by this means in achieving the most perfect work of cooperation of which the world has had example, and in allying in the political sphere the two principles held to be opposed, of the most unshakable order and of the most entire liberty; most of the thinkers of the old continent, submissive to routine and attentive to their prejudices, found their doctrines so belied, felt their notions so confounded, that they ended by not realizing what they were themselves seeing.

It will suffice me to adduce one well-known example. Tocqueville wished to explain to the ancient world the surprising phenomena of which the new was the theater; he studied them without passion or prejudice, and with sympathy rather than aloofness; he felt [p. 159] admiration for the solid virtues that have laid the foundation of that colossal civil and political edifice; he was dazzled before that vertiginous activity which seems to overflow at random and yet produces with admirable tact and precision the surest results, peopling immense solitudes, improvising magnificent cities, crossing a continent with convenient, safe, splendid roads, making wealth, well-being, and the comfort of all flow in torrents from the hidden veins that concealed or disguised them from the eyes of man; and, nevertheless, he did not arrive at a just and complete appreciation of the great social forces he saw at work; he did not extend his inductions to the intellectual and moral sphere; he sought to draw deductively from a few abstract principles the total development of a people’s life, and was deceived in his forecasts. Tocqueville condemned American society to mediocrity in all the higher manifestations of social existence, denied it the possibility of attaining high culture in the order of intelligence and of sentiments, sentenced it to sterility in the domain of artistic production and of speculation, attributing to democracy a leveling force, fatal to everything that tends to stand out or excel. Since then this judgment has come [p. 160] to be a commonplace in the pen of innumerable writers.

And yet the later development of the United States has been a continual giving of the lie to that arbitrary sentence. In the sphere of morals they gave the world the most beautiful spectacle that history records, on the day when, at the cost of the bloodiest sacrifice, they summoned to social life four million pariahs; and an entire people, that had been their redeemer, set itself to instruct them and prepare them for their role as citizens. In the field of study and the fine arts their effort has been so continuous, persevering, and happy that their culture shines today on a par with that displayed by the precursor peoples, heirs of the accumulated labor of so many centuries. The centers of higher education in the United States rival the foremost of Europe in the quality of their studies, and surpass them in the significance and moral importance of their institutions. Far from the level of the educated classes being lowered, there has been seen an incessant rising from the lower strata of all those who had in them aptitude and spirit; and to favor and provoke this ascent their whole system of education has tended and tends, the badge and purest glory of that democracy.

[p. 161]

In order not to reduce the demonstration of my assertion to a bare statistic, which would nevertheless be very easy to make, I have wished to set forth a single example, but of such quality that it should suffice by itself to bring conviction. I am going to consider under its various phases a man of letters who is American: by his birth, by his education, by his profession, by his character, opinions, and writings truly American, to see to what point the social environment in which he grew up and lived was an obstacle or a benefit to the development of his natural aptitudes. Thus it is that I propose to study Emerson and his works, and I promise myself that this study will make plain to us the happy influence of democracy upon literature, and in general upon the life of thought.

Emerson was born in Boston, a famous center of culture in the United States, of old American stock, in a family devoted on both sides to the priestly ministry, that is, belonging to one of the most temperate and enlightened classes of the Union. Everything around him seemed arranged to trace for him a straight path that should carry him without stumbling or deviation through life, along that middle line along which, in tranquil epochs, there goes, without great anguish or great aspirations, the common run of [p. 162] people. Neither did shocks too violent, never advisable at an early age, exacerbate or blunt his sensibility, and he was able to preserve it intact and exquisite—for so Nature had bestowed it upon him—to receive the beneficent influence of good things, easy to find in what surrounded him, and the fecundating influence of beautiful things, which he would know how to discover by native propensity in natural objects and in human actions. He began in good time the cultivation of his accessible, vivacious, and profound intelligence, and to prepare himself for the service of the Unitarian Church, for preaching and teaching, in the bosom of a religious community that distinguished itself by the elevation of its aims, by scrupulousness in practice, by the sobriety and good sense of its doctrine, and in its time particularly by the philosophic spirit that animated its masters and by the personal merit of these. The Unitarians esteemed, almost on a par with a dogma, liberty of investigation and examination, and held it baseness of spirit to retreat before the consequences of any sincerely conducted inquiry. The effervescence which in the second third of this century powerfully agitated the religious communions of England had its repercussion in this society of select spirits, who had begun [p. 163] to find *too cold, inanimate, and mechanical* the religion of the day, as Ripley expressed it. The need to seek a solution for their doubts and outward support for their attempts at reform, at a moment when, under the influence of Coleridge’s doctrines, the long reign of the school of Locke was beginning to decline, led them to range their glances over all points of the speculative horizon, and the disposition of their minds found itself particularly flattered by the bold theories of the German idealists and their French imitators; and from this there arose among the Unitarians Transcendentalism, one of whose principal representatives Emerson was for a long time to be. The two qualities that distinguished this school, as against the others professed in the Union, had a singular influence in fecundating his intelligence: the extensive knowledge of foreign literatures and philosophical systems; rationalism placed at the service of theology, making of reason a kind of transcendent sense, revealer of the divine essence. No artificial barrier, therefore, stayed his first steps through the field of science, nor did routine encumber him with its rusty irons: he could approach all the chairs and hear all the oracles; he entered into direct communion with Nature, and [p. 164] harassed her with the arms of experience that she might reveal to him her secrets, confident that in the end his reason would speak to him the last word of the great enigma he felt throbbing in her immense bosom; he studied men in the present hour, which we regard as that of maturity, and in the long infancy and boisterous youth of humanity, seeking the invisible finger that traces for them, in the dark depths of life, the luminous wake that seems to guide them; and he made ready to enter the common lists, rich in intelligence, resolute in will, with his imagination teeming and filled with full confidence in his reason, which for him was full of revelations. He felt himself strong, and he believed; the world spoke to him in an encouraging and fortifying language; Nature set before his eyes a smiling picture in which the picturesque was only the outward expression of the profoundly significant; the society of which he was to be a member stood ready to receive him; it asked no one: “Whence do you come? Whither are you going? What do you think? What do you believe?” It looked to see whether in the newcomer’s hands the instruments of labor shone, and in his eyes the purpose to be industrious and good, and it opened a path to him. Every honest conviction is there respected; for every activity there is room and cooperation; for him who excels there is applause and reward, because his services are to be more [p. 165] eminent. The task of life thus becomes much easier. Emerson undertook it serenely and actively. When there came for him (as for whom does it not?) the hour of doubt; when at a turning of the path, until then smooth, he discovered roughnesses he had not suspected and more than one road to the end he had not clearly perceived, the halt did not have to be lasting, nor the deliberation prolonged: he judged one road better, and followed it without wavering, though changing his direction. Those who had accompanied him thus far let him go, and went on accompanying him with their respect. He neither conceived, nor would it have been easy for him to conceive, of a worthy man without sincerity in word and in deed. Hence he has said and taught so energetically: “Speak what you think now in hard words and to-morrow speak what to-morrow thinks in hard words again, though it contradict every thing you said to-day.” When the moment came, Emerson declared his contradiction and proved it by his acts. The case deserves to be recalled.

He was exercising his ministry, beloved and respected by all those whom he edified by word and example; but his mind continued its powerful evolution, and he soon discovered that he withheld his assent from some of the oldest practices and the most significant rites of his church. He sought its reform with prudence [p. 166] and resolution, but in vain: his fellow-believers remained attached to what was established. He then convened them, set forth to them in simple and eloquent terms his dissent, took leave of them with tenderness, and left the ministry. “It is my desire, in the office of a Christian minister,”—he told them,—“to do nothing which I cannot do with my whole heart. Having said this, I have said all.” Admirable words, which reveal the man to us and depict a whole state of civilization.

In the great national conflict that put to the test the temper and the feelings of so many millions of citizens, and that, above all, left no one indifferent, in the ardent abolitionist campaign that preceded the tremendous war of liberation, Emerson stood in the front rank among the forerunners. Years before Channing, he openly embraced the cause of the slave, and amid the passions aroused by John Brown’s bold attempt, during his imprisonment and before his scaffold, his voice was raised in favor of the martyr, and began to demand the verdict that was belatedly to honor his memory. He fought Webster at the height of his popularity, and protected Harriet Martineau, persecuted and almost hated. Apart from these cases, which reveal to us the part [p. 167] considerable of his heart and of his intelligence that he gave to his duties as a citizen, his action upon his countrymen took other channels, and his whole life presents a gently lighted picture in which the colors blend harmoniously, so as neither to tire the eye nor to dull the mind. He lived domestically, in a home perfumed by the gentlest affections, sharing his love and his care among his mother, *whitest, mildest, most conservative* of ladies, his wife Lydia, and his children; attentive to his affairs, which prospered thanks to his assiduity and skill, giving daily proofs of his gifts as a practical man and a man of the world, and at the same time devoting to speculation a good part of his time, loving nature and enjoying its charms, communicating almost daily with other men in the loftiest form, giving them the best of his rich and pure intelligence in writings full of vigor and fancy, thus making evident that in his privileged organization the speculative and the practical, imagination and worldly sagacity, were allied almost in equal measure. Nothing more natural, therefore, than the considerable influence that he gradually acquired over the American public, to be changed at last into lasting and enthusiastic veneration. His [p. 168] merit did not impose itself from the first moment, nor did his fame spring up radiant in a day; rather it was like the Sun, which rises pale among the clouds, but as it climbs the blue vault and its warm rays spread, shows itself more resplendent, until, the morning clouds dispersed, it appears in all its glory and pours down from the zenith heat, light, and fecundity.

Those who know only by hearsay that society which seems to bestir itself solely in pursuit of the possession of material goods, and whose vertiginous activity is comparable to none other, will not readily understand the lasting influence upon it of a writer of eminently poetic temperament and of a wholly speculative cast of mind. But we must look to the depth of things and discover that Emerson’s accord with his race and his people was in every respect perfect. There are many sides of the Saxon’s life full of singular poetry: men of the Germanic races love passionately communion with nature; the diversity of aspects it presents to them contrasts with the monotony of urban life; so these indefatigable workers of the city voluntarily make two portions of their existence, and give the one to rest [p. 169] in the country, to bodily exercise in the open air or to profound meditation, far from the unwholesome bustle of the multitudes. These are the hours in which the beloved voice of the favorite author reaches them with a new emotion or a new teaching, and in which, at the evocation of the preferred artist or philosopher, an ideal of life takes shape before their eyes. These practical men make more use of theories than do some others, and this prosaic people has an intense love of the ideal. And if the writer who speaks to them periodically is the legitimate product of his society, and draws from it the elements, the blood, and the life of his intelligence, where they do no more than purify themselves and take on a more harmonious and suggestive form, the mentor begins by being the friend of the hours of repose and ends by being the oracle of every moment.

Among American writers few have realized this type so completely as Emerson. It is time that we see him in his style and in his works, in his way of conceiving the great problems that our sensibility and our intelligence set before us, and in his way of presenting and resolving them.

When we open an unfamiliar book, the first thing that strikes us is the author’s manner of speaking. It is his first revelation, the first part [p. 170] of his inmost being that puts us in a position to appreciate; and according as it is careless or correct, placid or vigorous, commonplace or given to innovations, such is the physiognomy we provisionally attribute to him. By his style an author captivates or vexes us: it is after he has held us by the charm of his speech that he moves us, stirs us, or teaches us. Emerson’s style tells us at once what manner of thinker he belongs to. It would be difficult to give, to one who does not know his works, an idea of the very peculiar character of a writer who, in hatred of all that is imposed and conventional, surprises at every step with the strangest contrasts of conception and expression, and is by turns luminous and obscure, vulgar and sublime, metaphysical and positivist, believing and skeptical, but always great and original. He belongs to a race of thinkers that has given few types to universal literature, but to each of whom a unique and exclusive place must be assigned. Like Jean Paul Richter, like Carlyle, Emerson thinks so profoundly, and his conception is at the same time so vast, that the common molds of ordinary language are necessarily too narrow for him, and his style is so overloaded with nuances and allusions, similes and metaphors so drown the direct sense of the sentence, [p. 171] there is such an expenditure of imagination and erudition in his conceptions, that the first effect produced by reading him is in truth dazzlement. But beneath this brilliant and even excessively affected form, there is revealed a mind so perspicacious, a reflection so sustained upon all the great human problems, and a sensibility so exquisite, sympathetic, and generous, that it is impossible to escape the charm, and the reader ends by confessing that on concluding those eloquent pages he has seen more and farther, has felt more and better.

The preferred forms in which Emerson has communicated to us his ideas on the world, man, and societies were the most popular in English literature: the essay, of shorter compass, less solemn and regular than the treatise, but capable of the same depth and able to exhaust a subject, which it can present in every light and clothe with all the attraction of the varied and unforeseen; and the lecture or reading, where, with all the pathetic resources of oratory at his service, the author may at the same time preserve the tone, the turns, and the freedom of familiar talk. He liked to condense the slow-ripening labor of his prolonged meditations into a few paragraphs, and to fix in one or two swift and luminous [p. 172] phrases the momentary product of his powerful intuition of all that was notable or significant in what surrounded him; for this reason he was averse to the patient effort of a lengthy composition, and as he also communicated with a public daily impressed by the novelties of every sort occurring throughout the whole world, it was necessary for him to touch upon the most varied subjects with the greatest concision and lucidity.

Hence there teem in his pages thoughts that express the widest range of impressions conceivable, and his admirers have been able to extract from them sentences or aphorisms to serve as profitable reading for each day of the year: so well do they answer the problems that life may set us or the situations in which circumstances may place us. He wrote also poems, rich in feeling and originality, but whose metrical form turns out forced, covering with a certain obscurity both the substance and the expression. The fetters of the versifier did not suit the free and hurried gait of the essayist.

A complete enumeration of Emerson’s works would here be tedious and out of place. It will suffice me to consider cursorily his general conception of the three great spheres of reality that may be the object of our meditations [p. 173] and the books in which he more particularly developed them.

In the order of objective priority, as in the chronological order, Emerson’s first work is his long essay on *Nature*.

In his peaceful and picturesque retreat at Concord, far enough from men to enjoy in peace the reinvigorating commerce of natural things, and near enough to them and their works that isolation might not be poisoned with the bitter dregs of misanthropy, he wrote these pages, impregnated with a sweet and profound satisfaction in the gifts of life. A perspicacious and almost inspired witness of the invisible labor that slowly renews the world, and of the mysterious circulation that makes life vibrate and throb in its bosom, he believes he discovers the supreme law that binds and links the intangible atom to the immense stars; and, placing man amid so many marvels that he may discover and contemplate it, he delights in enumerating all the aspects of this grandiose investigation, and in reproducing all the pictures in which the human mind can harmoniously epitomize the cosmos. Beautiful, noble, magnificent is Nature in his eyes, but not deserted; rather animated by the presence of man [p. 174] who scrutinizes it, possesses it, and, applying to it the reconstructive powers of his intelligence, makes it significant and raises it to complete loveliness. In a division full of originality he presents to us the four great categories that comprise all the phases of this incessant communication of subject and object. Man makes use of Nature for his *commodity*, and turns it into the abundant granary where he gathers the fruits that appease need and regale the taste, into the inviolable retreat where he finds rest and where the weary body is restored, into the field of action where he exercises and tempers his forces for the noble labor that dignifies him, into the theater of all the deeds that exalt him, into the pedestal on which he sits to raise his limited spirit to the dazzling conception of the universal spirit. Man finds in Nature *beauty*, and the limits of things seem to extend before his sight; he perceives new phases in objects, secret concordances, unforeseen relations that bring the dissimilar together, and an unexpected harmony reigns amid the apparent confusion, and the soul easily interpenetrates the manifold and the infinite. Thus fecundated, it feels the need to produce and creates a new world, a regular compendium, and in a certain way more perfect, of the objective [p. 175] one: the world of art. But beauty is no more than preparation and good disposition for higher ends: natural things acquire for man, on a higher scale, a language, a significance. The beautiful is the outer garment; however splendid, it does not go beyond the surface. At the bottom of universal mobility lies the final revelation of the permanent: the symbolic quality of objects is made manifest to us, the world speaks to us its language and reveals to us the great law of unity emanating from the single spirit. Man and things vanish, fused in the eternal essence, in the all-one. All is cold, all lies inert if it does not translate for us at least a particle of that supreme truth. But the idea shines forth and is discovered even in the object apparently most insignificant, and Nature herself offers man the means of arriving, by a fruitful *discipline*, at intuition, at the inner vision of these sublime inwardnesses. With activity trained, intelligence enlightened, will well disposed, human reason has at its service the forces necessary to penetrate the mystery of the world, to animate matter, to stamp it with its seal and to make manifest its unity with the divine essence, creating in itself and outside itself order, beauty, virtue, heroism; to realize, in a word, the [p. 176] ideal, to incarnate the idea. Thus there arises and ascends to the summit of Emerson’s thought the idealism that inspires it and gives it its tone and character. Nature intimately united with the spirit, placed at the service of the spirit, embellished, perfected, created in the end, by the spirit.

Emerson’s doctrines concerning man, his origin, his organization and destiny, the apportionment of good and evil in his life, the ends of his activity and the character that distinguishes him from other beings, are not contained in a single work, but scattered through all of them, though more especially in his two series of *Essays* and in *The Conduct of Life*. A most noble portion of Nature, in which the ideas or types sketched in the lower beings come to a happy consummation, man, endowed with the scrutinizing reason that raises him to the penetration of ultimate ends, is wholly disposed to action, and can subsist and improve only by action. Through it the sum of the goods and evils he encounters is balanced; by means of it he takes possession of the very ample share that falls to him in the domain of Nature; he preserves his personality intact against all the forces that tend to impair it; he maintains his rank as an equal among his fellows, and seeing his fruits, [p. 177] the work of his effort, he attains to full assurance of his power, which makes him inaccessible to all reverses and capable of all heroisms. Individualism, which constitutes the nerve and the security of democracies, has never had a more eloquent interpreter. But this doctrine, which teems and shows through in the whole work of our author, which is congenital to him, so to speak, because his surroundings inspired it in him, is not the one preferred and acquiesced in by his mind, molded from early on to other ideas. Idealism is his aspiration and his guiding star, and as there are dark regions where all idealism converges toward pantheism, and in them unprepared minds soon see the specter of fatalism take body and grow gigantic, the negation of every individualist theory, we ought not to wonder that when these theories about man come to seek their corollary in his principles about society, there results a manifestly contradictory conception, which denies to the social body the evolutionary power, and places in certain individuals a superior and hidden force that determines the movement of the whole aggregate: fatalism incarnate in great men; the theory of providential men rejuvenated and adorned with every sort of finery and amplification. For Emerson the great man is he who [p. 178] lives in those elevated regions of thought to which other mortals approach only with the greatest difficulty. In them are those who deserve that title, in permanent union with the being who sees all, penetrates all, and moves all, and so can translate in their works or in their actions the several aspects of the supreme essence, and are exceptionally great by intelligence, by character, or by activity; they present to their fellows the types they ought to imitate, and draw generations after them. Among them there is no priority: the conqueror and the philosopher, the poet and the legislator, the inventor and the prophet, are diverse revelations of a single manifestation, that of the universal being in particular beings: they represent in epitome humanity inspired and directed by the supreme reason immanent in it.

This theory, beautifully developed, is contained in Emerson’s most original and characteristic book, *Representative Men*. From the doctrinal point of view it may be asserted that it comes to be the development of this sentence of his great friend Carlyle: “Universal History … is at bottom the History of the Great Men who have worked here.” A thesis in every respect false, because it wholly ignores [p. 179] the laws that govern the organization and evolution of societies, and that explain, by the concurrence of cosmic, ethnic, and psychic forces, the appearance of those eminent individuals, the product, before being the cause, of social progress. But a singularly extraordinary book, and of most lively interest to the reader of Emerson, because it reveals to us the various beacons on the horizon of history on which his gaze preferably fixed, content with nothing less than traversing and scrutinizing the whole field of human activity, and the varied characters to whom he devoted his enthusiastic veneration. Influenced at times by the theories we have set forth, and docile at others to his humor and his tastes, he held and presented as ideal types in their respective spheres Plato, forerunner of free inquiry, and Napoleon, executioner of human thought; Swedenborg, lost in vertiginous flight through the inaccessible regions of mysticism, and Montaigne, advancing with assurance and prudence over the firm ground of discreet observation of the world and of methodical and voluntarily limited speculation. For him these contradictions vanished in the intimate unity of his conception of social life.

It cannot be denied that only a mind very [p. 180] free of prejudices and very disdainful of preconceptions is capable of rising to those heights, where eminences are effaced and the most broken terrains appear as vast plains; but it is not in this work, devoted to demonstrating a thesis, that the generality of readers could be convinced that such was Emerson’s mind. A book written on the occasion of his second journey to England, *English Traits*, would serve this purpose much better. It is curious to see this pure American, this wholly modern man, amid that old society so solidly seated upon its most ancient foundation-stones, upon which, nevertheless, he does not fear to raise the newest constructions, feeling himself a stranger to it by his most native habits, by his way of seeing political problems, by his manner of understanding life, and feeling himself drawn to it by the thousand secret ties of common origin, of the sediments of religious beliefs, and by the general lines of the conception of social existence. Much he finds censurable and censures; much laudable and applauds. His perspicacious sight goes to the bottom of customs, institutions, and traits of character, and separates in everything the pure elements from the dross; his judgment does not let itself be seduced by grandeur, nor deceived by the [p. 181] brilliant appearances, but neither led astray by local differences, nor, still less, surprised by accessory dissents; therefore the final balance, which turns out in the end favorable to the old Norman-Saxon land, appears marked with a seal of inestimable impartiality. And one cannot read without emotion the final salutation with which the citizen of free America takes leave of the old home where his race grew up and his traditions were incubated: “All hail! *mother of nations*,” he says to her, and never has a nobler phrase consecrated a fact more momentous in the domain of history. The greatest and best-deserved praise that England has obtained she owes to the lips and the sincere heart of one of her emancipated descendants.

As for the fundamental contradiction that I have pointed out in Emerson’s theory, between his idealism and the individualist principles of which he is the constant interpreter, between the equivalence in nature and dignity that he recognizes in all men and the absolute supremacy with which he endows the heroes of action or thought, though irreducible for a scrupulous critical sense, it disappears or is attenuated in the general result of his writings upon the mass of the public, by the predominance, perhaps involuntary [p. 182] but real, of the individualist thesis. This metaphysician, this idealist, at times almost a mystic, ceases not to preach, to sanctify independence of character, and, as its consequence, action. “Trust yourself, suffice for yourself, act for yourself.” This is the counsel that the American people receives from the beloved master. The lesson that everything around it gives it obscurely springs eloquent and luminous from those sincere lips. “An action is the perfection and publication of thought,” he says at one time; “good thoughts,”—he adds at another,—“are no better than good dreams, unless they be executed;” and so incessantly. It is amid the ebb and flow of that immense mass of men, which seems at times exposed to losing its marvelous equilibrium, that this incessant voice, warning each one that he is something more than a lost unit held fast in a coherent aggregate, teaches him that he too has his own course, and that it is lawful for him likewise to aspire to the attainment of an individual end. And thus, keeping the forces of the individual intact, it contributes better and more completely to the total coordination. Always independence, never isolation; always action, never inertia, never discouragement. When more than ever the individual [p. 183] seems exposed to being crushed and undone by the mighty maces of the social mechanism; when in the struggle for life the competition of individuals seems as nothing beside the tremendous competition of peoples; when a savage cry of war seems to rise in every corner of the inhabited earth, and industry is war between the workman and the capitalist, and commerce war, between the isolated merchant and the powerful companies, and war the cultivation of intelligence, put to the service of all the works of destruction, and a leaden atmosphere is seen to fall upon societies, infiltrating deadly ice into the most dauntless hearts, and the teachings of pessimism, professed from the lecture-chair and in the public square, enervate the forces and paralyze the will; there is not, there cannot be, a healthier and more fortifying task than that of this philosophy which arms the individual with his own arms, restores him to possession of his personality, which was in danger of being lost, and keeps him on his feet, with heart whole, against all assaults, when it does not impel him along the road of all conquests, of all progress.

This is the philosophy that endures in Emerson’s works; this the one that suits a people where there do not exist, or exist powerfully mitigated [p. 184] the causes that sustain and invigorate pessimism in other countries, in the greater number of contemporary nations. To his social environment Emerson has owed most of the characteristics to which he owes his eminent worth: his powerful originality, which will immortalize his works and which lends him so eminent a personality among the writers his contemporaries; the sureness of his criticism, which never leans with the wind of opinion or of interest; the freedom of action with which he chooses his subjects, with which he penetrates all and says all. And these qualities, at once moral and literary, are those that must flourish in every democracy, those that must give tone and color to its productions, those that must make them stand out above the conventional level on which it has been supposed they would stagnate. Nor can it be otherwise: democracy is the most perfect formula in which liberty is embodied for the internal government of peoples; and liberty, which ennobles labor, makes workers of all the associates; and liberty, which assures the equitable distribution of what is obtained by one’s own effort, stimulates every improvement and makes possible all honorable competition. From the effective and incessant concurrence of so many activities there results a general and incessant progress, whose last and [p. 185] most beautiful flowering is to be a higher morality and a full culture.

Liberty! Happy the peoples who love it and possess it, who know how to win it and to keep it! A thousand times unfortunate those who know and esteem it only in theory! All their prayers should be for it; toward it should tend all their efforts.
