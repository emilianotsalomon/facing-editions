I am most grateful to the Rector for his kind words of introduction, and I shall take the liberty of availing myself of your hospitality to speak on matters that I am certain will interest you, if only for their own sake. Just as we in Mexico, upon the arrival of an Argentine, would not wish him to speak to us of abstract things or of foreign philosophies, but rather of Argentina — which naturally interests us — I imagine in the same way that you will find greater interest in hearing from the lips of a Mexican some observations on the problems of Mexico than if I came with the pretension of speaking about some philosophy of past centuries or of foreign nations.

We are fortunate to be traversing a moment in the history of this continent in which a vigorous nationalism is taking shape in all our countries — a nationalism that does not confine itself within the nationalist concept under which we organized ourselves a century ago, but that seeks expansion within the feeling of the Spanish race, as if responding to a current that is already universal and that tends to break the molds of nations, doing away with the political evil of nationality and creating new social organisms grounded in race and language rather than merely in political treaties or geographical or commercial considerations.

To explain the situation of Mexico, and especially as it pertains to her thought and the manner in which she is applying that thought to the social problem, I shall need to make some brief recollections of our past, because what characterizes the present movement in Mexico is that this is not a theoretical tendency, a matter of study. What has happened in Mexico is that theoretical science has been discarded entirely. We have just passed through a period in which the uselessness of theoretical studies in the face of social needs became plain. When the people of Mexico cried out for justice, cried out for their betterment, the universities and the learned men responded, in the name of science, that this people was condemned and beyond remedy, because it was an Indian people, because it was an inferior race — and in the name of all the sages of Europe and all the theories of genius, they condemned it eternally, assuring it that every effort would prove futile because its facial angle did not meet the measurements that European anthropologists prescribed for the superior races of the world.

The Mexican people, outraged by these infamous responses from the learned, ended by sweeping away that science, and little by little a new one has been taking shape — wrought not from inspiration and conjecture but from observation and logic. It is for this reason that I have said that the contemporary thought of Mexico is a thought of action, a thought that admits of immediate practical application. We are passing through a period in which the thesis matters very little; what matters is the result of the thesis as applied to society.

As you know, we — just as you — were formed within a civilization that was the Spanish civilization, a very strong, very powerful, very great civilization for its era, despite a hundred years of propaganda from our enemies condemning everything Spanish. The eighteenth century of Mexico, for example, has not yet been surpassed in its harmony, in its homogeneity, throughout this entire continent. But it corresponded to an obsolete economic organization, an unjust economic organization, and it had to fail. You know that the fundamental problem, the one that resolves the evolution of a people's life, is their economic organization. To understand what education was in the time of the Spaniards and in the early years of the Republic, one must consider what the economic situation was of the various classes that composed the Mexican nation.

Mexico was composed especially, as it still is today, of a great number of Indians who were serfs, of many millions of mestizos who were bureaucrats, who were second-rate employees of the wealthy, and of a capitalist class formed by the descendants of the Spanish conquerors. It is evident that the race of the serfs, the race of the Indians, was not enlightened, nor could it produce types of distinction — not because it is inferior, but because it was poor, and to prosper in this world one needs material resources, the bare minimum for life, because he who faces the problem of painfully earning his daily sustenance, he who labors bent over a plow twelve hours a day, has no time to develop his mind. It is not that his mind is inferior; all minds of all human beings are equal; God has made all men equal, whatever the anthropologists may say. What comes to distinguish men is the time they can devote to the labors of the spirit, and that time is determined by the ease with which they can earn earthly goods. We thus always find the two extremes of imbecility: the imbecility of the man who, by laboring too much, cannot develop his spirit, who has no time to educate himself and to think, and the imbecility of the rich man who, by possessing too many material goods and devoting his entire attention to them, develops his body, his appetites, his desires, and nullifies his spirit. So the tendency of every modern organization must be to do away with those two extremes — that of indigence and that of excessive wealth. It must do away with the excessive toil of those at the bottom and with the excess of those at the top, in order to create a middle condition of man who devotes a little time to the struggle for daily sustenance but the greater part of his time to the cultivation of the spirit.

The education given in colonial times, naturally, took no account of this modern tendency toward organizing life for the benefit of all men; it was a society organized for the benefit of the conquerors, for the benefit of a class, and it therefore produced, in its education, types of distinction that became allies of the ruling class. When Mexico underwent the revolution of the Reforma — that great social revolution which shook the foundations of Mexican organization — it was believed the problem had been solved, because political liberty was secured, freedom of thought was guaranteed, Church and State were separated, the influence of the clergy in political and social affairs was ended forever, and religion was relegated to where it belongs — within the doors of the temple — without being permitted to enter the lecture hall, the home, or the halls of politics. But the economic problem was neglected through want of understanding. The economic power of the Church was broken, the properties of the Catholic Church were confiscated, but these same properties were little by little absorbed by businessmen who allied themselves with the civil power to exploit their fellow citizens. Around General Díaz there formed a capitalist class that came to monopolize all the wealth of the country, that came to replace the Church as landowner, and this class ended by developing universities and colleges of a certain luxury where professionals were manufactured and where allies of that same capitalist class were manufactured — men whose mission is to justify the state of social oppression under which the powerful classes hold the humble classes. These men found easy support in the doctrines of the positivist school, which were introduced in that era both in Mexico and in Brazil and throughout Latin America. The social summary of these doctrines was the thesis I noted earlier — that according to the conclusions of science, inequality among men was beyond remedy, especially on our continent, and most especially in Mexico, where a superior race, the whites, must constantly prevail over the inferior race of the Indians. And so these men, through their newspapers, books, and lecture halls, justified tyranny and stripped the country of all hope.

Fortunately there came another revolution, another great revolution, that took place more or less forty years after the revolution of 1857. It is apt to note here how the Mexican revolutions — which have served so well for our enemies, and the enemies of the entire Ibero-American race, to slander us — the Mexican revolutions, generally, almost without exception I might say, are characterized by the fact that they are social revolutions; they are not merely political revolutions that seek to remove one man from power in order to install another, but rather they shake all the foundations of social organization and transform the political and economic structure of the country.

The revolution that has just passed in Mexico had as its principal aim the transformation of the economic regime of the Republic. It is true that the battle cry was: "Death to Porfirio Díaz!" — because Porfirio Díaz was the dictator — and "Death to the scientists!" — because the scientists were the group of men who had enriched themselves in the shadow of Porfirio Díaz. They were called the scientists because they maintained, in the name of their science, that their power, their privileges, and their abuses were legitimate, in accordance with the science that preaches the survival of the fittest and the triumph of the strong over the weak. In this fashion the people condemned the doctrine in an epithet that became insulting and contemptuous: the epithet of "scientists."

This observation, this fact, is important so that you may understand the reasons why the universities of Mexico, and institutes of higher culture in general, have fallen into such disrepute — because the people realized that within the walls of those establishments no one was working for them, no one was working for the benefit of men, no one was disinterestedly seeking doctrines capable of saving man from suffering; rather, pedantic and comfortable theories were being elaborated to defend the power of the strong and the wealthy. Today, on account of that very situation, Mexico believes only in doctrines that have beneficial effects for the community. One may present, with every credential of wisdom and authority, whatever doctrine one supposes to be the most illustrious; but if that doctrine, submitted to the test of its practical results, does not establish that through its application the social condition of men will improve, then we shall declare — in the name of human science and in the name of morality, which stands above all sciences and all logics — we shall declare that that doctrine and that science are mistaken. For us the test of truth is the social benefit that a principle and a theory can produce. That is the sole criterion we employ: the superior criterion of human morality and the good of the greatest number. All else strikes us as a contemptible thing and no doubt an erroneous one, though we do not take the trouble to study where the error lies but simply reject it outright, for we have no time to argue. We are in an era of action, and we embrace the most generous theory, understanding by that the theory that conforms to a criterion superior to the criterion of the study, because generosity must coincide with truth. Therefore when a doctrine is not generous, we do not believe it to be true.

Defeated in that fashion — first the religious thesis, according to which the Spanish conqueror organized education; then the scientific thesis, according to which education was organized under the Porfirian dictatorship — we arrive at the present era and set ourselves the task of reorganizing on firm foundations the entire system of national education. And since we know that truth, above all in its theoretical expressions, is an exceedingly debatable thing, and since we do not believe ourselves possessed of any panacea for the logical resolution of social problems, we had recourse to this simple criterion of common sense: we asked ourselves what is the purpose of the State in founding schools and taking charge of the educational problem. We answered ourselves with an entirely elementary, entirely simple formula. Relating the general defects of our education, we reached the conclusion that the principal defect of that education was that it produced men incapable of creating wealth — it produced consumers of wealth. It produced lawyers who live as allies of the rich in their exploitation and their business dealings; it produced doctors who also live off the labor of others; in general it produced men who did not increase the wealth of the globe but consumed it. And so we set ourselves to organize education in such a way that it would increase the productive capacity of each citizen of the Republic of Mexico.

We do not pretend to create scholars or produce geniuses. We believe that genius produces itself without any need of State encouragement, and we believe that it is the duty of the State, on the contrary, to diffuse the elements of knowledge that are indispensable so that every citizen may be capable of providing for himself, more capable of extracting from the globe the wealth sufficient for his own needs and for social needs. And so we set ourselves to create producers and suppress consumers.

That is the general criterion of the organization of public education that we have adopted in these recent times. With this object in view the government devoted its attention principally to the diffusion of the primary school. The law of compulsory schooling had been promulgated in Mexico forty or fifty years ago; but it turned out — as with almost all the laws of governments that take no serious concern for social problems — to be a somewhat ironic law, because children were compelled to attend school but no schools were given them. There were no schools outside the populous cities, the state capitals, and the national capital. In the villages, in the settlements, on the haciendas — and the population of Mexico lives a very dispersed life, being primarily agricultural — throughout all those distances there was no school of any kind, and the former government, General Díaz's government, in its later period, when it had more money at its disposal, had occupied itself with developing higher culture in the cities, consistent with the policy of creating modern elites.

We therefore had to follow the opposite tendency. We have no time to create elites; but we do have the duty to seek out every man born in Mexico in order to give him the opportunity of education. In this way the greater part of the government's resources has been devoted, and continues to be devoted, to the diffusion of schools in every possible form. In places where a school can be founded, one is opened; when resources are insufficient to found a school, a traveling or missionary teacher is sent who settles in a region for six or eight months at a time. For this purpose, the schoolmistresses in particular have yielded very good results, because they have more apostolic vocation, because they resign themselves more easily to country life, to rustic conditions, and because they are more self-sacrificing. Generally we send our most distinguished teachers — not the refuse of the teaching corps, as was formerly the practice. The best schoolmistresses receive increased salaries, receive honorary distinctions, and are persuaded that they should go not to enjoy themselves in the large schools but to the rural settlements to teach Indians to read and write, and even the wholesome social customs of civility, order, and hygiene. All this is ordinarily done by the normal school teachers. They travel through the settlements, gather statistical data on population, on the needs of the region, and these data serve afterward as the basis for founding schools. In this way we learn which localities have the greatest school-age population, which need a school, and where the local inhabitants are in a position to collaborate with us in establishing one. In many instances — the most common by far — the community provides the building and guarantees, for example, the money needed to purchase benches and supplies, while the federal government pays the teacher's salary, and in this way, at the cost of nothing more than one schoolteacher, we have succeeded in founding a school in every town. This work unfolds throughout the country, and we complement it with the creation of technical institutes — a variety of the old schools of arts and trades, but more specialized now in modern industrial and agricultural matters.

In accordance with the particular characteristics of each region, specialist teachers are sent out — teachers of industry — to educate those who have already passed through primary school in specific trades. Instead of opening lyceums, instead of creating faculties of letters, instead of manufacturing new lawyers and doctors, we purchase machinery and establish schools of mechanics, electricity, and chemical industries. The result of this new orientation has been so eloquent that, for example, in Mexico City, where the faculty of law once reached an enrollment of nearly four hundred students, today the law school has an enrollment of two hundred; whereas at a school of chemical industries, founded along the model of similar schools in Germany, enrollment that stood at forty in the first six months has now reached nearly six hundred students. There are two categories of courses: short courses and complete courses — one and two years for soapmakers and tanners — and the category of industrial chemist, which is a true university career and spans six years.

In this way we have succeeded in gradually relieving the congestion in the state schools, in order to fulfill the fundamental purpose of our education, which is, as I said, to create producers and suppress consumers. But since the school alone does not suffice for the work of civilizing a country, and since Mexico suffered from the ill of having few books — and still has very few books — and since our race itself has spent a century suffering from the habit of reading books in French or English but very few in Spanish, and since even in Spanish there are few works, especially of the modern kind; and since all of this has brought the bad habit of reading in languages that are not our own, we set ourselves to return to Mexican Spanishness. We faced the problem of the invasion of the English-language book. We were overrun by the English-language book. The somewhat cultivated person had libraries in English. This was due in great part to the difficulty of obtaining books in Spanish. And so a publishing house was created that has begun to produce editions of classic works from throughout the world in the Spanish language and sells them at cost, dedicating them to universities, scientific institutions, schools, and so on. In this way we have waged the campaign against the foreign tongue — which was the greatest danger to our very nationalism, to our very national spirit. Alongside the publishing of these books there was founded a library department to diffuse reading throughout the country, creating not great establishments but small libraries — collections of a hundred to five hundred books chosen in such a way that even the smallest collection contains the most fundamental books of humanity on every subject. These collections were placed in premises that we generally rented, since we have not yet had the money for buildings. And in every town of the Republic one may now say there is a small reading room. For regions where a permanent reading room cannot be established, collections of fifty to a hundred volumes of circulating libraries have been assembled that travel through the country and are arranged so that they can be carried by a mule, since the most remote regions cannot be reached by railroad.

The work of the Ministry of Public Education has been completed by the creation of a third department — that of fine arts. It thus has three departments: the scholastic department, the library department, and the fine arts department. The fine arts department, as its name suggests, has charge of the artistic education of the people. The first innovation that was made was to remove the teaching of drawing and music from the normal-school teachers. It was customary in our schools for the teacher, after giving her classes in general subjects, after teaching grammar and geography, to rehearse and conduct the children's choir, then to set them to drawing and correct their drawings, and then to direct their gymnastic exercises. At first glance this strikes anyone as absurd, for it presupposes in a teacher an encyclopedic range that no one can attain; and the result was what all of us have seen in certain schools: the poor quality of the school choral singing, the wretched system of drawing instruction, and the inefficiency of school gymnastics. To remedy this ill, and overcoming the opposition of the normal-school students and professors, it was decreed that the courses in music, gymnastics, and drawing would not be taught by normal-school teachers but by teachers from our national academy of fine arts — drawing masters, artists from the national conservatory of music — meaning, in short, that music would be taught by musicians, drawing by draftsmen, and physical exercises by athletes. The pedagogues told me that this was very grave, that it broke the unity of the school, because the intrusion of all this multitude of teachers disrupted the timetable and destroyed school discipline, and that moreover the musician or draftsman might be a very fine musician or draftsman but, not having studied pedagogy, could not teach, because he was unacquainted with the methodology of drawing or music instruction.

When one of those scientific problems overwhelms me, I generally have recourse to what any ignorant man has — common sense — and I told them I preferred music to the methodology of music, and drawing to the methodology of drawing, and that we had no choice but to undertake that experiment. Fortunately the results have been so notable that no one disputes the procedure any longer, and it has been accepted that the professors of music and drawing shall be dependent on the fine arts department and not on the school directorate. And that unity of which the pedagogues spoke, that unity they cannot find in the school — the facts have demonstrated to us that it has taken refuge in the conscience of the student, for it is the student who establishes in his own mind the unity of all the instruction he receives. As for the order and discipline of the school, a director with common sense who organizes a convenient timetable was entirely sufficient.

But since our effective organization was born from the life of the masses — because this has been a popular movement, a national movement, not the work of any one man, not the work of any elite or group of men, but a work inspired by the entire country — we have not sprung from the study or the lecture hall but come from popular aspiration; we are intimately bound to the needs of the people, to the needs of the laborer, and we work as much with the laborer as with the student. What was formerly called university extension has gained such importance among us that it is properly something more than university extension — it is the university in action throughout the country. Labor's involvement has developed to such a degree that almost the most important work that the Ministry of Public Education is carrying out at this moment is that of the night schools. And these teach the workers not only to read and to write; little by little the courses have been multiplied in these schools and instruction in geometry, mechanics, and so on is given. These schools grow every day, and as the university contracts, the industrial and technical schools multiply and intensify their work.

In these night academies the library department and the fine arts department also take part, for the worker, the man who attends the night schools, is given books and is educated in music and in drawing according to his capacities. In Mexico City, for example — a city of seven to eight hundred thousand inhabitants with its surroundings — it has been possible to found twenty choral societies, one in each district, composed of a thousand voices each. Twenty thousand workers have already sung together on occasion. Theater and cinema owners have been required to give their premises free of charge on Sunday mornings, and a program is organized in which the workers themselves take part with their choral society; the schools also contribute a chorus and a dance, and a lecture is given. Professors and students take turns delivering lectures to the workers on social subjects of immediate interest. They are told how a cooperative is organized, what a trade union is; the history of Mexico is explained to them — not from a political point of view, as was formerly the custom, but from an economic point of view and with an eye toward the present moment.

All this work unfolds not only in Mexico City but in all the state capitals and in the most populous cities. And in artistic education, for example, is where the best vehicle is most readily found for imparting to that education the spiritual character it possesses, because if at the outset we said we were founded on the desire to create producers and suppress consumers — this, naturally, relates to the practical life of man. But it would not suffice, because it would leave education without a head, without an aim superior to the satisfaction of man's ordinary needs. We therefore became much interested in finding the tendency that aesthetic education should carry, for it is the one most directly directed at molding the spirit of the people. But we found ourselves in a period of debate over all ideals — a period of debate even over patriotism, which is one of the most deeply rooted ideals in all men — and we had to think about the manner of imprinting a character on that education. The first tendency we imposed was a nationalism in the teaching of drawing and music. We required, for example, that in the primary schools and in the workers' choral societies preference be given to national songs, to the folklore of the country. Mexico is very rich in popular melodies, in national songs; but in the era of Porfirismo we had reached, as all of America had by then, such a degree of servility toward Europeans that we were ashamed of our songs and did not dare to dance in a school the popular dance of Mexico, the jarabe — a dance resembling the cueca of the Chileans and the pericón of the Argentines. No one dared to put that in a school. Instead, white wigs were placed on our poor Indians and they danced minuets from the court of Louis XIV — ridiculous. And so the work we performed was to expel what could not ally itself with us in a natural way, and to return to the natural depths of the popular inspiration of the race. We introduced popular and national songs and dances into the schools and among the workers.

High society must have looked upon these things with scandal; but I shall give you only this one piece of evidence. About a year after we launched that movement, the jarabe was already being danced in the drawing rooms of Mexico City, and the ladies were endeavoring to imitate the workers — for in Mexico it is the humble who provide spiritual direction. This nationalism, which is one of the manifestations of the need felt in Mexico to shake off the yoke of European thought, has expressed itself not only in art, in music, in drawing, but in every manifestation of social life.

Fortunately, in recent years, Europe treated us so badly — and in saying Europe I include the United States, I am speaking of everything that is not the Ibero-American race — they calumniated us so, they called us barbarians because we would not give them, as they say, the riches of the country as a gift, for that in the end is the substance of the situation. The people instinctively came to resign itself to the epithet of barbarism and no longer cared much what was thought of us beyond Mexico. What Mexico does care very much about is what is thought of it in South America and in the countries of its race. And in this way, without willing it, without feeling it, unawares, we have been emerging from the European vassalage in which these peoples have lived for a hundred years, and we have created all our organizations, schools, and manifestations of thought with a sufficiently marked independence from the paths that the foreigner formerly imposed upon us.

This is not to say that I believe we possess the elements to develop from this moment onward a civilization of our own. It is evident that for many years still we shall have to bring from Europe and from the United States the machinery — all that constitutes the material organization of society. But I maintain that in matters of thought it is now necessary that we learn to think without taking into account foreign opinion and the book just arrived from the European presses. In Mexico, at least, this has already come to pass in a complete manner. I must note that in Brazil I found an identical tendency; Brazil is a country that has no communication whatever with Mexico, and I understand that in Argentina there is already a strong current defining itself in that direction — the desire to shake off the literary and philosophical yoke of the more advanced countries and to begin, shall we say, a life that, though more modest, is one's own and national. National not exclusively of Argentina or exclusively of Mexico, but national in the broadest sense — national in the Hispano-American or Ibero-American sense, so as to include in that tendency the Brazilians, who must necessarily work with us in this movement and who are fortunately very eager to do so.

To ensure that this social ideal which we inculcate in Mexican education is not given the lie — to ensure that what we tell the children in the schools of Mexico is a truth, so that they do not find tomorrow that what we explained to them is not so: that we on this continent are not only Mexicans but also possess another power, that we are Hispano-Americans — it is necessary that in every country of the continent a parallel movement arise in the same direction, with the same orientation. And among the many obstacles to creating this ideal in all consciences, I find the obstacle of exaggerated nationalism. Almost all our countries live afflicted with a morbid patriotism. Fortunately Argentina possesses a patriotism that, if pronounced, always takes generous forms, because Argentina has no pending questions — no grave ones, at least — with any of her neighbors. Unfortunately there exist among our peoples countries that have serious boundary disputes, serious quarrels with their neighbors, and there patriotism takes aggressive forms and forms destructive of the Latin American ideal. I therefore believe that it falls to countries such as Mexico — which fortunately has no quarrel and has never had a quarrel with Guatemala, because if tomorrow Guatemala wants land we shall give it, for it belongs as much to her as to us — and it falls also to countries such as Argentina, which has been generous with its weaker neighbors, as in the war of Paraguay, to define the problem clearly and to dissolve somewhat in our own consciences the narrow concept of national patriotism.

I consider that national patriotism corresponds to the bourgeois period of civilization. These are political organizations formed, in most cases, to defend material interests. All economists are agreed that the phenomenon of the division of these countries into nationalities upon their separation from Spain obeyed economic and geographical reasons. But economic and geographical reasons are inferior to spiritual ones. Bolívar had already said, anticipating all objections, that science and geography would oppose for a century the union of these countries. The great genius, superior to the criterion of the scientific and practical men, said — I do not recall the exact words — that if nature is opposed we shall struggle against her and overcome her, for that is the mission of the spirit: to reform routine, transform the medium, modify the moral conditions of human life. This is why the scientific thesis that would have man be a product of his medium and his environment is to be condemned. No, sir: man must change the medium and place his ideal above the terrestrial conditions of life.

It is necessary, then, to consider nationalism in its origins. That nationalism corresponds to the narrow economic and geographical criterion according to which these countries were fragmented in the wake of the war of independence.

I must be entirely candid with you; I must tell you everything I think in the only opportunity I have, for my stay here is very brief. I believe that in the fragmentation of these countries the conscious or unconscious intrigue of England played a very large part — almost certainly conscious. England always had agents at the side of the liberators of these countries, and since England in helping us achieve our independence was seeking, as she always sought — and it is even legitimate that she should seek it — the benefit of her own race, of her own nationality, it was very convenient for England that the colonies be separated from Spain, because in that way she destroyed Spain, which was already the age-old enemy against whom she had struggled without success — Spain, which had won a world from under England's very nose and had condemned England to the role of pirate, for she could not conquer kingdoms but lived off the spoils seized from ships. Discontented with the miserable situation in which she then found herself, England found the separation of the Spanish colonies from the mother country most convenient. But her triumph would not have been complete had these countries preserved what she had fought against — the consciousness of race. It proved very beneficial to her that we, forgetting the concept of race, should fall into the merely political concept of nationality, become fragmented, and in many cases even begin to fight one against the other.

In any event the phenomenon has already occurred. For a hundred years we have paid the consequences of that fragmentation — a period that will have signified nothing in the life of the world. We have been manifestly inferior countries, backward countries, countries of imitation, countries that do not create culture but copy it. All of this is the consequence of our fragmentation into small nationalities. How can anyone remain seriously attached to the concept of nationality when at this moment the world is debating universal problems through which the very concept of race, which is far broader, is beginning to appear narrow to us? We must therefore react within ourselves and make the reflection we made when constituting the nationalities. For example, in the period when the Argentine nationality was not yet well formed, there were divisions among the provinces just as there were in Mexico. In Mexico, for example, we had provincialism, which was a far vaster thing — almost a nationalism of educated men — and pride was felt in the patria chica, in the city, in the province where one was born. Wars and the changing concepts of the world have erased this difference in Mexico; no one in Mexico now thinks about which city of the Republic he was born in — we are all Mexicans. In the same way it is necessary that the countries of this continent begin to build a concept of patriotism that reduces national pride in the same proportion as we reduced provincial pride, and that every day each Argentine or Mexican — and principally the members of the stronger countries of America — should set the example of feeling themselves less Argentine, less Mexican, and more Hispano-American, more identified with the common interest of the race than with national problems, which naturally must not be neglected since they fall to be resolved in the first instance, though they should not be pursued with an exclusive passion — an exclusivism that would distract us from the superior work of organizing these peoples by race, so that they may count for something in the life of the world.

It is evident that the definitive tendency of social organization is toward that internationalism to which the progress of mankind will fatally lead us. But while we have not yet arrived at true internationalism — founded on the equal power of all peoples — if we neglect the feeling of race we shall find ourselves in a position of weakness before peoples who do cultivate it. We live, like all the world, facing a powerful race organized to perfection, conscious of its historical mission and master of the planet: the Anglo-Saxon race. I do not preach hatred; quite the contrary — we must admire it. It has achieved immense conquests, and precisely one of its strengths, one of the causes of that power, is that it keeps alive the feeling of race and always places the feeling of race above all other concerns. It censures us for this very thing and is accustomed to mock our aspirations toward Hispano-American union; but it is in that race where we must see the strength that the feeling of race gives to a people.

It is evident that after the Treaty of Versailles — and even within the Treaty of Versailles itself — it was very convenient for the strong countries to shatter us all to pieces, because in that way we count for nothing. But it falls to us to imitate the peoples that have attained greatness by following their methods, and one of the secrets is precisely this: the fostering, the consciousness of the feeling of race.

To make that feeling of race firm, I was intimating earlier, it is necessary to provoke change, the improvement of social organization, because nationalism corresponds to the unjust organization of society into oppressors and oppressed. Nationalism is very convenient for the aristocracy, for the powerful class of a country, because it guarantees them within a certain territory a strong predominance, and the dominant group of the other country finds itself in comfortable conditions. When conflict arises between the interests of two peoples, the oppressed population — which has no advantage, which draws no benefit from that patriotism — is used to go and defend the interests of the two oppressing groups. In reality the modern world has done nothing more than substitute for monarchies powerful groups that continue to enjoy a predominance and a privilege over the masses of the population. This structure is very comfortable within nationalism; but as this situation transforms itself throughout the world — as the masses, through their education, go on conquering rights, and the value of man grows stronger in the eyes of the community — it is natural that the social concept of nationalism and patriotism should be modified in turn. The movement toward intensifying the feeling of race must thus become more pronounced as a society grants greater well-being to the majority of its population.

In this way, naturally, economic development will go on producing the new organization of peoples by race, and will subsequently bring good understanding among all races — when the causes of economic conflict have disappeared, when the world is organized so that war may be suppressed as far as possible, war which generally has economic covetousness as its cause. In the order of the spirit there are no conflicts among men. For the spiritual interests of the world it is good, it is beneficial, that there should exist the Saxon race, the Spanish race, the Germanic race, for all three — by their distinct capacities, by their distinct aptitudes — enrich the common patrimony of humanity. They are useful for progress; each brings a new invention, a new contribution to the spirit.

It is in the economic order that conflicts arise, and so long as the social organization that governs us persists, wars must persist as well, for if the world ever achieves peace it will be through the transformation of its economic regime. While this strong organization of all races and universal harmony cannot yet be achieved, it is elementary that we should organize ourselves by race — because if we were to introduce suddenly into our countries the internationalism preached by certain advanced socialist schools, this phenomenon, not being universal but for the moment limited to us alone, would serve only to weaken us. The result would be that all the races already stronger than us would continue to enjoy over us the advantages of cohesion, the better to dominate us and keep us in the role of vassals — a role in which, it must be repeated and clearly seen, we have lived and continue to live.

It is necessary, then, to react against all of this and to use this second century of the spiritual life, of the free life of these nations — a century just barely begun — to go on creating the consciousness of spiritual independence, to achieve spiritual emancipation.

It is evident that we men of today will not see these things accomplished, for they encounter resistance everywhere in the world, even on the terrain of theory alone. But we need to enlighten ourselves, if only to open the way for the generation that is coming — a generation that will in all probability already be in a position to put all of this into practice. It should clearly be noted that we do not preach the formation of a great powerful and aggressive empire in which the nationalities of Hispano-America would be fused. Those times, fortunately, have passed. The nations of America will be as free as they are today within the Latin American community. But it is indispensable that barriers be broken among us — barriers that sooner or later must be broken among all the countries of the world. For example, it is absurd that customs posts should exist among the countries of Latin America. It is inexplicable and deplorable for the entire intellectual class of this century, which has been capable of making essays in international law on the relations of peoples in the universe, and capable of writing books and theses on the law of the Romans and the Goths, that it has never thought to turn to its own countries in such a way as to increase their economic development — by suppressing, for example, the customs houses among us.

Those races are right to hold us in contempt who see that we have spent a century commentating upon Europe, talking of Europe, concerning ourselves with what the politicians of France and England are doing, while it has never occurred to us to put in place the means to resolve an elementary problem such as, for example, the abolition of customs. The very statement of the thesis strikes us as something strange. Yet we have before us the example of a country formed in precisely that fashion — everyone knows the example, but it occurs to no one to apply it to our countries. And when this has been said, all shrug their shoulders and read it with curiosity, because we are possessed by the preoccupation, the mania, for things European — the eagerness to resolve everything as it is resolved over there, in accordance with the authorities over there — and we will not look at what is before our very eyes.

These errors, which we ourselves will not succeed in correcting, will have to be corrected by the generation that follows, for in these times the world no longer progresses by centuries but by decades. All social work is now accelerated. And the only recommendation I wish to make to the students of Córdoba — who have been the directors, who have been the leaders of an illustrious movement for the liberation of thought in Argentina, who have made such efforts to shake off the yoke of old ideas — is this: as soon as you conquer power, as you undoubtedly will, for power belongs to the strong and goes fatally to firm characters with a definite orientation, as soon as you conquer power in Argentina, do not forget all these theses that today circulate in the journals and in the minds of all men. Do not relegate them to second place as curious things; rather, commit yourselves to putting them into practice — especially since, fortunately, the Argentine movement is seconded by the Chilean, Brazilian, and Mexican movements, and in all our peoples there lies latent the necessity of at last doing all these things that have been the subject of a century of experiments, in order to achieve a rational union of the Latin American countries — a union in which no one sacrifices sovereignties, but in which one wins at the very least the capacity to act always in agreement on international questions, so that questions affecting one country are considered as one's own affair by all the rest, and so that the sovereignty of Central America cannot be injured without injuring likewise the sovereignty of South America. The politicians and the directing men must understand that it is not enough to be a good Argentine or a good Chilean — it is necessary to be a good Latin American.

We will not accomplish it. I am certain that the young Argentines, this revolutionary youth of Córdoba, are those who will accomplish all these elementary things of which we now speak as though they were dreams, but which are fundamental to the persistence of a race as a force of value in the world.
