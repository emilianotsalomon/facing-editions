It seems incredible that the old conflict which is one of the fundamental motifs of history—the protean conflict between democracy and tyranny—should persist in the Americas not only in the practical order but also in the sphere of theory.

From these exotic lands of ancient Byzantium there reaches me the kilometer of printed text that the Court poet Don José Santos Chocano has dedicated to my person. His false and absurd imputations, and his twisted opinions of my conduct, neither move nor compel me—given their source—to write a single line in my defense. Besides, reputation is defended by deeds and not by words. And I, after reading the ill-fated poet's reply, feel satisfied for having written the article that provoked it—"Poets and Buffoons"—and I affirm it in every particular, near or far. For whether or not Señor Chocano said that he and his current chieftain were the two immortals of the century, his article conveys exactly that impression and even adds to the family of savage Gods another figure well known throughout the Americas: Don Juan Vicente Bisonte. The devil breeds them and they find each other.

But what is truly a duty to prevent is the use of certain men's fame, for base ends, to deceive the ignorant and prolong injustice. The legend of enlightened dictatorships and beneficent tyrannies is a thesis that cannot withstand historical judgment. Nor is it true that great art requires, like a sickly plant, the paternal protection of the Sovereign. On the contrary, all the great artistic epochs of humanity correspond to periods of democratic and liberal flourishing—from Pericles in Athens, through the Italian Republics, to our great churches of Latin America, which are the work of the brave individual efforts of the missionaries. By contrast, everything produced under the Divine Augusti and the Sacred Basilids and the Sun Kings and the Charles the Fifths never rises above mediocrity and frequently collapses into the emptiness of the pompous—figures who might have suited Comedy had they not left traces of pain and blood in the wake of their accursed passage through history. If the judgment of critics has so often been clouded, if there are still those who argue the opposite, it is because in a great many people there still weighs, with abject force, the shameful sentiment of the slave, who not only obeys but goes on to applaud and glorify his master.

In many respects the whole of history remains to be written. Sometimes the body has been freed, but consciences remain in chains. And not only history—our critical faculties also require reform. Let us gather data that may serve that reform. Let us cast at random, then, some observations, some particulars from the life of a great people that lived and failed more than a thousand years ago.

Byzantium had everything: an extensive and fertile territory, sheltered and numerous ports, the axis of the ancient world as its foundation, the finest races of the planet, the lucidity of the Greek, the sacred genius of the Syrian, the administrative organization of the Roman state, wealth, tradition, and power. Byzantium had everything. Yet all those powers, gathered together, Byzantium placed in the hands of one man—the hands of a despot. Little by little the citizens were reduced to nothing as imperial power grew, and with imperial power grew the army. And every time an army grows, the liberties of the country that sustains it are extinguished; and every time absolute power appears in a nation, its decline rushes forward in great leaps through a succession of catastrophes.

External catastrophes and internal calamities. The loss of provinces in the struggle against the barbarians, and pain, slavery, and misery in the countryside. It was not that there lacked those who pointed to the causes. The causes were the eternal ones. The land was in the hands of a few. The peasant, weary of enduring the tyranny of the great landowner, emigrated to the cities to swell the ranks of the pariahs. Then the emperors did what despots always do: they issued decrees. The agrarian decrees of the Byzantine emperors read like the work of contemporary economists, and they were not without their humanitarian and demagogic aspect either. "The terrible evil of greed," declared Basil II, "causes the rich to speculate upon the misery of the poor and to purchase their goods at a vile price." "We will not leave the goods of the poor in their hands," he added in another edict, presenting himself as the protector of the destitute—or, as one would say today, as an agrarian leader. But the edicts were mere farce, for the emperor, as absolute chief, as tyrant, rested upon the army, and the army's commanders would pursue one landlord or another, only to end up as landlords themselves. They became estate-owners—and who would dare call them to account? Such is the vicious circle into which tyranny inevitably falls. It can correct nothing, organize nothing, because it carries within itself the seed of all corruption. The Byzantine agrarian reform remained a dead letter; the land grew increasingly impoverished and the population, ever more oppressed, became powerless to defend itself against external dangers. After some centuries it could no longer even furnish soldiers, and recourse had to be made to mercenaries—not so much to defend the Empire against foreigners as to defend the emperors from the violent protests and the despair of the enslaved multitudes.

And so a regime of absolute tyranny was prolonged for several centuries, with no check, as a historian quoted by Charles Diehl tells us, WITH NO CHECK save assassination and revolution. For, periodically, the military commander of greatest prestige or greatest ambition would assassinate his rivals or defeat them in battle and proclaim himself emperor. He would proclaim himself the incarnation of the fatherland, the leader of the revolution, the savior of principles. Wretched are the nations that produce this kind of savior! Each of them sacrificed some advantage, some territory of the Empire, which was increasingly beset by its external enemies.

But the processions and the pomp continued, and a constellation of poets—illustrious predecessors of Señor Chocano—sang the glories of the Empire and of tyranny and proclaimed the cult OF THE SWORD. Yet the sword, whose reflections carry the sinister gleam of the curse of Cain, constantly turns against its worshippers. The sword has grown dull from so much losing throughout history. The sword lost when the Persians raised it against the harangues of Hellenic democracy. The sword of Caesar put an end to the Roman Republic and prepared the barbarian invasions, following the long and shameful decline of the Empire; it showed the barbarians the way. The sword of Napoleon led France to disaster and gave dominion over the world to England, which was governed by Pitt—an orator. And even the very sword of Alexander, by destroying internal democracy, prepared the decline and death of Greek culture and left it defenseless before the powerful Roman Republic, governed in that era by orators. Before the word, the sword has always been powerless.

Only in democracy and within democracy will the world and Latin America be able to consummate that transformation of economic institutions which is the supreme aim of our tormented age—an age that is nonetheless certain of its path.

Let not these gentlemen come to us, then—men who, after some banquet, will have read a page or two from a book of ancient history and, without the capacity to penetrate its meaning, fling conclusions at us in which they believe they have found a justification for their tainted lives.

America was created for liberty, not for tyranny. Over the vile corruption of caudillismo, civilized democracy will impose itself, sooner or later. No memory remains of those who justified Rosas, saying that tyranny was necessary to defend Argentina from Europe's ambitions. Sarmiento overthrew Rosas even with European aid, for when the fatherland comes to be called Rosas it is no longer a fatherland—it is a curse. Sooner or later, throughout all of America, Sarmiento will triumph. And no one will remember the caricatures of Empire that still linger in our homelands.
