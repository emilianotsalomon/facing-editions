## Part I

[p. 13.1] If any of you—professors and men or women of thought—were to appear in the halls of the University of Mexico or in any one of our Iberoamerican universities, I am certain that you would be greeted with a single, unanimous, and heartfelt plea: "tell us what contemporary Austria is like, what it thinks, what it yearns for." To such or similar questions, you would have to improvise answers. That is precisely what I will have to do here, to respond to the questions stirring in your minds when in the presence of a Mexican or Iberoamerican.

My voice will have to<sup>1</sup> strain itself,<sup>2</sup> and my spirit will have to broaden itself so that it can gather impressions, so that it can draw for you a sketch of eighty million souls: what they are, what they do, and what they think—souls still in formation, striving to fill and integrate an entire continent, a continent that is the hope and ultimate aspiration of all the races of the earth. Who are we? What are we?

<sup>2</sup> esforzarse

[p. 13.2] The problem of each human life is a strange one, and one runs into difficulty even in formulating it. This is even more so in our case, where the symptoms of youth are mixed with the dangers and ills of decline. It is exceptionally difficult when dealing with a race constituted by contrasts, with countries that are immensely rich in natural resources but where the availability of goods is extremely scarce. The situation is also contradictory from a spiritual point of view. For, we possess inexhaustible veins of inherited [PDF 1] culture and a generous universality of conscience, but we at the same time suffer from widespread ignorance and a complete absence of constructive plans.

From the depths of this chaos—which, after all, may only be an inevitable moment of the grand process of the formation<sup>3</sup> of an original culture—from the depths of all these details, facts, judgments, and events, I will attempt to extract some threads of warp to weave into a vague fabric. We will attempt to follow the mysterious waves of the crystallization of the traits of a race, traits more impalpable than the waves of the crystallization of chemicals. I will, thus, try to link together the facts within certain sets of concepts that, perhaps, will help us uncover the direction, the constructive tendency amidst the confusion and tumult of events. Perhaps too—since no one knows the limits of mental powers—this effort of ours, along with other similar, repeated, and renewed efforts, may contribute to the creation of that direction which the facts [alone] lack, that current which pulls things, shapes minds, and compels peoples<sup>4</sup> to become creators of ideals. In this way, the near future may take from us, from our will and our thought, its meaning and its law: the force that things need to organize themselves and ascend with splendor.

Unfortunately, all culture always appears as an attempt that is never fully realized.<sup>5</sup> Every process of advancement seems threatened by a kind of vacillation in events that fail to align with a higher calling,<sup>6</sup> events that do not manage to align themselves to the commanding voice of the ideal. The collective work consequently founders. Energy disperses, and existence, left devoid of the spirit,<sup>7</sup> succumbs to stagnation and torpor. The individual soul, too, infected by the lower rhythm, subjected also to the law of things<sup>8</sup>—which are more closely related to us than one might initially think—feels as if it were ungoverned and torn asunder, as if it were subject to the same fate that disintegrates physical energy and gradually turns even the hardest rock into dust, through the slow and constant imbalances of temperature and the friction of combined movements. The Great Ball of the homogeneous—constantly devouring the glimpses and attempts to create the particular. We and the physical world are, in truth, *a single whole, threatened with falling back into the lower processes*, into what we call chaos in the sphere of the physical, what we call evil in the ethical, and what we call ugly in the [p. 14.1] aesthetic. Everywhere— [PDF 2] the same weakness, the same impotence to achieve the higher forms of destiny. We and nature are imperfect proceedings of the potential,<sup>9</sup> clumsy proceedings in the hands of beings who return to the abyss at the very instant when they lack the breath of the divine, the mysterious trembling of joy.

But the advantage of the soul over matter lies, so to speak, *in its faster crystallization*. While it takes centuries for dust to form granite anew, the soul can sometimes, in the matter of moments, reconstitute, renew, and illuminate itself entirely. We thereby see that divine processes are slow (solidification), multifarious (germination), and fast (light). But none is faster or more prodigious than the soul. Hence, among all the factors that shape the history of a people,<sup>10</sup> none is more important than the race that constitutes it. And, thanks to this innate virtue,<sup>11</sup> it is still possible for us to believe and hope after so many defeats and such an embarrassing<sup>12</sup> succession of failures. Due also to the variety of our nature, history does not repeat itself but, whether in a blind or deterministic manner, never ceases to invent and create.

Let us now turn to what is being created in our America. From its first appearance in history, two currents have converged to drive our world forward, two processes of civilizing action. On one hand was the desire to satisfy appetites with new treasures, to feed the imagination with new landscapes. On the other, the longing to seek proselytes for a boundless faith. The conquistadors and the missionaries: the work of discovering and organizing peoples, the purpose of spreading light in souls. Domination and proselytism.

It may be said that the discovery of a great land that would serve as a homeland for the liberated races was already foreseen since the command was given in Judea—at the very instant that the foundations of a new law of nations<sup>13</sup> was laid—to 'go forth and preach the gospel to all nations, call for the conversion of the Gentiles.' Further driving the purpose of spiritual expansion was the physical needs of an overflowing population.<sup>14</sup> By virtue of the Christian mandate, the principle of culture moved beyond the tribe, beyond the homeland,<sup>15</sup> and even beyond the lineage<sup>16</sup> itself. But the ancient world proved [PDF 3] too small for the new conception of life, and Asia was much too crowded. Overpopulated areas do not understand what pertains to doctrine or morality. What they need is space. In search of space, Mediterranean ships set sail, and, on the continent they found, a new process of history had begun—a period of fraternal culture that sooner or later must replace this current-day Medieval era<sup>17</sup>: a Medieval era in which barbarism has disguised itself under a new name, staining with blood what is for many an honorable term, though it be in truth perverse: the contested name of *nationalism*.

Our America, it is true, is, in many ways, nothing more than a reflection,<sup>18</sup> a copy of Europe, and it is almost entirely the work of Europeans. However, this does not oblige us to make our history merely a repetition of European history. Human experience is not completely infertile, and times do not pass in vain. Each seed renews and multiplies<sup>19</sup> in the fruit. That is why we work to bring forth from ourselves an original form of culture. The improvement of the lot of the common folk, the conquest of justice and wisdom, the reform of institutions and souls: such are the conditions of our patriotism and a fundamental component of its content. No attachment to the errors of today, no return to the past. Ardent work in commitment to the future,<sup>20</sup> to compel it to shine with glory—that is how I would define our form<sup>21</sup> of nationalism. A dawn, a birth<sup>22</sup>—not a rebirth<sup>23</sup>—but the creation of better and higher forms of life.

[p. 14.2] Such has been, in truth, the Iberoamerican ideal since the beginnings of our independence, and even more so since the conquistadors and missionaries initiated their inspired and marvelous work in our lands. Adventures of legend converted to living reality by the genius of captains and the most astonishing preachers that history has known. I know not where they might have reached had they not been hindered by the swindlers and profiteers sent to our lands by the Crown of Spain.

Unfortunately, the Spaniards who, full of genius and daring, arrived in America were no longer free. They did not come from republics, such as Venice or Florence, but rather fled their homeland at the very instant when political freedoms began to decline. This was due to the gradual suppression of [PDF 4] privileges<sup>24</sup> in Castile, Aragon, and all the free provinces whose sacrifices for the Reconquista were rewarded by the kings by robbing them of their privileges of autonomy and citizenship.

<sup>24</sup> fueros

From Isabel, with her false legend of the jewels—false because it is unproven that she offered them to Columbus, and it is evident that America did not cost her any but rather produced jewels for her—to Ferdinand VII, the degenerate upon whom many have wanted to place all the blame for failure, as if the others of his lineage had not been just as bad. All the monarchs of Spain, and even those of England, did nothing but delay the plan to make America a land of choice<sup>25</sup> and justice for the benefit<sup>26</sup> of all people. Both monarchies imposed monopolies that violated the tacit agreement<sup>27</sup> of America, monopolies that closed off the continent to the free utilization<sup>28</sup> of human effort and turned it into a fiefdom of petty interests or rewards for incompetence and flattery. It is certain that, as an exception to the rule, we had some good viceroys, but it is even more certain that the system of irresponsibility inherent in the monarchical institution had to be, and was, for everyone, fatal—fatal for Spain and fatal for us.

The America of the North broke away from the English Crown fifty years before we did, and today they are a hundred years ahead of us. We at last broke free, but many of our ills still stem from those centuries of blind obedience. Even the sporadic outbursts of localism and nationalism today bear much of the slave's adherence to the owner, the cacique, the general, the master of the land or the State. Such despicable and sinful submission of person to person has only been able to yield fruits of misfortune. Almost all our victories are nullified in practice. We open our borders to all peoples, but the police pigs<sup>29</sup> of personalism harass nationals and foreigners whenever they cross the dividing line of our nationalities. We proclaim the equality of all, but there are very few who can take advantage of the opportunities offered by our nature. General poverty, ignorance, and geographic and social conditions have delayed our progress. And the despotic systems of government inaugurated by the Kings found successors in the form of crude and ignorant local military leaders who have been the scourge of all the new States, types of fierce condottieri whom we call caudillos. It happened that the heroic, visionary individuals [PDF 5] who accomplished Independence were almost entirely exhausted by the struggle. Bolívar, our most illustrious captain, quickly lost power and was replaced by lesser militia gang-leaders. Sucre, the most noble and pure of all our idealists, was assassinated, and one of his alleged murderers proclaimed himself the president of one of our republics, thereby founding the long and still unextinguished dynasty of assassin-presidents. In Mexico, almost all the true patriots perished in battle or were pushed aside. At the hour of triumph, a certain Iturbide proclaimed himself Emperor despite being accused of double treason, for he fought for years at the behest of royal armies against the rebels. San Martín, the glorious Argentine leader, had to leave his country, retiring in disgrace. Power there too fell [p. 15.1] into the hands of petty generals and assassins. Such is the lineage of our *caudillaje*,<sup>30</sup> the lineage of Latin American condottieri. Such was the political inheritance of the monarchy, the system of government by the sword.

<sup>29</sup> polizontes

In each of our great liberating revolutions, the same thing always happens: those who at first neither risk joining nor understand the movement align themselves with it only once it is succeeding or in its second phase. They then gather soldiers and, without any other aim than that of seizing command, eliminate the patriots and install themselves in power. Tyranny, in this way, changes its executioners but not its systems.

So harmful have such politics been that only countries like Argentina, which eliminated *caudillaje* long ago, or like Brazil, which never had it, can boast of real progress. Colombia—a land governed from its beginnings by men of letters, not soldiers—along with Uruguay, Costa Rica, Cuba, and perhaps one more nation have escaped the wretched tradition. But for the remaining peoples, the struggle between barbarism—in its crudest and most primal form—and civilization—in its elementary and impotent forms—persists and hinders national development. Ambition, ignorance, and the armed struggle for power impede the development of any constructive plan. Each dark age of our history has thus been baptized with the bloody name of one of these dictators or caudillos, who stains on our lineage.

And not only has our *caudillaje* perpetuated ignorance and tyranny among us, but also, with respect to our international relations, *caudillaje* is the [PDF 6] natural enemy of Hispanic-American rapprochement,<sup>31</sup> and it is the support of a jealous nationalism that is so contrary to our good tradition and the spirit of our culture. It is contrary to our tradition because from the start we were one nation under the scepter of Spain—one continuous territory, one language, the same religion, and the same idiosyncrasy. It is likewise contrary to the highest interests of our race, interests clearly defined by the founders of our independent life—the Sucres, Bolívars, Hidalgos—founders who committed themselves to creating nations open to the entire human species. Yet, again and again, the ambition and idiocy of the caudillos have prevented these salvationist aims from being realized.

<sup>31</sup> acercamiento

Just yesterday, we saw fail for the tenth time one of the most pressing coalitions. Upon the fall of that type of demonic abortion named Estrada Cabrera, the revolutionary civil government of Guatemala came to an agreement with the civil presidents of the rest of Central America, and, on one glorious day, the telegraph informed the world that the five presidents of Central America had resigned their offices and in place took on roles as provincial governors, so as to convene a Constituent Assembly of the Central American nation. But afterward, a military coup—a coup d'état, a resurrection of *caudillaje*—overthrew the Guatemalan government, and the plan for a union collapsed, much to the delight of the North American interests that intervene in Central American politics. Here, as always, the caudillo appears to us marching on the edges of betrayal, despite the fact that he daily, in his carpetbagging rhetoric, labels as traitors those who do not follow him in all his infamies.

But the caudillo has not only been a criminal of the State, a criminal of politics. In the economic order, the caudillo is often the principal pillar of support for the *latifundio*. Although they sometimes proclaim themselves to be enemies of property, there is hardly a caudillo who does not end up being a *hacendado*. The truth is that military power inevitably brings with it the crime of the exclusive appropriation of the land. Whether the soldier-caudillo calls himself King or Emperor, despotism and *latifundio* are correlated terms. And, as is natural, economic rights, just as political ones, can only be preserved and defended within a regime of liberty.<sup>32</sup> Absolutism inevitably leads to the misery of the [p. 15.2] many and the pomp<sup>33</sup> and abuse of the few. Only democracy, for all [PDF 7] its faults, has been able to bring us closer to the best realizations of social justice—at least, that is, before it degenerates into the imperialism of overly prosperous republics surrounded by peoples in decline.<sup>34</sup>

In any case, among us, the caudillo and military rule have contributed to the development of the *latifundio*. Even a superficial examination of the property titles of our large landowners would suffice to demonstrate that almost all owe their assets first and foremost to grants from the Spanish Crown and, secondly, to concessions and illegitimate favors granted to the influential generals of our false republics. These grants and concessions were given in each case without regard for the rights of entire populations of indigenous or mestizo communities, which lacked the power to assert their dominion. From this system of simple brutal occupation comes the wealth of the *hacendado* of Mexico, the *estanciero* of Argentina, and the *gamonal* of Peru.

Some of the leaders of our War of Independence, men like Morelos in Mexico or, later, like Alberdi in Argentina, saw and proclaimed from the getgo the need to tear these monstrous monopolies apart. Each of our revolutions fights them, but as the revolution degenerates into *caudillaje*, it is the caudillo himself who emerges as a landowner. Such is how exploitation and abuse persist. Even in countries like Argentina, where military *caudillaje* has been dead for years, the heritage of *caudillaje* persists in the form of large *estates*,<sup>35</sup> which are not sold at any price and are only subleased to those who, driven by misery, agree to work them under conditions of slavery. If it were not for this small aristocracy of landowners, Argentina, the great southern nation, would already be on its way to rivaling the United States of the North, a country whose prosperity is due to the great freedoms of its earliest days<sup>36</sup> and the judicious distribution of land, parceling it out among small landowners who in turn became the support of liberty. We likewise will not achieve any serious progress so long as we allow the two social scourges to persist: the landowner and the military caudillo.

<sup>36</sup> primera época

And not only will we not make progress, but we will also not secure peace as long as there are landowners and caudillos. The Mexican Revolution of the last fifteen years has been nothing more than an effort to overthrow the exploitation of workers and the monopoly of land and politics, along with tyranny, re-electionism, and militarism. Similar upheavals will have to occur in [PDF 8] the other countries of our America, if governments do respond to the people's desperation and place a saving hand on the most urgent social problems. A simple glance at our history confirms this thesis. Each one of our secured rights, of our social achievements, invariably comes from those short periods in the history of some nations—longer in others—in which the government has passed from the hands of military leaders and takes on civilized and democratic forms. The development of public education, which almost always coincides with these brief periods of liberty,<sup>37</sup> tends to do away with the influence of the caudillo. Since Sarmiento of Argentina implemented his great educational reform, Argentina has not produced any more Napoleons or incarnations of revolution or saviors of the homeland. The same will eventually happen in the rest of our homelands. The growing power of the doctrine of socialism in countries like Mexico, Argentina, and Uruguay will end up installing better people in government and more adequate economic systems. Only then will we be able to convince the emigrant that those lands are truly destined to produce a generous and universal civilization. For now, in large part, and with rare exceptions, it is a duty of truthfulness<sup>38</sup> to affirm that economic injustice and despotism hinder the development of our culture and impede us from achieving the fraternity and communion of all people.<sup>39</sup>

[p. 16.1] Nonetheless, for those valiant optimists who prefer to see the brighter side of things, I will say that, as far as theory, deep conviction, and spiritual dominion go, all our convictions and tendencies lead us to conceive and attempt<sup>40</sup> the realization of the highest forms of human coexistence.

<sup>40</sup> procurar

It is interesting, for instance, to observe that while nationalism in postwar Europe has intensified<sup>41</sup> and returned to almost aggressive forms, among us, on the contrary, the old plan of creating a powerful Federation of all our isolated nationalities gains more adherents by the day.

<sup>41</sup> recrudece

In this way, while Europe disintegrates into nationalities, we move toward the formation of a vast State. While other countries erect the isolating walls of nationalism, we seek to open our doors to external influences and foreign immigration. In proceeding in this fashion, we trust, undoubtedly, in our vast untapped resources and the assimilative power of our culture—a power [PDF 9] of assimilation that grounds itself on flexibility and liberty rather than on the rigidity of norms.

Naturally, we have since long ago possessed a peculiar type of culture, an enlightened tradition that has protected us from disappearance during the darkest periods of our barbarism and tyrannies. Spain blended its blood and spirit with the proud and solid indigenous lineage. We then broke for eternity with the monarchy but not with the Spanish people. At different times, other influences have also made themselves felt. To France, for example, we owe the cult of political liberty and the faith in social improvement. The genius and art of Italy, the philosophy of Germany, the music of Austria, the literature of Russia—all of this has left traces and produced echoes in that region of the soul that is fertile, plastic, and free of prejudices of thought.<sup>42</sup> So much is this the case that even in the worst ages of tyranny, freedom<sup>43</sup> of thought has remained unblemished in all kinds of philosophical, religious, artistic, and even social and economic questions, for usually the only demand of the despot is that his person not be touched. Since he does not understand the rest, the tyrant judges it harmless and insignificant; that is, of course, unless vanity leads him to feel like Maecenas, in which case he surrounds himself with second-rate intellectuals, who have never been lacking in any court in the world.

In all cases, our civic terminology, our patriotic vocabulary,<sup>44</sup> speaks in a generous tone of crossing borders and expanding hearts to embrace all people. And, to remain loyal to the truth, all this has not always remained mere sermon. We should not speak solely about the ills, but of the good deeds too, for, in this way, further good deeds can be encouraged,<sup>45</sup> and justice is done to the past. Our very nationalism, that fragile point of the morality of all peoples, has often assumed among us certain aspects that could well serve as precedents for a new conception of the laws of nations.<sup>46</sup> To verify this assertion, I will refer to only two notable cases.

To eradicate the cursed plant of *caudillaje*, Argentina, during Sarmiento's government, and in alliance with Brazil and Uruguay, had to wage war against Paraguay, perhaps the most monstrous manifestation of *caudillaje*. In the annals of criminology, a place of infamy is reserved for that fierce despot [PDF 10] who dishonored the names 'López' and 'Solano'. To depose him, the joint armies of the three powerful southern nations invaded Paraguay. Unfortunately, poor leadership led the Paraguayan people to a resistance that was as heroic as it was futile. It tears the heart to learn of that dark and magnificent epic<sup>47</sup>— magnificent for the desperate valor of the patriots who believed they were defending their territory, and dark because it was dishonored by the leadership of a murderer. It is said that almost all the men of Paraguay were wiped out, for those who weren't hanged by Solano López as enemies of the cause were instead killed in defense of that unfortunate homeland. But as soon as the entire country was subdued, as soon as Solano was eliminated, and negotiations could begin with what remained of the Paraguayan nation, Sarmiento [p. 16.2] began and ended the treaties with this sublime phrase: "Victory does not confer rights." And Paraguay did not lose an inch of territory, nor did it have to endure burdens or humiliations at the hand of the victors. The Iberoamerican idea achieved a splendid triumph over the national idea. Any notion of conquest in conflicts between Spanish-speaking nations seems absurd after such a precedent.

<sup>47</sup> epopeya oscura y magnífica

The other case refers to my particular homeland, the unfortunate and generous Mexican nation. Between the former New Spain and the General Captaincy of Guatemala lies a territory called Chiapas. When Mexico organized itself as an independent nation, Chiapas was included within Mexico's borders. But, a few years later, the people of Chiapas proposed annexing themselves to Guatemala. They discussed it, voted on it, and did it. Not long after, they thought it better to rejoin Mexico, so they separated from Guatemala and returned to the Mexican Federation. Throughout all these comings and goings, it never occurred to anyone, either in Guatemala or in Mexico, that such affairs could be a *casus belli*. It has not even crossed our minds to label the people of Chiapas as traitors to the cause of Mexico or Guatemala, for Guatemala is undoubtedly as dear to us as any part of Mexico or Spanish America. This also demonstrates that ethnic unity<sup>48</sup> inevitably overrides false, merely political, barriers driven by national interest—an interest that is temporary, relative, and subordinated to the interest and mission of the race.

## Part II

[PDF 11] [p. 22.1] What, then, is the strongest link that unites us all? What is the predominant trait of this vast aggregate of nations and peoples? There is one that is important and which certainly interests foreigners to know: that is language.<sup>49</sup> Indeed, law, habit, and the force of circumstances all contribute to imposing, with unquestionable<sup>50</sup> authority, the use of the Spanish language<sup>51</sup> in twenty of our countries as well as Portuguese in Brazil. Portuguese and Spanish—two easily interchangeable Romance-Iberian languages<sup>52</sup> that, among us, replace the Babel of the different European tongues.<sup>53</sup> Knowledge of one of these two languages is truly all that is required to gain spiritual citizenship in our lands. A linguistic patriotism: such will be the final<sup>54</sup> formula of our Iberoamerican nationalism—a spiritual kind<sup>55</sup> of patriotism, within reach of the whole world, and which signifies for all who achieve it something more than acquiescence to a local tradition or obedience to an imperative of custom or law.

It signifies, rather, the possession of a mental vehicle, tried by centuries, testified by a great literature, simple and logical in its forms, clear in its accents, and lexically rich—just as much as any other cultured language.<sup>56</sup> It is perhaps, after English, the only global language,<sup>57</sup> given that millions of people across all four corners of the globe speak it, millions of people who will inevitably have to come together, at least<sup>58</sup> in a kind of cultural amphictyony, as indicated by certain trends which have started to link our restless homelands with those of our siblings of speech and, in part, of blood—with our siblings from the Philippines, and with those other distant spiritual kin known as the Sephardim, or the Spanish Jews of the Near East, an estimable and loyal branch of our linguistic family that will have to forgive us, for we too suffered from the same unconscious and harsh hands, hands that managed to destroy their own empire but not the unity of the race, the unity of the emigrating and powerful culture that recognizes as its cradle the austere and fiery plains of Castile.

However, we shall not raise walls in that place where everything ought to be a path. We will zealously defend the language<sup>62</sup> against the threats of treachery or force, but we will not turn it into a new untouchable idol, into a monstrous taboo that closes the path to progress.

<sup>62</sup> idioma

On the contrary, we will remain ready to sacrifice even this last refuge of our nationalism the very day that others decide to do something similar, when we all work to extract from the languages<sup>63</sup> of today a more perfect and universal form of communication.<sup>64</sup> We foresee the day when a kind of Academy of the International Language<sup>65</sup> will be established, an academy that will render services to the new global language<sup>66</sup> akin to those performed today by The French Academy for French or The Royal Spanish Academy for Spanish. This body would serve the purpose of adopting and legalizing the use of certain terms that are already common to various languages.<sup>67</sup> It would also impose a uniform vocabulary<sup>68</sup> for geographic, scientific, and historical terms and would choose and recommend for general use those modes of expression in each language<sup>69</sup> that bear the closest resemblance to related tongues.<sup>70</sup> [p. 22.2] It would approve and enforce a common alphabet, which would, indeed, eliminate the terrible confusion of Balkan and other similar scripts.<sup>71</sup> The list of common terms could grow each year. Terms that are too parochial would tend to disappear, as would those that are, in the local language,<sup>72</sup> mere equivalents of universal terms. For example, if many cultured languages<sup>73</sup> already agree to call railway stations "station," then there is no reason to [PDF 13] maintain local equivalents like the French "gare" or the English "depot," which is in use in some far-off regions.

Certainly, many venerable, beautiful words from each language would have to be sacrificed, but this loss, if seen as a sacrifice in service of a new patriotism—a human patriotism—should cause a kind of joy, for each word of the new language would in turn appear to us as a mysterious sign that opens to us the souls of millions of people. In truth, the Academy of the new language<sup>79</sup> would have to do nothing more than hasten to gather the fruits of the work that social instinct has been carrying out by itself<sup>80</sup> for a long time. Indeed, a new language<sup>81</sup> already floats amidst us, and one need only crystallize its creations, channel its turns, define its laws, and catalog its terms. Once the creation of the new language<sup>82</sup> begins, the question of propagating and disseminating it would remain. But those who know the omnipotent power of elementary schooling will effortlessly understand that, if the resolutions of the new Academy were incorporated into a loyally and patriotically adopted mandatory program of instruction, then, within a few years, we would find ourselves in a period of the full creation of a language<sup>83</sup> through completely natural systems. In any case, science should undertake the task, attempting, at least this time, to collaborate with life, since it commonly dedicates itself to passively observing and noting the slow and disconnected variants of reality. The Academy of the International Language<sup>84</sup> should be composed of great travelers and linguists young and wise, and their expenses should be assumed by all civilized nations.

As it is an embarrassment that so many thousands of years have passed without us being able to invent a general means of spoken and written communication,<sup>85</sup> the decisions of such an Academy would have to be [PDF 14] embraced with ardent pleasure. We would all, little by little, sacrifice our native tongues<sup>86</sup>; we would surrender, in a voluntary and sublime holocaust<sup>87</sup> to the new ideal, the purest and most beloved portion of our patriotism and nationalism. The last feather of the glorious but vain plume of nationalism would thus glide away, carried off by the breath of intelligence and love of humanity. After everything, nobody would lose all that much, given that all living languages,<sup>88</sup> perhaps with the exception of Italian, are rather absurd and ugly to those who lack the innate prejudice of finding them beautiful, and none of them is truly worth a tear or a sigh. But, returning to our subject, to the peculiarities of the national sentiment in our America, perhaps you all will ask me: Is not<sup>90</sup> all this talk of tearing down walls and broadening horizons merely a fantasy of intellectuals, one with [p. 23.1] no relation to the social and ethnic realities of the new continent? It is true that in America, as in all parts of the world, the masses are slow to move, and the half-cultured folk often prove themselves to be selfish, blind, and skeptical. Nevertheless, it is enough to look at our map attentively to feel that from those vast solitudes emanates an invocation to humanity, a longing<sup>91</sup> for that land to be populated and organized in another way, one different than before. The immense beauty of those landscapes prevented the conquistadors and missionaries from settling in one place. They instead constantly pushed forward, extending human dominion over mountains and deserts. The same harmonious grandeur of those vast, select lands instills in the souls that develop there a certain kind of need for expansion and greatness,<sup>92</sup> along with a sense of artistic religiosity comparable to that of Indostan of the glorious past. Our particular emotion has the advantage that it is modern and that it forms itself in an era when the world is no longer limited to the traditions and capacities of only one people.<sup>93</sup> [PDF 15] Civilization is now, for the first time in history, a truly universal phenomenon. It is not English, though the English materially dominate the world; nor is it French, though the French extend their thought to many nations; and neither is it German or Italian. It is not even European, since there are many fundamental things that Europe has had to take from other parts of the world, and there are still some things that it has yet to learn. It can thus for the first time be affirmed today that civilization is starting to become worldwide.<sup>94</sup> And the predestined site, the non-substitutable place for the development, expansion, and crystallization of that worldwideness,<sup>95</sup> lies among our countrysides, our forests, and our seas. Our national soul also responds to such a vast destiny because it possesses, more than any other in the world, two elements that jointly constitute the best foundation for constructing a future: a mentality completely free of prejudices of tradition or caste, and a fine and profound sense of beauty that, if it can develop within ethical and social norms, will produce the highest flourishing that time has ever seen.

However, the work ahead is much too vast, too urgent, and too important to be undertaken by a single race. Our continent is not reserved for Whites or even for the Indigenous population;<sup>101</sup> it also should not exclude Blacks and Asians.<sup>102</sup> On the contrary, the continent has a task and reward for all. We are on the eve of the moment when two great creative powers must come together, two powers that will bring a beneficial revolution to the world. Those two powers are the vast untapped resources of America and the technology<sup>103</sup> of European engineering, which constantly perfects its very own methods. When these two currently latent forces develop and unite, humanity will conquer a period of abundance and happiness during which it will have the opportunity to revise its purposes and address its problems. The task that unfolds then may well be a salvationist endeavor for civilization.

It follows from all this, therefore, that the creation of a great free State in Hispanic America is an enterprise that concerns all of humanity and, as such, must receive the loyal support of all peoples. For that homeland will be one in which no one is a stranger, and every free soul in the world should take as a personal offense each of the abuses of contemporary imperialism against [PDF 16] our weak republics. I do not exempt the good citizens of North America from this duty of respecting our nationalities, precisely because they represent a nationalism that tends to destroy the very essence of the consolidated nationalisms of our time. And, in honor of the truth, it must be acknowledged that within the United States itself, there are many pure Yankees who help us in our struggle against the danger of conquering and capitalist nationalisms. Many in the United States understand that the most powerful force that can be set in opposition to contemporary imperialisms is the union of the oppressed nations, a union that opposes [p. 23.2] the systems of privilege and blood<sup>104</sup> as much as it opposes the dominion of brute force.

As for social and spiritual life, Latin America has already begun to follow its own path. It has the right to its own thought, just as you have the right to yours. Fortunately, this autonomy is not a cause for discord. Thought belongs to no one. It is a common treasure. In philosophy, art, and social matters, we will take examples from the entire world, in accordance with our own judgment and with the end of building better. Send, then, your artists and thinkers to Latin America. Their seeds will bear fruit, though the fruits will inevitably appear more or less transformed. Latin America is no longer a field of unlimited proselytism. Though our capacity for assimilation remains great, we have begun to produce and think for ourselves. But you, and all people, will have the opportunity to influence the character of our intellectual creations. Send us your ideas, but above all, send Latin America your technology,<sup>109</sup> your engineers, and your builders. At first, it will be necessary for you to support them from Europe, in the form of supplying tools, machinery, and small loans. But very soon—and if no malicious intent to exploit one another arises—the fruits will surpass the promises. Moreover, this kind of collaboration that we [PDF 17] request from you is but a local aspect of the same great longing<sup>110</sup> that currently stirs the world.

Until quite recently, social organization was the work of force, of the soldier or the King. But such [an organizational scheme] was barbaric, and it is being replaced everywhere by republican and more or less socialist forms of government. This requires us to replace the soldier with the person of laws,<sup>111</sup> the lawyer or the economist. That is, rather than a government that commands to be obeyed, we have a government of legislators; rather than of the will of the Lord or the king, we have the counsel of citizens. Yet, it is already easy to see that this government of thoughtful people who impose laws, though far superior to the government of the soldier or the King, must itself be surpassed, for it is a means and not an end.

<sup>111</sup> *hombre de ley*

Its task is to organize labor, production, and distribution on just foundations. And, consequently, a new form of government, which has already manifested itself occasionally, will have to emerge: the government of the engineer and the technician.<sup>112</sup> All the forces of the state will then reconcentrate on extracting nature's treasures and powers. An era—not of industrialism, but of industrialization—will come to pass, in which natural resources will be exploited on a grand scale for the benefit of all people. What industrialism has done for the benefit of the privileged few, the technological industrialization<sup>113</sup> of the State shall achieve on a large scale for the common good. And Latin America is precisely the region of the world best suited for the development of this new type of free and industrialized State. To be sure, just as the soldier is replaced by the person of law, and the person of law is replaced by the technician, after the technician will come the period of the philosophers' governance. Production, which will have become an organism set in motion,<sup>114</sup> will no longer be the fundamental task. Rather, it will be replaced by education, the task of cultivating and developing spirits.

But the government of philosophers—if its mission does not become corrupted—must lead us to a still higher degree of social progress: the disappearance of the State, which too is a means and not an end. For, there is no other end than the individual, the divine spark, the Soul and God, and all else is but straw and vanity. With the disappearance of the State, as having [PDF 18] proved unnecessary, the last trace of barbarism will thus vanish, and all forms of nationalism will be things of the past.

We have, then, five stages in the processes of governance, five types of rulers<sup>115</sup>: the soldier, the lawyer, [p. 24.1] the engineer, the philosopher, and the period that we must call anarchist,<sup>116</sup> which is truly the definitive one. But we find ourselves today in the era in which all soldiers should obey the legislators, and when all of us, more or less, must work as engineers and builders. There is so much waste, so much incapacity, and so many unmet needs in the contemporary world that it is indispensable for the period of the engineer to be anticipated and advanced. Work and production are the saving motto—but work and production for the benefit of all who work, without distinction of class or color. All other purposes can wait.

Our civilization will be a failure so long as it continues to produce, like inferior species, more and more people without the means to feed and educate them. Let us ensure that in our America we do not regress into the same errors. We need immigration,<sup>117</sup> but without forgetting that before needing many children, we need schools for the children who have already been born. One of the fundamental causes of the lack of prosperity of a civilization lies in the fact that inferior populations reproduce excessively and outnumber advanced ones, which naturally reduce their offspring. For this reason, civilization will only feel secure once culture and true morality, which presuppose responsibility, are propagated among all groups. So long as civilization remains, to a greater or lesser degree, a national matter, there will be no hope for radical changes in the history of the world. Invasions and conquests will continue to produce many more medieval periods, for as long as there are nationalisms, there will be imperialisms and ambitions of conquest; that is, medievalism and barbarism. Civilization, on the contrary, is universality, conscience, and fraternity.

What is our part, at last—you may ask—in this vague question of the problems of Latin America? Your part can be as important as mine or that of anyone else who exerts influence there. Latin America is a task, and the task belongs to everyone who lends an active and generous hand to it. That is why we are not strangers to one another. I come from afar and speak to you in a language that is neither yours nor mine, a language that I do not nor bother to [PDF 19] master, since, like all languages of today, it represents only one of the imperfect ways of expressing thought. But it has allowed us to communicate with one other, and for that we owe it gratitude. Let us learn from this how deeply connected and united we all are as human beings.

Given my origin and name, perhaps I have awakened distant memories in your minds. A Mexican must remind you of the history of Maximilian, the unfortunate prince who paid for his mistakes in Mexico. It is true, our institutions and conviction did not allow—and I hope never will allow—us to accept a prince as a Lord. But we have always been ready, and will remain ready, to welcome every person as a sibling. The subjects of Kings are freed from their oath and submission the moment they set foot on our soil. This is what the Argentine Constitution declares in the far south of the continent, and this is confirmed by the Mexican Constitution in the north. Let us ensure that this liberty be efficacious.

Unfortunately, there are many slaves in our lands, slaves of economic and political conditions that stir outcry.<sup>118</sup> But you can help us with your insights into social questions, with your technology<sup>119</sup> and your science. You will find there employment for all types of aptitudes. If you seek a stable situation with unlimited potential, go to Argentina or Brazil, to Colombia or Cuba. If you are after a somewhat uncertain fortune, fraught, perhaps, with risks but rich in marvelous prospects, come to my Mexico. Life there resembles the wines of the region of Vesuvius—juicy, fiery, and unparalleled in taste, with something of the spirit of the unstable, threatened, and fertile land. Choose your path among the thousand roads of the most marvelous of all continents.<sup>120</sup> Wherever you go, you will find [p. 24.2] two powerful forces at work, two fascinating and constructive powers: chance and faith.<sup>121</sup> No other region of the world more rightfully deserves the title of 'the country of the future.' But you, as well as all of us, must contribute to forging that future. Let us work toward it, remembering that the present is always the parent of what soon becomes the past, as well as of a large portion of what will become the future. From us, tomorrow can be born. Let us work together to ensure, at the very least, that the future yields its best, its highest fruits.
