Civilization was born in the tropics and must return to the tropics. As studies advance and progress becomes more pronounced, this truth is confirmed — a truth that would have seemed reckless barely fifty years ago, when the thesis equating temperate climate with the very definition of culture held unchallenged sway. Even from prehistory it is easy to observe in America, as in other continents, that the populations of temperate lands fail to construct notable monuments, while the immense architectural kingdom of the Quechuas and Mayas and Zapotecs and Toltecs — all inhabitants of sun-drenched lands — continues to yield new discoveries. The data of prehistory leave no doubt as to the immense intellectual superiority of the inhabitants of the warm zones of America over the moundbuilders of Missouri and over the hunters of New England and Canada. To the south, the same phenomenon is observed: primitive savages in Patagonia and a growing civilization as one ascends the Bolivian plateau and reaches Cuzco or descends to Pachacamac.

The discovery and conquest did not appreciably alter this situation. [p. 68] From the beginning it seems that those very winds, driving Columbus's ships southward, helped ensure that the initial work of the Europeanization of America would begin in the heart of indigenous culture, in the two most prosperous kingdoms of the time, the Aztec and the Inca. But coinciding with the colonization of the New World was the beginning of a historical period whose causes originated in Europe and were felt afterward throughout the entire world — a period in which the centers of culture shifted. Rome and Byzantium yielded the scepter to Paris and London, and the Mediterranean was no longer the world sea; first the Baltic took that role, and then the Atlantic. At the same time, England, Spain, Holland, and the United States became the directing powers.

During the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries the Nordic powers consolidated and reached such dominance that conflict arose among them. The two most important branches of the blond family, the Germanic and the English, dragged the world into one of the most dreadful disasters recorded in history: the worldwide disaster of the Great War. Yet the power of Nordic civilization did not emerge from it broken. London remained the true capital, the imperial capital of the world, the new Rome, and New York was like another Byzantium, the second head of Anglo-Saxon power. The rhythm of culture's displacement from its place of origin in the warm zones toward cold and temperate lands is manifested also in Asia by the awakening of Japan, which has made of a humble empire — in the days of the splendor of Persians and Hindus — the preponderant power of the Eastern world.

America could not escape the process, and did not escape it for the simple reason that the cause of the process lies in the application of fuel to labor, and both [p. 69] because of the distribution of the natural resources then known and because of climatic and social conditions, the races of the cold zone were charged with developing the large-scale use of fuel. For this reason, the fact that in our continent as well the culture that flourished throughout the sixteenth century in New Spain declined there, while during the nineteenth century it grew in the United States, from which it now overflows, does nothing but confirm the indicated law. At the same time, Peru and Bolivia entered recession, while the new contemporary powers of Argentina and the temperate zone of Brazil arose.

Faced with the general confirmation of the growth of temperate-climate civilization, those who attend only to the present hasten to formulate the constant that cold and culture are inseparable circumstances. Yet two centuries of history weigh little against the five thousand preceding years, which were so many days of dazzling glory for the civilizations that are children of the sun.

Without entering into analysis of what these last two centuries have truly added to human culture; without probing into ideas, beliefs, or art — poor Nordic art, which scarcely deserves the name — without denying a single one of the transcendental and definitive advantages that culture owes to its displacement toward the pole and to its relative conquest of the cold, there are already facts that demonstrate the beginning of a return, the inevitable reversal of every rhythm.

Now it is the Northerners themselves who are readying themselves — we might say to return, or we might simply say to come — to the warm regions. Proof? There is no need to cite much, especially in Puerto Rico, where Nordic civilization has come seeking renewal. We also have proof in Central America, full of Nordic entrepreneurs, and in Africa, gradually invaded by French industrialism. The colonies and protectorates that Europe [p. 70] divides among itself over the previously disdained zones of the tropics also attest to it. Disdained by the superficial observer, but never by the thinker.

Longing for the sun, a yearning to return to the places where life throbs and expands beneath a mantle of glory. Civilization is returning to the tropics!

And this return implies one of the most transcendent events in history, because the tropics mean not only a change of externalities but also essential modifications in the spirit of culture. The tropics mean profusion of active elements, air and freedom, light and joy and the multiplication of rhythms. Latin America, which in its greatest extent is warm land, has therefore suffered the vicissitudes of its body, which is tropical. It has a past, an illustrious past, and it has remained relatively dormant, lethargic throughout the fateful nineteenth century, and now it attempts to awaken. But it must rise and move in accordance with its ancestral law, in accordance with the law of tropical culture. This does not mean, it should be understood from the outset, that we propose to take the Moctezumas and the Capas as our models; reaction is not life, and it is precisely the tropics whose essence is eternal and vigorous freshness. What is meant is that all the contributions of culture must come to the tropics to take here their final and synthetic form and context. It matters not from where the race or the concept comes; here it must be transmuted for the definitive expression of its value. All this is incontestable; but let us reflect that not only the land imposes the law, that not only the climate gives norm and color and sap to its fruits. Let us remember that man is the element of transformation of all uniformities, of all processes, and let us study man — the Ibero-American man of the past and present — so that we may then imagine the man of tomorrow.

From the whole tangle of hypotheses and legends of American prehistory, one assertion seems confirmed: the existence [p. 71] of an ethnic type, the red man, mistakenly called Indian, though we can no longer stop calling him Indian — the Mongolioid Aztec of some ethnographic classifications; the one who came through the Bering Strait, according to one estimable theory; the one whose image was carried to our coasts by vessels whose likeness was engraved on Peruvian huacos; the one who came from the interior of Patagonia, according to a certain Inca tradition; the one who had dealings with Atlantis, as all exclaim who contemplate Palenque and Chichén Itzá and Uxmal. At bottom, perhaps all the legends are accurate. But how did all that prodigious Maya-Quiché culture come to be reduced to nothing — a culture that, when the Spaniards arrived in Yucatán, was already only what it is today: a melodious mystery of palaces and towers and pyramids?

Be that as it may, all of it disappeared, as all great partial cultures have disappeared.

And just as in the geological history of the earth each period leaves an indestructible formation, a tangible and characteristic trace, so too is it evident that each period of the flourishing of the spirit leaves in the general plasma — in that generative plasma from which all races come — some hidden treasure, some infused consciousness that manifests itself at the moment when collective consciousness is again put to the test. Compare, if you will, the roughness of the red-skinned man, in whose zone there was no culture, with the keen aesthetic perception, the fine draftsmanship, the subtle eurythmy of all that issues from the hands or the consciousness of an indigenous person from the traditional zones of Anáhuac or Peru.

We have, then, as the first ethnic element, the indigenous Indian of America. He constitutes a race because he is a body of individuals of similar origin and characteristics. We do not doubt that the unity of the indigenous race may be disputed; to the naked eye, and only within the territory of Mexico and even today, there is an enormous difference between the indigenous races of the high plateau and those of the [p. 72] coast, between the barbarous races of Sonora and the more refined and cultivated ones of the South. In some tribes the Mongoloid type predominates; in others, the well-drawn features of the classical type are evident; and there are even some that preserve mysterious resemblances to the Hindu. Are not all these differences also found among the nations of the white race? The use of a single generic term would not be possible, and therefore thought itself would not be possible if we were to carry distinction and scruple to the minuteness of detail. Every classification involves the sacrifice of a countless number of particularities in order to achieve a generalization that tends to be more real than all those details that preoccupy the analyst.

Not only thought — life itself is a manner of generalization and synthesis, given that our selective faculty and our will must at every instant disregard an infinity of impulses in order to grasp even a few that serve us for thinking and acting. In order to judge the value of each ethnic factor, we would need data that science has not yet uncovered. The very importance of the ethnic factor is still a matter of controversy. The temporarily celebrated thesis of the incontestable superiority of the blond dolichocephals — Gobineau's providential conquerors, and the thesis held by the majority of Anglo-Saxon anthropologists — has not been confirmed. On the contrary, it is being abandoned. (See Pittard, The Races; Jensen of Yale and history.) Natural selection, as interpreted by the political Darwinists in the manner of Herbert Spencer, also amounts to no more than one of those arguments implicit in every imperialism, by which the philosophy of the dominators of an era unconsciously justifies the vandalism of those who carry out conquest. For the Ibero-American thinker who views from a distance and without the author's passion the conclusions of European science, there are two [p. 73] ways of judging the importance of the ethnic factor: the old way, which we will call classical, and the particularist scientism of recent times. The first point of view, the classical one, supported more or less directly by the tradition of common knowledge, holds that man is the primary and decisive factor of history. According to the observers of the scientific-experimental school, on the other hand, man is no more than a product conditioned by his antecedents and by the environment in which he must live. But it is curious to see how those very people who deny or diminish the value of human initiative do not hesitate to then sponsor certain hypotheses that border on classical providentialism. For example, the thesis of the almost miraculous reappearance of certain physical characteristics among the dominant group of each era and each people — which demonstrates the insistence with which we return to the belief that man is a factor not only capital but capable of transforming the conditions and perhaps the very essence of life. Once the transcendence of the human factor is accepted, the problem of race immediately takes precedence over all the others that constitute an era.

A kind of indeterminacy, a progressive mobility of the spirit, also leads us to consider that the very factor of race is far from being a fixed element; however much the environment conspires, as is well known, to perpetuate certain types in particular regions, the spirit does not always obey the fatality of circumstance. Among all the processes of creation there is none that changes more quickly, more unexpectedly, and more fatally than that mystery of internal human constitution. So powerful is the force working within us that in advanced races no two human faces are identical. It seems that each soul manages to mold its own external structure. The fact is that there is nothing more variable or richer in aspects than the human subject. And if types from a single lineage change [p. 74] in such a manner within a single generation, how much greater must the change be that occurs over time among peoples? Race, then, is a concept subordinate to a reality in perpetual transformation. The contemporary Indian, for example, will reveal resemblances of physical features to his pre-Columbian ancestors. Matter is slower to obey the perennial law of ascent to higher forms; but the Indian's idiosyncrasy has been radically modified by the advent of European culture. To such a degree that those tribes that have remained attached to their language and customs, enclosed within the reduced and remote environments of valleys and mountain ranges, find themselves today as if they had lost all consciousness — for the sin of withdrawing from the general current of life does not go unpunished. The law of the spirit is perpetual change forward. Therefore those who isolate themselves and cling to a single tradition renounce the law of the spirit and, as it were, relapse into the law of physical things; and the essence of man does not have the simple quality of stone, which permits experiments in eternity: man, when he wishes to endure in his present state, decays. He decays because his condition is frail and like a desperate transition from which one must escape in order not to perish. The way to escape is to press forward with the magnificent and tragic burden.

The physical world of America seems made to counteract that law of eternal renewal which is inseparable from the life of the spirit. The great distances, the deserts, the forests, the mountain ranges — all are obstacles to the meeting and renewal of groups. Perhaps this explains those mysterious decadences, those total disappearances of cultures that had reached such great heights, like the Maya and the Zapotec: they lacked contact with other cultures.

The races of America had remained, as it were, asleep, and this marks the importance of the arrival of the [p. 75] Spaniards. When the first captains appeared on the coasts, the indigenous populations hailed them as gods. The solitary races felt the fascination of legend fulfilled, of vital yearning satisfied. Only when the fateful period of struggle was declared in all its harshness could a Cuauhtémoc say: "They are not gods, but bandits, and as such we shall have to fight them." The truth is that that holy war marks the end of the indigenous race, which will never again be what it was, and it also marks the transformation of the Spaniard, who will never again be the European subject of the Catholic monarchs but rather the turbid yet resolute and vigorous factor, if you will, of a new world culture.

When Spaniards joined with Indians in America, a case entirely unlike any previously known in history was produced: the Spaniard and the Indian, after fighting, united and mingled their blood. Not that there had been no mixing before. It is well known, as ethnologists confirm, that there is no single pure race, and when relatively pure human groups are found, they are seen to be declining and prostrated in their isolation; but the case of America is the first of a rapid and large-scale mixing. There is no ethnic kinship whatsoever between the Spaniard of the peninsula and the American indigenous person. Had the conquerors of America been Mongols, the contrast of bloodlines might perhaps have been far less marked. We affirm this without attaching great importance to the theories that have at times been offered as experimental truth regarding the Asiatic origin of the American man. Supposing those assertions were accurate, the identity of the types studied by Professor Hrdlicka in Siberia with the types of certain American savages would prove only the existence of migratory currents through the arctic extreme of the continent; but the fact leaves standing the mystery of the civilizations [p. 76] that appeared in the Center and the South. What is indisputable is that the two races that mixed to form what today constitutes the substratum of the Ibero-American population represented two ethnic poles, and the fact of their intermixing seems one of those surprises of time and one of the most curious, most transcendent physiological experiments ever produced.

This brings us squarely to one of the cardinal problems of Ibero-America: the problem of mestizaje. We have already said that, in general, mixing has been the law of all humanity and that purity of blood — meaning descent in a single line from a single type — can only be claimed by small, isolated populations. Moreover, the slightest contact with another human group is enough to produce a mixing that does not proceed from the lower strata of one group to the lower strata of the other, but quite the contrary, since it is always the strong and powerful man of the dominant class who seeks among the neighboring population new opportunities for pleasure. This leads Pittard, for example, to exclaim "that it is the poor devils who possess the greatest ethnic nobility." Despite this, the case of America was so singular that a certain hesitation occurred in the functioning of the normal law of human sexual relations. The hesitation reached the point of abstention, for example, in the zone later occupied by the English. In the classic zone of New England, marriages between a white man and an Indian woman were rare; but in the American colonies of Portugal and Castile, licentiousness was the rule, and the practice obtained sanction the moment the Catholic Church recognized the quality of persons with souls in the indigenous people and consequently authorized them to receive the sacraments, including, among them, matrimony. We should not overlook the fact that there are still some who judge that the legalization [p. 77] of a process questionable from the ethnic point of view was an error. For many, the failure of Latin America consists precisely in that mestizaje, which they classify as inferior, while the success of the Saxon depends on the continued and strict domination of the Puritan bloodline, which would constitute a kind of ruling and directing nobility. I deny neither the opposition nor the force of the two theses. But just as so many have already occupied themselves with providing arguments in favor of the thesis of pure race as the dominant factor — and, in sum, in favor of the thesis that a good Indian is a dead Indian — I have been devoting myself to finding arguments, and what is more important, evidence, that the contrary thesis, the thesis favorable to the mestizo, also has foundation and value.

I maintain that the work of this mestizaje will prove more fruitful in the long run, and is of greater importance to humanity in general, than the work of any previous race. And this notwithstanding how easy it is today to condemn the mestizo, who is threatened with disintegration, while the power of the white man renewed in North America extends victoriously through the world. One needs a criterion hardened to resist the seductions of success in order to persist in a faith that leaves nothing in its path but bitterness, disillusionment, and disaster. Nevertheless, there is something profoundly distasteful and almost suspicious in every success that is too abrupt and resounding. Commonly, success responds only to the limitation and diminishment of the ideal, and then it is preferable even as a spur to ascension toward what lies beyond: a desolate despair is worth more than a mean and pragmatic conformity. Let us prefer eternal salt in the mouth to having to proclaim that happiness is like a piece of candy! Let those who are satisfied with the present flatter it. It is comfortable, but very tedious, to be nothing more than the philosopher of success — and moreover one is exposed to no few surprises.

[p. 78] For we have already seen that the American races, principally because of the isolation imposed upon them by the physical environment, had remained in limbo, while the European races, constantly worked upon by displacements and conquests, emigrations and the mixing of bloodlines, put such a quantity of life into action that the impulse carried them to the discovery of the unknown, carried them all the way to the New World.

The Spaniards came to America, and let us be clear: the best came. Because at the beginning they did not come from the Court, some historians of the servant-class mentality suppose that invasion was one of brutes. But in those times, as today, not coming from the Court is already a recommendation, and besides, what the soldiers of Spain accomplished here surpasses what was done not only in the Reconquest: in many cases it surpasses everything that had been done in history. The epic of the Castilian missions is one of those heroic chapters that antiquity never imagined — heroic chapters in the struggle of the soul against darkness. The truth is that the best of the Spanish caste came to the continent, the best in devotion and in effort. As the able dominators they were, it is natural to suppose that they chose the flower of the indigenous populations, whether for the household, or for teaching and labor.

The origins of Ibero-American mestizaje are thus not as murky as we are accustomed at first glance to suppose. In a certain sense our lineage proceeds from two vital aristocracies: selection roughly tested and refined by effort. So abrupt was the encounter, however, that the new situation thus produced entered once again into a kind of fermentation during the three hundred years of the Colony.

The first autochthonous expression manifests itself in the movement [p. 79] of emancipation: autochthonous, that is, with respect to the new mestizo race, not to the indigenous people, who would never again act on their own account. Indeed, the rebellion against the Spanish Crown was occurring at the same time on the Iberian peninsula itself, and Spaniards were the movement's best auxiliaries. The new patriotism pursued economic and political ends rather than ethnic ones. Nor could it have been a movement of liberation for the Indian, simply because the Indian no longer existed — he had perhaps never existed as a national entity, and no longer existed spiritually, given that everything he knows, everything he thinks, everything he is today, proceeds from the European invasion. What was his own had disintegrated, just as all ancient cultures have disintegrated, never to return.

And at the center of the conflict, to concentrate and synthesize it, there remained the enormous mass of the mestizo population, the first truly new race that history has known. And here are the mestizos presenting themselves to the world amid the insults and distrust of some, amid the patronizing and self-interested gaze of most, and falling prey, in the best of cases, to a tutelage that none of the great peoples disdain to exercise, even as they classify us, muttering, as dark-skinned inferiors. So childish is the craving for collective domination! One might say that Ibero-American mestizaje — the Benjamin of history — is passing through that laborious yet mystery-laden and promise-filled state that all new cases of life must traverse in biology. With the qualifications that must be made on account of the difference in species, the life of Ibero-American mestizaje reminds me of the experiment by the naturalist Paul Bert, cited by Romanes. Freshwater crustaceans are taken and their water is gradually changed to salt water. The crustaceans suffer immense distress and die; but not before generating a generation of crustaceans already adapted to the new brackish element. This is how I [p. 80] explain the evident sterility of the Ibero-American mestizo race throughout the entire first century of its political autonomy. Apparently it has done nothing but continue dying, ill-accustomed as it is to an environment that is neither the ancient American one nor the European one. Blessed, however, are the strong organisms that must quicken their dynamic and punish their fate to compel them to respond swiftly to life's solicitations. And when that dynamic is employed not only, as in fish, to descend to the environment and submit to it, but to force the environment and force history so that all of life improves, then one cannot but recognize that this tremendous palpitation of ours is like a challenge to the capacities of nature. I do not know whether Latin American mestizaje will only learn, like the laboratory crustaceans, to sustain itself in the salty and already somewhat putrid water of the old ways, or whether it will manage to surpass the crustacean and extract from the exercise of its adaptation enough force to turn back upon the environment and engender a new culture. But I see in the remote yet not impossible triumph of this adventure of mestizaje the only hope of the world.

And proceeding as the Castilians did who left Mother Spain because the despair of a fundamental anguish drove them, I depart from the secure road of ideological imitation of European models and take refuge in the mysterious confusion, in the uncertainty of the impulses of the new race and of the continent's coming flowering.

Nevertheless, let us guard ourselves once more from the seduction of mere phrases and try to make our thought more precise. I do not believe that any sincere person would dare to recommend a single model of development to peoples still in formation. Even today the life of the entire species is a turbid and often nauseating process. Let [p. 81] no one then impose his own culture as a model, nor is anyone authorized to condemn a case that has barely emerged from the fertile and tangled entrails of ethnology. Every variety, according to the law formulated by Hugo de Vries, tends to merge into a standard population type unless a factor intervenes to protect it — that is, to preserve its peculiarities. The protective factor tends to be, in the case of animal species, the segregation of which naturalists speak. History has already applied these processes to human life, and we have seen that time punishes them with decadence. Segregation, moreover, is today outside the practices of the time. The future belongs to increasingly marked and multiple mixtures and combinations. The mestizo population of Latin America is no more than the first flowering of a mode of mixing that the new conditions of the world will generate throughout the entire planet. To the period of segregation and isolation of nations corresponded the division and autogenesis of races. To the period of civilization — no longer national, nor even racial, but planetary — must correspond a total race, a race that in its very blood is a synthesis of mankind in all the varied and profound aspects of man. There is the bold but inevitable conclusion we must formulate. The germ of this future cosmic race I find in the contemporary population of Latin America.

The variety that this race represents will not be able to follow De Vries's law or, more precisely, will conform to the second part of that law, because the accident that must constantly be renewing it is found not only in changes in the physical environment but in the stimulus of all the modes of universal culture. The error of Darwinism, the error of all the doctrines that are the fruit of observations from natural history, consists in wanting to apply to man the constants discovered in realms [p. 82] that do not know the influence of inspired or simply intelligent will. In this way the most important factor of human potential is disdained: the breath that periodically stirs peoples and leads them to fulfill their destinies. It is acknowledged that the humblest clay is capable of acquiring significance when an artist imprints pulsation upon it, yet there is no willingness to see that if the same clay obeys, with greater reason must the conscious element that permeates peoples obey — precious substance from which proceeds everything that before our eyes imitates splendor. The vital element, suddenly inspired by a lofty purpose — is that not all of history? And what is not that is no more than zoology!

And there is scarcely a race that has not been capable at some moment in its evolution of embodying one of those movements that set the scales of foresight dancing. Let us not forget that the great tidal waves of the ideal — the ones called Christianity and the Crusades and the Discoveries — what were they but accidents of a superior force, accidents that saved the European from falling into the solitude and dejection of ancient Egypt or of the aboriginal civilizations of America? Constancy, genius, and audacity: these are the driving forces of every destiny.

In this same America, were not the two constructive forces of what we are perhaps two ideal forces — swift, uncompromising, dominating, and exalted: the missionary spirit of the Catholics and the inflexible creed of the Puritans?

We have been denied the capacity to generate an elevated movement of any kind; we have habitually been condemned in the name of small and questionable laboratory conclusions. It is time that we too proclaim our own theory. If the laboratory gives us nothing, [p. 83] let us draw from the air the forces of our salvation — from the air, whose inextricable rhythms contain summons more imperious than the dull and superable regularities of what lies below. But let us not stop at pure imprecation; let us not wait for the air to blow — let us convert it into wind. Let us reflect that if we do not set the present in motion, the future will not be consummated. Let us discipline ourselves, illuminate our action and make it run within that same law of open doors that has been the source of our own idiosyncrasy. By this I mean that I do not advocate the consolidation of a sense of mestizo caste, simply because all such sentiments are like rest stops in the course of progress, and we must be that which is always in motion. Let us press forward with our mestizaje, without renouncing it, but also without boasting of its privileged freshness of heart. Let us press forward with our criollos, who represent in some cases the most exquisite refinement of the Castilian spirit, in others its most lamentable decadence — but in both cases a leaven full of ferments. Let us also pay attention to the fact that alongside the primitive Hispano-indigenous mestizaje there has produced another mixing more humble but also important as a human factor and endowed as well with rare virtues: the mixing of Spaniard and Black, and of Portuguese and Black — the mulato, who has given so many illustrious children to our nations and who contains so many treasures of inspiration and art in his freshly worn depths. Add to these mestizajes, which we will call debatable, the indigenous mestizaje, the Black mestizaje, and the combinations of these two types — types being tested in the crucible of life; add to this entire complexity the European-type mestizajes that have been constituted vigorously in the South of Brazil and the North of Argentina, mixtures of Italian and Spanish and Portuguese and Polish and Russian; add still the Asian [p. 84] emigrations settled along the Pacific, and you will recognize that our America is already the continent of all races. For the first time, so many and such diverse peoples have come together, on one and the same vast zone of the world, on an equal footing and with the common aim of beginning all the tasks of destiny anew.

Before this vigorous tableau there is undoubtedly reason to ask whether the traits being gradually acquired by each group will not contribute to the continuation of the process of dispersion that began in the early nineteenth century with the independence of the colonies. Particularly among the groups of pure European blood — such as the Paulistas of Brazil and the porteños of Argentina and the Uruguayans — certain enlightened minorities have manifested or now manifest the tendency to constitute separate entities within the old Ibero-American tradition. "We have nothing in common with the Indian," descendants of the recently imported Italian have often said; "we are pure Europeans," is repeated in Montevideo; and even the Spanish lineage is sometimes dismissed in order to imagine ethnic formations similar to the unmixed white stock of the United States of the North, only based on the Latin populations of Europe. Those who defend this thesis point with alarm to the failure — an evident failure for now — of countries like Mexico, Peru, and Bolivia, which have a large indigenous population and a mestizaje that tends to be more antisocial and more inept than the dark indigenous people themselves. There was a moment when it seemed that, indeed, the ancient indigenous soul and its final hope, which is the mestizo, would be absorbed and annihilated by the invasion of European immigrants, more industrious and better prepared to prevail in the fierce economic competition of the times.

The European war, on one hand, and the advances of socialism, [p. 85] which tends to make life happier; the limitation of the birth rate, which will lift the punishment that accompanies the sins of excess population; the disease of nationalism, which has once again become acute in the old world; and the restrictions that the new world places on immigration — all these diverse and conflicting causes combine to produce a reduction in the European emigration current. On the other hand, the European immigrants already established in Ibero-America have been undergoing the influence of the new environment and are transforming themselves, in spite of themselves; and in adapting they have no tradition to fall back on other than the Hispano-indigenous tradition or the Luso-Portuguese-Brazilian tradition. The children of Italians, Poles, or Russians, descendants of very European parents, begin by feeling very porteño, end by feeling Argentine, and will finally confess themselves Ibero-American. It results, then, that those already established retain the power of orientation — the mold, so to speak — even while the new arrivals contribute esteemed and mildly transformative virtues. This is also explained by the fact that the immigrant always arrives in economic and even cultural conditions inferior to those of the natives. It is not the university graduates of Europe who emigrate to America, but the working classes, almost always ignorant, who upon improving their condition educate their children in schools shaped by an environment modeled on the Hispano-indigenous tradition. In this way, in the South, as in what occurs in North America, a cultural absorption of the newly arrived takes place. Thus, even without counting the mixing of bloodlines, each new ethnic contribution furthers the development of what we have called indología, to give it once and for all a name that encompasses the argentinidad of Ricardo Rojas, and chilenidad, and Peruanism, and the Antilles, and Colombia and Mexico.

Not only in the ethnic order, but in the economic order and [p. 86] in all orders, the European war has been a reprieve for us Ibero-Americans. Not only insofar as it concerns Europe, but because it also distracted the United States somewhat, led it to deploy its energies in different regions of the world; and this entire period, during which we will remain relegated to oblivion, can and must serve us to consolidate our common interests and to bind firmly our continental relations. If during this wait we manage to assimilate the recently arrived populations — note well that we do not intend to crush the germs latent in them, but rather to incorporate those germs into the growing genealogical tree of the continental homeland — if we likewise take advantage of this casual wait to strengthen our economic autonomy, along with our spiritual and political autonomy, then the entire cause of Ibero-Americanism will be preserved. Let us remember, however, that history is generally sparing with opportunities and does not go around offering the grand prize in the lottery of destiny twice.

Immigration will return and we will receive it, as always, with pleasure, for we know full well that America's destiny is to shelter all peoples; but it is necessary that this immigration find us worthy of continuing to build an ideal that, by virtue of being vaster and higher, replaces the national ideals of the new immigrant. It is well known, for example, that immigration from Spain presents no problem for us, since it assimilates immediately into our environment and enriches it with its strong virtues — immigration from Latin peoples in general offers the same advantages — but we must endeavor to make ourselves so broad that even the Saxons find among us an atmosphere of cordiality and opportunities for the development of aptitudes that will be for us a stimulus, an example, a new germ. [p. 87] Let us not forget that the Saxon is not excluded from that syncretism of cultures which is the basis and law of our concept of Ibero-Americanism.

The so-called New World, like the old, has witnessed how civilizations flourish and decay. Civilizations do indeed come and go, but the human composite — the particular race that each period of cultural flowering creates — generally survives the era of its own constructive capacity. The social development of a people is interrupted at a certain moment and yet the people continues living and reproducing. The Greeks of the era of Roman power had ceased to exist as members of a powerful culture, and yet they endured as a race, mixed with others, being modified but at the same time always keeping something of their original idiosyncrasy alive. When Cortés arrived at Anáhuac, the Indians of Moctezuma had not even heard of the builders of the pyramids of Teotihuacán; yet there can be no doubt that a great number of these Indians carried in their veins the same blood as those ancient builders of monuments. The Aztecs, who were relatively newcomers, may not have been subject to such influences; but the subjugated strata, the older inhabitants, undoubtedly possessed in flesh and soul the inheritance of the remote creators of civilization. The invaders, in turn, not only cannot entirely destroy the subjugated people — they inevitably mix with them and, through sexual relations, receive an influence whose effects manifest themselves at once in the new generation. The fact is that our indigenous races, at least the indigenous peoples of the tropics, are far from being a primitive race, as so many anthropologists have wished to judge them. They may perhaps constitute cases of decadence, but in no way examples of retarded or barely initiated evolution. The [p. 88] title of primitive race might perhaps be applied to the Indian of New Mexico — though he would more properly be classified as a case of provincial civilization, since all his culture was a reflection of the more advanced centers of the South — but it is evident that the races which built, before the arrival of the Spaniards, the cultural centers of Mexico, Guatemala, and Peru cannot be classified as primitive races. Primitive, on the other hand, are the red-skinned peoples.

During the era of indigenous flourishing, as well as later during the Spanish colonial period and through the end of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the rhythm of New World civilization moved from South to North, the reverse of what occurs in our times. Indeed, beginning with the Declaration of Independence of North America, the impulses of progress are generated and transmitted from North to South, from cold climate toward warm — and this explains the passing discredit of everything tropical.

But returning to the subject of race, let us observe that the mass of our indigenous people constitutes an ancient and refined race that has known days of splendor and is now passing through a long eclipse full of bitterness. When these people came into contact with the Spanish invader, the most characteristic phenomenon of our current ethnographic situation was produced: the unlimited, unrestrained mixing of conquerors and conquered.

There was created from that moment in Latin America an ethnically mixed type entirely different from the type of the pure white who was to dominate in the United States. And a whole social system also completely different from the North American one, since in North America the different races coexist but without intermarrying. The Black man, for example, constitutes in the United States a world apart, while among us he marries the Indian and produces the mulato. The influence of the Black on North American culture is purely cultural — we owe to it, [p. 89] for example, the music and dance of that great country — but among us the influence of the Black is far more intimate, since after one or more mixings he merges with us, is lost in the dark tide of the mestizo population.

The first time the world could become aware of the appearance of this new ethnic being that is the Ibero-American mestizo was in the sixteenth century, when the works of Inca Garcilaso de la Vega — celebrated in our literature — began to be published. The Inca descended from a Spanish father and a Peruvian mother. His youth was nourished by the legends of the defeated, and although he was personally educated in the dominant class, his maternal relatives and his local connections imbued him with the indigenous feeling. Later he lived in Spain and was there filled with the soul of his paternal race; all his complex emotions he left written in pages of the most vivid interest; his soul was the first bridge thrown between the ancient races and the new race of Spanish America. It would be difficult to find in his time a greater figure in Spain itself than this mestizo Garcilaso de la Vega, who took upon his conscience the task of making a single soul from the lacerating conflict of the two rival civilizations. No one thought then that this mestizo was inferior to either his indigenous ancestors or his Castilian ancestors.

The fate of the Ibero-American mestizo has had to suffer, among other setbacks, that of having been born within a civilization that was being relegated to secondary status. The extraordinary progress of North Americans of pure European stock has caused the mestizo to fall into even greater discredit than the Latino of Spain or Italy, because at least the Latinos of Europe have a great and illustrious past, while we were born in an era when the center of culture had already shifted very far from our cradle. [p. 90]

[p. 91]

But the ethnic problem represented by the mestizo remains, and remains still unresolved. The racial problem so grave among us is no less acute in the United States, though there it presents itself not in the form of mestizaje but as a system of absolute separation of bloodlines. To judge more fairly the Spanish system of mestizaje in comparison with the English system of prohibiting marital relations between ethnic groups, compare, for example, the work of the English in India with what the Spaniards did in their American colonies. From a cultural point of view, there is no doubt that the effects of Spanish colonization are far deeper, for while the English remain in India as foreign invaders, the Spaniards made of America a greater Spain. And they see reproduced there their blood and their very soul, even though they no longer hold it under their political domination. This is understandable: the Spaniards undertook a true conquest of souls. They managed to replace a retarded civilization, such as the indigenous one was, with a civilization in ascent, such as theirs was in the era of discoveries. For an ideal enterprise of this kind, one might believe it legitimate to undertake a conquest. The modern systems of colonialism and mandate in the English and French manner, cynically supported by commercial interests, do not even propose the only thing that might justify them: the evangelization of the Muslim world. Replacing Oriental despotism with the civilized and democratic methods of the West could be a moral banner comparable to the struggle of the missionaries who abolished human sacrifice. But not only is no such crusade attempted, but the great powers of the day commit the vileness of founding Institutes of Muslim Wisdom and Mosques. I do not say that mosques should be razed, but I do say that all the effort of the new colonizers should be employed in moral conquest — that is, in the spiritual improvement of the subjugated populations. One must not destroy a race, but one may undertake its education. But this aspect of the interchange of social relations has been understood only in the two great eras of history. Only nations under the influence of the two greatest spiritual movements that have shaken humanity have achieved it: Christianity and Buddhism. Ancient India and Spain with Portugal: these are the two truly conquering races, because they did not limit themselves to occupying territories and selling slaves, but rather won over wills and liberated souls.

In general, from the most remote times, whenever two peoples have encountered each other, the sexual approach that creates more or less complex mixtures has followed, and it has generally been thought that these mixtures are advantageous for the development of the species. The prejudice of skin color is rather a modern sentiment and perhaps proceeds from weakness on the part of those who abstain from marriages with strangers. The Spaniard in America took an indigenous woman without much reflection on what he was doing, very certain that, being the master and lord, all his acts would redound to the good. The Englishman in India, on the other hand, probably feels the fear of being absorbed by a population more numerous than that of his homeland, and in some cases more refined and cultivated. Hence that insistence on defending the original caste, since one is not certain that it will impose itself on the outside as Castilian did.

The fact is that in our day both systems of relating different races on the same territory persist: the system of voluntary crossings and the system of absolute separation in matters of sexual life. [p. 92] And if we in Latin America suffer the still debatable effects of crossing, the United States keeps alive within itself the dreadful Black problem.

The least that can be said of our system — and perhaps also the most — is that it is more humane and more Christian because it brings men closer to one another. Among us it is taste that determines — and each day mere taste will prevail more — which race, which type is most desirable for the work of reproduction. Our system, based on emotion, is perhaps closer to the profound ends of life than the other system, made of calculation and rigorous prohibition.

On the other hand, it is too late for us to wish to attempt a change of practices; we have no recourse but to press on bravely with the adventure of mestizaje, fortifying our attempt with all the powers of reflection and knowledge, but leaving intact the still unfathomable sense of its mission.

Within Mexico itself, the mestizo — who if any homeland can claim as his own, it is that one — is nonetheless not exempt from rivalries that are at times fierce; disdained by the white and viewed with suspicion by the indigenous person, he suffers the fate of all who venture into the new. Of Mexico one can say that if tomorrow, by virtue of some cataclysm, communication with the outside world were severed; if the country were to return to the isolation in which the Spaniards found it, within a few centuries we would see that not only the white had disappeared from there, but the mestizo as well, and with few variations one would find there a new conqueror and a kind of renewed reign of Moctezuma. Something of this could be felt at certain moments of our most recent revolutions, which in many isolated cases become a true indigenous reclamation that easily exceeds the limits of the economic and political to impose even certain traits of custom and dress. This clearly reveals the spiritual intention of these outbursts, which on the other hand never reach clear expression [p. 93] — simply because there never existed what could be called indigenous nationalism, much less a collective psychology. The relative isolation in which nations and tribes live, with no bond of language or institutions, explains why any design for resurgence is absolutely illusory. Nevertheless, during the Zapatista rebellion, the curious case was observed of European dress being entirely discarded in an entire extensive region, and of descendants of Europeans who joined the movement adopting indigenous dress, incapable of preserving — let alone imposing — their own. In contrast, the ideology was frankly imported, and religious devotion was maintained around the old teachings of the Catholic missionaries. Which proves that the European spirit had already imposed itself even in places where habit and body may display isolated signs of resistance.

Just as we have seen that morally and spiritually the mestizo race demonstrates qualities and defects that are still a consequence of the formative period through which it is passing, in physical terms as well one observes the variety of faces frequently encountered in Mexican lands, as if the definitive model had not yet been formed; if by exception types of extraordinary beauty are produced, the average of the new race is rather unattractive. Much of the indigenous race is also unattractive; but this depends not so much on inheritance as on the working conditions in which the Indian and the mestizo live. Peasants of European races as beautiful as the Portuguese are seen to have degenerated, grown ugly, because of rough labor; the same can be said of poor Dutchmen. Ugliness is commonly a consequence of poverty. Beauty is the fruit of care and luxury.

Whether or not the white aristocracy that dominates our times likes it, the hour of the mestizo is approaching. Europeanized mestizajes in Latin America; [p. 94] confused mestizajes throughout all of the East — the East has always been mestizo — mestizajes prohibited in North America and Canada. But what will happen if the bridges are cut? What will happen with the Black man, who, precisely because he is socially inferior, reproduces more than the white of the dominant class? What will happen with all these contemporary elites? They have already begun to ask themselves this, and the problem is generally discussed in terms of militias and battles for existence. For the damned theory of the white dominators goes on speaking of extermination and fails to notice that at the hour of vengeances it is always the select minorities that are sacrificed, even if they often represent superior forms of collective life. The remedy, however, is very simple — the remedy is a procedure that corresponds to the universal era in which we live. The remedy lies in applying the same types of education and life to all races; that is, it is necessary to end racial prejudices and raise the level of the inferior. For the inferior race always takes revenge on its dominators by devouring them. The defense of the weak lies in numbers. A diabolical instinct leads the masses of slaves to multiply without limit until the mass moves and becomes a wave that sweeps everything before it. And Cain is devoured by the bastards of the discordant family. There remains, then, nothing but mestizaje or tutelage: the mixing of all in pursuit of union and increase in love and excellence, or at least the improvement and assimilation of all the populations of the earth.

What we must defend with intransigence is the principle that in our zone the doctrines and practices of the closed caste and the unilateral tradition are worthless. We insist that our portion of the continent is not the exclusive patrimony of the Indian or the Black, nor of the white either, and that the Asian is not excluded, because the fundamental meaning of the cycle of civilization that [p. 95] begins with us is the reunion of all types after the long era of the punishment of dispersion. From the harmonious mixture there will not emerge, no doubt, the Nietzschean superman, Darwin's select being with tiger jaws who devours his own kind. What may emerge is the Totinemo (from the Latin totus = all; inem = man), the total man, the synthetic man, the prototype and final type of the species.

To combat dispersion and to direct all isolated forces toward a common purpose: such is the most urgent task of this part of the world. So that the isolation to which the physical environment inclines does not again make of the recently constituted populations so many nationalisms that enter lethargy, like the ancient indigenous nationalities, let us put modern technology to work, establishing communications across seas and above mountains. To combat the relapse into the single type that stifles the fecund variants and provokes the relapse of which the naturalists speak, let us insist on the incessant innovation and interweaving of races. The invigorating exercise of spiritual power will carry not only the races forward; it will also gradually imprint upon the physical model norms suited to higher ends. A whole series of new processes and processes contradicting the past — there lies the enormous difficulty of the Ibero-American task! Will we manage to replace local patriotisms with a generous continental patriotism? Will we manage to create the perfected ethnic type or will we only succeed in finishing off the contemporary autochthonous characteristics? These are some of the questions for which imagining answers is not enough — improvised solutions must be found. Improvisation is our calamity and also our fate. Life has taken us by surprise. This is the continent of no waiting. And we do not wait because the other continents are pressing upon us and because our hour has begun to sound. In us [p. 96] the centuries rise. For the time being, we must resort to those inevitable scaffoldings of all construction — scaffoldings that permit the raising of the tower and the correcting of the characteristics of its structure. One of those scaffoldings that must serve us to verify the transition from nationalism to universalism and from mestizaje to Totinism, of which we spoke a moment ago — one of our most effective instruments of cohesion — we find for now in the common language. The Spanish language in the Hispanic homelands and Portuguese, akin to Castilian in Brazil: there is the common bond, more vigorous than any treaty or any political charter. Our very patriotism will tend to become a linguistic patriotism. We will feel bound in this way, across the seas, to populations like the Philippine one, which shares with us moreover the kinship of a Hispano-Asian mestizaje, twin of the Hispano-indigenous mestizaje. Vast as our continent is, the Castilian language gives us in this way a homeland still vaster than the continent and more universal: a homeland that has as its colonies no less the Sephardic Asia Minor than Moroccan Africa. Perhaps the entire world tends toward the integration of these vast linguistic empires, which would be far more rational and far more in accord with the spirit than all the nationalisms that commerce, chance, and the sword have forged until today. Faced with the powerful evidence of the English-speaking world, is it not legitimate to speak of a Spanish world — a world as free as that of the English, respectful of all local sovereignties, yet one before danger, one in labor, and one in glory? This is how I conceive the next stage of this indigenous civilization, giving to the term indigenous the universal sense I have already indicated. I conceive that indigenous culture will spread across the planet, but not according to the English rule, which brings with it the useless Darwinian selectionist doctrine of the elimination of inferior races for the [p. 97] survival of the white. Our doctrine will be, as I have said, Totinist. It will seek a manner of selection to which all races contribute the best of their gifts. Pan-Hispanism, but in order to achieve Totalism. No longer Pan-Germanism, nor Pan-Anglicism, nor even white hegemony, but the hegemony of the best from all castes and the appearance of the final caste: the Totinist one. Total life upon the renewed planet.

More than the land itself, language will then be the vehicle of this powerful synthesizing movement of human energies.

You will tell me that this will not then be a race, because the concept of race presupposes kinship of blood, while language is an intangible bond that can shelter within itself different races and different nations, often at odds with one another. And we will reply that it is not, in fact, a matter of giving preeminence to a given race, but of creating one that embraces the best aptitudes of all, and that for now mestizaje and language will be the factors of the new creation. All the old concepts must be corrected and transformed in accordance with the demands of a new cycle of culture, which will be, as is natural, the creator of its own aims and its own arrangements. What kind of political league must be established among all these enormous human associations is something we can barely imagine for the immediate future, though no longer for the distant one. Nor do such petty arrangements matter — they have brought nothing to this point but misfortune and shame upon the human family. Will the Ibero-American Republics form defensive leagues, in the manner of the ancient and classical Amphictyonic League? Perhaps it will be more readily achieved, before arriving at political formulas of limited value, to enter fully into the unification of our economic base through the suppression of international tariffs, and into the unification of education, and so on.

[p. 98] What is certain is that in order to raise the spirits today depressed in some nations by caudillismo and in others by economic oppression, it is necessary that we become conscious of our purpose and of the means that will help us achieve it. It is particularly necessary that we think with the heads that rest on our shoulders and perceive with the senses of our bodies, for it has been our habit to spare ourselves the work of thinking by reading and accepting what is said of us outside of America — idle reports and judgments tainted by inaccuracy. In the disaster we suffer, not only has our territorial unity been destroyed — a unity that would have allowed us to constitute a magnificent common country — but also what constitutes the soul of Ibero-American nationhood: the Spanish language is presented by some as a speech already in decay and fractured into dialects that "would not be understood in Spain."

Fortunately, in reality the differences are of the kind that enrich and invigorate a language, far from depriving it of unity. Except for the use of a few local terms and idioms — which moreover are already being adopted by the common dictionaries — there is not the slightest reason for incomprehension in the spoken language from North to South. There is no doubt that greater linguistic unity exists in Spanish America than in Spain itself, where local languages continue to subsist alongside Spanish. In England itself, Scottish and the Welsh dialect survive, and yet no one takes it upon himself to say that English is in decomposition. In our America, Maya and Zapotec and Guaraní and Quechua and perhaps twenty other dialects, which have no connection with one another and lack written expression and the power of diffusion, continue to be spoken. They thus offer no resistance whatsoever to Castilian; on the contrary, each of these languages has contributed to enlarging [p. 99] the Castilian lexicon, just as happens with the languages of the colonies. In contrast, those same languages are slowly disappearing and will disappear altogether, because the age in which we live tends toward simplification in lesser matters and therefore does not permit the luxury of managing many languages. The ever-growing necessity of knowing at least two universal languages carries with it an implicit death sentence for all languages of limited reach, however illustrious they may be. The rooting of Castilian throughout all of America of Hispanic origin is therefore firm and growing.

And while it is true that the language appears to have suffered a stagnation in recent times — in the last two centuries — this is probably nothing more than the consequence of the enormous diffusion it had to undergo as a result of the unparalleled vastness of its conquests. The ideal mantle of the language extended its shelter over alien peoples in a way that no language had ever encompassed, and so many of us have been covering ourselves with its splendor that the threads have necessarily been stretched and here and there a gap has opened; and perhaps Castilian today lacks all the terms required for the handling of this or that art, this or that recently invented discipline; but on the other hand, no other language has so extended its ideal frameworks, no other has channels so broad and lush for receiving within its own structure all the useful discoveries, all the modes of the traveler returning from remote distances.

No language has made a comparable effort, I have said, and I do not except even English — for the simple reason that English has engaged in colonialism rather than proselytism. The English tactic of placing a perimeter around the overseas establishment and engaging the enemy from within it, with a view to gradually expanding the perimeter, is far simpler than the Spanish system — one of total surrender of personality [p. 100] with all its perversities and all its sublimities. It is only in recent times that English has undertaken the task of making itself spoken by subjugated populations, while Spanish has been gaining adherents for four hundred years among peoples who are not of the Spanish race. This surpassing of the ethnic limit, carried out in a systematic manner — since the missionary's first act was to teach the language in which the sacred texts were written — had never been attempted on the American scale. What is surprising, then, in the Spanish genius appearing afflicted by a certain sluggishness when it comes to neologisms and certain refinements of style, which are more properly the province of literatures made idle by the absence of a great human task?

If the miracle of America is to have produced, after so many centuries of history, a completely new race — such as the one constituted by the Hispano-indigenous mestizaje that today is the basis of more complex mixtures — is it not also a cause for wonder that this new race has not had to elaborate, as others have done, a language of its own — a task that would have cost it centuries of mental effort — but instead presents itself armed with a language already evolved, already made, already illustrious, and now flourishing with idioms and turns of phrase and terms? The danger that may have arisen at some point — the danger that the language might degenerate into dialects suited to the peculiarities of each region — is averted by the written language, which has always been kept uniform and has been preserved as a common point of reference. Moreover, this same relative fixity of the literary language has contributed to the failure of certain incorrect or highly local ways of speaking to prosper, because those localisms have generally not been elevated to the status of writing. The Argentinisms of the modern Argentine theater, like the numerous Mexicanisms of the national short story, have appeared as literary [p. 101] varieties in an era when their use was no longer dangerous, merely curious, and at times — though rarely — agreeable. The most powerful defense of the language's uniformity is found in an institution unknown to antiquity and never put into practice by the old imperialisms: I refer to the primary school. The first country to devote careful attention to the organization of elementary education was Argentina, and this circumstance has been fortunate because it was precisely there — in the country most distant and most recently colonized — where the danger of cultural uprooting was greatest. When one contemplates the classes of the primary schools of Buenos Aires, composed of children who come from illustrious races of Europe and yet carry out all their tasks and sing their songs in pure Castilian, one understands how futile was the alarm — not always sincere — with which some denounced the corruption of the Spanish language in America.

Thanks to the work of the schools, the language is being saved and perpetuated in its essential ways, and above and beyond the localisms that were the creation of the criollo of a certain era and that disappear with the criollo of that time. The same will happen with the Italianisms of the recently assimilated Buenos Aires population: some will be permanently incorporated into the language, but most will disappear along with the intermediate generation that invents them. The schools thus represent a systematic organ of purification, and precisely because this implies a plan, it ultimately imposes itself upon the variations that arise from nothing more than the groping and confusion of a multiplied usage.

The Ibero-American will not trade the treasure of the Spanish language for any other. Not the criollo, who rightly sees Castilian as his own language; nor the mestizo, who has no other; but the Indian as well will not be able to remain unfaithful to a linguistic tradition that came accompanied [p. 102] by atrocities, it is true, as all other linguistic imports have been — but that already represents for the Indian himself a conquest and an advantage. It will be said that there are Indians who still do not know Castilian, and this is accurate to a limited degree; but given the deep currents that today stir our peoples, it is not reckless to affirm that within twenty years Castilian will have finished penetrating to the soul of the most remote populations. No other language is in a position to achieve this diffusion in so little time. So that even if there were no other reason than this, it would be sufficient to affirm that the possibility of another language taking root as a native means of expression in the populations of the former Castilian countries is beyond discussion.

[p. 103]

In keeping with the vast cycle of civilization that is beginning in Latin America, we have shown ourselves generously broad in the definition of all our formative norms: a nationalism that yields before the superior interests of the new race; a concept of race that does not confine itself to a single bloodline but accepts the contribution of all the aptitudes contained in the different varieties of the human type; a free civilization that ensures justice for all men. But do we perhaps fall into narrowness when we cling to the Castilian language as the common and necessary medium for the constitution of the personality of the new race? Why could not so complex and advanced a race be bilingual or trilingual? It should not be so simply because neither should Europe be so, and what is a calamity bequeathed by the past is not something we will erect into a model. Precisely so that the confusion of Babel that reigns in poor Europe will not be renewed in America, we will not promote multilingualism, it being simpler, more useful, and more civilized to pursue simplification of what is merely means and method. Note that we are not speaking of linguistic supremacies — an argument that in the case of Castilian would not be easy to dismiss without reflection — but of advantages that redound to the benefit of civilization and the spirit. It causes pain to see the time wasted in managing words that more or less say the same thing in different sounds and with different signs, when that time and attention are demanded by life and thought. A man of European education is terrified when he learns that the Chinese spends the best years of studenthood deciphering hieroglyphs that do nothing but indicate and enumerate things — that is, a function that our alphabetic languages perform in six months of instruction. Something similar happens to the Ibero-American who has traveled from the Rio Bravo to the Río de la Plata with his limpid Spanish and then enters Europe to traverse states and statelets through which every twelve hours of travel, or every six, he changes not only language — frequently even the signs of the alphabet change, as in the case of the Balkan peoples. To have been spared this chaos is one of the services we most owe to Castilian.

The emigrating and reincarnating power of this vigorous language has not been equaled by any other. And precisely because rapid growth has been its destiny, it does not fear change and is ready to enrich itself and even to evolve into another, more universal speech if necessary, or to merge into the language that modern life — made of communication among all peoples — will have to elaborate to replace all these medieval languages in which we express ourselves and which are like the walls of the era from which they proceed. To tear them down will be to progress. The wind of a new universal language will someday blow over all this decay of the common tongues. No Taboo then — not even the Taboo of language. All our values ready for the sacrifice the moment the superior voice of life demands it. But let it be well understood that the renunciation [p. 104] must be free, voluntary, and conscious, when it so suits the interest of the species. Never, on the other hand, if it is only a matter of substituting one limited tradition for another, and much less if it is the imposition of force that consummates the exchange. Will tempered and flexible, imagination unbound, so that no noble adventure finds us indifferent. But let us not forget sound judgment, which is the secret of builders — sound judgment to assess obstacles and to make use of resources: clear perception of the end, and wings to keep the end receding.

When this book was nearly finished, I learned of the one published by Madison Grant, with a French preface by Lapouge, who is, as I understand it, one of the most authoritative in the matter of skulls — one of those who have made a pseudoscience of anthropology. Reading this book has suggested to me some observations, which I set down in the order in which they occurred to me.

One circumstance that deprives Grant's statistics of all value is the fact that he takes as indicators of superiority characteristics that do not in themselves imply any superiority. He tells us, for example, that four percent of soldiers of English origin fell below the average height, while the other races contributed 22 percent among the Irish; 27 percent among the Greeks; 39 percent among the Russians; and 46 percent among the Poles.

I could point out to Mr. Grant indigenous races of America, such as the Yaqui and the Tehuanas, whose stature surpasses, or at least equals, the Scottish. But it has not been proven that the tall man is more intelligent, more capable, or even stronger than the short man. The only conclusion to be drawn from the aforementioned statistics is that the English have an average of great height. Let us suppose, however, that this greater proportion of height represents a military and ethnic advantage. It is not surprising that in the United States the descendants of the English are the ones who display the best physical qualities, since being the first to arrive they have taken advantage of the increase in North American wealth to create for themselves conditions of life that allow them to cultivate their [p. 105] bodies through sport and above all by freeing those bodies from the burden of excessive physical labor. If for several generations they have been the dominators and have lived in better material conditions than the other races, it is evident that the mark of this good treatment must manifest itself outwardly, just as it does in a breed of fine horses. The new Polish, Italian, and other immigrants have had neither the time nor the occasion to attend to their pedigree, occupied as they are with their jobs as laborers. We see in this that physical differences are less a result of ethnic origin than of the social conditions in which a race lives. In Mexico it is observed that the indigenous races that remain semi-independent and live by hunting and farming but with the character of small landowners are tall and handsome of feature, while the servile population of the plateau, after three or four centuries of poverty, has decayed physically and morally in the most lamentable manner. Beauty and property — at least small property — go together; which is why we condemned socialists want everyone to be a property owner so that everyone may be beautiful. The human root is the same; the thickness of the trunk depends on cultivation. What does change, admittedly, is the prejudice of beauty. If the Blacks were the dominators, ebony color and full lips would be the delight of all those occasional aesthetics that are, however, our enchantment and our torment. My sorrow is not to have been able to be the lover of Karsavina, the Russian dancer; when I call life to account, that will be my first claim; but this does not blind me to the point of preventing me from conceiving the possibility of a great Black aesthetic.

The contemporary social movement, according to Grant's French preface writer, is nothing other than "a rebellion against the civilized, carried out by the parasites of the well-being created by the civilized." Yet it is not the powerful of the era who push a civilization forward. In the vital sense, the powerful tend to be the parasites, for they owe their power to circumstances and to vices of social organization and live off the labor and initiative of the modest and the humble. In every people there is an alert minority of mixed, blended race and varied social condition, and it is this minority that drives progress with the sweat of those below and in spite of the stupidity and greed of the parasites above — pure-blooded or upstart alike: a worldly aristocracy that has never produced genius or determination either for art or for science, and in the present age not even for war.

[p. 106] Grant's book is, despite everything, interesting, because it exposes the antithesis of the doctrine I formulate as the gospel of Latin America. Since the outbreak of the European war, Latin America, faithful to a biotic instinct, remained for the most part neutral. Many of us said: it is not our war. That war of imperialist greed was to decide whether it would be England or Germany that would continue the politics of contemporary capitalism over the world. What was important to us was that both rival nations and their conscious or unconscious allies would destroy or weaken each other, because that weakening meant for us an extension of the period before we would be attacked and invaded. Unfortunately, we did not know how to take advantage of the conflict to have created by surprise, for example, the Ibero-American Zollverein. The Europeanization of our mentalities kept us attentive to the development of the battles and we spent the five years of the European war gaping at the maps of Europe or earning a few pennies on the sale of raw materials: but the great occasion for the assertion of our identity was allowed to pass.

And with folded arms we watched as Italy and France sacrificed torrents of blood in the struggle only for England to finish consolidating its Empire. In any case, however, the war was not our war — because if England had lost, it would have been Germany that led the imperialism and the pride of the blond white who has set out to dominate the earth. Unfortunately, the war wore down English imperialism and German imperialism; but it left standing, increased and more formidable than ever, the North American power, which today dictates its mandates to almost all of Latin Europe. In this way, the enormous imperialist power grows and swells, and yet there are still in Latin America certain colonial souls who experience enthusiasm for the victories of the natural enemy of our freedoms. For the little white blood we have, we think as if we were an outpost of imperialism among the Indians, rather than the root of a cultural period that must revise all values — the values of the white and the values of the Black or the Asian. This spiritual servility is what leads me to disown ourselves and to say once and for all that we are not white. This declaration scandalizes especially the semi-white folks who wander about Europe affecting Parisianism. Let them hide behind the door of any of the luxury hotels they usually frequent at the expense of the sweat of indigenous slaves and they will see how they are treated — they, the lords of the Indians — by any of these [p. 107] citoyens de France; they will immediately hear themselves called métèques. The white Argentine is a métèque just the same as the dark Mexican or the Dominican or the Cuban. And here at least we have a name; in England they do not even bother to name us.

The personal consideration that the European sometimes shows us here is human and welcome, but it should not cause us to lose sight of the fundamental trait, which is as follows: that we belong to a semi-independent colonial population threatened by the white imperialisms of Europe and the United States, and that our salvation lies in the integration of new types of culture through the mestizaje of all the races that inhabit our section of the continent. Let us note the following in capital letters: THAT OUR GREATEST HOPE OF SALVATION LIES IN THE FACT THAT WE ARE NOT A PURE RACE, BUT A MESTIZAJE, A BRIDGE OF FUTURE RACES, A CLUSTER OF RACES IN FORMATION — A CLUSTER THAT CAN CREATE A LINEAGE MORE POWERFUL THAN THOSE THAT PROCEED FROM A SINGLE STOCK. By virtue of being mestizos of blood and culture, we represent something new in the world and have a right to the future. If we were not mestizos, what would we be but a diminished, decayed Spain — diminished in culture?

Grant affirms that there are permanent ethnic traits transmissible by heredity. This affirmation is completely arbitrary — as arbitrary and discredited as the old theory of the blue blood of aristocracies whose only merit was having grown old in the servility of Courts. In France the pimps of the Louises called themselves counts and marquises. In Spain there still exists something resembling the Camerlengo, whose ancient function was to remove the King's chamber pot. Another title proceeds from the very illustrious service of putting on and taking off His Majesty's shoes. But since the invention of the electric bell and the water closet, all the Chamberlain nobility has been left without employment. There would be greater reason to grant a title to the descendants of the inventor of the siphon toilet, because his ingenuity has freed a good part of humanity from a nuisance that was formerly more embarrassing. The democratic nobility we could organize with the authors of all the details of modern plumbing would be worth without doubt far more than all those dirty denizens of Versailles, who knew no more of bathing than the impression suffered on some night when they fell drunk into the fountains of a park that, along with its Palace, has been the cradle of an entire school of worldwide pretentiousness.

[p. 108] Madison Grant again cites Gobineau — that drawing-room conquistador who paraded his diplomatic spleen through the world without having dared to brave so much as a good tropical sunburn. No; Madison Grant again says that throughout history one observes the preponderance of certain dominant and conquering races. There is no doubt that in each era there is a dominant group, a dominant race; but each one fulfills its task and departs or is transformed. There is no hereditary succession of faculties of dominion. There is no single privileged superior caste. All castes have known eras of power and eras of abjection. What abases or exalts peoples is the state of the soul at each instant of the struggle.

As we have already repeated in the present work and in The Cosmic Race, the theory that it is the physical environment that forges racial characteristics seems far more accurate than all other theories. Fortunately, modern life, by diminishing the value of the environment or at least by varying that influence, leads us to the creation of a more complete type of man: the man of all directions; the man who is no longer planetary, I will say, but universal — the cosmic man.

[p. 109]

It is scarcely worth commenting on some other assertions of Grant. He says, for example, that democracy tends toward the leveling of the human type and the diminution of the influence of genius. The same can be said of every tyranny and every legal aristocracy or State aristocracy. The tyrant or the ruling caste corrupts itself in power and maintains over the mass a domination that prevents all individual or collective development. But history tells us that all the brilliant eras of humanity correspond to the existence of political regimes that partake of the idea of democracy — which is the free manifestation of individual genius and the government of the best by delegation from the majorities. Bad as the system of selection is, all other known systems of government are still worse. Let the examples of relative democracy speak to this: the Athenian Republic, the egalitarian and free India of the Asokas, and the Italian Republics that produced Dante and all of modern Art. The United States themselves were creative and great when they lived as a democracy, and are today a threat to the world and a scourge of their own population, because they are managed by the aristocracy of financiers — the priesthood of Mammon — which, if not destroyed from within, will drag into decadence and ruin the entire great empire that has been built by the effort of masses that were free and the genius of a few sincerely and passionately liberal democrats.

What truly preoccupies the cause of North American privilege holders is the rough competition in which they are often bested by the greater talent and greater capacity of immigrants. Indeed, Gobineau's and Madison Grant's providential blonds cannot always prevail in a free contest against the Jew, who displaces them from commerce and banking; nor against the Irishman, who surpasses them in literature; nor against the Italian, who easily dazzles them in the decorative manual arts; nor even against the Black man, whose music and dances impose themselves upon the very austere sentimentality of those who rule by divine right, descended from New England. Fortunately, what alarms the privilege holders who are an obstacle to progress on the contrary confirms that the human task requires the collaboration of all races and all beings. Thanks to this irrepressible ferment of peoples over the inexhaustible variety of the earth, we may trust that life will not become throughout the entire world that kind of Presbyterian Sunday into which we would fall if the modern providential ones were to impose their norm on the whole planet. Not only will they not dominate the planet — they will not even prevail in the United States. For the struggle of ideas must be carried there, on their own vigorous terrain.

Before concluding these observations, I must affirm that I fully agree with Grant when he censures marriages between inferior types and degenerates. I have vigorously propagated among the masses of my country the gospel of the limitation of births. Oppressed races suffer the scourge of fertility; but the curse turns against the oppressors and ends by suffocating them. The double evil is remedied, however, only by raising the social level of the people. This is not a question of race either, but of economic situation and education. We of the North and the South agree, however, when we say that America wants quality rather than quantity. All of Europe's palpable decadence proceeds from its multiplication; a country excessively populated is a condemned country — it matters not that chemistry extracts food from air: what matters is that each man enjoy a house and a meadow. The moment people crowd into cities and are lodged one on top of another in floors, that is already decadence. May the broad terrain of America never be populated to its borders, like these poor degenerate continents. We demand that life there be served so abundantly with the wing that the right to space be as secure as the right to food.

But today [p. 110] the conflict of the two Americas subsists. The North wants the future in the service of the pure-blooded whites. The South is preparing the outlines of a mestizo civilization in which all lineages and all cultures will merge and transcend themselves. On whose side will humanity stand?
