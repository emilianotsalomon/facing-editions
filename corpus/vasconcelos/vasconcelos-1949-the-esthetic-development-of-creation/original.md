## The Esthetic Development of Creation

José Vasconcelos — 1949

Translated by Eliseo Vivas

*[Note on the text: *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, vol. 9, no. 3, "Second Inter-American Congress of Philosophy" (March 1949), pp. 463–468.]*

All that is life in the world is constructed esthetically, artistically. The first constructive element of living activity is rhythm. In man, the most elementary form of rhythm is that of the march, which is developed by counting the steps one, two. In the march, one and two do not yield us three but give us again a composite one, the step. Nor is it possible to suppress the two in favor of the one, or vice versa, because the one corresponds to the left foot and two to the right foot, and the left and right elements are heterogeneous. This heterogeneity is the very condition of the efficacy of the march. Should we equate the one and the two of the march, we would get a mechanical robot; and the reason is that where there is life there is need of heterogeneity. In order that there be rhythm, there is also need of heterogeneity of its integrating elements. It becomes clear that the nature of rhythm is different from the nature of abstraction or of summation or of logic, when we reflect more carefully on the mathematical condition already pointed out, that in a step one plus two do not become three, but one. In the case of a horse, four moving hooves produce a single step, a jump, not five. Thus we find ourselves before a form of activity which at the same time is a form of mental synthesis. There are cases in which four and one do not add up to five. And thus in the whole chain of life we shall meet with the downfall of mathematical automatism. We have already seen how the atom functions by virtue of the electrical heterogeneity of the electron and the proton. In chemistry, the elements must be disparate in order to produce new bodies — for instance, the H₂O of water. The mere sum of elements in chemistry is neutral and does not produce creative activity. It is necessary to have a combination that cannot be added up of two particles of one body and a particle of another, totally different, in order to get an effect. In the domain of biology, the non-mathematical but rhythmic law of the combination of elements is even more evident. In the differentiation of male and female, which is indispensable for creation, and in the most successful effects of this procreation, as for instance in the human body, the exigency of the heterogeneity manifests itself in a manner that could give rise to a cosmic law of the left and the right, copies of the like and unlike of electrochemical combinations. The human body would not be able to function without a rhythm of left and right that expresses itself not only through the feet and hands but also in the eyes, in the sides of the face, in the vertical halves of the body [p. 464] and perhaps even in horizontal halves. If then the whole creation is constituted in this a-mathematical and anti-geometric way, how is it possible to explain it by means of ideas derived from geometry or logic?

It should be clear that the method of the common denominator, applicable to arithmetic and to the syllogism — for in the syllogism the middle term functions as a factor making towards homogeneity and as a common denominator — is totally inadequate even to keep pace with the natural development of the living world of which we are a part.

On the contrary, a mental development that should have rhythm as basis carries us inevitably to the discovery of a series of elements, each of which is more complex but each of which is more representative. Thus, for example, from rhythm we pass naturally to melody. And melody conveys the result of putting together the disparate and disharmonious elements that have filled the world of beings. Melody relates these elements in a creative scale which holds them together, binding them by their affinities. And this is the opposite of the simplistic unification of the common denominator of the mathematicians. We therefore maintain that the esthetic criterion which consists in coordinating the disparate is more appropriate for philosophy and lies closer to living reality than the criterion of the logician. The colors of the spectrum develop in a series of elements which vibrate and are individualized. Nevertheless they are so intimately bound in their existence that the return of white light is enough to cause all of them to be dispersed and fused again in the white light, without endangering their ability to reappear when a prism or dew creates anew the rainbow. Musical notes, in analogy to the development of color, lend themselves nevertheless to irregular and complicated developments in which the human will intervenes with greater freedom than in color. In the development of the species there is no doubt present a certain melody, a serial rhythm, that man modifies when he intervenes in the development of plant through cultivation. And education is nothing more than the attempt to intervene in the conformation and variation of the extremely complicated series of the appearance of souls. In the laws of heredity, in the very act of procreation, in all the developments of existence, a penetrating mind discovers this melody and music of creation. Art is nothing else than the effort to discover this music.

And among the multitude of melodies the mind experiences the need to establish an equilibrium, of discovering a sense. Plato, who in all domains was the precursor and is the father of idealists, can also be claimed by us as our source, since he strove to define harmony and managed to point to it as one of the elements indispensable to the explanation of the cosmos. Modern music resolves the problem of harmony by means of counterpoint, which we have characterized as the esthetic syllogism, that is, a means of creating unity through affinity, a way of constructing a togetherness without making the parts homogeneous, a togetherness which is functional in that all the voices maintain their individualization and nevertheless form a harmony and produce a message.

[p. 465] From the foregoing reflections we have derived the thesis of the esthetic a priori, which consists in maintaining that just as the mind has categories of space-time, of genus and species, it is also endowed with special forms of understanding applicable to the esthetic phenomenon, forms which clarify beauty, and these forms are rhythm, melody, and harmony.

But we must go farther and maintain that the forms of rhythm, melody, and harmony are ordering forms, constructing forms; but they do not constitute an end in itself, just as beauty is not an end in itself either.

Plato himself distinguished Eros or love from beauty. Harmony is the necessary proportion of beauty, but its tendency is not in itself but in the fact that it develops as love. To whom is this love addressed?

Ancient philosophy was not able to conceive love except as related to the instinct that joins couples in the biological world. But the immense order of creation does not depend on the functioning of couplings. Only the biological order is thus subordinated, but God knows many other ways of creating. Creation obeys a genetic process, as the Bible guessed it; but this genetic process is double only in the creature, and in the creature of biological type. Whatever exists is the work of the divine will, but that will operates in distinct forms: the mineral grows by molecular aggregation in accordance with the process of crystallization. A certain harmony presides over these processes at the same time as a peculiar action which in chemistry is called catalysis. Sexual love also obeys equilibria and affinities which are comparable to a chemical catalysis, although they are more complex and more elastic. In the domain of Spirit, the conditions of harmony necessary for cooperative friendship require a type of superior harmony, although in essence identical with the harmony of the mineral or the animal. Thus do we arrive at the proof of the fundamental truth which Dante expressed when he asserted that one and the same love moves souls and stars. In this elevated sense it is legitimate to affirm that love is the fundamental and highest law of creation. Without love that maintains united and structuralized the monads in accordance with the will of the Heavenly Father, creation would tumble down.

What I believe to be original in my contribution to philosophy is the conception of the esthetic a priori, which for the law of gravitation substitutes the law of the affinity of love; and this law is necessary for the integration of the heterogeneous elements that make up bodies, as they make up the peace of the soul. It is here a question of a law of proportional arrangements and, in short, of harmony which has nothing to do with abstract [p. 466] reason and conceptualization. But it is not for that reason arbitrary and confused. On the contrary, it becomes clear in its functioning when we notice that all creative processes, in order to be fruitful, must follow in the ways of rhythm, melody, and of harmony, such as are revealed in music.

If we push the thesis of the a priori to its natural conclusion we resolve all at once the problem which idealism found insoluble during two thousand years: that is, the problem of the unity by agreement of heterogeneous elements in constructive action.

The great concert generates a music which Pythagoras suspected came from the turning of the stars, but that cannot be heard because it is silence, that is, consummate harmony, synthesis of sonorities that are enclosed in silence and in it become hushed and realize themselves in the same manner that colors are fused in white light, in search of repose and plenitude. It is of course clear that in the natural order these rests, white light and silence, are only provisional, since the plenitude of being can only be achieved in the Creator.

### Philosophy of Coordination

I have been concerned with the problem of the unification of heterogeneous elements for many years, and I first took it up in my *Aesthetics*, published in 1937. I resolved it then by means of what I called the esthetic a priori and its elements, which are rhythm, melody, and harmony, showing counterpoint to be a model of the unification of the aggregates, not through the abstraction of parts, but through the agreement which produces new significant and expressive entities.

Following this same road I came upon the thesis contained in my *Lógica Orgánica*, published only three years ago, in which I affirm that to think, for modern man, no longer consists in the reduction of the particular to the general but in the coordination of aggregates. Commenting on this thesis, one of my readers extended it to my whole system, which he called a philosophy of coordination. In respect to method I believe the designation is quite exact, and, in view of the importance of method for systems of thought, I thought that there was no objection to accepting the name, which applies to the method, as an extensive designation applicable to the whole system.

Briefly I shall remind you of the thesis as regards the logic of thought: when we say "man" we do not perform the classical syllogistic operation, man, particular case of the genus humanity; we do not refer the individual to his genus, but we think of man as a living rational body, or man as a member of society, or man as a being with a destiny, as soul, or as man, which is to say, as biological composite whose characters will be given to us by physics and chemistry, etc. In short, about man we see drawn a series [p. 467] of circles or zones of reality, each subject to its own laws, the laws of physics, of chemistry, of logic, of morality, of literature. Thus viewed, thinking operates by binding together areas of experience, aggregates of knowledge: that of physics, of chemistry. Taken separately, each one of these aggregates is made homogeneous by the laws of its activity; taken together they are heterogeneous; they cannot be reduced one to the other, we cannot unite them by means of simplifications such as those of arithmetic or of abstraction. Mentally we unify them, not by means of ontic or logic relations, but by means of the connections which they reveal as existing in fact among themselves, namely, physical existence can only be explained by means of the laws of physics, of chemistry. In turn biology obeys diverse laws, although these are not independent of the physico-chemical laws. Next we judge man in terms of the laws of the spirit, such as those of logic, of poetry, and of religion. All of this obliges us to recognize a Whole, that is not chaos but strict order, not a logical or abstract order, but an existential order of harmony and proportion such as that of colors or sounds, but immensely more varied. In the last analysis it is Eros who realizes the unification by predetermined affinities and bonds. Thus emerges unification through affinity, and in the last analysis through coherence and coordination, in respect to a maximum factor which cannot be other than the Creator of the vast universe. We thus achieve a unification which leaves the individuality, the personality of each of the parts of the aggregate intact, even if it is a question of so vast an aggregate as the Universe, a unification which necessitates the postulation of an axis or maximum personality that rules the aggregate or aggregates.

### Unity and Truth

The problem of unity is closely related to the problem of truth, but it is not identical with the latter. Truth presupposes diverse manners of unification.

Philosophy began unifying by elimination. It was the simplest way. All is matter, Thales and Epicurus said; all is air, said Anaximenes. All is fire, said Heraclitus. And then the Eleatics, struck by the fixity of ideas and their immutability, began the idealistic tradition which, reduced to system by the Platonists, ended up in caricature with Hegel, Husserl, and the Neokantians. By reducing the many to the simple unity of number and idea, mathematics has made progress, since it reduces the heterogeneous to the homogeneous in order to handle it in an obvious form. But this retarded and sterilized philosophy, and when I turned back more than two thousand years to the origin of philosophy in Greece, I found Empedocles, the first man to philosophize, not through elimination and subtraction, but by coordination and synthesis.

[p. 468] Do not try to reduce quality, was Empedocles's advice. And in accordance with this principle he rejected the falsifications of reality which is all that those systems amount to that reduce everything to sensation, idea, or number. With great insight, Empedocles asserted that the essence of things is not to be found in air, or earth, or fire, or water, but in the combination of these four elements. The four elements in cycles of union and separation, the cosmic periods of love which establishes the unity of the Whole, and the intermediate periods, these give us, according to Empedocles, the unity of concurrence of processes, rhythmic and harmonic unity, which is much closer to the thought of us moderns than all the Pythagorean divagations about tetrahedrons and triads and the simplistic dialectic of Hegelian idealism.

Let us however hasten to remember that we know today that Plato was not the narrow dualist of the Theory of Ideas, but the organized pluralist of the Timaeus, who falls back on seven elements in order to explain existence. According to Whitehead the science of our day goes back to these elements as indispensable in explaining reality, namely, matter, force, idea, receptacle, psyche, eros, and harmony.

Among these seven elements we shall not ask which one establishes unity but coherence. The answer is simple — consciousness.

Plato did not posit the problem of the coherence among the seven factors of creation. But just as he joined in God the three Socratic categories of Truth, Goodness, and Beauty, it is easy to assume that he gave God the task of unifying the dissimilar factors. We do not say "to unify" but "to coordinate." If reality is coordination of elements which are active and structuralized, truth must also be a process of functional coordination, which is live and intelligent, rather than the finding of the coincidence of the mean between two or between several premises. Instead of the obvious although sometimes complicated truth of equality given us by mathematical equations, the philosopher searches for the coordination of the inequalities in which to achieve the higher harmonies of existence.

Consciousness or psyche unifies in this manner in order to create, to live, and to think, but who accomplishes the coherent unification of the Universe? A sensible element like water or air, as the physical philosophers believed? A concept, as was imagined by the Eleatics and later the Idealists? Plato had to forget the theory of ideas in order to point to God as He in Whom are united Truth, Beauty, and the Good, but he could not take the step that consists in transforming the one of Parmenides into the Personal God of Christianity.
