I have in mind a book that would be positively useful if I could write it some day, and if in its execution it came even close to the ideal I conceive. It would be a study of the way in which men think, argue, get things right or go wrong— above all, of the ways in which they go wrong; but in fact: an analysis of the most common confusions, of the paralogisms most frequent in practice, such as they are, not such as they would be if psychological processes could be superimposed on their verbal schemata. Not a Logic, then, but a Psycho- Logic.... Quite simply, a book (which would be, if you like, the second part of any of the ordinary treatises on logic), with many examples, taken not only from science but from everyday life, from daily discussions; intended, not to demonstrate or to apply any systematic doctrine, but solely for the positively practical end that any person whatever, after having read that book, should be somewhat more capable than before of reasoning well, on the one hand, and more capable, on the other, of avoiding some errors or confusions which before he would not have avoided, or would have avoided less easily.

As I conceive it, the book would not even have

[PDF 5]

a systematic composition. More: in reality, I consider it indefinite; or, better, what I conceive is not one book, but a type of books which could be written in indefinite number, because their matter is inexhaustible, and they would always be useful. As for my personal project, it becomes still more complicated for me, because some of the analyses, examples, rules, etc., would belong rather to a didactic work, or one usable by readers of ordinary culture, whereas others, more subtle and profound, would serve for a special public; and I do not quite know whether it would be advisable to write two works: one for students and for the non-specialist public, and another for specialists, or whether the best thing would be to accumulate all the material in a single penetrable work, from which each person would take as much as he could.

Here are some of the headings that might serve to group (without too much strictness) the material of a book of that kind:

Usual paralogisms; their manifestations, their causes; circumstances that must be kept in mind, or mental habits it is advisable to acquire, in order to avoid them (the schematology of Fallacies has been almost completed by the work of the logicians; but not their psychology).

Examples of bad reasonings (taken from reality); their analysis. Many of these bad reasonings could be used didactically, as exercises (at different levels of instruction), the student being set the task of analyzing them.

Logical and psycho-logical study of discussions taken from reality (the same previous observation applies).

## Errors of False Opposition

One of the greatest acquisitions of thought would be achieved when men came to understand—not only to understand, but to feel—that a great part of the theories, opinions, observations, etc., that are treated as opposed, are not so. One of the commonest fallacies, and one on which the greater part of humanity’s thinking labor is spent in pure waste, is the one that consists in taking for contradictory what is not contradictory; in creating false dilemmas, false oppositions. Within that fallacy, the very common one that consists in taking the complementary for the contradictory is no more than a particular case of it, but a case of great practical importance.

Let us begin with some examples, simple, at times even crude, taken, like all the others, from reality, and which will serve to make us understand the nature of the sophism.

From a speech:

“The union between peoples is not formed today by community of language, of religion, and of traditions, but arises from the community of souls in an ideal of progress, of liberty, and of mutual sympathies.”

Here is a paragraph like so many that we read or write

[p. 2]

quite naturally every day, without anything in them, at first sight, catching our attention; it contains, nevertheless (if it is taken literally), a crude fallacy: a fallacy of false opposition. The union between peoples is not formed by community of language, of religion, and of traditions, but arises.... etc. For the one who wrote it, and for the one who reads it off guard, there is opposition between these things: if the union between peoples is formed by community of language, of religion, and of traditions, it will not be formed by the ideals of progress, of liberty, etc., and if it is formed by the ideals of progress and of liberty, as the author affirms, then it will not be formed by community of language, of religion, and of traditions—as if there were opposition. Thus are created the two alternatives of a false dilemma.

In reality, the union of nations is formed, or could be formed, by all those things together, in diverse proportions: all those elements may enter into it, in varying proportions; only some of them may enter into it; but there is no opposition between the ones and the others. It is a bad reasoning.

From another speech:

“Yankee energy, the Yankee soul, is not the work of the Washingtons or Lincolns, but of the Vanderbilts, Morgans, and Rockefellers; Argentine energy, the Argentine soul, is not the work of the Rivadavias, Sarmientos, or Mitres, but of the Lozanos, Pereiras, Oliveras Fages, Cobos, and the other great and noble lords of agriculture.”

Setting aside the concept itself, insofar as it lowers the role of statesmen and politicians to the point of annulling it, and insofar as it makes the action of those more or less millionaire industrialists entirely good; setting aside the concept itself, this but is absolutely paralogistic: it indicates, and makes us think of, an opposition that does not exist. It seems that, for the author, one must choose: either it was the politicians and the men

[p. 3]

of state, or it was the industrialists and the millionaires; if it was the former, it was not the latter; if it was the latter, it was not the former.

In reality, the greatness of peoples may owe a part to politics and a part to industry; the greatness of the United States may owe a part to its politicians and another part to its industrialists and capitalists. The sophism is, as in the previous case, that of false opposition.

Paragraph from an article of literary criticism:

“What is certain, after all, is that the only thing that endures in virile work is not technique, is not style, the word, the genre, the orchestration, the chromos, the geometry, greater richness or simplicity, transparency or mist, and still less the all-too-innocent definitions of their own authors and of the scholastics who explain them, with apostolic gravitude, to posterity; but that which often escapes the net of the word itself and persists against it and in spite of it; it is that familiar fluid which impresses us, that imponderable substance which touches us, making us tremble, as it sympathizes with our own substance; it is that something....”

Let us set aside the concept here too, as in the previous case. It is easy to see that, in the mind of the one who wrote this, the same sophism had formed: What endures of virile work is not... such a thing nor such another, but...; which means that, for him, only one thing makes the work endure: if such a thing can make the work endure, the others cannot have any influence at all.

There is, then, false opposition: the non-contradictory taken for contradictory. In reality (and supposing that all those terms have a more or less clear meaning), technique, and style, and orchestration, and “the chromos,” and geometry, and all the rest, could all have their part in the endurance or in the glory of literary works, without prejudice to “that imponderable substance, that something,” etc., having its part as well.

[p. 4]

From another article of literary criticism:

“The age of sculptural poetry seems definitively dead, the poetry that dared neither weep nor laugh, fearful of disturbing with any abandon whatever the majestic solemnity of its gestures. Je hais le mouvement qui déplace les lignes [I hate the movement that displaces the lines], sang impassive beauty in Baudelaire’s sonnet. And it was a fine display of aesthetic virtuosity and of professional skill to carve a stanza with blows of the chisel in radiant and unblemished marble. But what matters is to infuse a soul into inanimate matter; to fill with light the empty sockets of the statue; to set a tremor upon the glacial and hermetic lips that never knew how to smile or to kiss....”

Between impassive and “majestic” art, on the one hand, and expressive, felt, and passionate art, on the other, the author of this article has felt an opposition: an opposition of such a nature and of such scope that, for him, if he were understood literally, one of those two forms of art would have to exclude the other. And thus the paralogism is produced. It would be legitimate for the author to try to justify the existence of passionate art; it would be legitimate, further, for him to prefer it, if such is his taste, to impassive, Parnassian art; but he tells us: what matters is such a thing (passionate art). So that the other no longer matters; the other is now excluded, is condemned, as if there were opposition between the two forms of art. The sophism has been formed by taking the non-contradictory for contradictory (1).

This observation, which, fine and penetrating as it is, nevertheless implies (if one wishes to present it as an objection) a certain incomprehension of the matter, serves me better than a direct explanation for explaining, in connection with it, something that it is important to note.

In psychological reality (the reality I want to study), any paralogism whatever is not a fixed and permanent thing: it is a changing state.

Thus (as would be seen by meditating on the examples in the text), at times there is paralogism in

From an official’s report on a question of education:

“The teaching of morals in the schools must not form a subject given at a set hour with a scientific method and with an established syllabus, but must inform and vivify all the lessons, taking advantage of every opportunity in which an example presents itself, to set in its true light the beauty and the usefulness of good actions, the ugliness and the harm of bad deeds.”

Exactly the same kind of error. To teach morals in the form recommended here is good: it is excellent. The sophism is not in this praise of one form of moral teaching; but it is in the condemnation of another form of moral teaching which is not incompatible with the first, which is not contradictory to it; on the contrary: which can and should be complementary to it. The best way of teaching morals would be to teach them in both ways: to do the one thing, and to do the other; to teach on the one hand methodically, at fixed hours, etc., and to teach besides at every moment, vivifying the teaching on the basis of concrete facts, etc. But our sophism has caused an exclusion to arise in the mind of the author of this report, and in that of everyone who reads it off guard, an exclusion which, as you see, is in no way purely theoretical, but will be translated into practical effects—and into practical effects of great consequence.

I am going to show you another case in which precisely the same error appears applied to the same example, but less clearly, less grossly: more vaguely.

From the Revue Pédagogique:

“Mr. D...... has not thought it possible, however, to leave us without

the literal expression and not in the thought; at other times there is paralogism in the thought although there is none, properly speaking, in the literal expression; and, what is more: one must not even believe that, even in the same person (in the author, for example, or in one and the same reader), there would be identical states at every moment. From this result extremely complex things: changes, degrees, tendencies to paralogisms, which I have tried to suggest, although a deeper treatment of all this is not possible on the didactic plane.

[p. 6]

information on the teaching of morals. He rightly holds that this teaching (he is referring to French teaching) is too intellectual and does not contribute enough to the cultivation of character. The French seem to him too deeply imbued with the old Socratic idea that virtue can be taught; he makes every reservation about the practical efficacy of such teaching. Example is better than precept. It is worth more to acquire good habits of thought and of action than to possess clear ideas on the principles of morality.”

Literally, perhaps there is no sophism here, or almost none. It is said that example is better than precept: that is true; that it is worth more to acquire good habits of thought and action than to possess clear ideas on the principles of morality: that is true too. But even in the literal wording itself we see the paragraph at certain moments as though shaded, I will say, by the sophism. For example, here: “The French seem to him too deeply imbued with the old Socratic idea that virtue can be taught.” So that the conclusion seems to be drawn that virtue cannot be taught. And moreover, the whole paragraph is written in a sense hostile to the special and express teaching of morals. Why? Because of the same sophism of false opposition: from the fact that the other procedure is excellent, one tends to conclude that this procedure is bad. In reality, they should be two complementary and auxiliary procedures; but the complementary has been taken for the contradictory.

From another report (they are all taken from reality) on another question of education:

“The teaching of Physics must be of an experimental character; only thus could it be educative and profitable. To give lessons in physics expositorily seems to me a contradiction in terms.”

Now then: this is not entirely just. If it were said that experimental teaching is superior to expository teaching, all would be very well: but this truth has been exaggerated, no doubt, and falsified

[p. 7]

by the sophism of false opposition. From the goodness of the experimental procedure, there has been drawn the conclusion of the absolute nullity, the complete inefficacy, the “contradiction in terms,” of the expository procedure. There is exaggeration here, which always results from the same logical process.

From a statement on the importance of the cultivation of flowers in educational institutions:

“On the other hand, do we perhaps think to form or to guide aesthetic feeling with what is imported? Will it not be more logical to draw inspiration from the slenderness of the American jasmine, from the powerful tones of the wild daisy, or from the strange coloring of the mountain lily, than to go seeking the poetic fire in the outlandish corollas of the orchid or in the incomprehensible hieratic seriousness of the lotus? Happy the day when the national heart and mind shall find and seek more in the Aztec pedestal of the Artigas of San José than in the now inexpressive writhings of the Laocoön! Lessing could sing it a hymn; the Europeans may venerate it still, as the repository of their laws and their history; we can no longer grant it anything but the value attributed to a document interpreting an environment, nothing more.”

The false opposition begins on the concrete point with which the report deals, that is: on the cultivation of flowers; and the conclusion seems to be drawn that if it is good to cultivate national flowers, it will be bad to cultivate foreign flowers: a sophism of false opposition. Afterwards, the subject broadens, and it becomes a matter of the whole opposition between American art and European art: the opposition, which is what is felt here. It is true that, literally, at times all that is said is that preference must be given to national art; up to that point, the thought is just; but it becomes false (falsified always by the same sophism) from the moment one tries to exclude foreign art or ancient art, or when they are treated, as here, to a certain extent, disdainfully.

Another example (this one is of a possible paralogism).

[p. 8]

Some time ago a rather interesting polemic took place between two writers, on what had been the true cause of the formation of the South American nationalities. The cause, said one of those writers, lies in the city: for certain reasons, particular cities arose: Buenos Aires, Lima, Montevideo, and each of those cities formed its nation.—No (said the other writer): that is not the true cause; the cause lies in geological reasons. It can be demonstrated geologically why Brazil is independent,      why   Argentina         is    independent;   why   the República Oriental is independent of Brazil and of Argentina....

The two writers made reflections well worthy of attention, and I do not recall that they fell into the sophism; but I cite this discussion, which comes to my memory just now, to show how easily it might have arisen, if, as is so human, that discussion had been posed on the basis of an opposition between the two causes. The logical danger lay in starting, subconsciously, from the principle that the formation of the South American nationalities could have no more than one single cause: if it is the city, it will not be the geological factor; if it is the geological factor, then it will not be the city; and each writer would thus have been inclined (subconsciously: through the special polarization created by the sophism of false opposition) to deny importance to the cause the other was trying to bring into relief. Examining the question in this form is never, then, as profitable as it would be if the false dilemma had not been formed.

Now, I must warn you that, in the majority of practical cases, our sophism is not seen so clearly. In reality, what the fallacy of false opposition does is, above all, to confuse things somewhat,—as it were, to shade questions. Look at some cases, now less easy to perceive.

[p. 9]

It is difficult, for one who is not forewarned, to perceive, for example, the little shadow of the sophism of false opposition that there is in this paragraph:

“All these books (the writer is referring to one by John Lubbock), which teach us the art of being happy, of having willpower, of prolonging life, and other such things, are of no use at all. No one is happy by prescription, nor does anyone convert his slack spirit into a conquering and active will because one day he found in the pages of a book the secret of that transformation. Nevertheless, countless people buy those books and read them with delight and with faith, believing that they are going to be of some use to them, that they will find there the secret of being happy, of being strong, or of living to old age.”

In reality, the truth on this point is the following: the influence of books that give prescriptions for happiness or other analogous things is secondary, and is never worth as much as temperament and as many other causes of happiness. But here one perceives the shading of false opposition: the author has exaggerated. The (false) contradiction that he has subconsciously felt between other causes or grounds of happiness and the reading of books has falsified his thought and has made him assert that the influence of reading books is null: not merely that it is less effective (this is the truth) than other causes, but that it is completely null.

Let us look at a much subtler case still. From an article in the “Revue Philosophique.”

“The sociologists, like the biologists with life, make social good the sole measure of morality, so that their morality is a morality not of struggle, but of solidarity. When it is objected to this that solidarity is a fact rather than a command, a reality rather than an ideal... etc.”

I believe that no one who was not very specially forewarned would notice that this whole paragraph is as though veiled by a shadow of false opposition, both in the ideas attributed to others and in the author’s own thought. First of all, there appear certain “sociologists” who, as the biologists do with life, make social good the sole measure of

[p. 10]

morality; they would be, then, in a paralogistic mental state, in believing that morality can be measured only by one single thing, and that this excludes the others. Then we are told that their morality is a morality, not of struggle, but of solidarity; as if a morality had necessarily and solely to be one of these things; and as if there could not be, as if there should not be, a morality that took everything into account and that was at the same time a morality of solidarity, a morality of struggle, etc. And then, to these sociologists and biologists, the objection is made that solidarity is more a fact than a command, more a reality than an ideal; and although “more” is said, one feels that there is an exclusion there; as is proved by the fact that the objection that follows (and which I do not read, for brevity’s sake) is based on the idea that, if it is a fact, it is not a command, and that, if it is a reality, it is not an ideal. There are three cases of the sophism (quite subtle and difficult to perceive) in six lines.

Look at this passage (it forms part of one of the finest studies, in substance and in form, that have been made of North American sociology):

“The present world is accomplishing one of its secular evolutions, one of its historical ‘epochs.’ Magnus sæculorum nascitur ordo [A great order of the ages is being born]. It would be childish, on the pretext of personal preferences, to fail to recognize what is evident..... Modern humanity was fecundated anew at the end of the last century: during the hundred years of its painful gestation it has wandered over the earth pregnant with the future, uncertain of the hour and the place of its delivery, hesitating between luminous France, profound Germany, mysterious Slavdom, remote and traditional Asia.... Doubt it not: it is here that she has given birth!”

Is it not true that, not being forewarned, and despite already knowing the sophism of false opposition from some gross examples; is it not true that none of you would have guessed it here? Nevertheless, it exists. The author, subconsciously, comes to admit (of course he does not admit it expressly; it is without realizing it) that progress is made by the evolutions or institutions that arise in one nation or in one region

[p. 11]

alone; that if at a given moment what is done in America has importance for progress, then what is done in other countries will not have it. This, set out in this way, is crude and absurd; and yet it is the clarification of the state of mind that the sophism of false opposition has produced in the author of this paragraph. “It is here that she has given birth.” So that progress, or change, at that given moment, is going to come from North America and from nothing but North America: she chose the United States; not “luminous France,” nor “profound Germany,” nor “mysterious Slavdom,” nor “remote and traditional Asia.” In reality, things happen otherwise: what is done in Germany, in France, in the United States, etc., influences progress. Perhaps, at a given moment, what is done in one of these countries may have more influence than what is done in others; but the sophism of false opposition has exaggerated this, has artificially simplified the truth, and has falsified it. And, as a consequence of this unconscious sophism, it is possible that the author may afterwards be led psychologically to attribute perhaps too much importance, more than he would otherwise have attributed, to what happens in that country.

A little shadow of the paralogism, subtler still, is seen in these other paragraphs from the same book:

“Civilization is above all a mental state and a moral superiority. The vulgar may stop short at its material and secondary manifestations; for a man who thinks, this is the question: in what does the difference that exists between a Chinese mandarin and a cultivated European irreducibly reside? It is not in manual skill, nor in the stock of practical notions, nor in the almost equivalent apparatus of material life, but in what the one and the other think and feel.”

The starting point is the principle that the “irreducible superiority” of the European over the Asian must reside in one thing only; then, by means of a false

[p. 12]

opposition, all the other things are excluded and a single one is left.

“The ascending ladder from barbarism to civilization is formed by these parallel uprights: collective intelligence,—branching out into progressive science, into impulsive and original art, into the ever vaster conception of the laws of the world; and morality..... It is by the rung that peoples occupy on that ladder of Jacob, and not by the weight and number of their tools, that they must be classified....”

In these two passages the false opposition ripples. Superiority “is not” in manual skill, “nor” in the almost equivalent apparatus of material life, “but” in what the one and the other think and feel...; in reality, it is in all of that, although it might depend preferentially on the last. And here, when he tells us again: “It is by the rung that peoples occupy on that ladder of Jacob” (that is, by their intelligence and morality) “and not by the weight and number of their tools” (that is, by their industry) “that nations must be classified,” there is something of fallacious exclusion: industry is also an important element in classifying the worth of nations, although it may and should come after the other two. The thought was just; but it is a little forced, a little exaggerated, made a little exclusive by the sophism of false opposition.

A shadow of false opposition, too, when a poet tells us that, this world being

“.... a garden.... in which we all plant

now an action, now an idea, he does things better

who, instead of lettuces, sows lilies and roses”; meaning to tell us by this that the man of letters and the artist are superior to the industrialist or the practical man. Even if it be granted that he is literally right when he tells us that he “does things better,” in reality, psychologically, one sees that a bit of opposition has been created here, that it has been felt that one must choose between poetry and industry, and

[p. 13]

the first is chosen, as if they were opposed; that is why it says: “instead of lettuces,” and the comparison is given, as you feel, a disdainful character.

Harder still would it be, for one not forewarned, to see the effects, the shading, of the sophism of false opposition in this paragraph, which I take from a newspaper clipping: the author, analyzing an article entitled The Second Destruction of Rome (it refers to the ideal Rome: to the “tutelary shadow which she has gone on casting over the civilized peoples, after her death”), arrives at the following conclusions:

“Many believe that the classical heritage of Greece and Rome can never be dispensed with. The truth is that humanism is in profound decline. Always, never, are words that lack meaning over the length of time. Ferrero himself confesses it: ‘A tradition cannot preserve indefinitely its power of attraction over minds. The most beautiful and the greatest things are condemned to perish one day.’

“Rome is dying! The second destruction of Rome, which Ferrero announces, is a fact; but a slow fact, of centuries, like the invasion of the barbarians. The barbarian who now destroys Latinism and Hellenism is much stronger than those whom Brennus led; he is invincible. It is time, which wears down and lays low the hardest and most durable things.

“This extinction of Roman prestige, or rather, of the prestige of classical antiquity, is natural. The fact is that the new European civilization feels strong enough to walk alone, without need of classical leading strings. It is understandable that in the medieval night Greece should remain, Rome should remain, like a distant beacon; it is explicable that when the Renaissance came, when the ancient beauty and wisdom were discovered, buried beneath the grime and dust of the barbarous centuries, they, those resurrected goddesses, should be the guides and teachers of minds. But all tutelage comes to an end. A new art has been created. A new Economics has turned the world upside down. The two great heritages of Rome, Civil Law and Letters, disciples of the Hellenic, are cracking; life no longer fits within them. Yes: as Ferrero fears, we are witnessing the second destruction of Rome. The spiritual Rome which is crumbling stone by stone, like the aqueducts, like the amphitheaters, like the triumphal arches of Rome’s material grandeur.”

[p. 14]

Well, then: look at the false opposition. The author tells us, and so far he is right, that classical civilization has ceased, or tends to cease, to have a directing character over contemporary civilization; that contemporary civilization is no longer dominated by the ancient one; but then he vaguely creates a false opposition, in this form: either it subsists, or it does not subsist: if it has no directing character, it must die; and that is how these other phrases are born: Rome is dying, and the very title of the piece, The Death of Rome; The Second Destruction of Rome, which Ferrero announces; The extinction of the prestige of classical antiquity; Rome crumbling stone by stone, etc. All this literature (which may be something more than literature: which may be translated into a whole theory of scientific appearance) is born of a false opposition: “if it is not directing, it must be destroyed”; there is no middle course: a false dilemma.

Naturally, if one is not prepared and well practiced in perceiving the fallacy as though by a kind of instinct, anyone lets himself be carried away by reasonings of this kind, and falls into it.... Anyone, I say: I am explaining it here; it is a kind of obsession for me, and I have something like a special instinct, formed by practice, for discovering it everywhere: meanwhile, the following has just happened to me:

Correcting the proofs of one of my books, I come upon this sentence:

“Meanwhile, social changes are not made by argumentation, by theory, but rather men change their state of mind.”

It was a false opposition: “if social changes are made because men change their state of mind, they are not made by argumentation, by theory...”; it completely excluded argumentation and theory as causes of change. So, on correcting the proofs, I put “mainly”: “social changes

[p. 15]

are not made mainly by argumentation,” and the sophism was corrected. Look now at this summary, made in the Revue Philosophique, of an article from another review (Experimental and critical contributions to the psychology of reading, with an exposition of brief duration):

“In the debate between Erdman and Dodge on the one hand, Wundt and Zeitler on the other, Becker places himself at the point of view of the first two. He defends it, above all, by showing that the reading of a very long word (of some twenty letters) does not imply a

“The principal role in reading belongs, as Erdman and Dodge have shown, and as Becker’s new experiments confirm, to the overall form of words. It is true that the dominant and characteristic letters.... are important; but only because they contribute to determining the overall form.”

And then the author of this summary concludes thus:

“In sum: the question remains disputed of knowing whether the word is read as a whole or whether it is read by successive parts.”

What was being discussed among these psychologists was the following (a point that may be of considerable interest for pedagogy): How does one read? how do we read, and how does the child read?: by reading the word globally, as a whole, or else by reading it letter by letter?—Two theories have been formed (those spoken of here): on one side are such psychologists, on the other side are such others; and the starting point has been— or at least the author of this summary starts—from the principle that one of those two ways of reading must be the true one: that is why he says here “either the word is read as a whole, or it is read by successive parts”; whereas there could be intermediate ways of reading, partially successive and partially simultaneous. Now, see how the mental process of false opposition can, if not render a discussion useless, retard it and enormously complicate its solution. It is clear that if one starts from the principle that the word can be read in only one of these two ways, absolutely, exclusively, and completely, and if the truth is that it is read in an intermediate manner, the psychologists of the one party and the other will be continually finding facts favorable to their own doctrine and contrary to the other, and they will never come to an understanding; whereas it is possible that they would come to an understanding at once if they admitted as possible these intermediary processes that partake somewhat of one way of reading and somewhat of the other.

[p. 17]

We have studied, with what are by now very numerous examples, the fallacy of false opposition, consisting in taking for contradictory what is not contradictory, and within which there figures, as a special and most important case, that of taking for contradictory what is complementary. In our examples, we have attended preferentially to the mental process of the confusion, and to its logical aspect. Now we are going to dwell rather on the effects of this confusion, which is what has the greatest practical importance.

Do not think that all I have tried to teach is something theoretical and without application; something of purely speculative interest, or of psychological or logical curiosity. In reality, in practical discussions, in action itself, that paralogism is translated into most pernicious effects. We continually encounter it in our ordinary discussions; and, for example, it is usually inhibitory. I am going to cite you a case taken from reality.

One day, in the University Council, I had presented a proposal to make obligatory in the Bachillerato readings in books properly so called: those readings that establish contact with the great minds and that have so much educative influence. I proposed in my project that the University should acquire a sufficient number of copies of certain specially chosen works, which were designated there: those works would be lent by the University to the students. So that this educational institution could attain the end that the students who passed through it and obtained a degree in it should not only have taken certain regulation subjects, and passed examinations in them, but should also have read certain books (it is not the same as reading textbooks), whereby a broader culture would be fostered.

When I set out my project, all my colleagues found excellent the end I had in view; but

[p. 18]

a discussion immediately arose in this form: “The end that Doctor Vaz Ferreira proposes—said one—is very good; but the means he advises for attaining it is not the one that should be adopted; the true procedure for elevating minds, for raising the level of education, would not be the one he advises, that of reading in books lent by the University, but that of organizing lectures given at the University by its own professors.” Another colleague asks for the floor and enters into discussion with the previous one and with me: “I admit too—he says—that the result pursued is excellent; but the procedure for attaining it”.... note this well, for this is where the whole sophism lies.... “the procedure for attaining it is neither the one Doctor Vaz Ferreira indicates nor the one our other colleague indicates, but would consist in the professors doing those readings in their own classes; each professor, apart from his textbooks, ought to do readings in class....” And so several members of the Council went on proposing procedures, each one of which, for the one proposing it, was the procedure, the only one that ought to be adopted.

Absolutely nothing could be done!

I strove to make plain to them that there was no contradiction between the procedures advised; that all of them were good, some in a greater degree, others in a lesser degree; that all tended to the same end, and that, instead of being contradictory, they were complementary. The ideal, then, was to admit them all. Now, if there were difficulties (as there were) in carrying out one of them, that was no reason to abandon those that could be applied. Nevertheless, so great is the habit, even in the most cultivated persons, of arguing by false opposition, that the project could not prosper.

I could cite you—and I do not do so simply for

[p. 19]

brevity’s sake—a great many other examples of daily, ordinary discussions, of discussions taken from life, in which false opposition produces this inhibitory effect: it prevents good and effective practical action.

Besides, there are truly whole branches of knowledge, or whole orders of human activity and thought, which, in their present aspect, are almost integrally nothing more than a sophism of false opposition. Pedagogy, for instance, may be said to have been up to now almost entirely an example of this sophism. So too the discussions, the theories, and the ordinary state of mind of men with regard to art are the greatest illustration of the sophism of false opposition or false dilemma.

You will remember that precisely some of the examples we have already studied were taken from Pedagogy or from artistic theories or criticism. Let us now look at other cases.

Let us begin with Pedagogy. Take Spencer’s well-known work. In its first part this question is posed: “What knowledge is of most worth?” And the author resolves it in the sense that the most useful knowledge is scientific knowledge. It is, no doubt, a tenable opinion; but in that book there floats, tacit, and at times condensed, express, a continual opposition between science on the one hand, and on the other art and languages. It is not simply a matter of that legitimate opposition that may result from lack of time to embrace everything: it is clear that, time being limited, one cannot teach science indefinitely, nor art indefinitely, nor languages indefinitely, and, consequently, the increase of one of these branches of instruction must be achieved at the expense of the others. It is not a matter, then, of this real opposition, imposed by circumstances, but of another, false, paralogistic opposition, which leads the author to diminish the value of those other branches of knowledge which are not the most useful according to him.

[p. 20]

This is better understood by reading the respective passages. Look at these two:

“When a father who has acted on false principles, adopted without examination, has deprived himself of the affection of his children, has driven them by his severity to revolt, to moral ruin, and has brought about his own misfortune, he might, it seems, make this reflection: that the study of Ethology (1) would have been worth more to him than Aeschylus. When a mother weeps for her first child, who has succumbed in consequence of scarlet fever, and a sincere physician tells her what she already suspects, that her child would have recovered if his constitution had not been weakened beforehand by the abuse of study; when she is crushed by the double weight of grief and remorse, it is a very feeble consolation to her to be able to read Dante in the original.”

One already feels here that the defense of science has carried the author beyond the just degree: it has carried him, not merely to assert, as he does at times literally, that science is worth more than art, but to feel toward art and toward languages a certain hostility.

Indeed: that hostility, and the false opposition that gives rise to it, are seen quite clearly in this other passage, with which the first part of the book concludes:

“Paraphrasing a fable come from the East, we shall say that, in the family of studies, science is the Cinderella who hides unknown perfections in obscurity. All the work of the house has been entrusted to her; by her dexterity, her intelligence, her devotion, all the comforts and all the pleasures of life have been obtained, and while she is ceaselessly occupied in serving the others, she is kept apart, so that her proud sisters may flaunt their tinsel before the eyes of the world. The parallel could be carried further, for we are soon coming to the dénouement, and then the situations would be reversed: the proud sisters will fall into a deserved neglect, while Science, proclaimed the best and the most beautiful, will reign supreme.”

Here we have false opposition in its fullness. Science, art, languages, are complementary: there is no contradiction between these branches of human activity; but Spencer has been led by the sophism to establish a false opposition, and, referring, for

Now then: all this is not theoretical. Those who write are pedagogists; those who read may be teachers or legislators, who will be led to steer education, at times even that of a whole nation, in that absolute and narrow direction.

The history of pedagogical procedures, of their vogue, of their falling out of use, of the discussions about them, is, in ninety per cent of cases, nothing but a history of this sophism. The pedagogists come to the conclusion that it is good and advisable to have the child be the one who discovers what one wishes to teach him; they immediately conclude that the other procedure, the natural one, which consists in the teacher properly teaching the child, is bad. Thus a good procedure is applied, but another procedure that was also good is completely banished. There was no incompatibility between the two: they were complementary; but because they had been taken for contradictory, one was excluded; and though something was gained on one side, something was lost on the other.

The pedagogists or the teachers discover or observe that it is good to teach without books, that teaching by word of mouth is good, useful, advisable; immediately it is concluded, consciously or unconsciously, that teaching with books is bad, and the book is banished; and a whole immense and long labor of practice and good sense is necessary in order to arrive afterwards at that so natural reconciliation which consists in teaching in certain cases with books and in other cases without books. A false opposition had been established: the complementary taken for the contradictory.

A good procedure for teaching languages is discovered; for example: one observes the goodness of

[p. 22]

oral teaching. But the pedagogists cannot extol the merit of oral teaching, or apply it, without completely suppressing translation and all the other methods of written teaching: “if this is good, that was bad”;—when, in reality, everything was good: everything was cooperating, or should have cooperated.

When an author (1) discovers that the method of reading familiar books, written in the language one wishes to learn, is of service in the learning of languages, he will not confine himself to making the assertion with that scope: unconsciously feeling an opposition between this method and all the others employed until then, he will conclude that all the methods employed up to now for teaching languages have been absurd, wretched; that the only method that is of any use is that one, and that it must be applied in an exclusive manner.

And it even happens that an author of textbooks who discovers the advisability of underlining the verbs in sentences has derived from this an exclusive method, and it has occurred to him that the way to teach languages is to underline the verbs.

The advantages of teaching geography on the map are observed. The observation is sound; the advantages, real. But from this it is concluded that one ought not, for example, to use geography textbooks: a false opposition has already been set up; the complementary has already been taken for the contradictory; already the introduction of a good method has resulted in the baneful consequence of excluding something that, to a certain degree or in certain cases, may be good as well.

And in art, false opposition is omnipresent. Each school discovers, or brings into relief, certain procedures destined to create or to perfect beauty in certain of its manifestations: but the attitude of combat is a universal attitude, and inseparable from them: they all present themselves as adversaries of the earlier schools; which, in their turn, attack the new one.

When the romantics discover all the advantage that can be drawn, for example, from the Middle Ages, they do not do so without attacking the tendency to seek subjects in classical antiquity; when they discover certain procedures of style, they do not fail to present these procedures as bound to supersede, to replace, the classical procedures; and the classics, in turn, will attack romanticism, both sides believing that the two tendencies cannot exist together. The debates about music, and the states of mind about music:

When Wagner sought to condense his doctrine into a clear form, he did so in a most beautiful comparison:

“Let us suppose,” he says, “that we enter a forest: at first we feel only the impression of an ever more solemn silence; then the many and varied sounds of the woods begin to be perceived; they grow, they blend, they mingle, they swell to gigantic size; the whole is an immense symphony in which we may not, perhaps, be able to distinguish the components, but perceive the grandeur of the harmony and the superb melody, in a broad sense, of the whole; but if afterwards, in our house, we try to recall the great melody of the forest, it will be impossible for us; and if we wished to reproduce it, and had for that purpose the singular idea of imprisoning one of the singers of the forest—of imprisoning a bird and shutting it in a cage—we could obtain only an Italian melody.”

Most beautiful, the image; but it ends badly. It is apt and truly exact, in what it has of positive, as the justification of a kind of music that has the right to exist, that ought to exist, that it is good should exist, and that comes to satisfy certain needs of the soul. What is weak and bad is that condemnation of another kind of music that also has the right to exist; a condemnation due, naturally, to false opposition.

[p. 29]

And when the Wagnerian innovations are attacked, it will also be through the same false opposition: “Music cannot be what Wagner advocates, because it has to be what Rossini, Bellini or Donizetti had achieved; if melody is finite, it cannot be infinite; it is not legitimate that there should exist, on the one hand, composers in the Italian manner and, on the other, Wagnerian musicians....”—such is the state of mind of the public, the state of mind of the critics; and this is baneful.

Even the smallest expressions bear the stamp of the dilemma: when one man asks another: “Are you a partisan of Wagner’s music?”, this question, if you analyze it psychologically, means something like this: “Are you in favor of all music being like Wagner’s? of there being no music like Wagner’s?”—Is it not true that, in almost every case, the question means that? Is it not true that only by exception will it mean: “Do you accept Wagner’s music as legitimate?” In that case, the answer will be affirmative, that is evident; but discussions are not posed that way.—“Are you a Wagnerian or a melodist?” And one must choose: so men choose, and, in choosing, they exclude unduly.

The expression “music of the future,” applied to Wagnerian music by the disciples in a laudatory sense and by the adversaries in a satirical or disparaging sense, contains within itself the whole dilemma: “music of the future” means, for the one side and for the other, the only music of the future,—and the one side affirms and the other denies that it should be that sole music. And the practical result of all this is that instead of opening our soul, we close it; owing to these unintelligent sophisms, we close our mind to understanding and to feeling, incapacitating ourselves for the perception of beauty in all its forms, save

The question of style: Analyze, for example, a work like Albalat’s: “L’art d’ecrire.” In its part that I will call affirmative or positive, that is, when it shows that certain forms of style, that certain procedures, that certain ways of writing, are beautiful, that work is good; but go over it and notice how, from beginning to end, it is as though impregnated with the illusion that a certain way of writing is the only good one. “Style”... and the style is the one advocated there: it is the one that resembles that of certain authors, who have a certain kind of style.... And people generally cannot admire certain forms of style without believing that this obliges them to deem the other forms illegitimate or inferior. When one writer compares good style to white light, for its simplicity; when another writer tells us that style ought to be like glass, which, when it is seen, is not good,—they are right insofar as they affirm that this style comparable to white light or to glass, the style of a Renan, of a Voltaire or of a France, is a good style; but they ought not to say the style, or they ought not to suggest that it is a question of a single legitimate one: another style, comparable, if you like, to a stained-glass window, is beautiful too, on condition that the painting is well done; and it is advisable and good that there should be clear panes and also stained-glass windows.

Nor will you be able to study sciences without

[p. 32]

coming upon this hallucinating sophism. Tomorrow you will study social sciences, and you will hear some problem argued; for example: the origin of nationalities: “What is it that truly makes a nationality?”—Race, one school tells us.—No, says another: geographical and geological conditions.—That is not true either, says a third: what makes nationalities is community of religion. False, says a fourth: what makes nationalities is community of laws. And for others it will be community of language, etc. In reality, nationality is a vague something, into whose formation and into whose explanation all those factors enter; in different proportions, no doubt, in general; and in different proportions, also, according to the case. He who believes he must give importance to only one of the factors condemns himself to giving no importance to the rest: he condemns himself inevitably to thinking badly.

You will study the discussion on the origin of religion, and you will find the thinkers divided into two great groups: the animists and the naturists. The animists will explain, for example, the origin of religions, as Taylor and Spencer do, by dreams: by belief in the doubles of dead persons, a belief originating during sleep. And the naturists will explain the origin of religions by the impression produced on primitive men by certain natural phenomena, notably by fire. It will not be common to find writers who consider it possible that both orders of causes may have exerted an influence on the formation of religions.

And notice a most interesting sophism, which is derived from the one we are studying: it is that of rejecting one by one the partial explanations, because none of them, by itself, succeeds in explaining wholly the phenomenon or order of facts that one wishes to explain.

There is discussion, for example, of the origin or the essence of

[p. 33]

nationalities. It is race, says one. And, since it can be proved that it is not solely race, then there is a tendency to exclude completely the factor of race. And, since the theory that seeks to explain nationalities by community of religion is not enough by itself either, there is a tendency to exclude completely the factor of religion. And so the various factors, the various explanations, are excluded one by one, because they are not enough in isolation. It is a common way of thinking badly.

Through this fallacy, then, a good part of the thinking effort of humanity is spent in pure loss. Each novelty generally presents itself against what exists; for each good thing that is established, there are generally several good things that fall into discredit for some time; and a very long and difficult labor, which ought to have been spared, is necessary to set things right again and bring them to their just degree.

When a group of ants carries an object from one place to another, many of them pull in opposite directions; a considerable part of their effort is wasted. The object, no doubt, will end by going where it had to go, owing to the predominance of certain efforts over others; but it is a pity to see the effort uselessly lost. Absolutely this same spectacle would be offered by thinking humanity to anyone who suddenly awoke    completely     from   this   sophism,    as   from    a hallucination.

One last observation on this paralogism. It is one of the causes, perhaps the most important, of those that make the history of the intellectual progress of humanity a sort of rhythm of exaggerations.

Hardly any idea has managed to prevail without

[p. 34]

having first passed through this period of exaggeration. That humanity is the “drunkard on horseback who, if they set him straight on one side, falls off the other,” is something of which the sophism of false opposition is one of the causes. To such a point has thinking by this false opposition of exaggerations become a mental habit of men, to such a point is it something that seems conditioned by some physiological anomaly, by some fold of the brain, that if by exception some observer or thinker presents an observation, an explanation or a theory in its just degree from the beginning, one of these three things happens:

Either it attracts no attention:

It seems that one of the first conditions for a theory to draw attention to itself, to force itself upon discussion, and even to become famous and endure, is that it should present itself exaggerated. If Rousseau had confined himself to making a few just, exact observations on the role that nature ought to have in education; if all the other great exaggerators of the history of thought had presented their doctrines already tempered and well adjusted from the beginning, it is possible that the good done would have been greater; but it is likely that they would not have become so famous: it may even be that today they would be unknown. There is a certain form of glory, not the higher form, no doubt, but still a certain form of glory, that makes one think... of indigestion; it seems as though it cost human thought an effort to digest certain theories, which for that very reason remain so long. Just as an easily digestible bolus of food fulfills its mission without making itself felt, while another, indigestible one stays in the stomach, so too certain theories give me the impression of formidable ideological boluses that have lain undigested in humanity’s stomach. They are those theories that appear in the syllabuses; that are “refuted,” as

[p. 35]

an eternal and permanent classroom exercise, but which always remain, however absurd they may be.

Second case: A writer presents his doctrine or his observations from the beginning in their just degree, with the due reservations and qualifications. Well, these reservations and qualifications are disregarded, and people proceed as if the doctrine had been presented exaggerated; to such a point is humanity accustomed to following that process of false opposition of exaggerations.

To take a practical case, a modest one, but well observed, because it is personal. I have maintained that, since a certain current pedagogical doctrine is false—that all the material presented to the child must be completely adapted to him, digested, artificially prepared—it is advisable in some cases and to a certain degree to present undigested material in order to stimulate the penetration of what is partially intelligible; well, continually, in practice, objections are addressed to me, and those objections have to do, not with what I have really maintained, but are directed against what I would have maintained if I had exaggerated; that is: they go against the doctrine (maintained by no one) that always and in every case penetrable material should be used, and never adapted and prepared material.

The same thing happens when we attack an exaggeration: the adversary attributes the opposite one to us. When in practice we attack, for example, a syllabus for being too extensive, it is inevitable that there should be addressed to us, as an answer, as a supposed refutation, the reasons there are for syllabuses not being too reduced. When we attack, in practice, the tendency of certain persons to eat too much, they immediately answer us with the arguments that are good against the tendency to eat too little. A very human tendency.

Finally, a third case may occur. The public,

[p. 36]

disconcerted by the reservations, by the qualifications that indicate the thinker’s effort to present his theory just and exact, tries to force him to give a simplistic and exaggerated formula: “But, in short, when all is said and done, in the final reckoning, what is your opinion? Make up your mind, decide!”—trying in this way to wrest from him a simplistic and exaggerated formula, on which the usual work will afterwards be carried out.

Questions of Words and Questions of Fact

Among the questions that men discuss, there are some of words and some of fact. There are also, very often, questions which, as they are discussed, are partly of words and partly of fact, in proportions that differ according to the case.

Now then: men have a tendency—and this is a paralogism that it matters a great deal, practically, to analyze—to take questions of words for questions of fact, wholly or partially. It is of the greatest importance, not only from the speculative point of view, but from the practical point of view, in order to reason well and even to act effectively when the occasion arises, to know how to distinguish the two kinds of questions as well as possible. Let us illustrate this with some examples.

Some time ago, two persons who had been carrying on a discussion asked my opinion on it. The question was the following: whether or not an engraver is an artist.

One of the disputants maintained that the engraver is not an artist, and he said: “The true artists are the men of letters, the musicians, the painters, the sculptors; the function of the engraver is too subordinate, too inferior; the engraver is not really an artist.” And the other replied: “I recognize, no doubt, that the art of the engraver is not as difficult or as lofty

[p. 37]

as painting or music; but it is still an art: it shares the same characteristics as the others, although, if you like, to a lesser degree,” etc. And the discussion went on along these lines.

William James, in one of his works, relates that he was named arbiter in a question which, as we shall see in a moment, is of a character analogous to this one. A squirrel goes round the trunk of a tree, and a person also goes round the trunk with the same angular velocity as the squirrel, keeping always on the side opposite to it. The question was this: whether or not the person who goes round under those conditions goes round the squirrel.

Now then; to analyze these questions and know whether they are of fact or of words, we must do the following: ask ourselves whether or not those who are arguing admit the same facts.

For example: do the one who maintains that the engraver is an artist and the one who maintains that the engraver is not an artist differ about what the engraver does? Undoubtedly not. Both admit the same thing about how the engraver works, about what he does and how he does it: entirely the same thing. And they do not even disagree (let us suppose) about the merit there is in doing it. In what do they differ? In knowing whether one who does that ought or ought not to be called an “artist.” This will depend on the signification given to the word artist; it is a question of words: purely of words.

In the case of the squirrel the same thing happens; both admit the same facts, the existence of the spectator, the manner in which the squirrel goes round, the manner in which the spectator circles, the direction of the movement, the angular velocity, etc.; both admit exactly the same facts; they argue over whether that ought, or ought not, to be called “going round”; and the solution of the question depends, as James explains well for this example, on the sense given to the word go round.

[p. 38]

If to go round, for example, means to be first to the North of the squirrel, then to the East of the squirrel, then to the South of the squirrel, then to the West of the squirrel, then the man goes round the squirrel; if to go round means to be first in front of the squirrel, then at its right side, then behind it and then at its left, then the man does not go round it. But the facts are the same; what is argued is whether or not the word go round is applicable in this case, by virtue of the meaning it may have: a question of words.

Now then: it is of great importance to grow accustomed to carrying out, at first consciously, afterwards by a sort of habit, this mental work: when any question whatever is being discussed, to find out whether there is a difference between the facts that are admitted, whether as to the existence of the facts, or as to the manner in which they occur.

Let us take another example. Suppose we hear argued that question to which a good deal of importance is now being given in the teaching of Grammar: whether or not there exist parts of speech. According to Benot and his disciples, there are no parts of speech, since one and the same word, one and the same verbal sign, may appear in different sentences performing a completely different role; there are parts in each sentence, but not, in general, parts of speech. According to the current doctrine, there are parts of speech: There are words that are verbs, without prejudice to their being able at times to perform the office of nouns, of adjectives or any other whatever; there are words that are nouns, without prejudice to their being able to perform the office of adjectives or of interjections.

Now then: let us carry out the same work with regard to this question. What do the one side admit and what do the others admit, as to the facts? Both parties admit that each word habitually

[p. 39]

performs a certain function, and both also admit that in certain cases it may perform others; consequently, there is agreement about the facts. In what do they disagree? In that, according to some, these facts ought to be formulated by saying that there are parts of speech, and, according to others, they ought to be formulated by saying that there are no parts of the sentence, that there are parts in the sentence. One or the other of these verbal formulas will be more practical or more convenient; but it is evident that the question is purely verbal.

Let us go on applying our criterion to other examples. Suppose there is an argument over whether or not a particular artist, Zola, for example, is a genius (a discussion I have witnessed many times). Let us imagine this discussion for a moment, with the arguments that might plausibly be made on one side and the other. What kind of question will it be?

On the one hand, there will undoubtedly be argument about facts. Indeed: critics or readers argue over whether such and such works of Zola, or such and such passages of his works, do or do not produce an impression of beauty, aesthetic pleasure, or over the degree to which they produce it: one will maintain that certain passages of “L’Assommoir” or of “La Terre” produce pleasure; another will maintain that they produce not pleasure but repugnance; another will maintain that they produce repugnance, it is true, but that this repugnance is beneficial, moralizing; another will maintain that this repugnance is harmful. All these elements that enter into that general and necessarily more or less vague discussion as to whether or not Zola is a genius are questions of fact. On the other hand, notice that there is also argument about the meaning of the word genius, which is a very vague meaning. Even supposing two persons who are in complete agreement about the factual part, even supposing, if you like, two admirers of Zola, they may argue over

[p. 40]

whether, given the degree of talent and the kind of talent of that writer, he ought or ought not to be called a genius. This depends on the scope of the word genius; on whether it has a more restricted or a broader sense. The discussion, then, would turn on a mixed question, partly of facts and partly of words; and I need not dwell on considerations to show you the importance there will be, in order to argue well in this case, in distinguishing clearly what is of fact and what is of words; without that distinction, the discussion will not be able to reach a good end.

Another case. I remember that, when I was a student of National History, I was made to argue this question: whether Artigas was the founder or the precursor of our nationality. What kind of question would this be?

Imagine the discussion. Will the adversaries differ as to facts? Very probably, yes. One, for example, will maintain that Artigas had the conscious purpose of making our country independent; and another will maintain that he never had it, or that he did not have it permanently or very intensely. All these questions are questions of fact. But, besides, there are also questions of words. Indeed; let us suppose that the interlocutors come to agree in admitting that Artigas had the deliberate and conscious purpose of making our homeland independent, but that he could not achieve that purpose. One who does that, one who finds himself in those conditions, ought he to be called “founder” or ought he to be called “precursor”? This is another question, a question of words, which, nevertheless, will be formulated in the same way and will form part of the same question. So that those who argue over whether Artigas was founder or precursor are, on the one hand, arguing questions of facts (what ideas Artigas had, what his purposes were, what his feelings were, etc.), and on the

[p. 41]

other hand they are also arguing, mixed in with these, questions of words (what meaning the words founder and precursor have, or what meaning it is advisable to give them). If all this is argued together, the discussion has very little chance of reaching a reasonable end; above all, because the questions of words are taken for questions of facts.

Another case. You know the debate, in psychology, between the partisans of unconscious psychological phenomena and those who denied their existence. What kind of question was this? Undoubtedly, part of the debate turned on facts; we can easily make sure of this by doing the work I have recommended to you, which is like a criterion or test to which all questions should be submitted before undertaking their discussion. What facts did the representatives of one side and the other admit? The former admitted that certain non-conscious phenomena have great value in psychological life; the others denied it. Consequently, it is evident that here there is a question of facts. But this question of facts was complicated by a question of words. If we read the authors, we shall find that, in that debate, both sides at times agreed in admitting certain phenomena of dim, low or inferior consciousness that have an important role in mental life; but they differed over whether these phenomena ought or ought not to be called psychological. The same with regard to non-conscious phenomena, to phenomena that are purely physiological, organic, cerebral, but that influence mental or psychological life directly; such phenomena some call psychological and others do not. The question of unconscious psychological phenomena, as it was debated in psychology, and as it is at times debated even today, was, then, in part, a question of fact, but, in part also, it was a question of words.

[p. 42]

In practice, the exercise I recommend to you is nonetheless very difficult, above all in certain cases, because there are endless difficulties in this regard; there are shades, gradations... Let us take as an example those very common questions as to whether or not a certain epithet can be applied to such and such a person or such and such a fact. Suppose the question of knowing whether or not such and such a woman is an “infanticide.” A child has been found dead in a well, and the question is whether that woman was the mother, whether she put it to death, whether she threw it into the well: if the facts happened so, she is an infanticide; if not, not: What question is this? an evident question of fact.—Now the question is whether Oedipus is a parricide. On what does the discussion turn here? Here the question is not one of fact, as in the preceding case; here it is purely of words: to know whether or not the term parricide ought to be applied to the case of Oedipus.

These are clear cases; but let us now look at another. Brutus, the slayer of Caesar, was he an assassin?

It seems, at first sight, that this too is a question purely of words; indeed: everyone admits what Brutus did, how he acted; it seems that they were arguing solely over whether or not the epithet of assassin can be applied to him. But if we analyze the question well, we notice that there are also, running through the minds of those who argue, doubts, questions and differing opinions as to whether Brutus’s act, and, in general, political assassination, is an act advantageous or disadvantageous to societies, and other analogous questions. So that this is not a question purely of words; also included in the debate, and vaguely fused with the question of words, are questions of fact.

The other Brutus, the one who condemned his sons, was he a filicide?—Again a question predominantly of words: we all admit what Brutus did; we admit the same facts: we argue whether he ought or ought not to be called

[p. 43]

a filicide. But it is evident that a moral question is also involved here; that of knowing whether he acted well or acted badly,—as in the preceding case. So that, with the question of words, there is fused a question that is not purely verbal.

The determination becomes more difficult still if we bear in mind that one and the same way of posing a question, one and the same verbal formula, may be a question of words or of facts, according to how, in what sense and with what intention it is discussed.

We may argue “whether Peter is good” because we do not know what Peter feels or what he does; we may argue “whether Peter is good,” knowing how Peter feels and how he acts, but not knowing whether that kind of acts or of feelings ought or ought not to be described as good. The verbal formulation is the same; the nature of the question or questions argued is, nevertheless, different.

We may argue over whether or not such and such a chemical substance is a metal, because we do not know its properties. We may argue over whether or not it is a metal while knowing its properties, agreeing about them, but doubting whether or not it is advisable, given those properties, to call it a metal,—as happened at a certain period with hydrogen.

Questions of words are extremely common. There are, no doubt, branches of knowledge favorable by their nature to questions of words: Grammar, for example. In those questions of logical or syntactic analysis, when it is argued whether such and such a term belongs to one class or another, whether such and such a clause is incidental or subordinate, etc., very often what is argued is whether that term ought to be called this or that; whether that clause ought to be called incidental or subordinate.

But do not go thinking that it is only in these branches of knowledge that questions of

[p. 44]

words exist: they exist in all of them; and it is precisely in the sciences that deal with facts that they are most dangerous, because it is there that they most easily assume the deceptive appearance of questions of fact.

Examples of questions of words are also commonly furnished by certain questions, extremely common today, about the delimitation of the domain of the sciences: about whether the study of this or that fact belongs to Sociology or to Political Economy or to the Philosophy of Law or to the Philosophy of History.... Generally, those questions, on which modern thought tends to spend too much time and too much labor, are in part, and at times wholly, questions of words.

In our day, for example, at this very moment, it is being debated, on the occasion of the works of a certain contemporary school, whether or not ethics is a science. There are those who maintain that ethics cannot be a science, because ethics does not aim to discover how things happen, but to give rules for acting, and the branches of knowledge that are in that position are not sciences. Others say that they are sciences of a special kind: normative sciences. And we continually find discussions of this sort:

“Thus some philosophers, and in particular Mr. Wundt, have proposed placing ethics among the ‘normative sciences.’ But the question is to know whether those two terms are compatible with each other and whether normative sciences really exist.”

At this point it seems that we are now dealing with a question of facts, since it is asked whether they exist or do not exist; in reality, the existence of moral norms is not called into doubt, but what is argued is whether or not that ought to be called a science.

“Every norm is relative to action, that is, to practice. It is related to knowledge only in an indirect manner, by way of consequence. Empirical, it proceeds from traditions, from beliefs and from representations whose relation to objective reality may be

[p. 45]

more or less remote. Rational, it is founded on exact knowledge of this reality, that is, on science; but it does not follow from this that this science, considered in itself, is normative.”

“To claim that a science is normative, insofar as it is a science, that is, insofar as it is theoretical, is to confuse into one two moments that can only be successive...,” etc. (1)

This is today an obsessive discussion; and yet, with the simple explanations we have given, you are perfectly prepared to understand that (as it is posed in those passages: there are connected questions of which the same could not be said) it is a simple question of words; that the only thing argued here is the meaning of the word science; whether, in the case of ethics, the word “science” can or cannot be used, not because ethics is one way or another—that is not argued —but because the word “science” will or will not have a meaning reconcilable with the meaning of the other word: “normative.” Undoubtedly, if those who argue these questions were aware that they were arguing purely verbal questions, they could employ a good part of their effort more usefully.

The consequence of all this is very simple: it is the advisability of acquiring a habit, a custom. Every time we prepare to argue, to examine or simply to understand a question, to begin by asking ourselves this question: “Does it refer to facts or to words, wholly or partially?” And, to resolve it, let us try—this is what matters—to see what each of the two camps admits in the matter of facts.

The first admit such and such facts, the second admit such and such facts. Are these facts the same, entirely? Then there is no question of fact;

[p. 46]

there remains, simply, a question about the meaning of the term. Are they not the same, wholly or partially? Then there is a question of facts; but let us see further whether there is not, besides, some question of words superimposed on it or confused with it: Even in this second case, our examination will be very useful to us for separating what is of facts and what is of words.

Now, do not be in a hurry to draw from this the conclusion that questions of words are completely sterile, useless or vain. Questions of words can have importance; not as great as that of questions of fact, no doubt, but a positive importance.

Thus, when words have a certain consecrated sense, which corresponds to the definitions adopted, and, what is more important still, to habitual associations, it is preferable to take them in that sense, and not to alter it without a positive advantage. From doing the contrary, serious drawbacks usually result.

I might remind you, as a good illustration, of Guyau’s celebrated debate against Spencer, concerning the aesthetic feelings. Spencer had maintained the doctrine that the aesthetic feelings are disinterested and are separate from vital utility. Guyau, attacking that theory, cites a series of examples that show clearly that the sense Guyau gives to the word aesthetic is completely different from Spencer’s, and also different from the usual one. In his refutation, Guyau cited examples such as that of a smooth, well-kept road that makes the carter exclaim: “What a beautiful road!”; that of the pleasure a thirsty person experiences in drinking a glass of water, and others of the sort. There was here a question of words, resulting from the fact that Guyau used the words “beauty” and “aesthetic feelings”

[p. 47]

in a much broader sense than the current one. But what I wanted to point out to you is this: that, in that question of words between Guyau and Spencer, we can say, in a certain sense, that one of them was mistaken; and it was Guyau. In fact, he had no reason to use the term beauty, the term aesthetic emotion, and all the rest, without any advantage whatever (and, on the contrary, with enormous disadvantages), in a sense different from the one in which we are accustomed to use them (and in which, moreover, the theory he was trying to refute used them). What good can be obtained by lumping together under the name aesthetic emotion, for example, every pleasure, every agreeable sensation? There is no advantage in this, and there are serious drawbacks.

Consequently, after having made sure that a question is one of words, we should note that questions of words have some importance, and that taking a term in an improper sense can lead, as in this case it led a thinker of Guyau’s stature, to the greatest confusions. But even then, whatever the importance that questions of words may assume at certain moments, we should know that they are questions of words: never confuse them with questions of fact.

Explanatory Questions and Normative Questions

The problems that men discuss could, more or less schematically, be divided into two classes: sometimes people discuss how things are or how phenomena take place; at other times they discuss how one ought to act.

We might call the first problems of being, or problems of existence, or problems of ascertainment,

[p. 48]

or problems of explanation; the second we might call problems of doing, or of action, or problems of expediency (to which might be added problems of the ideal, concerning, not how things ought to be done, but how it would be desirable for them to be); or else, again, normative problems, etc. All these names are more or less improper: some express certain aspects of the divisions better, and others another aspect; I have not found more precise names, nor have I troubled to look for them, nor does it matter, for that matter, that you should look for them; I shall use any of these names simply in order to speak clearly about our subject, choosing in each case the one that occurs to me most naturally.

What matters is to give an idea of the two classes of questions.

If people discuss whether the moon has an atmosphere, whether there are or are not one or more planets beyond Neptune, whether Radium does or does not cure cancer, whether man is or is not free, in all these cases what is discussed is how things are or how phenomena take place; the aim is to ascertain or to explain.

If people were to discuss how one ought to act in order to obtain such and such an end; or, in general, how one ought to act; or what organization ought to be given to some institution or other; or whether a bill is bad or good; if they were to discuss, for example, the expediency of divorce, or the best organization of the family, or socialism, or free trade and protectionism; in these cases what is discussed is not how facts take place, but how one ought to act, or what ought to be done; and these problems are, as the simplest examination shows, of a different nature.

The cause of the error I am going to explain consists in treating problems of the second class as those of the first are treated.

[p. 49]

Men seek, in effect, the solution of both kinds of problems. When the problem of whether the moon has an atmosphere is discussed, we say that the solution of this problem is being sought; and when the question of divorce is discussed, we say, likewise, that the solution is being sought.

Now then: we must realize that the word solution, in the one case and in the other, has a very different sense (which is what is generally not understood, or not understood clearly).

Problems of existence or of ascertainment, problems of being, problems about how things are or about how phenomena occur, have, theoretically at least, a single and perfect solution (1).

Does the Moon have an atmosphere? It is possible that science does not yet possess the data needed to solve this problem; it is possible that it does possess them; but, even in the first case, the problem is always theoretically solvable, in the sense that, if it cannot be solved today, it may be hoped that it can be solved some day; and even if it could never be solved, at least it is conceivable that there must be a solution, even though it lies beyond man’s reach. Does a planet, or do several planets, exist beyond Neptune? We shall know or we shall not; but this problem, theoretically at least, admits of a solution. There may be difficulties. In these cases the difficulties are difficulties of fact. In another problem, such as that of whether man is free, on top of the difficulties of fact, that is, the difficulties that result from ignorance of certain facts, there are superimposed other difficulties of a verbal order: those that arise from the inadequacy of the terms, from confusions in the sense of the terms, etc.; but

the solution is always possible (provided there is a real problem).

Let us now see, in the case of the other class of problems, whether they admit of a solution; and, if so, what sense the word solution must have for them.

Let us suppose a very simple case: I am going to undertake a journey to Chile, and I ask myself whether I should make it by land or by sea: it is a problem (of doing), and I seek its solution.

On the one hand, I shall find that the journey by land offers certain advantages; for example, safety from shipwreck; sparing myself seasickness, to which I am prone; the beauty of certain landscapes, etc.; on the other hand, the journey by land offers certain drawbacks, such as its greater length, the hardship of the means of transport, the cold of the cordillera, etc. The journey by sea, in its turn, offers certain advantages: greater speed, greater comfort; it offers, on the other hand, other drawbacks..... The sense that the word “solution” can have here, then, is first of all this: ascertainment of the advantages and drawbacks of each of the two possible courses, —to which must be added the relative appraisal of these advantages and drawbacks: the comparison. From this comparison there may result the predominance of the advantages of one solution; or there may not, in case advantages and drawbacks remain more or less balanced; but what cannot result, or what at least need not necessarily result, is the attainment of an ideal solution, that is, one that has only advantages; this may result in this or that case, but it is not necessary.

Problems of being or of ascertainment are susceptible of a perfect solution, theoretically at least; but problems of doing, and likewise problems of the ideal, which can be assimilated to them,—if it makes sense to use the word solution

[p. 51]

with respect to them, are not susceptible (or at least are not necessarily so, nor even commonly so) of a perfect solution (1).

Now then: the sophism we are going to study results from men’s tendency to assimilate the one kind of problem to the other; to seek “the solution” of normative problems in the same state of mind and with the same intent with which one seeks that of explanatory problems or problems of ascertainment; to believe that they must necessarily have perfect solutions; to suppose that these would have to be found.

Let us study some examples.

Men argue about divorce; it is a problem of doing, a problem of the second class: whether divorce should or should not be permitted; it is of the same class as the problem of whether one should, or whether it is expedient, or whether it is expedient for me in a particular case, to go to Chile by sea, or to go by land.

The only solution that this problem admits of is the following: to try to study, on the one hand, the advantages of divorce; on the other hand, the drawbacks of divorce; on the other side, to study on the one hand the advantages of the indissolubility of marriage, and on the other, its drawbacks. This done, to weigh, appraise, assess these advantages and drawbacks, and, if it be the case, to decide for one of the two solutions. But the tendency men have to confuse this class of problems with those other problems, of existence, which admit of perfect solutions, leads them, sometimes, to believe consciously, and almost always to believe unconsciously or instinctively, that these problems, the normative ones, must have a perfect solution. [p. 52]

As a consequence of this, they put themselves into a special state of mind: into a mental attitude that produces, in our case, for example, and for a “divorcist,” the tendency not to see the drawbacks of divorce (or, if he does see them, to see them as smaller than they are), and to see the advantages of divorce as greater than they are.

This mental attitude also leads to exclusivist tendencies; notably to that tendency (so common) to reject solutions that have drawbacks, for the sole reason that they have them, and without stopping to think that the other possible solutions may have them too, and, as the case may be, have greater ones. (This creates a cause of insincerity of minds toward themselves.)

More: the difficulties men have in coming to an understanding on these problems; the fact that in some of them it is so hard to arrive at practical solutions, depends precisely on this fallacy: on the tendency to seek solutions without drawbacks.

Any other case, among the problems of social organization, would serve us as an example. Let us suppose that socialism is being discussed; that is (I define it in a general and vague way), the tendency of the State or of some authority to intervene in social, economic, etc., relations. That problem will be well discussed as long as those who discuss it try to study the advantages and drawbacks of intervention and of non-intervention, and, this study done, to appraise the relative value of the ones and the others. But it will be badly discussed from the moment those who discuss it set out to seek “the solution,” in the sense of seeking a perfect solution for it, that is, a

[p. 53]

solution such that it has no drawbacks, and, on the contrary, only advantages.

People argue about property in land: some declare themselves partisans of individual property in land; others declare themselves opposed to it. Either of these attitudes may be just and logical, as long as being a partisan of individual property in land, or being an adversary of it, means only this: to hold that the advantages of individual property in land are greater than its drawbacks, or to hold, on the contrary, that the drawbacks are greater than the advantages. But in the great majority of practical cases, you, reading authors or witnessing a discussion, will notice the almost universal tendency to seek the solution in another sense, in the deceptive and fallacious sense; that is, the tendency of the partisans of one regime or the other to convince themselves, and to try to prove, that the regime chosen has nothing but advantages, and to exaggerate, on the other hand, the drawbacks of the opposite or different regime.

When a writer of treatises on Political Economy deals with protectionism and free trade, it is almost certain that he will find one solution perfect, e.g., the free-trade one: he will not see its drawbacks, or, at least, he will tend to diminish them, and to exaggerate its advantages; his appraisal of the opposite or different theory, of protectionism, will be falsified in the inverse direction. And if we study the psychology of that author, we shall find that this false appraisal comes from his having supposed, even if unconsciously, that the problem ought to have, as people always say, a solution, meaning by solution some course, some regime, that does away with all evils.

So common is this fallacy that there is an entire order of knowledge and of inquiry, perhaps the most important of all, whose history is nothing but an illustration of it: I refer to Ethics.

[p. 54]

The moral problem is essentially a problem of doing and of the ideal: it is the normative problem par excellence.

Now then: if you go over the criticism that has been made of all moral systems, you will find that this criticism inevitably consists in demonstrating that the system or the author criticized “does not found morality satisfactorily.” The morality—in what sense? A perfect morality: a morality that presents no drawbacks, that has no deficiency whatever.

These criticisms were directed notably at the positive systems, for reasons easy to understand: the positive systems, which dispense with religious or metaphysical hypotheses, can only offer more imperfect normative solutions; and so, if you go through the usual textbooks, you will find a demonstration, unassailable no doubt, that none of the positive systems, neither those based on pleasure nor those based on utility, have succeeded in founding morality; which, in these cases, means: they have not succeeded in founding a morality that has neither drawbacks nor deficiencies.

More: the crowning, the outcome of this attitude of ethical criticism has been the advent of a contemporary school that seeks to do away with normative ethics. Several modern writers (of whom Levy Bruhl may be taken as representative) maintain that the work aimed at founding morality was illegitimate; that normative ethics ought not to exist; that, if the name of ethics is to be kept, it must designate solely a science of ascertainment, a science that explains how moral phenomena occur, how men think, feel and act, but nothing more; and the fundamental argument from which they draw this negative conclusion has been the negative result of the efforts made until now to found morality. These authors pass in review

[p. 55]

the attempts of all the systems and of all the philosophers; they demonstrate that the ancient philosophers “did not found morality”; that neither Plato nor Aristotle “founded it”; that the Stoics “did not found morality”; that the hedonists “did not found morality”; nor Guyau, nor Nietzche...; and they conclude that morality cannot be founded, and that we must give up founding it.

What do they mean by founding morality? The same thing we have explained; that is, to give rules such that they lead to a conduct that has no drawback whatever; to found, in short, a perfect morality.

I was telling you that this school is nothing but the logical outcome of the attitude that humanity has permanently maintained with respect to morality; and the error depends on the confusion of the problems of the one class and of the other: on seeking, in normative problems, solutions in the sense in which they are sought in explanatory problems.

Now, do not believe that this sophism is observed only in theoretical questions, and that it only translates into speculative effects: with the same ease with which examples of it are found in speculation, they are found in practical life, where they swarm.

I am going to give you some examples:

In the common schools of our country there is a class called preparatory, instituted so that in it the children newly entered into the school may be initiated into the study of the first-year program, which is then repeated in the following year, that is, in the first year properly so called. But in a certain special school, under the same name of “preparatory class,” there is one organized in another way: it is formed with the children of any age who enter the establishment, with the object of preparing them to enter any of the years. The class, there, has as its object to equalize the pupils’ knowledge:

[p. 56]

if, for example, a child enters who could go into the third year on account of certain knowledge, but who on account of other knowledge ought to go into the second, in that preparatory class, which is a class of individual teaching, an effort is made to level his knowledge so that he can pass into the third year.

Now then: by reason of my post in Primary Instruction (you will notice that the examples I use in these lessons are always examples taken from reality; I could no doubt present more important or more striking ones; but my chief concern is to show how men reason and how they go wrong; that is why I take all my examples from living reality), for several years now I have been hearing a permanent discussion carried on about this question of the preparatory classes, a discussion that bids fair never to end, and that is falsified by our sophism. The question is the following: how should the preparatory class be organized: in the first way, or in the second? There are partisans of one solution and partisans of the other, and they make arguments; but what all of them admit, without realizing it, is that one of the two ways is the good one, and that the other way is bad.

The true way of discussing that question would be to think and express oneself thus: organizing the preparatory class in the first manner has such and such advantages and such and such drawbacks (they are explained). Organizing the preparatory class in the second manner also has such and such advantages and such and such drawbacks. Now, in which case will the advantages be greater and the drawbacks smaller? It may be, incidentally, and it is possible that this is the case here, that there is a balance of advantages and drawbacks: the case may arise (and it does arise at times in these problems of doing, normative problems, or whatever you wish to call them) that, more or less, one solution is worth as much as the other. But those who discuss the

[p. 57]

question in this particular case have put themselves into a special state of mind, in which they believe themselves obliged necessarily to choose one of the two organizations as excellent and to combat the other as downright bad.

In this way, the very ascertainment of advantages and drawbacks is falsified: part of reality ceases to be seen; another part is exaggerated or disfigured.

Another, more important case: when, concerning examinations, the partisans and adversaries of this institution argue, what is being discussed is a “problem of doing”: whether it is expedient to keep examinations, or else to abolish them, replacing them or not with something else. In that case it is perfectly possible (and it is what happens) that each of the two solutions has advantages and drawbacks. They should be studied well, weighed. The sensible thing will be, if one of the two solutions offers more advantages and fewer drawbacks than the other, to adopt it. But this is not what is usually done. Most of the partisans of examinations tend to exaggerate their advantages and to diminish their drawbacks, and even to deny them; most of the adversaries of examinations tend to exaggerate their drawbacks and to diminish their advantages, and even to deny them. And all these sophisms of observation, of appraisal, etc., depend on the unconscious confusion between the problems of the two classes; that is, on having supposed that the problem had to have a solution in the sense in which problems of ascertainment have one (or in which they can theoretically have one): a perfect solution, without drawbacks.

This paralogism once sufficiently understood, we can complete our study of it with three observations concerning it.

The first is the following (of great practical interest):

[p. 58]

the state of mind that constitutes it, and the way of proceeding that it shapes, turn out to be favorable to what exists and contrary to innovation. The sophism we are studying is a conservative sophism. At a given moment, society, for example, is organized in a certain way, and an attempt is made to introduce a modification. That modification probably has some drawback. The tendency to seek perfect solutions in this class of problems makes men tend to reject the innovation because, as is said in practice, it does not solve the problem; that is: because it is not a perfect solution.

Take the case of the abolition of examinations. A new regime is introduced; for example, the appraisal of the student’s yearly work, formulated by the professor; and, by virtue of the sophism we are studying, people say: “no: that organization does not solve the problem: it has such and such a drawback; consequently, it should not be established.” If, on the contrary, the regime being tried had been that of examinations, men would have proceeded in the same way: they would have observed the drawbacks and would have tended to reject the innovating solution. It is, then, an essentially conservative sophism, as we have said (1).

The second observation is the following: when one of these problems of doing or of the ideal is treated as it ought to be treated, something very curious happens, namely, that men tend to believe it has been left without a solution.

Socialism, for example, is being discussed. An observant, sincere and fair mind tells us: “The

In reality, both things happen: what is explained in the text (which is a part of the truth), and what is explained in this note.

(1) It could also be said, on the contrary, that the sophism in question is an innovating sophism: since existing institutions almost always have defects, the supposition that there can be perfect solutions leads one to desire and to seek change, without examining whether those drawbacks are, as may happen, unavoidable, or smaller than those of whatever it is proposed to substitute for what exists.

[p. 59]

intervention of the State in economic or social relations, or the substitution of collective property for the regime of individual property, or the socialization of the means of labor, or of the means of production, would have such and such advantages; it would also have such and such drawbacks.”

What impression do men feel before a book, for example, written in this spirit? The following: “the author leaves us the problem unsolved; he solves nothing, he tells us nothing concrete.”

Meanwhile, the impression of solution is produced in men precisely when the problem is badly treated: when a writer arrives, for example, at the conclusion that socialism has nothing but advantages, and, more or less fallaciously, by means of reasonings or of false observations, spirits away all the drawbacks (or when he arrives at an apparent contrary solution); it is then that it seems to men that the author has solved the problem, or that, at least, he has treated it as it ought to be treated.

And the third observation is the following: this sophism is one of those that contribute to giving birth to, and to maintaining, that class of theorists, so common and so baneful, who obstruct the practical solutions capable of improving what exists, combating them because they are not perfect.

Every time an attempt is made to introduce a modification, and it is attacked in the name of the perfect, those who speak thus commit, unconsciously, the sophism we are studying: “With that you do not solve the problem. With that you do not do away with the evil.”

I remember, for example (and I am going to cite a crude case), a case in which I had devised and proposed a small reform in one of the account books of a branch of the administration, with the object of suppressing a certain kind of fraud; that modification

[p. 60]

was objected to by several people, all of whom made, as an objection against my reform, the following argument: “With that you do not do away with fraud. It is true, no doubt, that it will no longer be committed in that book; but fraud will still remain in such and such another book, and in such and such another.”

This way of reasoning is quite common among men; and it would be useful, in a logic class, for the students to practice looking for practical examples, whether in books or in the ordinary discussions of life.

False Precision

The human mind desires precision in knowledge, and is satisfied by it. Precision is good; it is the ideal, when it is legitimate; but on the other hand, when it is illegitimate or false, it produces, from the point of view of knowledge, baneful effects: it hides facts, disfigures or falsifies interpretations, halts inquiry, inhibits going deeper, produces, in a word, a series of most harmful effects that can be condensed fundamentally into these two adjectives: falsifying and inhibiting effects.

There are scientific systems, whole theories (and even branches of knowledge), that may be considered illustrations of this fallacy; for example, the psychology of Herbart. This author tries to explain psychology by mathematics: all mental phenomena are explained by actions and reactions of representations, which reinforce one another or exclude one another from the mind in accordance with mathematical laws; each representation has a coefficient of strength that is represented numerically; there are mathematical formulas for mental phenomena.... And such an explanation produces a deceptive effect: it makes one believe that

[p. 61]

Psychology has acquired precision; it makes one believe that Psychology is a kind of exact science. This precision is false, it is illegitimate. In the same way, classical Psychophysics and Psychometry: those laws of Fechner that quantify sensation, mathematical formulas in which arithmetic and geometric progressions, or logarithms, are spoken of in connection with states of consciousness; or Psychometry, dividing reaction time into four precise moments: “a first physiological time, a first psychological time, a second psychological time, a second physiological time,” and trying to measure them. The experiments on the number of representations or states of consciousness that can coexist in the mind at a given moment, and the claimed solutions, for example, the one that twelve states of consciousness fit in the mind, not one more nor one less. And in the same way, in other sciences, those applications of mathematics to historical testimony, in direct or inverse ratio to the time elapsed, or to any other circumstance; to the likelihood of the depositions of witnesses.... All of them patent examples of false precision or of illegitimate precision.

In general (and a brief digression is in order here), Mathematics is responsible for many of these cases of false precision in the sciences; so much so, that whoever passed them all in review might ask himself whether the intrusion or the invasion of Mathematics has not been truly harmful to science in general. But we should at once see ample grounds for the opposite assertion: we should ascertain the advantages that the introduction of Mathematics has brought the sciences; the services that Astronomy, Physics, Chemistry, etc., owe to Mathematics. And then perhaps we should see the question rightly if we came to appraise the facts by saying that Mathematics has been useful to the sciences when

[p. 62]

it has put itself at their service, when it has tried to assist them; and that, on the other hand, it has been harmful to them when it has tried to replace them or to impose its own procedures on them artificially; which might be condensed by saying that, for human thought, Mathematics is the best servant and the worst master.

But the cases of false precision in the sciences are not solely cases of the bad application of Mathematics. To cite a well-known example, and one we have studied in our classes, the pedagogy of Doctor Berra, which had so much practical influence in our country, was a very characteristic case of false precision. To present Pedagogy in the form in which that author presented it, reduced to a fixed number of clear and precise laws, which can be counted, which can be numbered, and which are capable of being applied deductively with an infallible result; to derive those laws from a psychology that makes pigeonholes in the mind, and in which the so-called faculties are clearly distinguished, and separated from one another; to consider that there exists only one determinate method for teaching each order of knowledge and for acquiring each order of knowledge; all that was to give Pedagogy an appearance of precision completely illegitimate and false, which resulted in our case, as you know, in rather harmful consequences. By reading a few paragraphs we shall get an idea of this character of false precision:

“Observation teaches that it is enough for the senses and for consciousness to put themselves in communication with the respective phenomena in order that, without any series of mental operations being noticed, without any psychic process being noticed, they acquire the ideas of which they are capable. They know instantaneously; their only method is the intuitive.

“The intelligence knows the direct relations of phenomena, of facts, of things, in a single way as well: by comparing the notions of those things, facts or phenomena; so that in knowing direct relations it employs the comparative method.

[p. 63]

But if that same intelligence is to draw from a concrete notion, whether of phenomena or of things, an abstract idea, it proceeds in the way that consciousness reveals to us, but to which a name must be given: it is the abstractive method. If the same intelligence is to know a law, a rule, a general notion, inferring it from the knowledge it may have of the relation of certain particular facts, it proceeds in another way, which is the inductive method.

“In the knowledge of things that consist of simple parts perceived successively, as a melody is, the senses perceive those parts intuitively and the intelligence their relations comparatively; but besides, it observes in the succession of this knowledge a given order: it proceeds from the simple parts to the whole. This method is called synthetic. When things are observed whose ensemble is the first thing presented to the perceptive aptitudes, these pass, after the first vague perception, to the careful observation of ever smaller parts, and proceed, for that very reason, from the whole to the elements. That method is the analytic. If the object consists of several complex parts that can only be perceived one after another, the cognitive aptitudes analyze each part and successively compare the results of these analyses, going from the parts to the whole. Such is the analytic-synthetic method.

“All these methods are employed in the acquisition of pure science.

“If we now observe how one proceeds in order to know the utility that may be derived from this pure knowledge, or how it is to be applied to the satisfaction of needs, it will be noted that a reasoning always takes place in which the need in view is taken as the starting point, the pure knowledge related to it is sought, and the consequence is drawn. Example: I am thirsty; nature quenches thirst by means of water; therefore I must drink water. This way of knowing, this way by which pure notions are applied to satisfying human needs, is called the deductive method. All applied science is, then, deduced science.

“It is seen that, just as there are eight classes of knowable objects, there are eight methods of knowing, one for each class. And if one examines the possibility that a class of objects might be known by more than one method, or that several classes of objects might be known by one method, one will arrive at the certainty that the one is as impossible as the other.”

You understand the effect that this presentation of science produces on minds not prepared

[p. 64]

by in-depth study. All this is falsely simplistic: neither can the faculties be distinguished so clearly from one another, nor can the methods be distinguished so clearly from one another; nor can the real and effective course that the mind in fact follows in the acquisition of knowledge be schematized in such a way; nor, above all, is it possible to go so far as to count the classes of knowable objects, the methods of knowing. It is even concluded here that it is impossible to know one class of objects by more than one method, or to know several classes of objects by one and the same method...; in reality, each case, in fact, is different: the process that the mind follows in concrete cases, the real psychological process, does not correspond to any of these schemes; at most they might be used to make explanations easier: only for that, and while being well on one’s guard against them; but you understand the effect that a science presented in this way must inevitably produce: a falsifying and inhibiting effect, as I was saying: on the one hand, we know badly (in that way which the French call simplistic); on the other hand, the tendency to investigate tends to come to a halt: “There are eight classes of knowable objects; there are eight methods: they are of such and such a kind”; we learn this, we repeat it, and that is the end of science!

He who has no habits of going deep may even, as a consequence of this appearance of false precision, come to feel prejudiced against good observations and against good interpretations, which will strike him, as is natural, as more complicated, more imprecise, less decisive, less tranchantes [clear-cut], less perfect (in appearance). If, for example, a good observer and a good reasoner presents pedagogical observation with gaps and lacunae, and pedagogical theory with imperfections, doubts and even ignorance, he will produce on the not-well-disciplined mind the

[p. 65]

effect of teaching a pedagogy much less perfect than that of the other kind, falsely simplistic, falsely precise......

The important thing, however, is not merely to learn to recognize, which is very easy, those cases in which false precision has a character as exaggerated, as excessive, as here, but to realize that this fallacy hovers over almost the whole of human knowledge. Bacon already listed among the idola tribus [idols of the tribe], among the sophisms to which the human mind in general is prone, the tendency to give too much symmetry to knowledge. But, without going on looking for examples in pure science, let us observe some of a practical order.

Very often, in the facts of life, we observe cases like the following (I shall cite ones quite familiar to all of us). On the board of examiners for a language, at the University, there was the following custom: the examiners, in order to determine their judgment, went on noting down, as the examination proceeded, the mistakes the student fell into, classifying them according to their nature: mistakes of pronunciation, mistakes of translation, mistakes of spelling, etc. Afterwards, to award the mark, there was a kind of bookkeeping: each mistake of translation counted, let us suppose, for two mistakes of spelling, and for four mistakes of pronunciation; the student who did not reach a certain average of mistakes was to be passed; the one who fell below that average, failed; and I even believe there was some relation between the number of mistakes and the marks awarded him. In this those examiners saw a way of giving greater precision and justice to their verdicts, and of avoiding the discussions so frequent on these occasions.

It was a clear case of our fallacy of false precision. There is no need to explain, for example, how, under the name of “errors of pronunciation,” of

[p. 66]

“errors of translation” or of “errors of spelling,” errors of very diverse importance were classified together there; there are errors of such a nature that perhaps a single one of them may disqualify a student from passing an examination; there are other errors, classifiable under the same heading, which may indicate nothing serious; more: there may even be a certain kind of errors that actually indicate intelligence or superiority. In that way an apparent precision was given to the facts when, in reality, they were falsified utterly.

The human mind is, however, so constituted that many people, if one tries to make them notice the foregoing, answer that, if they proceed as they do, it is in order to avoid vagueness; otherwise, they say, we find ourselves faced with something uncertain, whereas here we have a fixed criterion. (This obsession with the fixed criterion is the source of an immense quantity of errors.)

Absolutely the same thing was seen to happen in some classes while the so-called “system of exemptions” was in force, a short while ago, in our University. There were professors who, believing they were proceeding rightly and believing they were giving themselves a more exact and perhaps even fairer criterion, also kept a kind of bookkeeping of the merits and demerits of each student in the class. Some, and this was the simplest case, struck an average: he who has a certain percentage of good answers may be exempted from examination, and he who does not reach that percentage shall not be.

Even within Mathematics itself, the application was bad: a percentage over thirty or forty answers does not mean the same thing, as far as probabilities are concerned, as the same percentage over three or four. But, even without going into this kind of objection, the greater part of reality escapes rules of such a kind.

[p. 67]

The student who stayed away from class on the days when he did not know the lesson had a chance, naturally, of obtaining a percentage of correct answers higher than that of the student who came to class that day for the purpose of learning; and absurd results of this sort were extremely numerous within such a system. The fallacy of false precision hid all this from those professors, and made them believe that with their system they were improving their criterion. I remember that one day I was objecting to one of them the absolute inadequacy of the numbers he used to make his class notes: How can you, I said to him, reduce to numbers conduct, application, intelligence, memory, everything, in a word, that a student may have or not have, show or not show? What difference translatable into numbers can there be, for example, between an intelligent but not very diligent student and a more diligent but less intelligent student? What convention could be established for numbering all that?

He understood, naturally, the bearing of my objections; but he defended himself by saying that he proceeded in that way in order to escape vagueness. And when I advised him to keep his class book as it really ought to be kept, that is, noting down the truth of the facts: writing: “such-and-such student gave an answer that showed he is intelligent, but that he had not studied; such-and-such other student always answers from memory, showing great application, but lack of intelligence, etc.,” he would answer me that in that way he would not know afterwards what to do; and, in fact, he found himself at a loss once his criterion was deprived of the leading-strings of false precision to which he had grown accustomed.

And there is so strong a tendency in certain men to seek this fictitious precision that our sophism is quite difficult to root out: look at this case. In a certain institution

[p. 68]

of learning, it was laid down in the regulations that the professors were to record in a class book, by numbers, the value of each pupil’s answers, daily; at the end of the year an average was struck, which was taken into account for the examination. I had occasion to get that regulation modified, and, in the new one, the use of numbers was categorically forbidden, since it led to the greatest errors under the appearance of a fictitious truth. It was necessary to repeat the order many times: and, at last, the disappearance of the numbers was achieved, or was believed to have been achieved; but some time afterwards I learned the following: many professors complied with the regulation, presenting a report in the form requested, that is: explaining what the student was like, without reducing him to numbers; but that report they deduced from some rough notes that they kept in class throughout the whole year, made in numbers. So that the report was always fictitious: it was simply given the form of an expression of reality.

An interesting derivative of this sophism: at times people even avoid, defend themselves, let us say, against being given data that complicate matters for them, as if they were afraid of the real complexity of things, which disconcerts their judgments, which robs these of their simplicity and their geometrism. I remember the following case: the question was being discussed whether, examinations on fixed dates having been replaced (in the primary schools) by examinations that were to be held unexpectedly, at any moment of the year, before a Commission; the question was being discussed, I say, whether, within that new plan, it was better for the three examiners appointed to visit the school jointly, or whether it was preferable, on the contrary, for each examiner to visit it on his own, separately, and for each to present a personal report, instead of the collective report.

I inclined, rather, to the second opinion;

[p. 69]

among other reasons, because I have observed that on examining boards, generally, it is the criterion of one person that predominates, whether because of his greater authority or for any other cause; also, above all, because in this way the schools could be seen for three times as long, and at different times; and, although it is true that the advantages of collaboration were lacking here, on the other hand the wealth of data was multiplied. Then the following objection was made to me: “Suppose those examiners give different reports on the school; that, for example, one tells us it is good, and another tells us it is bad: what do we do?” Here is a typical case of this interesting derivative of our sophism. What was cited as a drawback was precisely the greatest advantage. To judge something debatable, the best thing is to hear opinions in different directions, each of them well founded: one is never so well equipped to judge as then. The ideal, for judging a school, or anything else, would be to hear those who hold that it is good and to hear also those who hold that it is bad (if there are some of each). But, because of that need the mind has for precision, for a fixed criterion, this was considered an evil, and it was judged preferable that there should be a single report in which the school was declared good or bad.

All these are examples taken from reality; because, in these lectures, my purpose is not to present examples ad hoc, as is generally done in treatises on logic. Logic is usually studied as one would study anatomy on those wax or wooden models that are sometimes used in teaching and that are made for teaching; and it is better to study it as anatomy is studied on the cadaver, that is, on living reality. The sophisms set down in treatises on logic are generally prepared sophisms; those

[p. 70]

worth analyzing, even if perhaps they are not so interesting, are the real sophisms. That is why my examples are concrete and at times of limited scope. But, among the cases of this fallacy of false precision, we could cite others infinitely broader.

And I shall cite this one only: all primary and secondary teaching, it may be said, is (and this is to a certain degree unavoidable) affected by false precision. Everything we learn at school and at secondary school is simplified, symmetrized. It is not reality itself that we learn: it is simplified schemes.

The first impression one experiences on leaving this teaching, on leaving the class textbooks, is one of confusion: when, after having studied, for example, a philosophical theory in a textbook, one goes to read that same philosophical theory as set forth in the original work, we always find that it is something else; and the same thing, in greater or lesser degree, happens with the theories of physics, of literature or of whatever it may be. Up to a certain degree, I tell you, this evil is absolutely unavoidable; it lies in the very nature of things: one cannot teach, at a certain level of teaching at least, except by simplifying artificially. But the following application of our knowledge of this sophism is very interesting: I believe it evident that, on top of the unavoidable part, another is superimposed that would be avoidable.

Of course this would not be achieved with a few isolated lessons in logic, nor with lectures like these of mine; it would have to be the result of a whole change in the systems of education, and, above all, of a change in the spirit of those entrusted with giving it.

Instead of letting the student believe that things are as he understands them, as they are set forth in the textbooks, there ought to result, both from express

[p. 71]

and continual warnings of the teacher and, tacitly, from the very spirit of the teaching he gave, a kind of constant warning that there is something more beyond, and that reality is more complex than it is made to appear.

This does not always happen: at times the contrary happens. A short while ago the following incident was told to me as true (I repeat it as interesting, although it would be an extreme case): in a certain class, the theory of a certain author was being taught, summarized in the work of another; and one day a student had, spontaneously, the idea of going to the original to read the theory in the works of the author himself. He sets forth the theory in class, which, naturally, was not as simple as in the summary, and the professor says to him: “that is not X’s theory.” “So much is it his”—the student answers —“that I have just read it in X himself.” The professor is somewhat disconcerted, but soon recovers: “Well, read it in N.” (the name of the author of the summary he used in class).

Many times, thus, teaching even seems counterproductive, from this point of view; even when the fact occurs in a more moderate and common degree than in such exaggerated cases. No comparison can serve so well here to establish what I would consider the ideal as the following, which I use very often: when one observes the sky with the naked eye, the constellations have a definite form; one can, for example, trace over them drawings, which are what have given them their traditional names. When the telescope is turned on that region of the sky, everything becomes confused: the constellation ceases to be a chair, a fish, a lion or a ram: it is a luminous confusion, more and more confused the more luminous it is; more and more confused the more we see.

Now then: all primary and secondary teaching could be compared to an astronomical observation

[p. 72]

in which nothing were available but the naked eye. Naturally, the teacher directing it could show the pupils only the stars visible under those conditions; but he ought to let them know that these are not all: that the constellation is not exactly like that;—let them know it, and make them feel it.

Verbo-Ideological Fallacies

Under this name, intentionally rather vague, we are going to study some of the fallacies that have to do with the relation between words and ideas and judgments; between language and thought.

I must begin by summarizing a philosophical debate the recollection of which will better prepare us for our study: perhaps never has a question been discussed that was, in appearance, so purely speculative, and in reality of such practical scope, as the one that gave rise to a metaphysical controversy between Stuart Mill and William Hamilton, which the first of these philosophers summarizes in his “Logic.”

There exists in traditional logic a principle called the principle of the excluded middle, according to which, of two contradictory propositions, one must necessarily be true, and the other false. Stated in another form, which means the same thing, this principle says that a proposition must be either true or false, with no middle term possible.

From    this   Hamilton        drew        certain   consequences: Philosophy—he said—may perhaps be condemned never to reveal to us the truth about certain fundamental questions; it is possible that we shall never know, for example, whether matter is or is not divisible to infinity; but, at least, this principle of the excluded middle teaches us something about the nature of matter, and it is this: that either it is divisible to infinity, or

[p. 73]

it is not; we pose a dilemma to matter: we do not know which of the two alternatives it will choose; but it is obliged to choose one. Consequently, even though ultimate realities must remain forever unknowable to us, they will not be completely so. The same will happen with any other metaphysical question that may be posed: Has the Universe begun at some time, or has it existed from eternity? Possibly man will never know which of these two alternatives is the truth; but he knows something, and it is this: that either it began at some time, or it has existed eternally. The Universe is obliged to accept, let us say, one of these alternatives that man poses to it.

And Stuart Mill answered him with great profundity: It is not so: we cannot know even that about ultimate realities, because, between the truth and the falsity of a proposition, there is an alternative, there is a middle term or a third that is not excluded, and that is the lack of sense; it is not necessary that a proposition be either true or false: the proposition can, besides, lack sense; its attribute may not be applicable to the subject in an intelligible way. “Matter is, or is not, divisible to infinity”: perhaps this proposition has no sense; perhaps matter (if it exists; for it might also not exist) has such a nature that the attribute divisible or indivisible cannot be applied to it in an intelligible sense. This phrase (Stuart Mill went on): Abracadabra is a second intention, is neither true nor false: it simply lacks sense. And the same might happen with the phrases that it occurs to us to formulate concerning ultimate metaphysical realities.

Now then: the discussion between the two philosophers was confined to philosophy properly so called, and to special and well-defined cases; but if one observes the way men think, express themselves and argue, one sees that that question had a

[p. 74]

scope infinitely greater, and, above all, a practical scope that at that time was perhaps unsuspected.

This non-sense of a proposition can exist not only in the absolute form—the coarse form, let us say,—the brutal form, of Mill’s examples, but in a relative form, and in all possible degrees; besides absolute inadequacy, there can be relative inadequacies in all possible degrees.

So that even when a man has the instinct or the good sense needed to avoid discussing whether “abracadabra is or is not a second intention” (or questions almost equally absurd, like the one ancient philosophy discussed, whether virtue was square, and other similar ones); even when a man, I say, has the instinct or the good sense to avoid those discussions, he will fall into the fallacy of discussing questions that are also badly posed, but in which the inadequacy is much smaller: that do not lack sense absolutely, but in which the attribute is not totally, clearly and univocally adequate to the subject.

Goethe’s Mephistopheles, teaching logic to a student who asks him for lessons, utters this maxim: “Men generally believe, when they hear words, that they must necessarily contain some idea.” What is certain, in fact, is that men generally believe, when they hear or read propositions, that these must necessarily be either true or false; and they tend to discuss every judgment that is uttered, starting from the assumption that it must be true or false; that if it is not true, it is false; that if it is not false, it is true. Who has not found himself, at some time, as it were disconcerted, undecided and unable to answer, and almost to think, before certain questions that come up in the conversation of ignorant people, or before certain questions of children? For example: a child once asked (a question that was

[p. 75]

suggested to him at a moment when he was hanging from a tree) “whether people have more strength than weight, or more weight than strength.” The better one thinks, the more impossible it becomes to answer this sort of question. At once our mind is troubled, it bristles all over with distinctions: “more strength than weight”... in what sense? For example: it will be one thing to ask whether a person has strength enough to hang from a horizontal branch of a tree; it will be a different thing to know whether he has the strength needed to climb a rope with knots, or without knots; in a word: the question has no sense, or it has so many that it amounts practically to the same thing. It cannot be answered. Now, imagine what would happen if, through lack of culture, of good sense, of mental precision, or of any other cause whatever, two persons set about discussing such a question, starting from the principle that it is either true or false that people have more strength than weight.

Now then: let us analyze questions like some of these: the other day I clipped from a newspaper a manifesto of an “International League of Freethinking Women”; it begins thus:

“The League affirms the unity of all that exists; that man is one with all that exists; that man is one with other men; that man is feminine and masculine. It affirms that the manifestations that man perceives are will; that the will of man has its limit in the infinite; that everything is natural...” etc.

Let us take some of these affirmations; for example, this one: “that man is one with all that exists.”

For one who thinks well, the state produced before such an affirmation is neither an impression of truth nor an impression of falsity: it is an impression of lack of sense; not an impression of absolute absurdity, of non-sense through the words’ lack of meaning or through complete and absolute inadequacy of the attribute,

[p. 76]

as in those extreme cases of Stuart Mill’s, but this other one: that the sense is not sufficiently clear. At once a mind accustomed to thinking looks for the different meanings the proposition may have; in what sense it can be said that man is one with nature or with other men, or that he is not. And if, as would happen in this case, it does not find one or more clear senses that can first be made precise, so as then to begin a discussion, it sets the proposition aside; not as false, and still less as true: it sets it aside as a badly made, inadequate verbalism, about which one should not argue. For this, we even develop a kind of instinct.

But the fallacy into which many people fall consists in undertaking discussions about affirmations of this kind; and, once the verbal formulation is admitted as valid, and once the discussion is undertaken, all is lost; the more it is discussed, the infinitely worse the result will be. If it is admitted that, necessarily, either man is one with all that exists, or he is not, everything that is maintained on either side must be absurd and without sense. Which shows at once that being a good thinker is more difficult than it seems at first sight, because one must defend oneself not only against the solutions: one must defend oneself even against the questions themselves, against the very problems, against the statements.

From this there also follows the following consequence, which is like one sophism grafted onto another: when one of those questions is posed, there is a tendency, and it is easy to explain, to base the demonstration of each of the two contradictory formulas on the absurdity of the other, and there arises that kind of demonstration called by the absurd, applied to the cases in which it is the most dangerous and the most false of all.

You understand that nothing is easier than

[p. 77]

to demonstrate in appearance that virtue is not square, by proving that it is false or absurd that it should be square; that nothing is easier than to demonstrate in appearance that man is “one with the whole Universe,” or that he is not one with the whole Universe, by proving that the contradictory verbal formulation has no sense; and there results, then, a new fallacy, derived from the former.

Let us look at other examples, of the original one and of the derived one; I take them from an article published in a review:

“What are things? What is being? Being is vibration. If it were not so, how would things be related to one another? how could they affect us at a distance and produce innumerable impressions in us? This indicates that something is transmitted from them to us,” etc.

..... “If things emit vibrations, it is because they themselves vibrate. Being, then, may be defined as vibration. On the one hand, we have the vibration common to all things; on the other, the idea, the soul, which, establishing differences among them, characterizes them.

“This indicates that what is essential, positive, is vibration; since it is the essence in which all things participate; and that the idea, the soul, is negative.”

Suppose, then, that one of the propositions we find in these passages is posed: “is being vibration or is it not vibration?”, or this other, still more typical: “is the soul negative or is it not negative?” The fallacy I am pointing out to you is that of letting this verbal formulation pass, that of admitting the problem, do you understand?, whether to maintain or to oppose that the soul is negative; it is absolutely the same thing: as soon as the problem is admitted, as soon as the question is admitted, the sophism has already been produced and there is no logical salvation possible. These problems must be rejected; and we must accustom ourselves to acquiring a kind of instinct that makes us feel verbal inadequacy (not only in the cases in which it is absolute, coarse, but even in the cases in which it is partial, relative); even before we begin to think about the questions.

[p. 78]

And as for the second fallacy, the one derived from the former, here it is too: “What are things? What is being? Being is vibration. If it were not so how would things be related to one another? how could they affect us...?”, etc. Once an absurd question has been admitted, as I was telling you, nothing is easier than to demonstrate apparently that one of the two theses is true, on the ground that the contrary one is absurd. Of course: whichever of the two is formulated, it has no sense. One can demonstrate, which is a part of the truth, that one of them has no sense; and, then, we consider ourselves convinced of the other.

Now, leaving these small examples, would you like a big one: the biggest of all?

All metaphysics, all traditional philosophy, is, perhaps, an immense example, an immense illustration of the paralogism we are studying.

The great majority of the classical demonstrations of metaphysical theses are a case of this fallacy which Stuart Mill discovered as one discovers a new world; for the great majority of those classical metaphysical “demonstrations” consists simply in what we have explained: in admitting a thesis and taking it as proved by the demonstration that the contrary thesis leads us to absurdities, to contradictions, to inconsistencies or to impossibilities, without taking into account that possibly both theses are in that same case.

I open any treatise of philosophy whatever; let it be this one, by P. Janet, which served as the textbook in the University for so many years: we shall find at once what I would call the paralogism of the metaphysicians.

The whole question, for example, about the existence of God, as the “deists” and the “atheists” discuss it, is nothing but a collection of would-be “proofs” of this kind. In reality, a question like this one: “Does God exist or not?”, is one of those that cannot be reduced to the form of a fatal

[p. 79]

and compulsory dilemma. It is possible that the question has no sense, or no clear sense, even though it seems to us to have one (which can be understood only by one who has analyzed those ideas somewhat thoroughly and who realizes all the obscurity there is in them). And the would-be “demonstrations,” whether of the existence of God or of his non-existence, all consist, or almost all, in the following: the partisans of the existence of God prove that admitting the non-existence of God leads to absurdities or impossibilities; and the partisans of the non-existence of God prove exactly the same with respect to the contrary thesis. Both are right, completely right, as long as they confine themselves to affirming the fact that the opposite thesis produces all kinds of difficulties; but they fall into the fallacy we are studying because they take their own thesis as demonstrated once the inadmissibility of the contrary one has been proved, forgetting that other possibility of Stuart Mill’s, that third that is not excluded: lack of sense.

Let me read, then, some traditional proofs of the existence of God. Here is the one called a contingentia mundi [from the contingency of the world]:

“It is absolutely necessary that something should have existed from all eternity. Indeed: since something exists, it is clear that something has always existed; otherwise, one would have to say that the things that now exist came out of nothing and are absolutely without a cause of existence—which is a contradiction in terms.”

(Here the fallacy slips through: if the thesis of the non-existence of God is absurd or inadmissible for this or that reason, as is demonstrated, then God exists.)

“Therefore, if something has existed from all eternity, it must be that the being which has always existed is immutable and independent, or that there is an infinite succession of dependent beings or beings subject to change; but this last supposition is impossible, because....”, etc. “From this it follows that there must have existed from all eternity.”

Once again, the impossibility of proving one thesis having been demonstrated, the contrary one is taken as demonstrated.

[p. 80]

All the classical proofs are more or less analogous. The “proof from the prime mover,” of Aristotle:

“Everything that is in motion is moved by something (the demonstration follows): It turns out, then, that there is a middle term between the last thing moved and the prime mover; but this could not be infinite, and, therefore, it must arrive at a last term which is not moved by any other.”

It is easy to understand how the opponent could construct another argument that would have the same value as this one: it would be enough for him simply to begin from the other end, and say, the other way round: a first term that is not moved by any other is inconceivable; therefore, there must have existed an infinite number of movers.

The truth is that neither of the two demonstrations is conclusive: the one thesis as much as the other turns out to be for us either unthinkable or hardly thinkable, which may be due either to our mental deficiency or to the inadequacy of these verbal formulations; but, in the one case and in the other, that way of reasoning is paralogistic.

The proof “from human intelligence”: “God must exist because one could not understand where, in that whole which does not understand, this part which understands would come from.”

Indeed, we cannot understand where, in a whole that does not understand, the part that understands would come from; nor can we understand, either, the contrary thesis, because we cannot understand anything in this matter, at least not clearly.

The “atheists” argue the other way round, with exactly the same ease: they show that the idea of God is full of contradictions; that one cannot, for example, be at the same time absolute and creator, because the absolute is that which has in itself the reason for its existence, whereas, in order to create, one needs to create something; therefore, he who creates has a relation with something; therefore, he is not absolute... That one cannot be absolute and conscious, because to be conscious is to be conscious of something, and, consequently, to have a relation with something; con

[p. 81]

sciousness is, thus, a relation; therefore, the being that has consciousness is not absolute, but relative.... They show that there are the same contradictions among the would-be moral attributes of God: that one cannot be, for example, infinitely just and infinitely merciful, etc.; and, then, they take as demonstrated the thesis that God does not exist. In reality, what must be taken as demonstrated is that we do not understand, or that we do not know...

I could take examples from any other part of metaphysics. The proofs of the spirituality of the soul: Here is the “proof from the unity of thought”:

“The act of thinking consists essentially in reducing plurality to unity; what is consciousness itself, if not the act that unifies the plurality of sensations? Now then: the unity of thought presupposes the unity of the subject. But all corporeal matter is composed of parts. Therefore, it cannot be the subject of thought.”

It is, then, demonstrated that, since it is impossible for the subject of thought to be corporeal, it must be spiritual.

If one began the other way round, one would also find contradictions or impossibilities in the thesis of the spiritual subject; but the fallacy consists in seeing nothing but the unthinkability, absolute or relative, of one of the two theses, and in taking the other as thereby demonstrated.

The day all this is understood, metaphysics will be taught in another way; more: metaphysics will be different.

Perhaps in no discussion is the share of reason on each opposing side so impressive as in that never-silenced discussion between the adversaries and the partisans of metaphysics. Some defend it with good reasons, and others oppose it; and men have not yet realized that both sides are right: that those are right who de

[p. 82]

fend metaphysics, if it is a question of metaphysics as it ought to be, and ought to be taught; and that those who oppose metaphysics are not without reason, if one thinks of metaphysics as it is, as it has generally claimed to be; and, above all, as we practice it in teaching.

Metaphysics has committed the error of wanting to be precise, of wanting to be geometrical, posing questions and establishing verbal formulas affected almost universally by false precision, as are not only metaphysical demonstrations, but generally its very problems.

We may picture human knowledge as a sea, whose surface is very easy to see and describe. Beneath that surface, vision naturally becomes less and less clear; until, in a deep region, one no longer sees: one only glimpses (and, in another, deeper region, one will cease to see altogether).

If we imagine an observer of that sea who, trying to describe it, or a painter who, endeavoring to reproduce it, should obstinately insist on giving us, of the deep layers, a view or a representation as clear as of the surface layers, we would have the fundamental sophism, the central sophism of metaphysics.

Metaphysics is legitimate; more than legitimate: it constitutes and will always constitute the highest form of the activity of human thought, as long as it does not claim to have the appearance of clarity and precision of science; on the other hand, with the geometrical and falsely precise appearance that it has been sought to give it, metaphysics is simply the typical illustration, on the one hand, of the sophism of false precision that we have already studied, and, on the other, of the verbo-ideological fallacies.

[p. 83]

Thinking by Systems, and Thinking by Ideas to Be Taken into

Account

We are now going to study one of the most frequent causes of men’s errors, and above all of the poor use made of truths, in connection with the difference between thinking by systems and thinking by ideas to be taken into account.

There are two ways of making use of an accurate observation or of a sound reflection; the first is to draw from it, consciously or unconsciously, a system meant to be applied in all cases; the second, to reserve it, to note it down, consciously or unconsciously as well, as something that must be taken into account when one reflects in each case upon real and concrete problems.

Let us go at once into some examples.

Suppose it occurs to me to reflect that it is advisable in hygiene, in medicine, in teaching, in many other orders of activity or of thought, to follow nature. In favor of this tendency certain facts may be invoked and certain reasonings made. Facts: we would ascertain the superior adaptation of wild animals as compared with domesticated animals; in the human race itself, the much smaller amount of disease among savages than among civilized people; certain special ills of civilization, etc. And also, reflections: thus (we would tell ourselves), for some cause or other, and whatever the explanation one admits, whether the human race was created by a higher being who adapted it to the conditions in which it was to act, or whether it resulted from an evolution that naturally produced that same adaptation, it is a fact, in any case, that man is adapted

[p. 84]

to the world in which he lives; consequently, he should follow natural indications, he should not disturb, alter natural life, etc., etc.

Here are facts and reflections that are fairly sound. I was telling you that there are two ways of using them.

The first would be to make oneself a system (whether or not it bears a name ending in ism): to create, for example, a school, which might be called naturism, or something of the sort, and whose synthesis would be this: always, in all cases, we have an infallible guide in Nature.

And the second would be the following: for each case that presents itself to me, a case of dietetics, of hygiene, of medicine, of pedagogy, I propose to take into account the adaptation of man to natural conditions, and the tendency (note well, the tendency; because here we shall later find the whole question) of natural acts to be beneficial.

I ask you to analyze carefully the difference between these two states of mind.

At first sight, it seems that in the first case we are better equipped to think than in the second, since we have a fixed rule, we have a norm that allows us, it seems, to resolve all questions. When a case presents itself to us, we have only to apply our system. Is it good to be injected with a serum? No, because serums are not “natural”; the organism must be left to fight diseases itself. Is such-and-such a diet good? Yes (eating fruit), because it is natural; no (eating sweets), because it is not natural. How should we dress for warmth? According to the indications our organism will take care to give us: are we cold?... we wrap up; are we hot?... we do not wrap up.—See how easy it is, or seems, to think, in this case.

On the other hand, it seems that in the second way we have

[p. 85]

been left in uncertainty. “That idea must be taken into account...” in what cases? to what degree? within what limits?.... all this seems vague to us.

But, in practice (note this, for it is fundamental), he who has made himself, consciously or unconsciously, his system, has fatally condemned himself to one-sidedness and to error; he has condemned himself to think taking into account a single idea, which is the fatal way of being mistaken in ninety cases out of a hundred (it is enough, for the error to be fatal, that the reality in question should not be of an almost absolute simplicity).

He who became a “naturist” in our express and systematic sense would condemn himself never, ever to admit, for example, a surgical operation; never, ever to admit a remedy, an injection, etc. And what follows from this? That an excellent idea, such as that of following up to a certain point, to a certain degree, according to the cases, natural indications, has been spoiled, and, instead of being an instrument of truth, it has become for us an instrument of error; it has served us, for example, to destroy or to inhibit the action of many other truths.

How should one have thought? By reserving our idea. When the cases present themselves, and without prejudice to some general rules, which will never be too geometrical, we shall take our idea into account; it will serve us, for example, to combat the excessive tendency to artificial medication; to ask of a certain kind of medicine a return, in prudent and reasonable terms, to natural conditions, insofar as is possible and sensible; it will serve us to combat certain excesses, certain manias, I would venture to say, of science. And in such-and-such a particular case (for example: a certain dyspepsia) we shall say to ourselves: “No, this is not the case for taking many remedies; I prefer to follow a

[p. 86]

hygienic regimen”... Here I follow my idea. But another case will come, involving, for example, diphtheria, with its serum of an efficacy that may be considered proven; and in that case, yes, I accept the serum, in spite of that idea.

Now then: Humanity spoils the greater part of its accurate observations and of its reasonings through illegitimate systematizations. Let us try to understand how; let us try to understand the psychology of this fallacy, putting ourselves in the very position of the person who is thinking:

Let us take yet another case related to hygiene. How many times to all of us, on seeing certain excessive, undoubtedly exaggerated precautions to which the germ theory gave rise: that tendency to disinfect everything, to take care not to touch anything that may have germs, not to eat vegetables, not to eat fruit, not to drink water... how many times will the following not have occurred to us: “but, might it not be advisable to ingest germs permanently, with the object of producing a kind of attenuated and permanent vaccine, and so not to be defenseless in case virulent germs come into action? There are facts that seem to strengthen this opinion: some hygienists say that the inhabitants of Paris, as a consequence of the excessive purification of the water, are too prone, when they go out into the country, to contract typhoid, which is attributed to the excessive sterilization of the drinking water...”

Analyze this psychology; let us suppose we are thinking: immediately we feel a tendency to create a theory, the “theory of the permanent vaccine,” which, by itself, would tend to lead us to this consequence: “there is no need to guard against germs any more.” Note well how human, how psychological that process is! A good observation, excellent for having made a moderate and reasonable use of it, we have spoiled and

[p. 87]

have turned into a cause of error, and of baneful error.

In reality, we ought simply to have taken our observation into account, so as to guard ourselves against exaggerations; so as to guard ourselves, for example, against the opposite systematization, which followed the popularization of the germ theory. We would say to ourselves: “Yes: in the case of germs in their normal state, perhaps it is better habitually to drink unboiled water; but that does not mean that during a cholera epidemic, or in those cases in which the germs are likely to be more virulent, it is not prudent to guard ourselves against them.” In this way we think soundly; we think with many ideas, balancing them according to the cases; there remains, let us say, a kind of free play of ideas; they all function, now one predominating, now another: sometimes one must not be taken into account, and it disappears; sometimes another must predominate, and we shall take it alone into account: the ideas play and combine. In the other way, we think with a single idea, we systematize falsely and we fall fatally into error.

Take another case. We observe, as it is easy to observe today, that a certain contemporary pedagogy, too refined, tends to give everything to the child already digested; to prepare the assimilable material too much, and really to leave the pupil in the situation in which a healthy and normal being would be left who was fed on peptones and pap, from which an organic weakening would undoubtedly result: it is really a mental weakening that this exaggeratedly simplified pedagogy has tended to produce. And we would say to ourselves: “No: just as the organism seems to need substances that are not wholly digestible, so too it seems that the mind needs, as a ferment, the partially intelligible. Not everything should be wholly intelligible: it is good that there should be something that is not

[p. 88]

completely understood; that effort should remain, that penetration should remain.”

This idea is undoubtedly a good idea. But suppose that it is you yourselves who have observed the fact; analyze yourselves, and you will discover a falsifying psychological tendency that comes into play at once: whoever makes that observation tends immediately to construct a system, to base all education, the whole of teaching, on the “penetration of the partially intelligible”; and then, to the opposite system, to the system, let us say, of pedagogical peptonism, he will oppose a system that is also going to be exaggerated and falsifying.

Meanwhile, if we know how to think, we shall keep our observation, with the reflections that accompanied it, so as to take it into account in each case; and if we are spoken to, for example, about the teaching of Literature, we shall say: “Here, yes; this is the moment: let us avoid presenting everything digested, everything prepared, simplified in some small, easy textbook, with simplistic definitions and pigeonholes.” Next the case of Mathematics presents itself to us, and then we shall say: “No, here our idea is hardly applicable: in Mathematics it is better to proceed in order, taking everything step by step; penetration, the partially intelligible, has little to do with it here; it may have something to do with it in some cases, but here it is not going to be the guiding, predominant idea.” In this way we think well; we resolve each case well.

Note this: when we teach men to think in this way, at first sight they have the impression of being deprived of something they possessed before; they felt so secure and so tranquil with their systems (conscious or unconscious) that, when we teach them to think in another, better way, they believe something has been taken from them, and they continually ask for the formula, the rule, the system, that would spare them the examination of the

[p. 89]

cases. But, in reality, no teaching in the world is capable of qualifying anyone for this last result; what well-understood teaching can do is to leave people qualified to think: not to suppress thought, but to teach how to use it.

The paralogistic tendency we are analyzing has been observed, above all, in the cases that are, let us say, coarse; in the cases in which, exaggerated, it leads to its natural aboutissement [outcome], which are the great general systems, closed, crystallized, such as are observed in science and above all in philosophy.

But the object of my lessons is not precisely to analyze the logic and psychology of these great systems, nor to show the state in which they put the mind: this has already been done, and well done. If we had time, I would read you passages that would show you to what point the human mind degenerates and is perverted by thinking in this way: to what point—what seems impossible—we even become incapable of observing: not merely of reasoning, but of observing reality itself, though it stare us in the face. I should like, for example, to be able to quote here certain passages on the problem of instinct. If you were to read the naturalists and biologists (and the philosophers too) of some fifty years ago, a very curious phenomenon would strike you; and it is that almost all of them denied animal instinct. In the works of Buchner, for example; in those of Lewes; in Brehem’s “Life of Animals” (to cite a popular work for you), you will find things that today seem inconceivable to us. These authors try to deny, for example, the heredity of instinct: It is not true, they tell us, that a newly hatched duck has a tendency to throw itself into the water: a duck raised by a hen flees from the water; nor is it true that chicks, on hatching, peck their food accurately: we see them learn. Birds learn to make their nest with considerable labor, very often making mistakes...., etc.

[p. 90]

Now then: those stupendous errors of observation, do you know why they were committed? For the following reason: At that time, many biologists, naturalists, philosophers, etc., followed the “materialist” movement, opposed to theological explanations. Now then: instinct had until then been explained by the intervention of the Creator: God was supposed to have given each animal the instincts necessary to guide it;—and no other explanation was known: those of Darwin, those of the evolutionists, the natural explanation of instincts by means of evolution, natural selection, adaptation, direct heredity, etc., had not yet arisen. Consequently, instinct had to be combated: instinct could not exist, since until then it had been explained only by theological causes, and, since God did not exist, or since it had to be proved that he did not exist, instinct could not exist. And so all those men, some of them naturalists who spent their lives observing animals, did not see instinct, did not see heredity, and denied it in their works. And, very probably—surely—they were sincere: today it occurs to us that there must have been scientific insincerity there; no: it is that this is the state into which systems put us.

And I will show you another example. The type of systems, as regards their effects, is undoubtedly the religious systems; they are the most closed of all, the ones that most enslave the mind. I am going to read you some passages from a Spanish philosopher who has precisely the merit of having been the first who undertook—and who carried out in some part—what we are doing modestly here, that is, to create a living logic, a logic drawn from reality, with examples from reality and dispensing with the purely verbal schemata of traditional logic.

I am referring to Balmes, and to his work “El Criterio.”

[p. 91]

It is a book that, for us above all, is of great interest. It is built on the basis that informs the following paragraph:

“When authors treat of this operation of the understanding...” (he is referring to reasoning) “they heap up many rules to direct it, supporting them on certain axioms. I shall not dispute the truth of the latter; but I very much doubt that the usefulness of the former is as great as has been claimed. Indeed, it is undeniable that things which are identical with a third are identical with each other; that of two things which are identical with each other, if the one is distinct from a third, so too will the other be; that what is affirmed or denied of a whole genus or species must be affirmed or denied of the individual contained in them; and it is moreover very true that the rules of argumentation founded on these principles are infallible. But my difficulty lies in the application; and I cannot convince myself that they are of great usefulness in practice.

“In the first place, I confess that these rules help to give the understanding a certain precision that may serve in some cases to conceive more clearly, and to attend to the vices that a piece of reasoning may contain: though at times this advantage will be neutralized by the drawbacks brought on by the presumption that one knows how to reason because one is not ignorant of the rules of reasoning. One may know the rules of an art very well, and not succeed in putting them into practice. A man might recite all the rules of oratory without mistaking a word, who could not write a page without clashing, I will not say with the precepts of the art, but with good sense.”

This reading alone shows you that we are not dealing with an inferior mind; this is revealed, first, by what he set out to do: and second (you see it here already), by this tendency to think without exaggerations, taking into account one thing and another, stopping at the right degree, not, for example, denying all importance whatever to the rules of logic, giving them the importance that it seems to him, more or less, they may have; nor exaggerating that importance either. Well then: this book has an extremely curious composition, a curious plan. The author goes along making reflections on many theoretical and practical questions, reflections generally extremely sensible, which show, above all, very good judgment; these reflections then appear to him as dangerous to the religious system that

[p. 92]

he professes, and then he stops, before concluding each chapter, to make reservations and to try to prove to the reader, with reasonings that in that case become logically horrible, that what was said before does not apply, as may have occurred to some reader of critical mind, to Catholicism. The whole work, one may say, follows this plan:

“For these considerations one must proceed with great caution in declaring a phenomenon naturally impossible. It is well not to forget: 1st, that nature is very powerful: 2nd, that it is very little known to us: two truths that ought to inspire great circumspection in us when it comes to passing judgment in matters of this kind. If a man of the fifteenth century had been told that in times to come the distance of twelve leagues would be covered in an hour...”

He continues in this spirit. But, before he finishes, he will not fail to make his reservation:

“From these observations there seems to arise a difficulty, which the unbelievers have not forgotten. Here it is: miracles are perhaps effects of causes which, though unknown, will be no less natural for that; therefore they do not prove divine intervention; and therefore they are of no use in supporting the truth of the Christian religion. This argument is as specious as it is futile.”

.... And then comes the refutation, which, as I tell you, is logically, and at times even morally, very inferior to the rest of the book.

Now then: I am going to show you only two or three passages, among so many characteristic ones. Look, first of all, at these, which are good sense itself. The author makes two observations; the first is this (in my judgment, something that ought to be repeated constantly):

“Just as in mathematics there are two ways of solving a problem: one by hitting upon the true solution, the other by showing that the solution is impossible, so it happens in every sort of question: there are many whose best solution is to show that for us they are insoluble. And let it not be thought that this last is without merit, and that it is easy to discern between the attainable and the unattainable: whoever is capable of it gives a sign that he knows thoroughly the matter in question, and that he has occupied himself at length in the examination of its principal questions.”

Other reflections (which also ought to be repeated continually):

[p. 93]

“Prejudice in favor of a doctrine.—Here is one of the most abundant springs of error; this is the true drag upon the sciences; one of the obstacles that most retard their progress. The influence of prejudice would be incredible, if the history of the human mind did not attest it with unimpeachable facts.

“The man dominated by a prejudice seeks neither in books nor in things what is really there, but what suits him for supporting his opinions. And what is most regrettable is that he behaves in this fashion at times with the greatest good faith, believing without a shadow of doubt that he is working for the cause of truth. Education, the teachers and authors from whom we received our first lights on a science, the persons with whom we live continually, or with whom we deal most frequently, our station or profession, and other similar circumstances, contribute to engendering in us the habit of looking at things always under the same aspect, of seeing them always in the same way.

..... “If it were not so, how will it be possible”.... (note this, for it is most remarkable)... “to explain that for long centuries there have been seen schools as organized as disciplined armies around a banner? How is it that a series of men illustrious for their learning and virtues should all see a question in the same way, while their adversaries, no less enlightened than they, saw everything in an opposite way? How is it that to know what the opinions of an author were, we did not need to read him, it being enough for us, as a rule, to know the order to which he belonged, or the school from which he had come? Could it be ignorance of the matter, when they spent their lives in studying it? Could it be that they did not read the works of their adversaries? This would be the case with many, but of others there is no doubt that they consulted them frequently. Could it be bad faith? Certainly not.....”, etc.

Well then: now you are not going to believe that I am reading the same man. From the apology for Catholicism... (I take two or three out of an immense quantity of arguments that, you may believe me on my good faith, are all more or less of the same strength. In parenthesis, I point out to you that I cite these arguments only as a logical document, and I do not want to omit saying that I believe a defense of religion could have been attempted with arguments infinitely superior to these):

“Besides, Catholics maintain that outside the Church there is no salvation, Protestants affirm that Catholics too can

[p. 94]

be saved; and thus they themselves recognize that among us nothing is believed or practiced that can bring eternal damnation upon us.”

(Attention, now!)

“They, in favor of their salvation, have only their own vote; we, on behalf of ours, have theirs and our own; even if we judged solely on grounds of human prudence, it would advise us not to abandon the faith of our fathers.”

Imagine an argumentation made entirely in this form! I am no longer referring to its logical character: we are dealing here with arguments so amorphous, let us say, that it is not even possible to criticize them; but note even the state of mind into which he has put himself; how this man has gone and looked for precisely a point at which his religion is inferior to the other, the point at which it is evidently narrower, and out of that inferiority he tries to make a superiority. If Protestantism has allowed its adherents the breadth of judgment necessary not to believe condemned to the torments of hell those who through ignorance or through error do not profess its religion; if Catholicism, from this point of view, is inferior to it, for every well-made mind, inasmuch as it holds (according to the author, here) that those who do not follow it will be damned, —from all that, anything at all might be drawn, except an argument in favor of Catholicism against Protestantism. And yet this is the same author who has described to us so well the state of mind into which the adherent of a system is put!

Look here:

“In the examination of religious matters many follow a mistaken path. They take a dogma as the object of their investigations, and the difficulties they raise against it they believe sufficient to destroy the truth of religion; or at least to put it in doubt. This is to proceed in a manner that shows how little one has meditated on the state of the question.

“Indeed: it is not a matter of knowing whether the dogmas are within the reach of our intelligence, nor whether we give a complete solution to all the difficulties that may be objected against this one or that: religion itself is the first to tell us that we cannot comprehend these dogmas by the light of reason alone; that while we are

[p. 95]

in this life, we must resign ourselves to seeing the secrets of God through shadows and enigmas, and that is why it requires faith of us. To say, then, ‘I will not believe because I do not understand,’ is to state a contradiction; if you understood everything, clearly there would be no talk of faith. To argue against religion on the ground of the incomprehensibility of its dogmas is to reproach it with a truth that it itself recognizes, that it accepts, and on which, in a certain way, it makes its edifice rest.”

Well then: this is the same man who told us a moment ago that there is a certain class of questions in which the true solution is not to solve them; he is the same who made us feel that at times the difficulties of thought are so great that human dignity itself requires us not to pronounce; and he is the one who should have been fatally led to tell us, and to make us feel, that if there is a typical case of questions of that kind, it is the questions concerning first realities, in which there is room for hypothesis, possibility, supposition, sentiment, and hope if you like, but never, ever, for absolute and closed conviction in this completely inferior form.

And I am not speaking to you of metaphysical systems. Traditional Metaphysics—a curious thing!—the branch of knowledge that is most ignorant, is the one that has tried to present knowledge to us with the greatest appearance of clarity and precision; and it has always been the one most concerned to dissemble its ignorance, and to dissemble it from itself.

We have already compared human knowledge to a sea, in which what happens at the surface can be seen and described clearly: as the depth increases, one sees less clearly: down there at the bottom, one glimpses, less and less, until one ceases to see altogether. So that, if whoever wished to describe or draw those realities presented to us the realities of the bottom with the same precision, with the same clarity, with the same sharpness of outline as

[p. 96]

the realities of the surface,—I mean to say: whoever gave us a metaphysics resembling science,—we could affirm infallibly that he would give us error, instead of the partial truth of which we are capable.

And the human mind completes everything, symmetrizes everything; it is like those children’s kaleidoscopes, in which each little colored stone is multiplied several times, on all sides, symmetrically, and where it is impossible, however much the sand is shaken, to obtain an asymmetrical or incomplete figure.

I once wrote the following (giving it perhaps a form, owing perhaps to literary preoccupations: somewhat sibylline, for which I have been called to account; and I take this opportunity to speak about it more clearly and more plainly):

What did I mean by this, which has every appearance of madness? Just this: that it would be necessary, that there will have to come some day an epoch in which philosophers know that they do not know everything, and say so; that, just as a man of science, speaking, for example, of the satellites of Neptune, can say, as the most natural thing in the world: “I have observed one satellite, but I do not know whether there may be another,” or can say: “At such a time it seemed to me that I was observing a satellite; but later, in another observation, it seemed to me that it had been an optical illusion”; that some day, gentlemen, philosophers may also speak in this way; that it may occur to them to do so; and that they may make up their minds to it; that they may give us their thought, not artificially falsified, but just as it really is; that a philosopher may, for example, say: “On reaching this point of the analysis, I can no longer think clearly,” and give us his confused thought when it is

[p. 99]

really confused; that he may say: “Symmetry would lead me here to maintain that.... but....”—do you understand?— Without prejudice to having his convictions, at times: (“on the other question, yes, it seems evident to me...”). That he may also change in the face of an objection, that he may yield before an argument, that he may do what a man of science does when another man of science sees the satellite he had not seen: confess that it exists. That he may tell us that there are points on which he wavers, on which he has no settled opinions....

And I pointed out, in the rest of this fantasy, the advisability, too, of something else. Often, on comparing the notes that serve for the preparation of works, which are made sincerely and naturally, one sees that there is something, no doubt, that is gained, from the notes to the work; but that there is also something that is lost: all that part of sincerity, of doubts, of ignorance; the author’s oscillations, his very changes of opinion, the arguments against certain opinions, even when he decides in favor of the arguments for them; all this is lost from the notes to the books (and part of it will already have been lost from the soul to the notes).

And I thought that philosophy will be completely different, will have been born anew,—or will have been born, simply,— on the day when philosophers are able to give us their whole soul, all that they think and even all that they feel, all that they psyche, I shall say, to use a more comprehensive verb.

Imagine, if you will, that a Kant had not given us only his systematization; imagine that we could know today, not only about the divisions Kant made, how he separated the mind into compartments, how he put up partitions and how he said that A was this, that B was that and that C was divided into first, second and third, but that we had known what

[p. 100]

Kant doubted, and what Kant did not know; and, above all, how he did not know: how profitable this would be to us, as thinking ferment! Kant’s theories have done their good; they have also done their harm; and a moment has come in which they have perhaps ceased to be useful to humanity; but that thinking ferment would have been of eternal usefulness. If we could see the psychological fringe, the penumbra, the halo, what lies around what is absolutely clear; if we could know today, for example, how a Bergson thinks, what doubts he has, in what contradictions he sometimes finds himself entangled (from which he saves himself by this or that artifice of logic).... That was the “future book,” and that must be the philosophy of the future....

But I have spoken of systems only incidentally; my interest was above all to have you study the psychological process by which the mind gradually falls into certain states. Above all, I did not wish to deal with the classified systems, but, as one would say in legal terminology, with the innominate systems. In law, besides the contracts that have a name, there are others: the innominate contracts. Well, in Psycho-logic, there are innominate systems: those which float, vague, imprecise, through all minds, and form at every step, condense like nebulosities of the mind and prevent us from seeing and thinking with accuracy.

Note what is perhaps the most important thing to be observed in this respect. When one thinks as I have recommended to you, by ideas to be taken into account, not by systems, there immediately arise, in the immense majority of cases, questions of degree. As long as one thinks by systems, they do not: one has a ready-made system, and one applies it in every case, because only one idea is taken into account and one thinks with that idea alone; but when one thinks with many ideas, when one thinks with all the possible ideas, then

[p. 101]

the questions of degree immediately arise.

Is it good (a simple example from everyday life), is it good to praise beginning writers, even when what they do is worth little? Is it good, on the contrary, to criticize them severely?

Notice how easily I could make a little system to prove either of these two things. I would reason thus: “It is good to praise writers who are just starting out; it is true that what they produce is worth little, but what matters in these cases is, above all, encouragement; most great writers began by producing weak works: perhaps, if they had been criticized severely, they would have been inhibited, and they would have been cut short in their career, perhaps...”:—I can go on along these lines indefinitely. Now, I can make the opposite reasoning, also with equal ease: “Beginning writers should be criticized with the greatest possible severity; in this way we prevent the mind from going astray, in that period of thoughtless youth, along mistaken paths, or anyone from imagining he has a vocation he does not really have, or from acquiring an excessive and vicious facility, or a tendency not to observe himself, not to concentrate; it is advisable that they be corrected at the age when correction can still produce its effects....”;—I can go on reasoning as long as I like.

In reality, how must one think? One must take into account all these possible effects, good and bad, of benevolent criticism and of severe criticism; and, according to the cases and the moments, praise more or less, according to the free play of these two ideas, which tend to balance each other.

At once, then, the question of degree arises; and the question of degree cannot be solved in a geometrical way. The only thing that can be formulated is this: “For, there are such and such reasons; against, there are such and such others; one must

[p. 102]

take them into account, the ones and the others; think and act sensibly according to the cases.”

Any other analogous practical problem occurs to us: “Should the secondary schools be very demanding in the entrance examinations of their pupils, or, on the contrary, should they be lenient?”

Thinking with a single idea, I can apparently prove anything. With great ease I would prove that it is good to be rigorously demanding in such cases:—“it would bring disadvantages for all the classrooms, if educational institutions admitted badly prepared pupils; these same pupils, in the first place, would hinder all the others; they would prevent the classes from following their regular course; they would make the teacher fall behind with useless explanations; in the second place, the pupils themselves would suffer a grave harm: they could never profit from the teaching in the conditions in which they would normally profit from it,” etc.—and I go on in this way as long as I like. With equal ease I could prove the contrary: “At that age, the aptitudes that show themselves have little to do with the real ones, which will show themselves later on; it would be regrettable for an educational institution to reject, because he did not know well or because he did not know something, a child who may perhaps be infinitely more intelligent than another child who seems so at that moment. Moreover, one must take into account that at that age the child is defenseless, he depends on the face the examiner shows him, on a gesture, on the way he speaks to him, on the way he questions him: if they do not put the questions to him in the form in which his teacher used to put them, he will not answer; he is not like a pupil in the upper years, who knows how to defend himself; fear produces much more effect on the small child...”—I can also go on indefinitely.

In reality, what must be done, and this is the difficult part, is to balance these ideas; and, for this, neither I

[p. 103]

nor anyone else is capable of giving a formula: the more or less just, more or less sensible solution is found, in the cases of practical life, by taking into account all the reasonings; for example, those we made in one direction and the other in the preceding case. No one can be exempted from the task of thinking; no ready-made system can be given where there is a question of degree.

And these questions of degree always tend to take a similar form: Is it advisable to bathe, for example: to wash the body? I can set about reasoning in this form: “The pores are extremely useful...” (I explain the function of the pores); “perspiration is extremely useful”... (I explain the function of perspiration) ...“Consequently, it is necessary to remove from the skin everything that accumulates on it and prevents those functions from being carried out in the best possible way; one must, then, bathe as frequently as one can: at every moment.” But could one not arrive at exaggeration, even with so reasonable an idea? It would be possible, no doubt, that certain substances secreted by the skin perform a certain protective function; it would be possible that, if we removed them too frequently, if a person bathed or rubbed himself down too many times...” There will come, then, a degree at which that so reasonable practice may become exaggerated; but how many times are those “too many”? A question of degree, for which there is no formula.

Should one always act in accordance with the ideal? It is a problem, and a great problem: whether one is always to act taking into account what ought to be, or whether one is to take into account the real circumstances. Here there are two fatal exaggerations: I can make a system: “always and in every case I must act as if all men were good and as if all men were intelligent, always asking for the best that can be obtained. Let us suppose that a people, for example, wishes to reform

[p. 104]

its electoral system: it must ask for the most perfect system; let us disregard whether the circumstances permit it or do not permit it, whether men are or are not disposed to grant it.” This is a systematic theory; with it, I am going to fall into an exaggeration, since at times, by asking for everything, one obtains nothing. Now we can make the contrary theory: “No; let us not think about ideals; what must be asked for is what can be obtained at a given moment; one must take into account only the practical circumstances, and the psychology of men.” In this case, I go off to another theory, as bad as the first or worse, because in fact asking for, and seeking, and longing for ideals, is what modifies practice itself; and in a great many cases, if everything were not asked for, not even something would be obtained. What must be done, then? No doubt, take the ideals into account, and also take into account the practical circumstances; and balance them. But to what degree? According to what formula? No one can give it: that is thought and felt in each case.

Now: what is to be deduced from this?

One could deduce a kind of apology for good sense or for common sense; but not for vulgar common sense, or, rather, for common sense vulgarly understood, but for another, higher common sense: what I would call, not infra-logical common sense, but hyper-logical common sense. Bad common sense, the kind that with so much reason has been the object of the stigma of philosophy and of science, the kind that has denied all the truths and all the discoveries and all the ideals of the human mind, is common sense irreconcilable with logic: the kind that does not admit reasoning. But there is another common sense that comes after reasoning, or, better, together with it. When we have seen and weighed by reasoning the reasons for and the reasons against that exist in almost all cases; when we have done all

[p. 105]

the logic (the good logic) possible, when the questions become questions of degree, there comes a moment in which a kind of instinct—what I call hyper-logical common sense—is what resolves the questions for us in concrete cases. And it would be good if logic did not deprive men of this higher form of common sense.

The day people came to think as we wish, many disciplines of the human mind would take on a different aspect. One would be Metaphysics, of which I have already spoken to you. Another would be Ethics.

Ethics has been made up to now by closed systems, each of which has condemned itself to take into account only one of the possible factors of conduct. A theory has arisen, and has said: “The only factor that must be taken into account is the divine will; the others, no.” And another theory: “The only factor that must be taken into account is duty.” Another: “No: the only factor that must be taken into account is personal pleasure.” Another, still: “The only factor that must be taken into account is collective utility.” And Spencer has told us: “The factor that must be taken into account is progress.” And Guyau has told us: “The factor that must be taken into account is the expansion of life.” Meanwhile, all those factors exist; and if we think, not by systems, but by ideas to be taken into account,—see now how our subject has grown on us! —we shall understand that man on earth must take into account progress, the expansion of life, personal pleasure, collective utility, etc., and furthermore all the hypotheses, possibilities or hopes that relate to the unknown. Now, how is this combined in living ethics? No one is capable of presenting it to us formulated with numbers or with letters; but whoever knows how to think in this way, even without formulas, will be the one who has the most

[p. 106]

chance, will be the one who has at least some chance that the teaching of ethics will bite into his soul.

Another extremely interesting observation is this: man defends himself so badly against the fallacy of false systematization that there is even a kind of glory, not the best glory, but without doubt a form of it: a cause of persistence in the memory of men, which depends on that very fallacy. It is a very curious phenomenon. In our programs of instruction, or in the histories of human thought, there figure with rather marked preference certain great systems of such a nature that what has prevented their dissolution—their oblivion— is, one might say, a certain kind of indigestibility. Suppose that a Rousseau, for example, had made a series of practical reflections, sensible, well observed, moderate and exact, on the role of the will in the origin of societies, or on the role we ought to give to nature in education; those observations would have been studied and used; they would probably have been given back in action by the men who had assimilated them; and—an interesting thing!—it is possible that, in that case, the name of Rousseau would not be so much remembered today. But he was, this Rousseau, a formidable exaggerator; every time he saw an idea, he rushed headlong toward it and swept everything before him. So he constructed his theory of the social contract, according to which society has no other origin than the human will; he constructed his theory of “natural” education, in which there figures that Emile whom his tutor would rather see dead than see him enter the shop of a candle seller, because the light of candles is not “natural”.... Well then: the mind of man is such that this assures its author a form of glory: negative glory, if you like; but the fact is that there is not a program in which

[p. 107]

certain theories of Rousseau do not figure, even if only to make their “refutation.” Humanity has not been able to eliminate those theories. And this resembles a difficult digestion. A nourishing and easily assimilable substance is converted in the organism into force and energy, and that force and energy are used, and the substance disappears; others cannot be dissolved, and they remain. And, like them, certain theories: the ones of Rousseau already mentioned, the law of Malthus, the utopias of Fourier and a great number of systems of social reorganization, are something like formidable ideological boluses that humanity could not digest.

All this should be summed up, and the summary is very simple. What I try to teach you, that is, to think with all the ideas one can, taking them all into account, taking them as tendencies, in each case, balancing them, adapting them, is very easy to understand. If it is difficult to apply, it is, above all, because it is hard for the human mind to free itself from the impression of abandonment in which it seems to find itself once it is left free.

Suppose a generation of schoolteachers trained solely and exclusively in the theory of Rousseau. They would have been taught that there is an absolute criterion: the return to nature; and they would have fixed, ready-made, infallible rules, which would lead them, for example, even to prevent their pupils from buying candles, because lighting oneself with them is not natural. Let us suppose that a person of good sense appears in that school, and says to them: “Gentlemen: you have been accustomed to think badly. Undoubtedly, one must take into account nature and natural indications; but that is not all that must be taken into account, if only because we live in conditions that are in part artificial,

[p. 108]

in which what was artificial is perhaps becoming natural, in which one does not know well what is natural and what is artificial, in which perhaps these words have no clear meaning...”; in short, suppose he taught them to think: “Take into account the one thing and the other: what nature is, what man was before, but also what he can be, what he ought to be and what he wants to be....” The interesting thing is this: that those pupils would at first sight feel as if forsaken. “All right: this gentleman takes away our rule; but, now what do we do? what does he give us in exchange?” If it is difficult to think as I recommend to you, it is because it is difficult to free oneself from the impression that whoever is deprived of one-sided systems, or of the habit of making them, loses something. The important thing then is to feel that one gains.

That impression, from which one must know how to free oneself, is so strong that at times the very person who is trying to dispel it feels it. I myself, who have given you all this lucubration, have sometimes found, while teaching a course in Pedagogy in which I tried as far as I possibly could to teach how to see everything, to teach how to see things from different sides, to teach how to reconcile the different ideas in practice,—I have found, I confess, that I felt something like the fear of giving a kind of negative teaching. Saying to myself: here I am teaching insistently what is not to be done: “One must not exaggerate this thing, one must not exaggerate that other”; but what have I taught them to do?

I am not speaking of that reproach having been made to me, which is very common; the point is that I myself, at times, have made it to myself, but only owing to that tendency of the human mind, which is so false. Whoever has grown accustomed to thinking by ideas to be taken into account, instead of thinking systematically, has not lost: he has gained; and whoever can feel it will have profited from

[p. 109]

all that I have tried to explain to you in these lessons.

Logic and Psychology in Discussions, etc.

In discussions, as in argumentations, speeches, etc., in the verbal communication of men, the logical value or scope of what is said is one thing, the psychological effect it produces is another. There is not always coincidence; although in many cases there will be. It is good to grow accustomed to understanding this well, and to knowing how to observe it and recognize it well in each case.

No doubt, the fact in itself, and its recognition by all men, is commonplace. To cite a single example: the ancient conception of oratory was based on the psychological effect rather than on the logical one; and, consciously or unconsciously, it distinguished them. A good speech, in criminal matters, for example, was not so much the one that proved that the accused was not guilty, as the one that could move the judges to pity and clemency. Even in modern oratory, more or less the same thing happens in criminal trials before juries. There are also found, in certain treatises, rules regarding the arrangement of the proofs: not to place all the best ones at the beginning, nor to reserve them all for the end, but to place some of those better proofs at the beginning, then the weaker or doubtful ones, and at the end other good ones, etc.; all these rules, which still survive, with a more or less archaic character, in some treatises on rhetoric, imply the recognition of the commonplace fact I was pointing out to you.

But we are not concerned with facts, like these last, of a more or less conventional and fictitious character, but with what happens in everyday discussions in

[p. 110]

reality. It is good to realize how, in discussions, logic and psychology can take different paths, can fail to coincide; how, for example, at a certain moment, without the logical position of the question being altered in the least, a psychological shift may have been produced favorable or unfavorable to one of the theses.

A simple case is that of the presentation of inadequate examples, comparisons or arguments. At a given moment, one of the parties to the discussion presents, in favor of his thesis, an inadequate or ill-chosen example or argument; and it is proved, or felt, that it was so. In reality, the fact that a bad argument has been made in favor of a thesis does not prove that the thesis is bad; it neither adds anything to it nor takes anything from it: logically, the question remains as before; but, psychologically, a change is produced (generally), a change of the psychological state: this becomes somewhat more contrary to the thesis in favor of which the bad example was put forward, or was badly used, or some party to the discussion intervened clumsily, etc.

The same as regards an ill-chosen occasion for upholding a thesis. I remember that some time ago the following very nearly happened to me: I was indignant then (as I am today) at the people who cut down trees in this country: the municipalities, the owners of country estates, the ranchers, etc.; and there had written itself in mente [in my mind] an article which I have had to restrain myself from publishing: it was entitled “A National Imbecility,” and, from the title, its tone may be imagined. Well: one day, the article very nearly escaped me, on the occasion of the cutting down of trees in a certain square. But I learned in time, luckily, that the Legislative Palace was going to be built on that square, and that, consequently, in that particular case, it had been necessary to cut down the trees. Now let us suppose that my article had been published, precisely

[p. 111]

in that case, one of the very rare ones in which it would have been wrongly applied, (because that cutting down of trees was necessary). Logically, that had nothing to do with it: the “national imbecility” went on being so as much as it is. Nevertheless, psychologically, my article lost its force; it even became, perhaps, counterproductive.

Well then: with regard to these psychological effects, distinct from the logical ones, it is good to grow accustomed to examining them in an express and conscious way, with the aim of knowing how to recognize them, and even to foresee them, and of acquiring (or improving it, if one already has it) a kind of instinct that makes one feel the psychology of discussions, and, in general, of communication among men.

The preceding example was a crude example. At other times, the same thing happens on account, not of an openly inopportune example, but of one that may be misinterpreted, or of a comparison which, though perhaps adequate, comes in fact to shift the psychology. I remember a case in which I was arguing, in an official body, about examinations: I was upholding the system of the teacher’s judgment in class, as preferable to the system of annual examinations. At a certain moment, I had a very good position in the discussion, and someone made me an argument—which was, precisely, an example of one of the commonest sophisms into which men fall. I was saying: “examinations are an extremely uncertain, doubtful datum with respect to the pupil’s capacity or knowledge; in judging that hazardous act, the greatest errors are committed”; and one of my opponents answers me: “that is true: in the examination there is that: but in the system of judgment in class, there always remains something of chance; the evil is not entirely suppressed: consequently... etc.” (By the way; this was, as I have said, a sophism: because it is not possible to suppress

[p. 112]

the evil absolutely, to do nothing: a very common and baneful error). I answer with a comparison which, rightly taken (taken in what was pertinent in it), might have produced a very good effect: “one day,—I say to my opponent—a minister of the Tsar of Russia proposed to him that he introduce constitutional government, in order to suppress the evils of absolutism, the rebellions of the people, popular discontent; and, then, the Tsar answered him that, in the nations that have constitutional monarchy, there are also rebellions and popular discontent, even if in a lesser degree; that, consequently, constitutional monarchy did not suppress the evil.”

You understand what I was aiming at with this comparison: I wanted to point out that, even though an evil is not suppressed, it is always good to attenuate it.

But lo and behold, my adversary answers me very quickly: “Sir: why, the partisan of absolute monarchy turns out to be you! because it is you who want to set up the teacher as an absolute monarch, by having him pronounce on the pupils all by himself; whereas the system of examinations represents constitutional monarchy, because the teacher has at his side two examiners who check him.”

This was to take the comparison under a completely “topsy-turvy” and irrelevant aspect: such an answer, logically, was nothing but an absurdity: the value of my comparison, logically, suffered nothing, as was natural; but I felt immediately (one feels that by a kind of instinct) that at that moment I was losing ground,—and indeed, so it was: the apparent aptness, the wit, if you like, of my opponent’s example, made me fall back a long way psychologically.

Now, it is good to grow accustomed to seeing this class of facts consciously; also to feeling them; and in classes in logic certain

[p. 113]

exercises would be advisable, which we do not do here for lack of time, but which would enter into the plan of a teaching of living logic. For example: to give the class a discussion (if possible, taken from reality; if not, arranged by the teacher), for the pupils to analyze it, following, determining and appraising separately its psychological course and its logical course. “At this moment,”—the student would have to observe—“such and such a thesis, logically, is in the same state as before: it has not been refuted, nor put in danger; but, psychologically, it has been harmed by such and such a cause,” etc.

Another case, also related to logical and psychological effects: the effect produced by the manner of presenting opinions, arguments, projects, etc.

I remember a passage in War and Peace, in which Tolstoy reveals that observation of genius which makes him one of the foremost concrete psychologists in the world. Hélène, the wife of Pierre Bezukhov, tired of her husband and wishing to divorce, presents her case to a minister of the Church in the following form: “Whom do you think I ought to marry when I divorce Pierre: A, or B?” And Tolstoy points out, with great penetration, that if Hélène had said to her priest: “I do not love my husband: Do you think I should divorce?”, the priest would have answered her immediately: “Divorce is a crime; it is forbidden by the Church”; etc. But, with the question put in that other form—taking the divorce for granted, and shifting the psychology of the person addressed onto the other question, namely, whom it will be best to marry, given the divorce, —then there is a great chance of getting the divorce accepted.

Well then: facts of this kind are not very rare, for example, in corporate bodies, where projects are discussed, where petitions are dealt with (in

[p. 114]

parenthesis, I need not tell you that I teach you certain things, not so that you may use them as tricks, but so that you may know how to defend yourselves against them). At this moment, for example, I am in doubt as to whether, under the present legislation on pensions, I, who am covered by the special law on school pensions, could legally transfer to the general law on civil pensions, which would suit me; now then: if I present my petition asking in it that I be permitted to pass from the first law to the second, my request will give rise to discussions; and it is almost certain that it would not give rise to any, if I came forward, for example, consulting the Board of the Civil Fund as to whether, in the event of my passing from the other institution to that one, I would or would not have the right to have the monthly installments I paid into the other refunded to me. In that way, it is quite easy psychologically that, by shifting the attention of the members of the Board onto this problem, derived from the favorable solution of the main one, I should get them to accept that favorable solution without examination.

Also in corporate bodies one often observes facts of the following order: I fear, or consider bad, a project that has been presented, and I wish to modify it. There are two ways of opening the discussion: one is to announce that I am going to oppose the project, or to propose radical modifications; another is to declare myself in agreement with the project in general, to say that I find it good, and that, in accordance with its own spirit and completing the author’s thought, I am going to propose two or three modifications, etc. Well: even though they be exactly the same, those modifications, whether I present them as contrary to the project, or as complementary to the project, completely changes my probability of success. To call my modifications “amplifications” makes it easy for me to have the author of the project on my side.... I have seen an extreme, an enormous case. There

[p. 115]

had been presented in a corporate body a program that increased the knowledge required in a certain examination, the grounds for that increase being stated. And a member of the same body, who believed that increase to be bad, presents a substitute program in which the knowledge required was reduced, declaring it to be in accordance with the purposes of the author of the first project, and suited to realizing them still better. The express and sole grounds of the project consisted only in the necessity of increasing the knowledge required in that examination; notwithstanding which, the author joined in voting for the “substitute” project. Naturally, such extreme cases will not occur frequently; but, in other, less excessive ones, the fact is very common.

Another thing I have also seen: There is a project which I consider good. I fear that it may be discussed on its fundamentals. Well, one way of preventing the discussion from going to the fundamentals is to open it, myself, on some secondary application or some detail, in this form, for example: “what is debatable in this project is such and such a thing; now then: on that point...,” and I open a formidable discussion about the secondary question. Everyone discusses that, and it is more likely that the rest, the main thing, will get through without anyone noticing.

The manner of presenting projects, plans, modifications: novices in discussions, or persons who are too sincere, have a tendency to present their innovations in a very frank way, as true innovations or revolutionary modifications; in such cases, the projects have much less chance of passing (even though they be exactly the same) than when they are presented starting, expressly or tacitly, from the principle that they are in agreement with what the others think; above all, to say that one is going to present a project “to which probably

[p. 116]

those present will be opposed,” or “which represents a radical modification,” etc., is to make sure of a great likelihood that it will not be adopted.

At other times, to suppose the contrary opposition is to create it by suggestion. To say, for example, as some do (it has happened to me several times, through excess of sincerity): “I know I am going to be alone; I know nobody is going to follow me here....” At once, to the opposition that would have arisen naturally, there is added an artificial opposition, which is suggested.

Cases like the following have even happened to me: in opposing something, to suppose (whether unconsciously, or deliberately because the course of the discussion gives occasion for it) that one’s adversaries hold a theory even more exaggerated or more absurd than the one they hold in reality; and, in that way, to suggest that theory to them, or to suggest to them that they hold it... I remember a certain very interesting case. It concerned an inquiry brought against a schoolteacher, from which it emerged, through the statements of many children of the school, and very evidently, that corporal punishments had been applied.

I believed it was a case for dismissal. But someone who was supporting the teacher sketched the idea that the children’s statements were of no importance (which is true in some cases, and in others not: the circumstances of each must be appraised). And then, in my refutation, which was written, it occurred to me to combat, with an ample argumentation, the theory that the statement of children was null, a theory which I supposed to derive from the false application of the principles of the Penal Code to administrative inquiries. I said: “It is completely absurd to try to apply the Penal Code here. Undoubtedly no one, according to the law, can be punished on the statements of children. These, under the Penal Code, cannot give evidence. But an administrative inquiry is not the same thing, in which it is not a question of depriving anyone of individual rights

[p. 117]

(penalty properly so called), but of deciding whether or not the State should keep an employee.”

In reality, the absurd theory that I was combating had not been held by anyone clearly and consciously (although, really, the subconscious confusion between the two such different cases contributed to keeping my adversary in error). But I, with my refutation, came to give clear form to that theory.

Now then: what happened? That, when that inquiry was referred to the Executive Power, a prosecutor who studies it becomes enthusiastic about the theory I had created as the type of the extreme absurdity to which the contrary way of thinking could lead... and adopts it! My thesis is defeated in that particular case, which was of little importance. But, and this was the serious thing, there was admitted as official doctrine the very one I myself had invented as an absurd theory, as the height of what could be maintained if the path begun by my opponents were followed... I said to them: “if you begin by saying that the statements of children never have any value, you will end by assimilating school inquiries to criminal inquiries”; and, by creating a fictitious enemy for myself, in that absurd theory, I turned it into a real enemy.

Another fact that has to do with the difference between psychology and logic: failing to notice the psychological effects produced.... Better than formulas will be some example: in ordinary life there commonly occurs a fact similar to another which almost all of you will have observed in connection with certain dreams. Sometimes we dream that a certain person has committed an unworthy act, cruel or offensive to us. Then we wake up: all that was a dream; but, if that day we meet the person in question, we notice that there is in us a certain antipathy, a something that is not logical, but psychological: an effect, a residue that the dream left, even after we have recognized that

[p. 118]

there is no real cause. Well then: certain opinions, or states of mind in general, which we must afterwards modify, nevertheless leave us that kind of residue. To have thought or felt something with respect to a person or thing, even though afterwards, logically, all that we have thought or felt ought to disappear entirely, ought to be completely annulled, leaves us psychological effects.

The commonest and most often observed cases can serve as illustration. You know something that happens, for example, in examinations: of two students who give an equal number of good and bad answers, the one who has given the good ones at the beginning tends to seem better to the examiner, if the latter does not guard himself against that psychological tendency: the examinee who answers his first two or three questions well may be said to be psychologically saved; he will need to talk a great deal of nonsense or to be very ignorant afterwards for the examiner to believe that he ought to fail him, because the impression has already been produced. If, on the contrary, the student begins by not answering the first two or three questions or by answering with a great piece of nonsense, he will need, in order to save himself, to give an examination better on the whole than the first student’s: that is the psychological effect; for, logically, the order of the good and bad answers does not matter at all.

When I begin to leaf through an inquiry, and the first two or three statements present the accused as guilty, an impression forms in me; and even if statements appear afterwards which logically destroy the first ones absolutely,—psychologically, a certain effort is needed, a certain resistance must be overcome, in order to annul that effect. And, on the contrary, if from the beginning of the examination of that inquiry I draw a favorable impression, in order to destroy it psychologically something is needed afterwards whose logical force is greater than what would have      been     necessary        without         that   psychological antecedent.

[p. 119]

Lawyers sometimes argue about whether or not it is advisable to give the defense full scope from the beginning and to present the best arguments; and some say that it is practical to reserve the best arguments for the end; not to present them in the first briefs, but in the last, when the other party no longer has the opportunity to answer them or to answer them well. It is admissible, from a certain point of view, a rather logical one; but, on the other hand, it has the serious drawback of producing in the judge a contrary psychology, a psychology that is difficult to destroy, or to destroy which an argumentation is needed that is logically superior to what would have sufficed from the beginning.

There is here, then, a series of small psycho-logical problems, regarding which no rules can be given. But this matter of psychology and logic, well studied, has many more consequences than appears at first sight. There are interesting problems in various sciences which these questions affect. For example: I cannot resist the temptation to believe that the teaching method which contemporary pedagogy almost universally regards as the best—so much the best that many practice or advocate its exclusive use—is a method that has certain serious drawbacks; and that the failure to see them depends a good deal on the confusion of the logical with the psychological.

The method I wish to speak of is the one that consists in having children, or pupils in general, “discover for themselves” the truths; the method of which Socrates was the initiator: not to teach, properly speaking, but to make the pupil discover. The teacher does not teach, properly speaking, how fractions are multiplied, but will present two fractions and will try to have the children, more or less guided, end by discovering, after long trial and error, how one must go about multiplying them. This is

[p. 120]

the method that is regarded as exclusively good; and, indeed, looking at the question from the logical point of view, no drawback (except, at most, the loss of time) seems to counterbalance the undoubted advantage of exciting the pupil’s spontaneity, for, in these lessons, however much the children make mistakes, however much they grope, however much they go astray, however much they walk blindly, in the end everything comes right: the discovery is reached.

Everything comes right, logically!; but, psychologically, something very different happens. Those children who have been guessing, and making mistakes, and walking blindly, and thinking vaguely and without precision for a very long time, do you believe that they retain in their minds no effect of all that? —In fact, the method (if it is abused) leaves their minds confused and damaged, accustomed to vagueness: even though the lesson comes right logically in the end, it does not come entirely right psychologically. And that is the fundamental reason why, although I believe that this method is very good, I do not in any way believe, as is believed today universally, or nearly so, that it is the only one that ought to be employed.

This is a case in which, besides the fallacy of false opposition, and the uncertainty of questions of degree, there is also at work this other confusion of psychological effects and logical effects.

You will notice one thing, which I have not wished to deal with until this moment; and it is that these questions, besides having a psychological and logical aspect,—we have said psycho-logical,—also have a moral aspect. I am sure that all of you have asked yourselves how far it is legitimate to use, for example, certain methods to carry conviction to other persons, or to conduct discussions successfully: and you will have noticed that it is a very difficult moral problem.

[p. 121]

For example: I want to make a good project triumph. I am quite sure that it is good. Have I the right to use methods of that kind? have I the right, for example, to suggest to my presumed opponents that they are in agreement with me, or things of that sort?

The problem could be generalized, and posed in this form: if the moral ideal is truth, which is the true truth?: to utter or write words that are literally true, or to utter or write words that produce truth as their effect? do you understand?

I am a physician, and a sick young woman comes to me; she is a factory worker, or a schoolteacher; she works; and I know for a fact that her father or her husband, who is the one who brings her, makes her work in order to make use of her wages. She has a pulmonary congestion, incipient for the moment, and, with a good regimen, curable; it cannot be said to be a case of tuberculosis; but it is something that will end in tuberculosis if the patient goes on working. The literal truth would be this: “Your daughter, or your wife, has to give up work; she is not seriously ill at this moment, but she will grow worse if she goes on working.” Such is the literal truth. Psychologically, the effect it will produce is not the true effect: owing to the interest there is in making that person work, it is certain that, if I speak thus, they will go on making her work.

Now, I can lie literally, and say: “This patient is in a serious condition.” Verbally, literally, in this case, I lie, because she is not in truth in a serious condition; but I produce as an effect what ought to be the effect of the truth; that is: a state of mind conducive to that person’s being freed from work.

Which is the true truth? There lies the problem.

Another similar case: that of the optimistic patient.

He is carefree, he attaches no importance to illnesses

[p. 122]

, he does not take care of himself. If I tell him: “what you have is an incipient pulmonary congestion, which may in time lead you to tuberculosis if you neglect it,” the patient forgets his congestion at once. If I, knowing his optimistic character, exaggerate the gravity of his illness to him, I lie literally: but, as far as the effects go, I produce the truth: the effect of my words is true, although literally they are false.

A short while ago the Director of the Pasteur Institute wrote (or someone wrote in his name) this brief sentence; four words, which may have done a great deal of harm: “alcohol is a food.”

He based this assertion—a much disputed one, for that matter—on the experiments of Atwater and Benedik, for those experiments prove only one thing: that alcohol is converted into energy, up to a certain degree; that it produces force. In this literal and strict sense, it is true to say that alcohol, to a certain degree, is a food. But, naturally, to say to the man of the people “alcohol is a food” was, psychologically, to tell him something more: it was to tell him “you may drink alcohol,” and it was even to tell him “it is good for you to drink alcohol.”

Ever since those four words were pronounced, every person who is not very cultivated or not very capable of distinctions believes he has at his disposal the authority of a scientist, the authority of the Director of the Pasteur Institute, to set against the incessant preaching against alcohol; and that is why I said that those four words may have done a great deal of harm; and yet, literally, and in a certain sense, it seems that the expression was true.

More special cases: I am a physician, and I am talking with a person who has a tendency to drunkenness; suppose I tell him: “it has not been proved that drinking a certain quantity of alcohol a day is harmful; eminent physicians and chemists believe that some 400 or 500 grams of wine daily, for example, cannot harm anyone;

[p. 123]

half a liter of pure wine, or a little glass of cognac, will do you no harm; now, if you go beyond that, alcohol will be harmful to you.”

Let us suppose that those who hold this opinion are right, and that this is the literal truth: psychologically, I am giving that person every facility for turning into a drunkard. If, on the other hand, I were to tell him: “alcohol is a poison; a single drop of alcohol a day endangers a person’s life in the long run,” it may be that, although my thesis were literally false, I should produce an effect that, in a certain sense, we might call true; that is: getting the person not to drink.

Here, then, there is a problem that is not only logical and psychological, but moral.

In general, I think or feel that one ought to tend to resolve these cases negatively; I mean: not to admit, morally, that “truth in fact,” and to respect the literal truth; the latter, in many cases, does produce, it is true, bad immediate effects; but, in the great majority, the other way of seeking the truth leads, in the long run, to the greatest errors and the greatest evils. Besides, whether this be so or not, the theory would be extremely glissante [slippery] in practice: once one has started down it, one does not in practice find the limit at which to stop. Thus, for practical reasons as well, one must renounce the advantages that may be obtained in certain cases by such procedures, so as to remain well habituated and keep one’s conscience clear. Nevertheless, and for exceptional cases, since not even in these matters can absolutely general formulas be given, and since some of the examples I have put to you—that of the sick girl, that of the drunkard, etc.—in no way give the sensation of an immorality, this is a problem that I leave to your meditation.

[p. 124]

And so it is well that you should get into the habit—and you have a good field for experiment in the class discussions—of practicing... (and still more in the daily discussions of real life, where there are such notable examples of sophisms); it is well that you should get into the habit, I was saying, of analyzing discussions (your own and other people’s) from the double point of view, logical and psychological. It is an extremely useful exercise for life.

I could give you examples of real discussions whose analysis would be interesting and useful, as class work. But, since time is short, I am going to point out to you something about other facts, also “psycho-logical,” of a subtler and more difficult order, but a knowledge of which will not be useless to you: I am referring to what we might call (to call them something) mental planes.

Edgar Poe tells of a certain boy who had a special skill at guessing right almost every time in the game of even and odd, and won from the other boys all the marbles they possessed. The game consists in guessing whether the other boy has hidden in his hand an even or an odd number of marbles. And that boy’s method consisted in putting himself into his opponent’s psychology, trying to gauge his degree of penetration. Thus, a completely simple boy would put evens the first time, in the next play he would put odds, and would go on alternating in that way; another, a little more intelligent, would put evens once, and would say to himself: “now the other one is going to suppose that I’ll put odds, so I’ll put evens again...” The boy of the example had the psychological instinct necessary to appreciate the degree of penetration of the one hiding the marbles, and to put himself in his place.

Again, in another tale by Edgar Poe, a certain letter had been purloined by a political personage, and, although he had to keep it at hand in order to make use of it, the police, in spite of all the investigations made,

[p. 125]

had not been able to find it. Well, the protagonist of the tale finds it easily, by visiting the thief’s study and taking from the most conspicuous letter-rack in the room a letter that had a completely contrary appearance: the purloined one had a small envelope, and here was a letter with a large envelope; the purloined one was in a woman’s handwriting, and here was a letter in a man’s handwriting, etc. In the absolute certainty that this letter was the stolen one, our investigator seizes it, and he is right.

Now then: let us make an observation of our own, which will later turn out to be interesting to us, apropos of either of these two examples.

Take the case of the marbles: a not very intelligent boy puts evens the first time and odds the second; another, somewhat more intelligent, puts evens twice; another, more intelligent still, goes back to doing what the first did, that is, what the dull one did, because he thinks that the one guessing is going to make the reasoning of the second, and he, then, goes beyond him by, let us say, one plane or one circle; and another more intelligent still will go back to doing what the second did; and so on indefinitely, alternating. In the same way I can set about reasoning whether or not it suits me, in order to hide the letter, to give the envelope an appearance opposite to the one it had. I can, in a first circle, for example, say to myself: “since the letter was in a woman’s handwriting and the envelope was small, I’ll give it a man’s handwriting and a large envelope”; but afterwards I might say, in a second circle: “they will think that I am making the previous reasoning; better, I’ll leave the letter as it was, and they will say to themselves: he won’t be so foolish as to leave the letter with the same appearance it had.” But I may be more intelligent still, or suppose them more intelligent still... etc., do you understand me?

I remember that once a poet had published a book of verse whose prologue, extremely eloquent,

[p. 126]

bore a completely unknown signature; and the suspicion crossed my mind that the author had written the prologue to himself. The verses were good, and the prologue was very badly written; and I said to myself: “this prologue cannot be by the author, who writes so well.” But at once: “and yet it is possible that the author, to throw his readers off the scent, tried to write the prologue badly.” I could have gone on, and said to myself: “and yet the author might have foreseen this reasoning of mine, and, in that case, it suited him to have written the prologue well,”—and so on indefinitely.

The interesting thing is that on the third plane one comes back to believing what one believed on the first, that is to say: one ends up holding the same thesis as at the beginning; and, on the fourth plane, one ends up holding the same thesis as on the second; and observe that the third, far more intelligent than the first, comes to coincide with it, though it is more distant from it in penetration than the second is.

Well: all this is not only schematic, but it will have produced on you a somewhat strange impression, as of something childish; they seem like games of wit, don’t they? But, in connection with important, real and vital things, something happens whose comprehension will be made easier by these schemes.

Suppose the question of the traditional parties, in a country like ours. There are “traditionalists” and “anti-traditionalists” who are on different planes. At first sight, only the vulgar classification appears: on one side are the upholders of the traditional parties, and on the other their adversaries; but this is crude.

We have, to begin with, on the lower plane, let us say, the traditionalist who is a “colorado” or a “blanco” out of hatred, or because his parents were, or just because; here comes in the whole vulgar psychology: “bull’s blood”: “wild colt’s bone”... all that belongs to the lower plane.

[p. 127]

Now, a man rises one degree above that plane, and says: “No; why must we Orientals be disunited?; why must we honest and good men be separated and set against one another? Let us unite, let us form the only possible party; let us good men unite against the bad men, and let us bring about the happiness of the fatherland, all united.” Morally, this is very beautiful; intellectually, the plane is still a little innocent; but already superior to the preceding one.

In rising a plane, we have passed from the traditionalist thesis to the anti-traditionalist thesis. But, afterwards, we climb yet another plane, psychologically, and we say to ourselves: “That is theoretical. No doubt, if men were not men, it might happen; but our sense of reality, our sense of life, tells us that good men will never unite against bad men. In every country there are parties, and history shows us that there always have been.” And, by this road, we come back to the traditionalist thesis, no longer with the psychology of that first traditionalist, but on a higher plane; in the third circle, let us say.

I climb higher, and pass to another circle, and say to myself: “it is true: there is no question of thinking of the union of all good men; but let us note that the problem has a special character in our country. I am not going to maintain that parties in general should be suppressed; but I do say parties like ours, which have no programs that are definite or different from one another. If one examines the programs of the ‘Colorado’ party and of the ‘Blanco’ party, it turns out that they are the same; then, why should these parties exist? Let others be formed; let there be liberals, conservatives, Catholics, or let parties be formed for the occasion....” I have become anti-traditionalist once more, in a higher circle, in the fourth circle.

[p. 128]

But afterwards I say to myself, or someone says to me: “that fact which seems to you so strange, so abnormal, and which you believe to be peculiarly ours—that the parties have no real difference of programs—is observed in many other countries as well: at bottom, even when it does not appear so. In the United States, for example, the ‘Republican’ Party and the ‘Democratic’ Party were at one time parties of principle, they held theses different in essence, by nature: one, for example, was in favor of slavery, and the other against it; but today they have no permanent programs properly so called; they may raise transitory programs: for example, at a given moment the Democrats may be free-traders, and the Republicans protectionists (as it might just as well be the other way round); but protectionism and free trade have nothing to do with the essence of those parties. In England or in Spain there may be an apparent difference between liberals and conservatives, but let no one believe that they have had very clearly distinct programs at every moment. In Spain one has seen, for example, in recent years, many liberal reforms upheld by the conservatives and opposed by the liberals: a question of Power, of political interests... Consequently, there is no reason to try to keep from happening in our country those things, undoubtedly illogical, but human, which happen in the foremost countries of the world. Let us not worry much, then, about our traditional parties not having fixed programs or not having different programs. Let us try to improve them as far as possible, by joining them.” And here you have me a traditionalist on the fifth plane.

And I can go on reasoning, and say to myself: “Well, yes: I grant that that fact is not fundamental; that there is no difference of principles between the parties is not a decisive reason. But what does happen in our

[p. 129]

country is something else, more important than that: it is that the democratic mainspring is broken. After a single party has governed for fifty or sixty years, and under certain conditions, not only the habit but the possibility of rotation has been lost; and, in fact, revolution is the normal recourse, instead of election. That fact, yes, is unique, different from the other countries. And because of it, we must try to change those parties. It matters little if others come along without programs, called ‘Green,’ ‘Yellow,’ ‘Blue’: that is not what is important; but the change must be made in order to destroy this antidemocratic fact, that rotation in power is impossible..... The political organization of our country, determined by those two parties, is, in fact, an organization for war, of which (civil) war is a fatal result. Consequently, if we replaced them, even with others that had no more meaning.....”

Here I am once again an anti-traditionalist, in another circle higher still as regards observation, penetration and practical sense. And one may, or may not, go on thinking and objecting.

Take another case drawn from reality. A short while ago it was debated whether or not the crucifixes should be removed from the Charity Hospital. On the first plane there would be, for example, the fanatical religious man who wants to keep the crucifixes because the Catholic religion must predominate: “let those who are not Catholics not enter the Hospital!” Against him stands an impassioned and absolutist “liberal,” who wants to suppress the image of Christ, because Christ has to do with the Catholic religion, which he hates. But another appears who, in thesis, comes to coincide with the inferior Catholic (in thesis, that is, in holding that the crucifixes should remain); and he will tell us: “the crucifixes should remain, not as the narrow symbol of a closed religion, but as a general symbol of Charity. Christ is the representative of Charity, as Socrates

[p. 130]

was of Philosophy,” etc. And on the other side there may appear an adversary who argues, not with reasons of inferior liberalism, but by saying: “very well; I do not condemn that tolerance; but the truth is that, in fact, the crucifixes were not there as a symbol of Charity, but as the symbol of a religion; or, at any rate, those crucifixes were used to try to convert the sick; at times, to torment them, etc. Consequently, they ought to be taken out.” This man coincides in thesis (removing the crucifixes) with the first “liberal,” but not in plane.

Now then (I come to what I proposed to show with such long preparation): this shows, above all, how there are relations, psychological and logical, among men, which are much more complicated than the simple grouping into two opposite theses; for example, you have already noticed how, at times, one comes back, on a higher plane, to the thesis in which one had once found oneself on a lower plane, or in which many find themselves on a lower plane; you have noticed that two men may coincide in thesis (1), and yet find themselves farther apart in psychological height, let us say, farther apart in plane, than two men who hold contrary theses. Thus, for example, our “traditionalist” of the elevated plane coincides, in thesis, with the traditionalist of the very lowest plane; and yet, in mental height, he is farther from him than from an adversary of the parties on an elevated plane. A Catholic of the elevated plane coincides in thesis with a Catholic of the lower plane, and both will be adversaries, for example, of a liberal of the elevated plane; it is probable, however, that the souls of the superior Catholic and of the superior liberal are closer to each other

(1) Each time I express myself in this way, it must be clearly understood that the coincidence of those two persons is not real, but depends on a verbal designation or formulation which happens to take them both in.... etc.

[p. 131]

than those of the superior Catholic and of the inferior Catholic.

This understood, please blur all that we have said, dilute it, shade it, make it vague, and take away from it this geometrical precision that we have given it with the schematic comparison of the planes and their symmetrical alternation. Above all, understand that what we call planes are states of mind—extremely complex states of mind, in which there is much unformulable psychology, and much feeling, and which cannot be reduced to simple theses; that, perhaps, there are no two men who hold exactly the same thing, since, in reality, the true question is not between theses-formulas, but between states of the whole mind.

But do always keep the general idea of what I have explained. Thus, probably, it could not make sense, in most cases, to say “first plane,” “second, third plane”; it would make no sense to say “I am a liberal of the first plane, or of the third, or of the fifth.” But what does make sense,—and one must see it through to the bottom—is this: one is a liberal on more or less low or high planes; and this occurs in most questions, debates, discussions; in the logical positions of men, political, or religious, or philosophical, to which a name is given.

Some time ago, that discussion between Brunetière and his adversaries on the “bankruptcy of science” stirred people’s passions. On one side there fought (schematically speaking): the adversaries of science, and, on the other side, its defenders. In such a debate, the schemes of planes could not really be drawn (or they would have an altogether fictitious character); but it is absolutely beyond doubt that one can be a defender of science on very different planes, and even that there are certain defenders of science going about on a

[p. 132]

completely inferior plane, against whom Brunetière was more than right. But he was not right against those who, above that plane, and above the other as well, after recognizing the insufficiency of science as the sole ideal for the human mind, came back to defending science against those who sought illegitimately to diminish its value and dignity. So that questions cannot be treated so simplistically. When we ask ourselves: “Was Brunetière right in attacking science?” we feel inclined to answer simply yes or no. But that is not it. He was right when he attacked certain inferior mental states of certain defenders of science, who see nothing but science, and who believe that science can fulfill the mission of all human ideals. He would be right, for example, against a Haeckel, who thinks he can answer the infinite question: “Who are we, whence do we come, and whither are we going?” in this form: “We are anthropoids of the genus primates; we descend from the apes, and we are going....” I do not remember where; but I know that for this last question too he had an answer. Against that kind of science, or rather of defenders of science, Brunetière was right. But, after having read and felt that critic, we find, in the very title he chose, an enormous share of injustice or of falsehood. We might say that there are defenders of science who stand above, and others who stand below, Brunetière.

A book of decadent poems of a certain kind appears (of the not very original ones). A “bourgeois” reads it, and flies into a rage: “What is all this about lilial moons and lyrical rings under the eyes? Verbiage, vain and ridiculous!” Censure on the lower plane. Higher than he will be some admirer (or the author himself), who feels certain forms of beauty, certain delicate aesthetic impressions. Then comes a third, who perfectly understands

[p. 133]

the author, and who understands him so well that he sees not only what is really beautiful in his book, but also what it has of the snob and the posé [affected], of the falsified, the exaggerated, the artificial, the too much imitated, or the ridiculous; and this other man says: “I don’t like the book”; but not on the same plane as the “bourgeois”: our new personage “doesn’t like” the book; but he is not below the book, but above it.

Zola publishes “La Terre.” The bourgeois reads it, and is enraged: “in this book indecent things are talked about, ugly words are used: that is bad, it is unworthy.” Above that bourgeois are the cultivated admirers of the book. Then some critic tells us: “no; Zola has forced the note; undoubtedly, one must speak of those things when it is necessary to speak of them; those terms may be used when it is necessary to use them; but here they are artificially sought out: one notices that there are cases in which they are used for the pleasure of it, and the author misses his effect. That is why it is coarse and inferior.”

This same critic, if he sums himself up, will perhaps come to say the same thing as the bourgeois (1): that “La Terre” is a coarse book; but he says it on a higher plane: above the book, not below it; he says it on the way back.

But now the following happens, which is very interesting: the partisans of our decadent book or of our naturalist book, or the partisans of a thesis like Brunetière’s, when they are attacked, will treat their adversaries as if these were on the lower plane, and will not realize that they may be either below or above their own. He who writes a bad little decadent book, if

(1) It is well understood (I repeat) that I am speaking in this way for brevity: he does not say “the same thing,” nor does he think the same thing. It is only that, if the readers of the book were classified as favorable and contrary to the book, those two would end up together.

[p. 134]

it is censured, will believe that his public is uncomprehending, imbecile, bourgeois: he will suppose it to be on the lower plane. For what happens is the following,—a very interesting thing, but rather disagreeable, and sad at times: he who is on the higher plane understands him who is on the lower plane, and he who is on the lower plane does not understand him who is on the higher plane. And, naturally, in practice, one man cannot argue with another by telling him: “My dear sir: the fact of the matter is that I am on a higher plane than you.” This, besides being impossible to prove, might have various other drawbacks...

In the philosophy of science there has just taken place an encounter, which I had been expecting for some time, between two of the most interesting mentalities of contemporary France: Le Dantec and Bergson. They are two great thinkers, endowed with an intelligence perhaps of the same value, and enormous: it would not be strange if they were the two most powerful reasoners writing in this age. But one of them, Le Dantec, has nothing but a scientific education, whereas Bergson, who is a philosopher, has in addition a very solid scientific education.

For a long time, in the same country, they had been treating the same questions. “It happened that they met,” and, in the discussion, which I cannot summarize here (1), Bergson was on a higher plane; but following that discussion is the most exasperating thing, because, naturally, Le Dantec did not understand; and when Bergson attacked certain scientific conclusions, or certain manifestations of present-day science, the other treated him as if he belonged to the inferior class of philosophers who attack science without knowing it. In reality, it was he

(1) See Le Dantec’s work: “Science et Conscience.” The author subtitles it “Philosophy of the XXth Century,” not realizing that his lack of philosophical knowledge has made him laboriously rediscover things which have, in general, been said a thousand times, and which are extremely backward (in time and in “plane”).

[p. 135]

himself who did not know philosophy, and who was on a lower plane than his adversary; but for that very reason precisely he did not understand, and spoke with great assurance, aggressively or ironically... As for Bergson, he could not argue...

Now, I want you to know that to be conscious of this psychological fact, of this existence of states of mind at different heights, is important, above all, in the present age, because there are many states floating about (through the soul and through the books of the greatest thinkers) which would be represented by this scheme: an inferior thesis, on elevated planes; and there is nothing more dangerous for thought and for feeling than the reading and the propagation of that class of authors: the bad thesis presented or upheld in states of superior souls. The psychological superiority of the author rubs off, so to speak, on the thesis, and presents it clothed in the most seductive coloring.—I do not know whether you understand me; I am going to prepare this with some analyses.

Look at this commentary which a lecturer in the United States suggested to a distinguished writer:

“The lecture I alluded to took place the following day, in Broadway Theatre; but in the audience, almost as numerous as in Madison Square, the ladies all but predominate, old and young, alone or escorted. The illustrious orator is alone on the stage, tall, standing, in evening dress, elegant and handsome in spite of his sixty years, clean-shaven like an actor, thrusting out his robust chest and of a much more athletic appearance than Jim Corbett. I bring the two celebrities together because Colonel Robert Ingersoll is another champion, as notable and officially recognized as the other: the champion of rationalist oratory; and so much so, that matches with wagers are commonly organized—one took place at the Nineteenth Century Club—between Ingersoll and any Protestant minister disposed to take the bet. He has four or five speeches learned by heart and published, larded with commonplaces and Yankee buffooneries, which he has been carrying about from city to city for twenty years, like Mark Twain his Jumping Frog: it is entitled the “Mistakes of Moses”; and with that vulgar parody of the Bible, Robbie has assured himself forty thousand dollars

[p. 136]

a year and lives on Fifth Avenue. Tonight it is, as always, Some mistakes of Moses. I arrive in time to witness the triumphant peroration, which raises an enthusiastic ovation. It is a rhapsody of the basest alloy, in which crass ignorance outdoes the coarseness of charlatanry, the foolish guffaw of the village esprit fort [freethinker] who reproaches the Bible for not being a treatise on physics and constitutional law. Then, after the Yankee fashion, the litany of the “rivals” of Moses is drawn out interminably, grotesque and extravagant, from Cavalieri, “who almost completed the science of mathematics,” and Franklin, Morse and Trevethick, “pioneers of progress,” down to Aeschylus, Burns and... Béranger, the poets of the world!!... Inspired by God, Moses, who did not know the diameter or the weight of Neptune, who had not even an idea of the size of the sun (any idea of the size of the sun), and perhaps did not so much as suspect the system of Copernicus!... etc.”

“One of the witticisms that draw unquenchable peals of laughter from the women consists in calling Moses, every five minutes, that inspired gentleman: this Atticism is simply irresistible. But he recites his boniment [patter] with a sonorous voice, exuberant gesticulation, grimaces and winks like a professional monologist: he is the Barnum of free thought.”

It goes on in this style. Now, note carefully the following complexities, which certainly were not foreshadowed in the schemes of the planes or the circles: the author from whom I am transcribing is on an elevated plane; the orator to whom he refers was evidently on an extremely low plane. And we feel the superiority of the one mental state over the other; and we feel ourselves brothers of the author in our psychology, and hostile to the psychology of the orator in question. Moreover, in thesis too, the author is right in much of what he criticizes. But notice these two little passages, which here pass unnoticed.

“It is a rhapsody of the basest alloy, in which crass ignorance outdoes the coarseness of charlatanry, the foolish guffaw of the village esprit fort [freethinker] who reproaches the Bible for not being a treatise on physics and constitutional law.”

And indeed, it is foolish and inferior to reproach the Bible with that, if one considers the Bible as a monument

[p. 137]

of human making; but it is in fact true that the fact that the Bible is so bad when considered as a treatise on physics is an argument against its divine origin. In this, yes, that orator of not very elevated psychology was right, in thesis. And when he says here: “...Inspired by God, Moses, who did not know the diameter or the weight of Neptune, who had not even an idea of the size of the sun (any idea of the size of the sun) and perhaps did not so much as suspect the system of Copernicus!! etc.”; we feel the inferiority of the orator’s state of mind;—but, making an effort to free ourselves from the antipathy he inspires in us, and from the seductiveness of the critic’s elevated state of mind, we see this truth, a commonplace, it is true, but entirely a truth: that the fact that the Bible presents the sun to us as revolving around the earth is in fact a rather serious proof that it is not a book of divine origin. To say this, we have to make an effort; it is hard to say it, because it seems that we are putting ourselves on a lower plane; but in reality, what happens is that, rising above the author of the criticism, we come back to the thesis he criticizes; not totally, because here there are no schemes: here we are in reality, which is undulating and very difficult to analyze; but, partially, or on some point, as for example on that one, we have to rise above the psychological plane of the author, who was already speaking on an elevated plane; and, above him, come to coincide, on some point, with the thesis of the one who was speaking on a lower plane.

Now then: as I was telling you, you must get thoroughly accustomed to analyzing all this. Notice how treacherous that passage was, so well written and so elevated; how easily we are carried from the true to the false, when at the same time we are being led from the inferior to the superior: do you understand me?

Another case. A professor of Philosophy of Law at the

[p. 138]

Argentine Republic, a rather intelligent writer, is at this moment upholding in his class, or at least has tried to demonstrate in a book, the following thesis: Morality, fraternity, truth, loyalty, goodness, what we have grown accustomed to call “universal morality,” ought in reality to be nothing more than the morality of those who are like us. Jesus was right when he said: “love thy brother”; but who is thy brother? that is the great question. And this professor maintains that the foreigner or the stranger is not a brother; that we must understand Christian morality, and, if you like, morality, as limited solely to those who are truly like us, who are our compatriots, or little more; but, from people to people, morality would be the reverse: not the morality of love, but that of hatred; not that of truth, but that of deceit, etc. He maintains that the instinct of fraternity, when it is exaggerated, when it becomes universal instead of concentrating itself on the national group, is a suicidal instinct, an aberration of instinct, like the instinct of the moth to fling itself upon the light and perish. He explains that the recent defeat of the Russians was caused solely by their Christian morality, and that that defeat has demonstrated once and for all the superiority of Japanese morality: love for the national and hatred for the foreigner.

Our writer has a great deal of talent, as I tell you, and a very lively and suggestive style; and, in the work I am speaking of, there are paragraphs like this one:

“But the demerit of fraud, of lying, according to the ideas set forth, depends directly on the bond between the deceiver and the deceived. To lie to a relative or a friend is more serious than to lie to a stranger; and when between the deceiver and the deceived there is no close solidarity, then the good sense of the public judges the act by its results, and the diplomatic intrigues of chancellors like Pitt or Bismarck are reputed illustrious titles to glory, leaves of the laurel of their crowns! To romantic-minded

[p. 139]

and simple-minded souls, Pitt and Bismarck, almost all the great statesmen.... would be contemptible rogues. Nevertheless, their respective peoples have immortalized them in bronze and in marble.... Well, those peoples are right! The healthy instinct of life has prevailed in their judgments in the form of a vigorous love of country, and it has prevailed by destroying, through its inborn energy, the false ethical orientation that their poor and ingenuous evangelizers had given them.”

Here there is something of what we have symbolized by the planes.

Indeed, the “romantic-minded and simple-minded souls” of whom the author speaks here, the “poor and ingenuous evangelizers,” exist, and they are in fact on an intellectual plane that is at least a little ingenuous: to maintain, as is often maintained, that every immoral act carries its punishment, those of nations as much as those of men, is indeed “romantic-minded and simple-minded.” Above that plane, intellectually at least, this author places himself, and because of this he exercises a dangerous action upon us, because he makes us see the ingenuousness of that plane below. But there is a whole series of intellectual and moral things the consideration of which would raise us above the plane of this book: reasons and feelings which make us maintain and believe, on a higher plane, that, even from the international point of view, in the relations of nation to nation, fraternity and truth are worth more in general, even practically, than hatred and lying. I will spare you at this moment a dissertation in which I should run the risk of not being brief: you know and feel that that plane exists. I will point out only two interesting points:

First, how dangerous books like this one turn out to be, in which inferior theses are upheld on an intellectually elevated plane.

Second, the following: Try to imagine the impression this author would feel if his thesis were attacked from a higher

[p. 140]

plane. He would not realize that we were attacking it on the way back, or from above him; and he would treat us precisely as “romantic-minded and simple-minded souls.”

Look at this other paragraph:

“And it is to be noted that those dreaming spirits of the XVIIIth century are diminishing.... Throughout the XIXth century, politics and law were egalitarian in form and anti-egalitarian in fact. The neo-humanist principles have been sincere only on the lips of the sans cullotes, of the socialist workingmen and of an occasional artistic and suggestible temperament.... Many wolves have I seen dress themselves in the skin of the flayed lamb, but none have I seen transform himself into a lamb.... No! One I have seen, not a wolf but a lion cub, which, still clinging to the udder of a lioness in a red Phrygian cap, one I have seen, a white people, a unique people, which has always thought, homesick for Arcadias, that victory gives no rights! Of a child people, yes, still so beautifully ingenuous, so beautifully generous, that it sees brothers in its Negroes and its Indians... That people is called, on the maps and in history, the Argentine Republic. Its infancy is the infancy of Hercules. But wait until its fangs come in and it leaves the udder that suckles it, wait...!

In sum, vaccinated by the historical school against the black smallpox of the philosophical school, vaccinated by biological theory against the chickenpox of romantic philanthropism, it will no longer be possible to have any but a positive concept of law.....”

Note, once again, the same two things:

First, the action of this teaching upon minds, especially upon young minds. If the youth of any country is presented simply with the apotheosis of a policy of force and deceit as a desirable thing, many intellectual and affective forces will resist. But if that policy is presented to it on a higher plane, as something that is above the “chickenpox of romantic philanthropism,” as something that is over and above a certain ingenuous, generous and “white” psychology, then that theory has many more chances of exerting its action.

[p. 141]

And the second observation is this: that if we rise above this plane, and if, on a plane higher than that of the ingenuous man, we sympathize as he does with the “white” peoples, and not with the others,—the man above whose mental plane we would be rising does not understand it: he will believe that we are attacking him in the lower circle; he will still attribute the “chickenpox” to us. That is why I was telling you that to become aware of, to penetrate, this question of mental planes is more difficult than it seems, but extremely interesting, and not only in theory.

A great philosopher, Renouvier, has upheld certain theologico-metaphysical hypotheses which also tend to give me the impression of inferior theses defended on a superior plane. He has taken most of the religious theses, such as, for example, the “fall” of primitive man, and other analogous ones, and has upheld them with such talent, and applying to them arguments of such metaphysical depth, that he lifts them, one may say, out of their plane, and presents them as extremely elevated things. Lacking the time to give you readings, I can nevertheless point out that example to you, so that anyone may become aware, in a case that is already more complicated, of what this difference of height between the plane and the thesis is.

And that example may lead us to the greatest of all, which in our days has become considerably more intense: I am referring to the positive religions.

The positive religions, professed literally, are no doubt on a lower plane: to read the Bible and believe in it; for example, to admit literally the creation as the Bible narrates it, the anecdote of Adam and Eve and the divine curse, or the stopping of the Sun by Joshua, etc., all this can only occur on a lower plane, absolutely ingenuous, or one of mental narrowness.

Reason then raises us above that plane, and

[p. 142]

we say that all that is elementary, primitive; that those anecdotes seem like children’s stories today...

But in our time a certain class of defenders of the positive religions has arisen, among whom figure some very high names in thought, who defend them on a superior plane. Some, like Renan, by placing themselves at a special point of view: presenting the divine as a sort of human creation which is gradually being made; but always preserving in it many of the characteristics of the classical divine, let us say: on the one hand, maintaining that the positive religions will always be necessary to humanity (especially Christianity); on the other hand, oscillating in a certain way between the religious thesis and the non-religious one; all with thoughts and with feelings that place the author far above the common adversary of the positive religions. Others, like Taine, without being personally religious, at one time preached the expediency of educating youth in the principles of certain elevated positive religions (Taine judged that Protestantism has that character). But more complicated and difficult to analyze still is the position of writers like William James, like R. Eucken, enthusiastic defenders of religions, who nevertheless do not admit them literally.... You will notice that I find it rather hard to set forth what this position is, and the reason is that, in fact, reduced to reasons, it is impossible to set it forth. I could not tell you, for example, how James or Eucken think and feel: one has to read them. But you may believe me: when read, they produce the impression that they are above us: they produce the impression that when we say: “the Bible is immoral; the creation as it is narrated there is absurd, etc.,” we are inferior, and are uttering intellectual and moral commonplaces, above which stands the religious thesis. I could not explain to students why, nor explain to them

[p. 143]

what the talent and the feeling of such writers are like; but believe me that they clearly produce that impression.

Of an analogous character are the new forms that the positive religions are taking; the best example, “modernism,”— also a thing difficult to explain. Catholics have arisen who deny the authority of the Pope, who deny the literalness of the Catholic dogmas, who have an extremely broad spirit, who are enthusiasts of science, partisans of tolerance; but they go on calling themselves Catholics; they go on affirming that they are Catholic, Apostolic, Roman: they deny all the theses of Catholicism concretely; but they go on being “Catholics.” These are states essentially of the present day, and a fundamental philosophico-religious problem, at this moment, is that of determining our attitude toward those states.

Now then: those states are an example of discordance between the thesis and the plane: taking a thesis inferior in itself, as any of the positive religions is, and feeling it and presenting it on a superior plane. This produces the following effects:

First, it seduces minds to such a degree that it may be considered the only form of presentation that gives a chance of survival to theses which would naturally be destined to disappear. Upheld literally, for example, the positive religions cannot defend themselves against intellectual criticism, and, above all, against moral criticism; but presented in that other form by writers of that stature, on a superior plane, they not only defend themselves, but attack, invade once more; they are spreading like a trail of gunpowder through souls.

And those who combat those states are treated as inferior minds.

I sum up, then: in my judgment, these new states: religious modernism, those religious philosophies of

[p. 144]

James, of Eucken (in some of their manifestations), etc., are examples, or contain examples, of inferior theses upheld on superior planes. This turns out to be disconcerting for modern thought, because, naturally, a broad mind feels closer to a James or a Eucken than to the majority of those who combat them. For which reason, all this is psychologically complicated: one must rise still higher above that plane, and come back to thinking and saying, on a superior plane, some of the same things that anyone says on inferior planes.

Now then: there is nothing more difficult, nor more irksome, once one has risen.

It is absurd to believe that God punishes the faults of the fathers in the children: absurd, and immoral. It is absurd and immoral to suppose that God could have ordered Saul to put the vanquished to the sword, sparing neither the women nor the children, and that the said king was the object of God’s wrath for having taken pity on the children and not having put them to the sword. This we say on a simple, elementary plane. And then come the Jameses, the Euckens, the “Modernists,” and they disconcert us, presenting the religions to us on an extremely elevated plane, as something superior to our criticisms. And then we need to raise ourselves high enough to understand them, and to feel them; and, further, to climb above them, and come back to thinking, feeling and saying, now on a superior plane, the same thing once again: that it is absurd and that it is immoral that a book which claims to be of divine origin should say that God did not forgive Saul for having had compassion on the children and not having put them to the sword, and all the rest.

Naturally, in this way we are confused, for the one who looks on from outside, with those who criticize on inferior planes. That is the effect we shall produce today; but one must act thus: at least, one must be

[p. 145]

prepared to be able to do it, and one must know how to discern these states in every case, and to separate the theses from the psychological planes on which they are upheld.

Because of this same question of the planes (and this is the second effect we were interested in analyzing), the truest and most just theses, by virtue of their tendency to become commonplace, sometimes end up even repelling select minds. Really, we have to overcome a certain resistance when, in modern times, we must defend, for example, freedom of thought, or political freedom; censure the Inquisition or religious persecutions, etc. Why?... because all that has become very commonplace, and it seems to us that we are on an inferior plane when we utter those commonplaces that are within everyone’s reach. To a child, provided his soul has not been deformed, they already occur: if a child takes up the Bible before his mind has been perverted, he finds horrible the attitude of Jehovah in the case of Saul. If a child hears it said that at one time men were burned because they did not think like those who burned them, he will feel that to be absurd and frightful. Then come literature and philosophy, breadth of judgment and of feeling; we climb, and we broaden, and certain natural, simple and upright appreciations come to seem to us a little commonplace and a little declamatory; and a real effort is needed to say those commonplaces; and this gives an advantage to many bad theses; and, besides, it takes authority away from the good ones, because he who upholds inferior theses on superior planes treats patronizingly and with superiority those who uphold commonplace theses, true and good though they may be.

Which happens in a great many orders of facts, from those of the highest speculation down to those of everyday life; for which reason it is well to be

[p. 146]

prepared against all the effects, disconcerting and disturbing for good logic and even for good morals, that result from this interference of mental “planes.”

Among the fallacies of observation there is a very curious one, whose scheme is the following: acting in consequence of a belief tends to strengthen faith in it, as if it had been verified experimentally. It is an illusion of experience. And so common that, in ordinary people, a good part of the “experience” that life gives belongs to that illusory order.

It is extremely easy to find examples of this illusion of experience in the everyday life of men; for example: in that of those who practice some profession or art empirically and by routine. A poultry breeder forms—or receives ready-made, which is the most common case—a belief: let it be that eggs must be set to hatch in a particular one of the phases of the moon. He acts accordingly: he always sets the eggs to hatch in that lunar period; and, psychologically, the mere fact of acting in accordance with the belief strengthens it, quite independently of the results of the act. That same breeder has at some time believed (in the most common case, because he has been told so) that certain diseases of hens are prevented by giving them onion to eat; he does so, and the repeated performance of the act goes on fortifying his belief: it fortifies it psychologically, without logic having anything to do with it: the results of the practice in question are not observed, or are badly observed (in this case, they could not be observed in a truly logical manner except by methodical procedures

[p. 147]

that are not within the agent’s reach, and thanks to scientific habits that he does not possess), or, in any case, the observation of the results produces no appreciable effect, or produces some effect or other, legitimately or illegitimately favorable or contrary to the belief; but this is another question: what is interesting is that the act of giving onion to his hens, all by itself, acts illusorily, in our breeder, as an experiment confirming his belief: when he is old, for example, he will invoke his practice of many years in support of it.

The illusion of experience continually keeps alive false beliefs and erroneous practices, without true, real experience being able, very often, to overcome it, or even to affect it at all, in the majority of men.

The women of the common people give pap, or meat (or alcohol), to their babies of two or three months, in order to strengthen them; an immense number of the children die on them, in circumstances such that they could clearly see the relation of cause and effect: there is hardly one of them who has not lost a child, or several, to indigestion. And they go on believing and saying indefinitely: “they have to be fed: that’s how they grow up strong.”

A gentleman who visits at my house says to me invariably, on seeing my children: “why don’t you shave their heads, so that their hair will grow in strong? I did it with all my children.” His children, precisely, have weak and scanty hair; but that has nothing to do with it.

I remember a very interesting case. A neighbor of mine, a man of some culture, who raises hens on his country place, once sent to ask me for a brooding hen, because he had none at the moment and wished to set some eggs to hatch. I sent him one; but some days later he returned her to me, and came to tell me that my hen was no good because she had the habit of eating eggs, and had already eaten part of those

[p. 148]

that had been set under her. Now then: I had observed that there are no brooding hens that eat eggs, or that, if there are, they must be a very rare case, and that what ordinarily happens is that the nests are often badly made, either by making them too concave or by putting too many eggs in them, which causes some of the eggs to ride up on top of the others, or because too little straw is put in and the eggs are left almost in contact with the hard bottom; in all these cases some eggs get broken, and it is once they are broken that the hen eats them. I explained this to my neighbor; and, as he would not admit himself convinced, I went over to his house, where I was able to verify that, in fact, his nest had very little straw, so that the eggs, shifted about by the hen, or under her weight, broke against the wooden bottom. Faced with this verification, he was disconcerted for a moment; but then he gave me this reply, which is extremely interesting and characteristic: “Oh no: there are brooding hens that eat the eggs even when they are whole, because I, with my hens, very many times used to rub their beaks against the flagstones, or trim them, to make them lose the habit.”

In other cases, it is not exactly acting according to a belief, but simply thinking, feeling, judging in accordance with it, that strengthens it illusorily: it is the same fallacy; the psychological process is of exactly the same order.

I have a friend, very intelligent, but not accustomed to speculating or to observing with broad views: too exclusive a dedication to politics has produced in him that kind of “myopia,” as it has aptly been called, which allows one to discover only the proximate causes, and to foresee only the proximate effects, of facts. Many times we have argued about political or social matters; but he, who naturally considers me an incorrigible theorist, incapable of dealing

[p. 149]

soundly with such matters for lack of practical sense, cuts short every discussion on such subjects by reducing me to silence in terms of a protective superiority. In almost every case, the facts have afterwards proved me right, not because I had exercised any special clairvoyance in discovering or interpreting them, but because he is accustomed to seeing them in a way that is almost inevitably deceptive. But this more or less remote verification never has any psychological influence toward making him modify the unfavorable opinion he always holds of my practical sense; and, meanwhile, I notice that each one of those discussions in which he has the impression that I judge much less soundly than he does, confirms him more and more in his opinion that I am a sort of moon-dweller, completely destitute of practical sense. (Apply this example, in passing, to what we explained earlier about the logic and the psychology of beliefs.)

The illusion of experience, in its various forms, is a very frequent fallacy: it contributes, for example, to swelling, with a false experience, illusory and illegitimate, the real experience of physicians as regards the use and efficacy of therapeutic procedures; this, naturally, when it is a matter of practitioners who are not very intelligent, or not accustomed to observing for themselves with exactness and rigor. A doctor receives the belief (it does not matter to the case how he receives it: it may be based on his own reasonings or those of others, on reports of observations, or simply on authorities) that the ingestion of benzo-naphthol contributes to the antisepsis of the intestine. Accordingly, he habitually prescribes benzo-naphthol in cases where intestinal antisepsis may, in his judgment, be advisable. Whether the remedy produces effects, and which ones, is not a very easy thing to verify: one must know, in the event that the intestinal infection does not set in, or in the event that, having set in, it subsides, whether the phenomenon really occurred

[p. 150]

because of the benzo-naphthol, or independently of it, or in spite of it; one must also ascertain that same thing if the infection, on the contrary, does set in, or grows worse... For this, it will be necessary to institute very numerous methodical observations, or, at least, to study the particular cases with a special methodical rigor. Such is not, as we all know, the observation that most physicians are able to carry out in their ordinary practice; in any case, it is not common for them to attempt it. And, meanwhile, a great many doctors, by dint of prescribing benzo-naphthol to disinfect the digestive tract, feel their conviction that it is effective growing. “That conviction has been confirmed for me by a practice of many years”: such will be their language, not only in the case, perfectly possible, that this practice has been carried on under conditions that constitute a true verification, but also in the very frequent case that it has consisted only in proceeding in accordance with, or in consequence of, the belief, —which is thus psychologically strengthened, even though such a process is logically illegitimate.

Psychology and Logic of Classifications, and Related Ideo-

Verbal Fallacies

I have written that there are two kinds of minds: those who handle classifications and those who are handled by them;—which is schematically true; let us see why.

Let us analyze, first of all, different classifications. Let us take first, as an example, mathematical classifications; say, that of triangles into equilateral, isosceles and scalene (or any other taken from mathematics; they all have one and the same character). Its application to concrete cases is absolutely rigorous, clear. For example: for a triangle to be

[p. 151]

equilateral, it must have its three sides equal; if it has its three sides equal, it is equilateral; if it does not have them, it is not. For a figure to be a circle, for a line to be a diameter, for a number to be prime, it is necessary that that figure, that line, that number, fulfill certain conditions, specified in a definition; if it fulfills those conditions, it is; if it does not fulfill those conditions, it is not; there are no middle terms, nor degrees, nor indecisions. That is to say: there may be indecisions that depend on lack of knowledge of the facts: I may, for example, not yet have measured, or not have measured correctly, the sides of a triangle; but supposing them correctly measured, that is, supposing that I know the facts well, then everything is clear: A figure is an equilateral triangle or is not; a surface is or is not a circle; and so on in the other cases.

If we examine other classifications, such as, for example, those of Natural History, the facts do not present themselves to us in absolutely identical fashion. Still, in a general and relatively quite precise way, it can be said that an animal is or is not a vertebrate, that a vertebrate is or is not a fish, etc.; but already here certain doubts appear. An animal like the amphioxus, for example, which has a dorsal cord but lacks many organs that fishes, or vertebrates in general, possess—will it be a fish? will it even be a vertebrate...? We classify it, or not, among the vertebrates, among the fishes; but one notices that this classification no longer has a rigor as absolute as that of the mathematical classification.

And, if we pass on to other classifications, we notice that rigor is lost more and more.

Suppose it is a matter, for example, of classifying mental illnesses; of classifying the insane. First of all, already in the very classification into insane or not insane there are great doubts; there are degrees, transitions, a whole series of questions

[p. 152]

that prevent one, in many special cases, from saying: “Such an individual is insane or is not insane,” with the clarity and exactness with which one says “Such a figure is a triangle or is not.” Even in a general way, it is impossible to say when, at what precise moment, insanity begins. And, if we go into the special classifications, we meet with the greatest uncertainties. It is possible that theoretically, and on paper, classifications of very rigorous appearance can be made; but in practice, to say whether such an individual is or is not melancholic, whether he is or is not manic, whether he is a monomaniac, whether he has this or that special form of insanity, will offer great difficulties, and in many cases it is possible to do so only in a rather conventional way.

Something similar would happen if it occurred to us, for example, to classify geniuses, as many treatises on rhetoric claim to do: already the very distinction between genius and mere talent is extremely doubtful, full of degrees, of shadows, of questions, of doubts, of oscillations; if afterwards, within the geniuses, we try to classify them into geniuses of this or that kind, we find great vagueness; and we end by noticing, in these problems of classification, those questions of degree of which we have already spoken in class. Many classifications resemble the one that might be made of lights, into very intense lights, intense lights, lights of medium intensity and lights of little intensity; in which the limit chosen to establish the difference between the very intense light and the merely intense one turns out to be completely conventional, and so for all the other degrees.

Now then: the logical attitude of the mind with regard to classifications of the first kind, to the classifications that resemble mathematical classifications, is very simple, and here there are no problems. The problems appear when we have to deal with classifications of the other kind (which are, for

[p. 153]

that matter, almost all of them); that is, when it cannot be said or thought in an absolutely clear and precise way, “such an object is or is not within such a class”; when the classes are, let us say, penumbral: when they do not end in a precise line, but have a penumbra around them. In these cases, the human mind can take three attitudes: two bad ones, which are the ones I want to teach you to avoid, and another which is the good one.

The first of the bad and vicious attitudes, which is precisely the commonest among men, is to take vague classifications as if they were precise classifications; to take, for example, the classifications of Medicine, or of Psychology, or of Pedagogy or of Sociology, as if they were mathematical classifications; when, for example, a treatise on mental medicine presents us with a division of the insane into fifteen, or into twenty-eight classes, or into two hundred, to believe that in fact any madman must naturally belong to one of those types and present all the phenomena described in the typical class, and no other; to believe that each real madman must enter into one class and be pigeonholed in it, and that this work can be carried out the way objects are put into drawers with different labels. This is the first vicious attitude.

The second, which would represent a reaction against the former, would be the following: An observant mind notices at once that, apart from certain special cases, of which that of Mathematics is the type, in the others, in the immense majority of the real and concrete cases of life and of science, realities cannot be pigeonholed, labeled, within the theoretical types of the classifications; and then, this second vicious attitude—a reaction against the first—is to conclude that classifications are of no use; whether to conclude in general that no classification is good (which is psychologically unlikely), or to conclude in particular

[p. 154]

that this or that classification is bad (and this is very common). In our example: a medical student or a physician who noticed that it is not possible—which is true—to fit each real madman into each pigeonhole of the theoretical classifications, might think that the respective classifications, or some particular one of them, are bad.

And the true attitude toward such classifications is the following: to take them as they are, that is, as schemata for thinking, for describing, for teaching, and even for facilitating observation.

I can hardly take in the phenomena of insanity if those phenomena are not given to me classified in some way, by means of the more or less artificial creation of theoretical types of the insane; for the same reasons I could not write or speak about insanity without an express, or at least a tacit, classification. More: the very observation of the phenomena of insanity can be made easier for me by a classification, on condition that I take that classification as I ought to take it, that is, not demanding that it be the expression of reality, but that it be simply a guide; everything lies in not believing that reality must adapt itself to classifications; in not asking of the classification more than it can give; in asking of it only that for which it has really been created. There is no objection at all to my speaking, in practice, of weak light, of light of medium intensity, of intense light, of very intense light, on condition, precisely, that I know what there is in this of the more or less conventional, gradual or vague. I would, on the other hand, be a victim of this ordinary classification if I believed that there is a precise limit at which light ceases to be intense and becomes very intense. That would be a bad mental attitude. Just as it would also be a bad mental attitude for me to say to myself: “This business of intense light, of very intense light, of weak light, lends itself to every kind of confusion; there are degrees, there are

[p. 155]

penumbras, that is, there is nothing precise; consequently, let us speak no more of intense light or of weak light.” That would be absurd. What I must do is make use of that classification: handle it, without letting it handle me.

We were saying a moment ago that classifications, apart from certain special cases, are simply schemata for thinking. Let us take some examples. Ethology, or the science of characters, presents us with descriptions. Let us take, for example, that common classification, the best known: men, it tells us, are of three types; there is an intellectual type, an affective type and a volitional type. The intellectual type...—and here comes the description: men in whom intelligence predominates, who think much, feel little, act little, etc.; another type in which feeling predominates, and another type in which the will predominates; and here the descriptions, more or less well done.

Very well. The first of the vicious attitudes would be to believe that every man must necessarily be classified in one of those types and must correspond exactly to the description given of it.

In reality, it is possible that there is not a single man who corresponds to a theoretical type.

Now, the second vicious attitude: “Real men do not fit well into that classification; consequently, the classification is of no use”; and we abandon it. This, too, is bad.

The classification must be used as a schema, in the following way: having described theoretically an intellectual, or an affective, or a volitional man (theoretical intellectual, affective, volitional men, who perhaps do not exist, of whom perhaps no example has ever presented itself in reality), I make use of those theoretical descriptions to explain reality. Suppose it is a matter, for example, of a real man, and I say: “He has, of the intellectual type (of the theoretical type described), such

[p. 156]

and such characters; he differs from it in these characters; in this, he would resemble the affective type; in this other thing, he does not resemble it....”; which makes the description easier for me.

The schemata of classifications are thus utilized for describing; when I try to describe the amphioxus, I say: “It resembles fishes in such characters, but differs from them in such others, in which it resembles worms; it resembles vertebrates in such characters, but differs from vertebrates in such others.” In this way I am not a victim of the classification, and, on the contrary, I make use of it.

In treatises on psychology the types of imagination and of memory are described; the “motor type,” the “visual type” and the “auditory type” are described. Now then: precisely in our lessons we saw that a real being can hardly be pigeonholed in any one of these classifications; and when I invited you to introspection, it turned out that none of you could say: “I am solely and exclusively visual, solely and exclusively motor.” Does this mean that those classifications are bad? By no means: they are quite useful for description, and for observation itself. Whoever knows how to make use of that classification will look in himself (or in others) for the various characters of the motor and of the auditory type, and will then describe by making use of the schemata, saying, for example: “Such a person has, of the visual types, such and such characters (of the theoretical visual, of the type-visual, the schema); he does not have such others, etc.”

The classes of aphasias (another example); the division into motor and sensory, with a subdivision of each of these aphasias into two types: Possibly they have never been found realized; there is no real aphasia that is aphemia, or agraphia; but those theoretical types, and the terms that designate them, are useful for observing and for describing real aphasias.

From this same conception of classifications there

[p. 157]

follows another thing: that in a great many cases that obligation which some believe themselves under, of opposing classifications to one another, and of choosing among them as if there could be only one good one, may be paralogistic. If nothing is asked of classifications but the function we are attributing to them, of schemata for description, one sees clearly how there can be many useful classifications of the same facts or beings, and that there is strictly no need to oppose them or to destroy some in the name of others.

I suppose you will have understood all this. Now then: all that we have said does not refer solely to the classifications that are called classifications properly speaking, that is, to classifications conscious of themselves. Certain sophisms that I want to point out to you occur in an order of facts of much greater scope; and although logicians or men of science have sometimes seen what we have just explained about classifications, they have not seen something else; and it is that, with respect to all our language, fallacies are committed—vaguer, more obscure, sub-intelligent—of the same nature as those that affect the use of classifications.

I ask you for your greatest and deepest attention on this point. Our ordinary language, or our ordinary way of thinking, consists in qualifying, in applying attributes to subjects; in short, in classifying. We are continually thinking, or speaking, or arguing about whether such and such a concrete person, Peter, is “good,” or is “bad”; about whether such a historical action, or such a battle, was or was not “glorious”; about whether such an act was “moral” or “immoral”; about whether such an execution of a political character was or was not a “murder”; in short: all these discussions of life, continual, daily, consist in applying qualifications to subjects.

Well then: what logic has not yet clearly seen is that in all these cases there hover the

[p. 158]

same fallacies that show themselves in connection with conscious classifications; and that these qualifications that we apply to beings or to phenomena in ordinary language are also schemata.

Let us take the simplest example: Such a man, Peter, is he good or is he not good? Let us suppose a debate on the subject, or, simply, the assertion that he is good, or the denial that he is. “Good” is a schema; “good” means (more or less vaguely, because the meaning of that term is fluctuating) a being who acts in such and such a way as a father, as a son, as a friend, as a citizen, as a brother, etc.; “good” means a being who would do certain things that we represent to ourselves theoretically. Now, some real being may perhaps perform all these acts in accordance with the schema of good; but most of them will not; they will perform some of those acts, and not others; for example, Peter may be a good father, may also be a good son, and be a bad citizen; he may be generous with his friends, but at the same time not very honest as a public employee, etc. Then, is Peter good or is he not good, in that case? The important thing is to notice the following: Peter is as he is: each person is as he is; supposing that we know him, to know whether he is or is not good is to know, as we already explained in an earlier lesson, whether it is or is not fitting to apply to him the word “good.” We have already made it evident: the fluctuation that results in cases like this one (namely: supposing the facts known, or supposing agreement about them) does not depend on doubts of fact, but on a question of the meaning of the term. But now we analyze what the meaning of the term consists in, and we notice that the term “good” is a schema, like “vertebrate” in the classification of animals, or like “aphemic” in the classification of aphasias, and that, consequently, what exists is a fundamental inadequacy of

[p. 159]

language for expressing reality (for expressing it in many cases, at least) and of which we must not be victims. We must not, for example, insist on arguing whether Peter is good or whether he is not good, as though feeling the need for Peter to enter into one or the other of the two classes (an insistence observed in so many ordinary discussions); let us keep clearly in mind the true mission of the word; in these cases, the mission of the adjective, of the qualification: it is a schema for thinking.

If all words were applicable to things in the same way as mathematical terms—with respect to which one is or one is not—, these kinds of fallacies would not exist; but the words of ordinary language are almost never like the words of mathematical classifications, but almost always like the words of the other classifications. This, besides the fact that words do not generally have an absolutely fixed and clear connotation (meaning); most of the usual ones have a fluctuating, vague, penumbral, diffuse connotation.

Meanwhile, and this too is most important, all classical logic has been founded unconsciously, implicitly, on the principle that all terms have a permanent meaning with a clear boundary; all logic has been founded on the principle that “one is or one is not,” do you understand?, which, practically, is equivalent to saying that it has been founded on the principle that to each thing a word can or cannot be applied exactly and without doubt or ambiguity of any kind.

Look at the theory of the classical syllogism: There are three terms, it teaches us; by combining those terms, by establishing the relations that exist between them, the truth is demonstrated. “All men are mortal; Peter is a man; therefore, Peter is mortal”...

[p. 160]

But in most practical cases, words cannot be applied in that way: If in the syllogism we had to say “Peter is good,” we have already shown how and why it is not necessarily possible to say clearly and categorically either that he is good or that he is not. Of the Peter of our example, good as a father and as a son, bad as a citizen and as an official, it can be said neither that he is good nor that he is not; Peter is as he is; and he has to be described with a great deal of labor. In reality, in very few cases can Peter, or any person, be described with one word, isn’t that true?

What classical logic has postulated is: first, that the connotation of each word is sufficiently precise, fixed, permanent and clear in its limits for it to be possible to say in every case whether beings do or do not enter into the classes those words determine; and, second, that there are, or can be created, enough words for all beings and phenomena to be named with absolute adequacy. Hence it is that classical logic has been of so little use in practice. As a type of its “demonstrations,” we might take the celebrated sorites exhibited in all the logic textbooks: To prove that the good is beautiful, people reasoned thus: “what is good is desirable; what is desirable is lovable; what is lovable is worthy of praise; what is worthy of praise is beautiful; therefore, the good is beautiful.” Notice, in each one of the propositions of this demonstration, how true it is that classical logic proceeded as if these terms lovable, desirable, worthy of praise had the property of mathematical terms, that is, as if it could be said of something that either it is lovable or it is not, as it is said of something that either it is an equilateral triangle or it is not. Meanwhile, there is a fundamental difference: one is or one is not an equilateral triangle; here, there is no distinction, no degree, no doubt,

[p. 161]

no middle term; but one cannot say, in the same sense, one is or one is not lovable, or desirable, because there are things that from certain points of view or in a certain sense are lovable, and from other points of view are not lovable; that from certain points of view are desirable, and from others are not; or that are desirable in a greater or lesser degree; something that does not happen with equilateral triangles: one cannot be more of an equilateral triangle or less of an equilateral triangle; one is or one is not; but one can be more or less desirable, be so in this sense or in that, not be so in some other....

So that all the rules of classical logic are rigorously exact; but for the cases in which terms can be used like mathematical terms; for the other cases, they are not, strictly speaking, applicable (although they may be so practically, as approximations). The facts fundamentally forgotten by all classical logic were two: the fluctuating, vague and penumbral character of the connotations of terms, and the incomplete adequacy of language for expressing reality.

Nowadays a revolution is taking place in logic, still partly unconscious, which will transform it radically, and which depends on the discovery of the true function of terms, on the discovery of the true ideo-verbal relations: what language is, what it serves for, what we can express and what we cannot express. On this subject, and if you should wish to go more deeply into the point, I could refer you to a study of mine, which I cannot summarize here, entitled “Un paralogismo de actualidad.”

Value and Use of Reasoning

It is commonly believed that one must always think or argue solely and exclusively by reasonings; or rather,

[p. 162]

by reasonings that can be formulated verbally. This is, on the one hand, a popular idea; it is, on the other hand, a postulate of classical logic, which claimed to give the theoretical rules of reasoning, starting from the principle, conscious or unconscious, that every belief, every discussion, etc., can be formulated exclusively by reasonings, or that, at least, it ought to be formulated exclusively by reasonings, this having to be regarded as the ideal.

Against this extreme opinion there has arisen, naturally, another, opposite extreme opinion, as always happens. Many observant logicians, many men of science too, have noticed that in practice reasoning turns out to be extremely deceptive and fallacious for men; they have noticed that everything, or almost everything, has been capable of being demonstrated, or of seeming to be demonstrated, by reasonings; they have noticed that there even exist reasonings (like those by which Zeno of Elea proved that the swiftest man, Achilles, if he gave a tortoise a small head start in a race, could never overtake it; or that an object in motion can never reach its destination; that motion is impossible, etc.); they have noticed, I was saying, that arguments like these, which prove false facts, are in reality as convincing in their appearance as the most rigorous mathematical reasonings, and that, possibly, if those reasonings, instead of being presented as proving false facts, had been presented as proving true facts, they would never have come to be disputed. They have noticed the ease with which all the inexact facts that man believes in are “demonstrated” by reasoning, sometimes even by mathematical reasoning; and they have noticed the ease with which, once it is verified that the facts do not happen that way, the demonstration of why they should not happen is at once found. In the history of many sciences (for example, of Medicine, and, although to a lesser degree,

[p. 163]

in Physics itself, Chemistry, the Natural Sciences, etc.), illustrations of all this are found at every step. And then comes that other extreme attitude, which consists in saying to oneself: “Reasoning is good for nothing; reasoning is fallacious, it is deceptive; to reason is a danger for the human mind: let us allow ourselves to be guided solely and exclusively by instinct or by common sense.”

Another fact, moreover, seems at first sight to corroborate this last opinion, and it is that a good many false minds are often tremendously fond of reasoning. Even in discussions they very often seem to prevail; and they reason indefinitely, and find arguments with the greatest ease: one feels that they are in error or in absurdity; but to refute them by means of reasonings is, as you know, very difficult; they often even seem the most consistent and the most logical of all; so that it has sometimes been possible to ask (as is done in a work which, in passing, I recommend to you very especially: “Espíritus lógicos y espíritus falsos,” by Paulhan), whether there may not be minds that are too logical (in the bad sense).

It is well for us to become accustomed to observing and understanding what truth there is in this question of the value of reasoning, whose solution is far from being as absolute and as simplistic as the one presented by the two opposite and extreme theories or tendencies. It might be anticipated, to begin with, that reasoning is very legitimate and extremely useful in practice, provided that certain conditions concur; one of them, that those who reason or argue find themselves more or less on the same plane; second, that their mind be not one-sided, nor prejudiced intellectually or affectively by systems (in this case, it may be said that reasoning is useless, that it serves perhaps only to falsify the one-sided mind still more; and, third,

[p. 164]

most especially, that one reason and argue in order to find out the truth; not as men ordinarily argue, that is, in order to win. But, even granting those conditions and all the other correlative ones, all those that practice has taught us to be favorable for reasoning to be useful, even granting those conditions, we must not believe that reasoning, as we are accustomed to exercise it, is everything, and is always enough.

We spoke in one of the earlier lessons of what we there called “hyperlogical common sense,” that is, that kind of logical instinct which, in questions of degree above all (and almost all questions are questions of degree, in practice), came in after reasoning, or simultaneously with it, to balance opposing reasonings, to keep up constantly the play of the multiple ideas and prevent one of them from predominating unduly over the others and leading us into false systematization. We are going to return now to those questions of degree, taking them from another point of view; no longer, now, as examples to show the drawbacks of thinking by systems and the advantage of thinking by guiding ideas, but studying them as cases in which pure reasoning “goes bankrupt,” and in which the kind of good sense that we are going to describe in a moment needs to control or complete reasoning.

Take a case similar to those we were analyzing in that lesson: Let us suppose that the problem under discussion is what the color of the paper of school textbooks should be. One reasoning would be the following: it is advisable that there be the greatest possible difference between the color of the ink and the color of the paper, so that the letters can be distinguished with greater ease, which saves the eyes work; from this it would be deduced, for example, that black lettering on white paper is the best. Another reasoning

[p. 165]

would be the following: it is advisable that there not be too much contrast between the color of the ink and the color of the paper, because contrast hurts the eyes and makes them suffer. Thus one would come to conclude that the paper ought to be yellowish, or perhaps almost black; in short, we would not know where to stop....

Here there are two reasonings. Each of them, in isolation, seems good. When we have made both, we notice that there is a question of degree; that we must combine the two reasonings and say to ourselves: “It is advisable, on the one hand, that there be enough difference between the color of the paper and the color of the letters for the letters to be seen well; on the other hand, it is also advisable that the contrast be not too great, because then it would come to hurt the eyes.” So far, reasoning. Now: what is the precise point that the difference should reach, and beyond which it should not go? When is it “excessive,” or when does it cease to be so? I do not deny that, theoretically, one may hope to settle this point by reasonings, and possibly it will be settled some day; but in the practical conditions in which we find ourselves, the most we can do with reasoning is what we have already done, that is: make the two reasonings, limit the one by the other, and arrive at the conclusion that there must be some point, some degree that is the most advisable or the most suitable. But how can it be settled which that degree is? Only by experience. Very well: in this case, experience would be possible: to observe the results produced by texts printed in different ways. But since in practical life experience in many cases is not possible, or is not within our reach, or is not convenient to carry out, or simply has not been carried out,—lacking experience, we would find ourselves completely disarmed in these cases of degree, if we did not have what may be called empirical instinct, that is, a kind

[p. 166]

of instinct that comes out of experience itself, that is a kind of summary and concentration of experience, and that indicates to us more or less, that makes us feel more or less, what that most fitting degree should be.

Note well that this empirical instinct does not come in place of reasoning, but in addition to reasoning.

Well then: in most of the questions we discuss in life, reasoning intervenes with this role and with this scope. There are plays of reasonings, which translate into questions of degree, which have to be settled by empirical instinct; neither of the two factors can be dispensed with, above all not the latter. Empirical instinct gains by having reasoning prepare the questions for it; reasoning is completed by hyperlogical common sense (1), the controller of reasoning.

Someone says, or thinks: such a poet had great gifts, but he produced a great deal, and very hastily; he did not correct, did not polish, nor concentrate his production; that is why it was bad, or was not as good as it should have been. If José Zorrilla, for example, or Lope de Vega, instead of writing such an immense quantity of works, had devoted themselves to finishing a few more polished ones, their production would have been far superior; Balzac himself, if he had stopped to correct his style, would have attained a glory as little disputed as Flaubert’s. But another says: and if those authors had produced under those conditions, is it certain that they would have improved their work? Given their temperament, would they not write better by letting themselves be carried along by the chance of inspiration than by putting themselves

[p. 167]

in that more artificial, less spontaneous situation in which works are polished and corrected? would not the same thing happen to them as to many who, in that labor of polishing, have destroyed the spontaneity and weakened the vigor of the work?

Here you see sketched two theories, in favor of each of which reasonings are made.

Now then: it would be bad not to reason (or to reason partially; that is: to see only one of the two reasonings; we have already taught the advantage there is in making both). Reasoning is good. But by itself it is not enough, in this question of cases and of degrees. In it, the point as far as which we can get by reasoning is this: on the one hand, it is advisable that artists polish, correct up to a certain point; if they do not polish at all, if they correct nothing, they produce badly; on the other hand, it is advisable that they not exaggerate, that in that task they not end by becoming artificial, because then it is proved that spontaneity is destroyed, that production loses force and freshness, that one makes an artificial work, sometimes even a mechanical one. The question of degree is clearly seen: “up to a certain point,” “not too much”; but where is that certain point?; when is that too much? What are the precise limits? Here, indeed, reasoning is now powerless; what gives us the solution, whether in general or for each case, is experience, when it is possible; but, when it is not possible, it is empirical instinct, the experimental instinct that we all have in a greater or lesser degree, which it is well not to despise, and which completes reasoning.

In vaguer form, we can see this same oscillation of opposing reasonings in many other questions:

One day, at a dinner, the following discussion took place. They asked me whether I boiled the water I drank; I answered no: that it seemed to me an excessive precaution; that I boiled it only when there was an epidemic,

[p. 168]

but that, under normal conditions, I drank unboiled water. The talk turned to the subject, and the following discussion began. One said: “The microbes in boiled water (if it is left standing for some time) become more dangerous than in unboiled water, because, when the water is boiled, all the weak microbes are killed, but the strongest remain, those that resist the boiling; afterwards, it is those that reproduce; and after a certain time, the boiled water is full of microbes of that more fearsome kind.” And another said: “No: when you boil the water, not only do you kill the weaker ones outright, but those other, stronger ones, if you do not kill them, you weaken; they reproduce under those conditions, and there is no danger; on the contrary: weakened microbes are bred.” In parenthesis: here there was a confusion that both of them committed, and it was taking for granted that a microbe’s property of resisting temperature is parallel or proportional to its dangerousness for the organism, and it is not so: a microbe may heroically resist high temperature and be harmless to the organism; and another microbe may be extremely dangerous to the organism and not resist a relatively high temperature. But let us set that complication aside, and notice the question of degree: Each of the two was partly right; the first was right in saying that boiling tends to leave only the strongest microbes; but the second was also right in saying that boiling weakened them. Both reasoned well, only each was making one part of the reasoning required. They could have gone on arguing indefinitely. A very similar question occurred to me one day while reading an article by Daudet on wet nurses. He attacks the custom that certain parents in Europe have of sending their children to the houses of peasant wet nurses, and says: “Let no one object to me that many of those nurslings of peasant wet nurses are very healthy;

[p. 169]

of course: those who survive were built bomb-proof; those who are suckled in that way, if they are the least bit weak, die; so that those who remain must be extremely strong. And to that argument of Daudet’s one might have replied with another. “Not so: that nursing being bad, even those who remain, if they do not actually die, will be weakened.”

That is to say—I have purposely given you these examples, vaguer than the earlier ones—that in the two reasonings there is a part of truth: on the one hand, resisting proves that whoever resists is strong; on the other hand, resisting any cause of destruction whatever tends to leave weak the one who resists. Now, to what extent are each of these two tendencies realized in each case? Here reasoning stops. Reasoning was indispensable in order to see the two tendencies; there was a danger in seeing only one, it was imperative to see both; and reasoning was able to bring us as far as this point: those two tendencies exist, and they act, one may say, in opposite directions. Now, to determine the precise degree, only experience; and, failing it, empirical instinct. It would be exposing oneself to equal dangers to dispense with reasoning in cases like this one, or to claim that reasoning gives us the whole solution, dispensing with empirical instinct. Reasoning by itself is powerless to finish thinking in these cases, although one must necessarily begin with reasoning.

Consequently, the partisans of those extreme theses were not right, neither the one side nor the other. The excessive partisans of reasoning, those who believe that everything can and should be formulated by reasoning, are mistaken, and also mistaken, needless to say, are those who claim to dispense with reasoning; what is necessary in practical life is to complete reasoning with empirical instinct. On this depends,

[p. 170]

precisely, the fact that false minds, as I was telling you, are often terrible reasoners: they lack that control of hyperlogical common sense. Many times they are false minds not because they reason, but because they do nothing but reason; not because they have one thing, but because they lack another.

If we had the time, I would never finish citing examples to you, to show you how common this mental case is.

We hear the vegetarians arguing against the partisans of a meat diet. The vegetarian tells us: “A meat diet engenders ptomaines and other products harmful to the organism; meat is full of poisons that the body needs to destroy; consequently, eating meat poisons us.” It is a question of degree. In fact: up to a certain point, the organism destroys ptomaines, etc., and, beyond that degree, it does not destroy them, or does not destroy them well. What is that point? To determine it is no longer the mission of reasoning: it is the mission of experience; and, if there were no experience, it would be a matter for that experimental instinct which some have in a greater and others in a lesser degree. On the one side, too, partisans of a meat diet will say: “A vegetarian diet leaves much residue; it loads the intestines to excess with unassimilable substances.” How, “to excess”? Where does the excess begin? In fact, the intestine may contain unassimilable food substances without this being bad; to a certain degree, it will even be good for facilitating the intestinal functions. What is the precise point, the quantum, the degree at which the intestinal load ceases to be good, at which it becomes indifferent; and, then, the point at which it turns bad? A question of degree, this too; experience must settle it; and, if not, empirical instinct.

Now then: apply yourselves, in the study of the

[p. 171]

various sciences, or simply in ordinary everyday discussions, to looking for these questions of degree (you will see how you find them at every moment), and, in them, learn to guard yourselves against the two dangers: the exclusive use of reasoning, and the proscription of reasoning or its insufficient use.

Other fallacies related to these same questions of degree are those, so common, that consist in forcing reasonings or in exaggerating them beyond the degree at which they are sound and at which the reasoning itself ends by arriving at falsehood.

The other day I clipped this from a newspaper (the subject was the abolition of the third instance in criminal matters):

“It is necessary, then (it is said), that a third court intervene to settle the conflict raised by the reversal of the judgment appealed. In a word, it is necessary that there be two concurring judgments.”

This argument, although it may be attractive at first sight, does not withstand the slightest examination, and Mattirolo has been able to say with reason that in defending the third instance one sins against logic and against justice. Indeed, let us suppose that the Tribunal or the Court of third instance, after having minutely examined the judgments of first and of second instance, should judge that the two judgments appealed are equally unjust and contrary to law. It will have to hand down a new decision, different from that of the first and from that of the second. There will then be three different judgments, and, reasoning in the way the partisans of the third instance do, why are there no grounds for supposing unjust those voted by the Tribunal that heard the case in the last resort, and for admitting on that account a fourth instance? No one has maintained any such thing, although the way of reasoning of the partisans of the third instance would warrant the demand for a fourth instance and perhaps a fifth and a sixth.”

This is a very common form of sophistry. When someone holds an opinion, to turn his reasoning against him, forcing it, and say to him: “You are obliged

[p. 172]

to go further, and to hold this other thing and that other, in order to be consistent” (all the things that fall under the same verbal formulation). What we have already explained about degrees is being ignored. The question in the example is of the same kind as the previous ones. On the one hand, it would be desirable for there to be as few instances of appeal as possible (bearing in mind the advantage of justice being prompt and cheap); on the other hand, it would be desirable for there to be as many instances as possible (with the object of obtaining a greater guarantee of justice in the verdict). There you have the two reasonings. Now, what will have to be done is to seek a number of instances which, on the one hand, sufficiently guarantees (as far as possible) promptness and cheapness, and which on the other hand sufficiently guarantees (as far as possible) justice. And here reasoning came to an end. How many are those instances? It may be that they are two, it may be that they are three; the solution will lie somewhere around there; very probably it is not one, and certainly it is not twenty-five; but as for determining how many they are (notice this, for it is the fundamental thing), the determination of how many they are is not made by reasoning; it is made by good sense (after having reasoned, and after reasoning has given us all that it could give us). Here hyperlogical good sense comes in, the product of all we have seen and all we have felt; that concentration, that extract of experience and of feeling; something already instinctive. Either of the two adversaries may fall into sophism here, as that newspaper paragraphist does. One will say: “If you admit the second instance, you have to admit the third, the fourth, the fifth, the sixth, the hundredth.” It is a sophism. And the adversary may say in his turn: “No: if you want to abolish the third instance, abolish the second as well.” Now both are in the sophism. Do you understand? Above all, do you not remember having heard this kind of sophism continually in life?

[p. 173]

Another case, vaguer but of the same kind: (If I very often present examples from my personal experience, it is to fulfill my purpose of offering you real cases, cases taken from life.)

I was arguing one day about the syllabus for the entrance examination to the University (secondary education), and I said: “It is preferable for the entrance examination to the University to find its basis in the syllabus of the primary schools; this even has a democratic significance; preparation in the public schools is free, so that, if the examination follows their syllabi, the rich and the poor can enter the University with equal ease. If, on the contrary, the University makes a separate syllabus of its own, then young people have to prepare in private establishments, or take tutors, which, as a general rule, is costly; a preference is thus established in favor of those who have means. To help democratize the University (I concluded), it is therefore advisable that the school syllabi be taken as the basis for entrance.” And an opponent makes me this argument: “Doctor Vaz Ferreira is greatly mistaken, since experience shows that, even in the cases in which the University, as until recently, had its own entrance syllabus, it was always a democratic institution. Let Doctor Vaz Ferreira answer me: was the University democratic or was it not? He will have to give me an affirmative answer. Therefore, even with syllabi that have nothing to do with the schools, the University can be democratic.”

Around here lies (vague, naturally, as in real cases) the ideo-verbal sophism, the sophism of degree, to which I am calling your attention: it consists in assuming that one either is or is not democratic. As if it were a matter of Mathematics, a term, “democratic,” was taken as though there could be no degrees in its

[p. 174]

application: “Is the University democratic or is it not?” In reality, between being (completely) democratic and not being so at all, there are all possible degrees. A triangle cannot be more or less equilateral; but the University can be more or less democratic. My argument was good; it was a question of degrees; what I meant was the following: with an entrance syllabus related to that of the schools, the University is more democratic than with an unrelated entrance syllabus. But since my opponent had fallen into the sophism of not seeing the question of degrees (a victim of that classification: democratic or not democratic), he thought he was making a powerful argument against me by pointing out that the University was already democratic, and that, consequently, it had nothing to gain from the establishment of a school-based syllabus, nor anything to lose by its abolition.

Now I am going to point out to you something very interesting, and it is that, in practice, in these questions of degrees, those who reason badly not only believe they reason better, but give those who are not good logicians the impression of reasoning better, because their attitude can seem much more decisive, much more rigorous. When my opponent challenged me: —“Let Doctor Vaz Ferreira say: was the University democratic or was it not?”, I, naturally, appeared timid and hesitant in my answer: —“I cannot answer you categorically; the University was fairly democratic, but not entirely....” —“No, sir: answer, and don’t beat about the bush: was it democratic or was it not?” And, for everyone who witnesses that discussion, he is the one who attacks, I the one who defends himself; and, apparently, I defend myself badly; before an audience, before people poorly prepared to reason, I would certainly seem the loser in that discussion. The one who forces reasonings, who goes to absolutes, who falsifies degrees, is the one who gives the impression of reasoning well; the

[p. 175]

other, the one who recognizes that sentences, propositions that apply an attribute to a subject, are true up to a certain point, within a certain degree, and who himself recognizes and confesses that he cannot say precisely what that degree may be, etc., that one is, generally, the one who cuts a poor figure in the discussion.

Besides, notice how easily the opinion of the first can be summed up, and how hard it is to do so with that of the second.

Also, very often, people try to oblige the good reasoner to “be consistent”; and men, in practice, call it being consistent to take a verbal formulation and apply it in every case. For example: suppose I concern myself with questions of education, curricula, reforms of syllabi, etc., and, as to the length of syllabi, I am in favor of their being neither too long nor too short; a fairly sensible position, although I cannot say in general what that too and that enough are: in each practical case, according to the syllabus in question, according to the subject, according to the kind of course of study, the enough and the too will fall nearer or further.

Suppose that partisans of long syllabi and partisans of short syllabi, just like that, simplistically, argue with me. What will our discussions be like?

In the discussion of the syllabus of such-and-such a subject and for such-and-such a course of study, I have maintained that one which had been submitted was too ample, and that it had to be shortened. The next day, another syllabus for another subject is discussed, and I say: “This one is very meager: it ought to be enlarged.” Whereupon the reasoners who do not feel degrees will at once fall upon me: “You are inconsistent; how is it that yesterday you wanted to shorten syllabi, and today to lengthen them? Either one is in favor of ample syllabi, or one is in favor of short syllabi: you have to choose.” And, before an unprepared audience, that attitude

[p. 176]

produces an imposing effect. As for mine, it seems that I contradict myself, that I am not consistent, that I do not know well what I think, that I have no fixed ideas, that I waver, you understand? and my answer will make a bad impression: “No: it seems to me that syllabi ought not to be very long, but that neither ought they to be very short; and that, besides, according to the cases....” This does not impress; besides, I cannot give a formula (and my adversary takes advantage of this impotence), because I am not able to say how far, up to when, in which cases, because that has to be felt (and here instinct comes in, the complement of reasoning). So that, apparently, at every moment they catch me in contradiction, and brand me as inconsistent.

In the problems of real life (not in mathematical problems (and that is precisely why an exclusively mathematical education is usually so dangerous and forms such bad reasoners, when it is exclusive), ninety per cent of the questions are questions of degrees. Truth or falsity (this must be understood: the truth or falsity of a verbal formulation) is a question of degrees: such-and-such a verbal formulation is good up to a certain point, and is bad beyond that limit. The ancients already exploited this fact in their celebrated polizetezeos sophism: If I pull out one hair of yours, are you left bald? No. Then I pull out one hair. If I pull out one hair of yours, are you left bald? No. Then I pull out a second. And thus it can be proved that the fellow will never become bald.... Well then: a polizetezeos sophism, less gross and for that very reason more dangerous, is the one men commit so often in all their discussions: the sophism of falsifying degrees.

Another class of sophisms important to study, and which also have to do with questions of degrees, are those which consist in not recognizing that

[p. 177]

what tends to obtain a certain result, and produces it in a certain degree, is good, even though the result is not obtained entirely: rejecting a project, any procedure whatever, because “it is not enough” to achieve such-and-such an end fully. The current formula of the sophism is precisely that: “it is not enough.”

Someone proposes to reform the procedure followed in written examinations. There is a great deal of cheating: the students bring sheets written at home, and hand them in as though done in the examination. Someone then proposes that a member of the examining Board sign the blank sheets that are to be used. And the answer comes: It is not enough: even so there may be cheating; the students may, for example, get hold of paper signed by the examiner, or they may imitate the signature, etc. There is the sophism: undoubtedly “it is not enough,” but it is something in the direction of what is sought; that alone is enough for the reform to be good; one must not reject it because it does not suffice to attain the end pursued entirely; it is enough that it create a tendency in that direction, that a part of what is sought be achieved.

It occurs to me to reform the voting procedure followed in competitive examinations for posts. There are examiners who cast unjust votes in such competitions. Very well: a clause could be introduced into the regulations for competitions obliging the members of the board to give the grounds of their vote: each one, before voting, must say, in front of all the others: “I vote for such-and-such a candidate, for such-and-such a reason.” And I am answered: There will still be unjust votes: whoever is disposed to cast an unjust vote will look for some argument or other to ground it, however absurd it may be; consequently, the reform is useless.—No: the reform is not useless; it creates a certain obstacle to the injustice of the vote. Will unjust votes still be cast? Certainly; but in a lesser degree, in a smaller number: that is enough.

[p. 178]

A parenthesis on the subject of questions of degrees. To such a point is the core of human sophisms concentrated, to such a point does it gravitate around this question of degrees, that one may say that men have a psychological incapacity for proportioning the degrees in their judgments. I am going to point out to you a fact of some interest.

Suppose you put to many people a question like this one: “What sort of stomach do you have? Do you digest well, or badly?”—Gather many answers, and, by the proportion, you will notice that the tendency of those questioned is to say either that they have a very good stomach, or that they have a very bad stomach; you will hardly find anyone who answers you, for example: “I have a good stomach, though not an excellent one.” That answer, or others of similar spirit, if they occur at all, will be quite exceptional. Some are going to say: “I have a disastrous stomach; anything I eat disagrees with me”; others: “I have a stomach of iron; nothing has ever disagreed with me; I can eat whatever I like.” Only with great difficulty will they answer us: “I have a bad stomach, without its being too bad,” or: “I have a rather good stomach, without its being too good,” in spite of the fact that in eighty per cent of the cases the truth will be the latter, and the exception will be the former.

Suppose I provoke a literary judgment on some celebrated author, and I ask a hundred people: “What do you think of Cervantes?” The immense majority will doubtless tell me: “He is a colossal writer, the first writer or one of the four or five foremost writers in the world.” Perhaps, too, several will tell me: “Cervantes puts me to sleep; he is an opiate; he is unbearable.” But it is possible that I will not find a single one to tell me this: “Cervantes seems to me fairly good, without going so far as to believe him so exceptional.” The judgment

[p. 179]

in that degree (about this or about any other celebrated writer) will possibly not be expressed by anyone, in spite of the fact that it is juster, at least, than the second of the preceding exaggerations.

A novel or a volume of poems appears.—“What is that book like?”—“It’s magnificent!” or—“It’s nonsense!” Such will be people’s judgment. How few will say: “The book seems to me good; but not very good”; or: “it is between good and very good”; or else: The book is not very good, but it has certain merits....” Judgments in this degree are “less common than judgments in the other degree,” in spite, however, of the fact that they are the ones appropriate in the greater part of practical cases; humanity has grown so accustomed to forcing degrees that judgments in the right degree no longer even occur to it; so much so, moreover, that the very expressions that would serve to express those unexaggerated judgments have ended by taking on a sense other than the literal one: fairly good, in practice, means very good, extremely good. Literally, the expression fairly good ought to be used for what is good in a certain degree: (“sufficient, enough”) but in fact, psychologically, the expression has taken on a different sense.—“What is that book like?—It’s not very good”; this means in practice that it is bad: the meaning tends that way.—“What is the book like?” Pretty fair! It means: very good. So that even the expressions that would signify judgments in the most commonly just degree have ended by losing their sense. When we experience a judgment in a just degree, we find ourselves almost without terms to express it, because all expressions have psychologically taken on the meaning of the extreme.

Let us continue on the value or the role of reasonings.

One is struck, and saddened, by the following

[p. 180]

fact: how easy—and you will have seen it in all our examples —how easy it is to cause a confusion; and how difficult it is to undo it! Just as a second of time and minimal effort suffice to tangle a ball of thread, and the work of whole hours is needed to untangle it, so too, in reasoning, a few words and no effort at all suffice to produce a confusion, and immense work is needed to undo that confusion by reasoning.

Whoever wished to combat by means of reasoning, for example, a book by a false mind (if it is a mind of great talent, so much the worse!), would have to write a hundred books of the same size as that one, and who knows whether they would be enough. If it occurred to me, for example, to put in the right degree, in each case, all the forced and falsified assertions of a book like “Dégénérescence” by Max Nordau, possibly my whole life would not suffice: for each assertion of one line, I would need to write chapters of corrections, distinctions, etc. This produces a disheartening feeling, because, besides being fewer, those who reason well have to work infinitely more: and it even occurs to us that they must keep falling indefinitely further behind in relation to the work of the others. What can console us is: first, that, the truth being one, the action of all those who “untangle” is concurrent, whereas the possible falsehoods are infinite, and each false mind goes its own way. But, besides, and this is more serious than the preceding, we should be consoled by the conviction that, as I am explaining to you in this lecture, questions are not resolved entirely by reasoning. If questions had to be resolved entirely by reasoning, not only would there be questions whose discussion would never end, but sophisms would go on swarming to infinity in ever-growing quantity. Such is the case with discussions of scholastic form

[p. 181]

The scholastics argued solely by reasonings: they would put forward a theological thesis, for example, on free will, on divine foreknowledge, and they would construct a series of syllogisms. Others would combat those syllogisms. To combat one syllogism they needed to make ten: each of those syllogisms was answered by new syllogisms, and by the fifth “generation” (as in the reproduction of species) they numbered thousands: and so on in hypergeometric progression. Had those discussions continued by reasoning alone, any one of them would by now fill the whole mass of paper at humanity’s disposal. But with them the same thing happens as with living organisms: it is proved theoretically that the descendants of a single pair of fish ought, at the end of a certain time, to fill the sea and turn it into a solid; but the fact does not come about, owing to a series of factors. In reasoning the same thing happens: and it happens precisely because reasoning is not everything, because it is not solely through reasoning that changes of opinion follow one another and that opinions oppose or replace one another.

In reality, what changes is rather the state of mind. There are questions, for example, that become “fossilized” (1), that fall into disuse, that end by no longer corresponding to the mental state of humanity; certain questions that were discussed in antiquity or in the Middle Ages are not discussed today, not because they have been resolved by reasoning, but because our mental state no longer corresponds to them; and, above all, truth is not made entirely by reasonings, but is made principally by changes in states of mind; men end by thinking and feeling better, and certain questions fade away of themselves. Scholastic “Theology,” for example, cannot be said to have been refuted; it is another

[p. 182]

thing: it has been made to fade away, dissolved, I will say, by the modern spirit....

I should like to speak to you about other practical and interesting cases; but I lack the time. I should like, for example, to show you how in discussions, in practice, there is nothing that disconcerts a good reasoner or a person of good sense more, nothing more impossible to refute, than an argument that is not an argument: words or phrases uttered by certain people with an obscure or vague feeling of the question, and which cannot be refuted precisely because they are not arguments. And I would show you good examples of it. What we would conclude would be, once more, the insufficiency of reasoning alone, and the dangers presented by ordinary discussions, above all and especially verbal discussions: and I would make you feel how we ought to accustom ourselves to discuss; how teaching institutions ought to accustom their pupils to discuss: how they ought to do precisely the opposite of what they do, and, instead of teaching the students each to uphold a given thesis and to combat the theses of the others, they ought to teach them to seek the truth on a point, and to discuss in a spirit other than the one that is not only tolerated but at times even artificially provoked in classroom discussions. We would show, too, what happens to good reasoners, to sincere inquirers, to those who pursue in good faith the acquisition of truth and the good understanding of matters; what happens to them, I say, in most verbal discussions, above all in those public discussions in which reasoners of the base sort, fanatics or declaimers, generally carry a decisive advantage.... I once read in the biography of Pico della Mirandola (that personage who wrote and reasoned about all the things in the universe, and who was ready to uphold at any moment and in any

[p. 183]

place some thousand theses, with which he traveled through Europe), that one of his youthful exploits was to have reduced a professor from Edinburgh to silence thirty times in a public disputation; and the idea once crossed my mind that that professor from Edinburgh, who cuts so poor a figure there, may simply have been a sincere man: and, if that had been the real cause, in that age of verbalism, of pure argumentation, of pure reasoning, the most intellectually respectable man of his time may have been that man who was capable   of   keeping    silent     thirty   times   in   a   public disputation.....! In any case, it will be well for you to become thoroughly imbued with this need to complete reasoning by experimental instinct and a good sense auxiliary to logic; and to understand at the same time that this is not to proscribe reasoning, nor even to diminish its role, but to give it the role that truly belongs to it.

I must conclude. What I have tried to do in these lessons is to give the study of human errors a character that the treatises on logic do not give it. Just as there are two ways of studying, for example, Zoology: one, to study living animals; the other, to study them embalmed or fixed with a pin,—so too there are two ways of studying logic. The treatises on logic study, we might say, errors dead and embalmed; in reality, they do not even study dead errors: they study Logic as one would study Zoology from those cardboard animals that are built for certain pedagogical museums; they study schemata of errors; they study, if you like, errors as they ought to be; they study men’s mistakes as they ought to be if men made their mistakes in accordance with the precepts of logic; for even to that point have things come! What logic ought to do would be

to study living errors, real errors. Naturally these could not be studied with the precision of classical logic. “Music is the accent of the word.

Each person, listening to the musical sounds, put into them something of his brain and of his soul, and in them saw and heard the very echo of his often ulcerated heart, the very illusion of his often disordered brain.

[p. 198]

“And when, with time, the banality, the nullity and the uselessness of those sounds became apparent, one felt shame for the feelings that had been prostituted.

“The old definitions of music that incomprehension has sought, ‘an infinite dream,’ ‘a world of sorrows and of joys,’ are nothing but the childish words of impotent invalids, repeated by the cohort of those who gather around the incurables at theosophical miracles. In the same symphony, in the same orchestral piece, each listener, reflecting his own soul, heard and saw a different image; the same piece represented for one a storm, for another a sunrise, for a third a battle and for a fourth a hymn of love!

“After a certain time, unless one were incapable, each person was surprised to see in the same piece nothing but a series of banal sonorities.

“And yet music had its mission. It was to be, and it must be, ‘the accent of the word’! An accent that should make but a single body with the word, forming but one indissoluble unity. Without being able to explain how, the public has found its road to Damascus, and, while celebrating one thing or another according to the taste or the fashion of the day, it remains attached nonetheless, in spite of itself, to the only works in which music, being nothing but the harmonious accent of the word, has produced immortal masterpieces.

“It is good form to go into ecstasies over such-and-such a work in which the symphony predominates; but, for oneself, for one’s own enjoyment, one goes twenty times to hear ‘Carmen’ or some other work of pure melody, because in them word and music are but one.

“That duet of the fourth act of ‘Carmen,’ in which Don José sings:

“Mais moi, Carmen, je t’aime encore; “Carmen, hélas, moi, je t’adore.” [But I, Carmen, I love you still; Carmen, alas, I adore you.] “.... is melody and is drama, and it is impossible to conceive the phrase with any sounds other than those Bizet put into it. “The same may be said of the invocation of Iphise in ‘Dardanus,’ by the first dramatic composer, Rameau.

“Par l’effort de votre art terrible.” [By the effort of your terrible art.]

“This invocation is so just that a hundred and thirty years later Wagner will make use of it, making it the principal leit motiv of the Tetralogy and bringing it back a thousand times in his work.

“If in the dramatic domain there has arisen, in this sense, a genius, Mussorgsky, especially in ‘Boris Godunov,’ we see another genius, an eminently comic one, Rossini, who, through having given just accents to the characters of Beaumarchais’s ‘Barber,’ through having possessed that gift, simple in appearance but supernatural in reality, has made centuries laugh with his ‘Barber of Seville.’

“So strong for the public is the influence of a just and melodious accentuation, that many pages endowed with that divine gift will remain forever engraved in its memory, like Gluck’s ‘J’ai perdu mon Eurydice’ [I have lost my Eurydice], and Donna Anna’s imprecations and the entrance of the Commendatore in Mozart’s ‘Don Juan.’

“A German polyphonic giant, Wagner, has attempted to replace the immortal accent of the word by a polyphonic torrent. He has given the orchestra a preponderant role. Today this one, tomorrow that one, will rival the colossus in polyphonic ‘tour de force,’ and if they do not manage to submerge him, they will succeed in wounding him with his own weapons.

“Always, or almost always, in Wagner, the orchestra creates the atmosphere precisely, but always too, or almost always, the singing has no relation whatever to the word, because it is there only as a counterpoint necessary to the unity of the orchestral polyphony.

“However much one may torture the imagination, it is the canticle of ‘Spring’ and the ride that will immortalize the Valkyries, and it is the song of the ‘Rhine Maidens’ that will remain in the memory of men. He is mistaken; his ‘leit motivs’ neither move nor fill the soul except at the precise moment when they take on body with the phrase for which they were created: the rest is ephemeral.

“The error lies in believing that music is an art that can suffice through the agglomeration of sounds. When it is merely symphonic, it can give the work an appearance, but it cannot breathe into it a life of its own, a real existence.

And for this no science and no study suffice: Inspiration is necessary.”

A study on psycho-sexual pathology ends thus:

“In the genetic formation of the functions of reproduction three distinct physio-psychical processes may be distinguished, acquired in the course of the experience of the species or of the individual: an emotion, a tendency, and a sentiment, which in ordinary language are designated as voluptuousness, instinct, and love.

The sexual emotion (voluptuousness) is the psycho-organic reaction to the sensory excitations specialized in the organs differentiated for the heterosexual reproductive function.

The sexual tendency (instinct) is a habit fixed hereditarily, as a product of the experience of the sexual emotion in phylogenetic evolution.

The sexual sentiment (love) is a psychical formation of individual experience on the basis of the specific tendency, being a useful psychological superstructure for the choice that precedes conjunction.

It is very interesting and useful to show the students examples, in great number, of the false importance that questions of words take on when they are mistakenly taken for questions of fact: and how men persist in disputing whether facts, things, or persons are or are not such-and-such a thing, without this meaning anything beyond whether such-and-such a name should or should not be applied to them; for, in the case we are speaking of, the facts are known, and everyone is in agreement about them.

Questions about the diet of man swarm with sophisms. Point out, for example, how all the arguments that may be applied in general to any animal, or simply to any mammal, and whose object is to prove with those general data that man cannot or must not do something that other animals, or other mammals, do, must be bad (for example: that we must not eat meat because it produces ptomaines and these are poisonous; that we must not be completely vegetarian because vegetables produce too much intestinal residue; etc.). (Almost all these arguments, by the way, involve questions of degree.) But lack of space obliges me to show here only three or four extreme examples (anyone can form in a moment a museum of veritable logical horrors, with a few readings on this question).

From the Traité de l’Arthritisme by Grandmaison:

“We do not have to put vegetarianism on trial here: we limit ourselves to considering that it is important to give our albuminous cells albuminoids of the same nature, if we wish to regenerate them and repair their losses of substance. Under these conditions, meat appears to be indispensable to our nutrition”.

After this reasoning (as I have written in another book), cows, horses, and, in general, all the animals that, being made of meat (the only basis taken into account: this is the case pointed out above), do not eat meat, are in duty bound to drop dead on the spot. (In this logical monstrosity there is the argument itself, horrible in itself; the false and simplistic supposition that each substance

[p. 211]

is repaired with elements of analogous substances; the special state of mind of not noticing that the argument (even if one did not perceive that it is bad in itself) runs counter to the most common facts, etc., etc.)

But perhaps there exists no collection of arguments as bad as those that Spencer, in his work “Education: Intellectual, Moral, and Physical,” presents regarding the question of diet, when he advocates a meat diet. I recommend that these incredible reasonings be read and analyzed in class; only a few of them are transcribed here:

“This greater need of nutrition (in childhood) being admitted.... the question is this: shall we meet that need by giving children a greater quantity of what we might call diluted foods, or a smaller quantity of concentrated foods? The nutriment that can be obtained from a small quantity of meat is obtained only from a greater quantity of bread, from a much greater quantity still of potatoes....”

(He goes on developing this comparison, tainted with simplism and false precision; and ends with this, which is a case of the same thing, in a much greater degree):

“The answer is clear enough. The more digestive labor is saved, the more energy is kept for growth and action.”

(And this demonstration continues. Afterwards):

“It is no doubt true that boys and girls can be reared on exclusively vegetable food. Among the wealthy classes one finds children who are given very little meat, and who, in spite of that, grow and appear healthy. Animal food is almost unknown to the children of workmen, and yet they reach a vigorous maturity. But these facts, apparently contrary to our opinion, do not have the weight commonly attributed to them. In the first place, it does not follow from them that those who live on bread and potatoes

(a special case, which is not that of non-meat diet in general): reach a happy development; and the comparison between the laborers and the nobility of England...

(As if the other circumstances were equal!) between the proletarians and the bourgeoisie of France

Idem. is in no way favorable to those who eat vegetables...

(In general again, now.) Between the children of the meat-fed class and the children of the class fed on bread and potatoes.

(He goes back to bread and potatoes. And he reasons as if, between those two classes, all the other circumstances were equal.)

[p. 212]

there is a marked contrast in this respect. From the point of view of vivacity, at once physical and mental, the peasant’s son is greatly inferior to the son of the gentleman.

(The height of it, to make comparisons of mental vivacity, above all, in those two cases! But now comes the incredible part):

“If we compare different species of animals or different races of men, or the same animals and the same men fed in a different way, we have still clearer proof that the degree of strength depends essentially on the nature of the food. In a cow, nourished on food as unsubstantial as grass, we see that the enormous bulk required calls for a vast digestive system; that the limbs, small in proportion to the body, are overloaded with weight; that in sustaining such a body, and in digesting that tremendous quantity of food, much energy is spent, and that, little energy remaining in reserve, the animal is sluggish. Compare with the cow the horse, an animal whose structure approaches hers, but which is accustomed to live on more concentrated food. Here the body, and particularly the abdominal region, is smaller in relation to the limbs than in the cow; and the limbs are not crushed by the weight of such voluminous viscera, nor exhausted by the fatigue of digesting so great a bulk of food; consequently there is more activity, more power of locomotion, more vivacity. If we compare likewise the stupid heaviness of the grass-eating sheep with the vivacity of the dog, which feeds on meat or farinaceous food, or on a mixture of the two, we see a difference of the same nature, but greater still. And after having visited the zoological garden, and noted the restlessness with which the carnivorous animals go to and fro in their cages, it will suffice to recall that herbivorous animals never habitually show that superfluous vigor, to see how clear is the relation between the degree of concentration of the food and the degree of activity of the animal”.

It would be an impossible task to exhaust, by criticism, all that this paragraph contains in the way of sophisms of every kind, bad observation, etc.; but here is something:

1st. Even if the comparison of the cow and the horse could be generalized, and it were true that animals like the cow, made to eat grass, were in general heavy, sluggish, etc. (it is not so, and that is an error of observation and generalization), even in that case those animals, made thus, would have to go on eating grass. Consequently, such an observation would not prove that man ought to eat meat, or not be a vegetarian, nor anything about the diet of man, for what is in question is to know what man is made to eat (what he ought to eat according to his structure, if one does not wish to speak in finalist language). And if man were not made in such a way that meat suits him, it might be a misfortune, but he would have to do without it, as is the case with the cow herself in spite of Spencer’s argument.

[p. 213]

2nd. The horses that are “accustomed to live on more concentrated food” are those that man artificially feeds with grain.

But the horse in its natural state lives on grass, like the cow, without having on that account viscera that crush its limbs and everything else, or being a “sluggish animal”!!

3rd. Instead of comparing the sheep with tigers or lions, one could compare the goat (herbivorous like the sheep) with the bear... Bring out everything that is superficial, unscientific, and, really, unintelligent in these ways of reasoning.

4th. If “carnivorous animals” (some of them) show “restlessness” and “superfluous vigor,” more of all that is shown, without being carnivorous, by the squirrel (which has been taken as the symbol of restlessness) or by birds, many of which feed on vegetable matter.

5th. Confusions and errors about the word “concentrated” (for example, a very concentrated food, since that adjective is applied to meat, may be, like meat, a very incomplete food, which shows the enormous simplism of the reasoning).

6th. Confusions and equivocations about the word “substantial,” and the vagueness of that word. For example: is meat more substantial than grains, or than nitrogenous vegetable foods in general? If one says yes, the relation established by Spencer turns out to be false (the earlier case of the grain-eating birds, and even of the fruit-eating squirrel). If one says that meat is not more substantial, then the conclusion that man ought to eat a great deal of meat was not justified... etc.

“The Australian, the Bushman, who live on roots and berries, varied by insect larvae and other poor meat, are comparatively small, have large abdomens, soft and poorly developed muscles....”

The Eskimos, great meat-eaters, ought to be tall in stature, with small abdomens....

“The ill-fed Hindoo prostrates himself before the Englishman, whose food is more substantial.”

The incredible simplism of these explanations. (I do not remember what scientist explains Waterloo by the inferiority of French food, and said that that battle was “the triumph of the beefsteak.” Some earlier French victories remain unexplained....)

“The argument is stronger still if we note that the same individual is capable of more or less work according as his food is more or less substantial. This has been proved in the case of horses. Although a horse turned out to grass may grow fat, he loses strength, as is easily seen as soon as he is put to work”.

[p. 214]

He may or may not lose strength, he may yield less work to man, but he will probably be healthier.

I shall note (in order to appreciate all the harm done by systematic and closed states of mind) that, even in these cases in which he piles up the greatest errors, the author is in an absolutist, dogmatic, and cassant [peremptory] state of mind: “To think otherwise is to return, in a disguised form, to the old error of perpetual motion; it is to believe that force can be got out of nothing.”

I cannot go on, but I recommend to the teacher (or to any reader who wishes to practice the criticism of bad reasonings) that he examine for himself these passages on diet, as well as others that follow on clothing, etc. In those relating to clothing there is a very characteristic example of false systematization, when he attacks the hardening of children, and ends by giving as the absolute and ideal rule in this respect that of avoiding, by means of changes of clothing, all sensations of cold, however slight they may be. It is clear that in this way the organism is weaned from resisting by means of its heat-producing organ, and, by blunting its reaction, it is disarmed. This idea should have been balanced against the other one in the text, which was true up to a certain degree, but which, left by itself, led to the sophism....

Returning to diet, I shall conclude by citing only two more examples:

From the book “La Higiene Moderna” by Dr. J. Héricourt:

“On the one hand, in fact, we see that an exclusively vegetable diet suffices for animals such as oxen and horses, from which considerable muscular work is required; and one could conclude, a priori, that a diet of the same nature, in which carbohydrates predominate, would suffice for workers from whom movement above all is required.

“On the other hand, we see that carnivores are distinguished above all by a considerable nervous power, and that their strength, differing in this from the strength of working animals, is made up rather of the power of sudden and momentary effort than of muscular work properly so called. Carnivores are fighting animals, whose effort, very intense, could not be of long duration, and whose activity has all the characteristics of an explosion and consequently closely resembles cerebral activity, whose explosive character is most evident. It is known, moreover, that sudden and momentary muscular effort is proportional, in man, to the degree of intelligence. It is not with his muscles that the individual produces the effort, it is with his brain...

Thus the carbohydrates, the vegetarian diet, with starchy foods, seem suited to the functioning and repair of the muscles; and the meat diet, the albuminoids, seem suited to the nourishment of the brain, to nervous production properly so called....

[p. 215]
