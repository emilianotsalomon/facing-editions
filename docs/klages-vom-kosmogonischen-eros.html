Ohne irgend auf Vollständigkeit und strenge Einteilung Anspruch zu erheben, tragen wir einige der üblichsten Liebesbegriffe zusammen und glauben dadurch soviel wenigstens außer Zweifel zu stellen, daß es nicht nur äußerst verschiedene, sondern selbst miteinander streitende Sachverhalte sind, welche dies eine Wort zu bezeichnen hat. Bedeutungsgeschichtliche Untersuchungen gänzlich beiseite lassend, halten wir uns allein an den heutigen Sprachgebrauch und werden denn wiederholt Gelegenheit nehmen, die sachlichen Ausführungen durch mancherlei Winke vorzubereiten.

We must preface the substantive considerations all the more with a linguistic delimitation, explaining why we are using the foreign word Eros instead of, say, the German word Liebe (love), since that concept of Eros which is to form the subject of this contemplation merely spins a single thread further from the fabric of ancient reflection—even if it is able to support itself on primordial phenomena of Pelasgian life. – Of the two most important reasons that argue against the use of the name Liebe, the first alone would already suffice to exclude it entirely: we mean its almost unparalleled ambiguity. Without claiming any completeness or strict classification, we will gather together some of the most common concepts of love and believe we can thereby place at least this much beyond doubt: that it is not only extremely different, but even mutually conflicting states of affairs which this one word has to denote. Leaving aside entirely any historical semantic investigations, we will adhere solely to contemporary usage and will repeatedly take the opportunity to prepare the substantive expositions with various hints.

In höchster Steigerung würde daraus „Begeisterung“.

We see it denote the state of lasting or transient pleasure in something in phrases like: I love (ich liebe es) to drink coffee, tea, chocolate, to travel second class, to read before going to sleep; and we find, in this pleasure, inclination and ”interest” emphasized with the ”love for the thing” (Liebe zur Sache), which may extend to all possible objects: love for one’s profession, love of country (Vaterlandsliebe), love for science, art, nature, beauty, for the theater; someone is a soldier with ”zest and love” (Lust und Liebe), and so on. In its highest intensification, this would become ”enthusiasm” (Begeisterung).

Man nennt aber Liebe auch die christliche Liebe, eingerechnet die ihr eigentümliche „Karitas‘‘, die zwar den Hauptton legt auf die Forderung unterschiedslos allgemeiner Menschenachtung, ihre besondere Farbe indes von der Annahme der Hilfsbedürftigkeit ihres Gegenstandes erhält. Je nachdem man mehr jene oder mehr diese Seite hervorhebt, bedeutet „Karitas‘‘ entweder pflichtmäßige Hochschätzung oder pflichtmäßige Barmherzigkeit, im Sinne jedoch der Mitbezeichnung eines entsprechend gearteten Gefühls. Beispiele wären: Menschenliebe, [p. 15] Nächstenliebe, Liebe zu den Armen, Evangelium der Liebe, Liebesgaben, Liebestätigkeit, Liebespflichten und so fort.

But one also calls love the Christian love, including its peculiar ”caritas,” which, while placing the main emphasis on the demand for indiscriminate, universal respect for people, nevertheless receives its particular color from the assumption of the neediness of its object. Depending on whether one emphasizes more the former or more the latter aspect, ”caritas” means either dutiful high esteem or dutiful mercy, yet in the sense of also denoting a correspondingly constituted feeling. Examples would be: love of humanity (Menschenliebe), love of one’s neighbor (Nächstenliebe), love for the poor, the gospel of love, gifts of love (Liebesgaben), charitable works (Liebestätigkeit), duties of love (Liebespflichten), and so forth.

Gleicherweise verschieden von der „Liebe zur Sache“ wie von der stets gesinnungsartig gefaßten „Menschenliebe“ ist die Liebe als wahlbestimmende Herzensneigung, wir meinen im Wortsinn des Namens Neigung (Inklination, Gravitation, Hang), fremdwörtlich durch „Sympathie“ gedeckt. Nach der Gattung der bevorzugten Gegenstände treten unter den Neigungen wieder mehrere Arten hervor.

Here we insert a psychological addendum.

Neigung zu bestimmten Dingen oder ihren anschaulichen Eigenschaften und Charakteren: Liebe zum Meer, zu den Bergen, zum Walde, zur Heide, zu den Sternen, Wolken, Gewässern, zu Blumen, Tieren (Hunden, Katzen, Pferden, Vögeln), zu Steinen, zum Süden, zur Sonne, zu Frühling, Sommer, Herbst und Winter, zu bestimmten Klängen, Farben, Düften, zum Flötenspiel, zu einem bestimmten Baustil, zu Gemälden, zu Kunstgegenständen jeglicher Art. Auch von der „Heimatliebe‘“ gehört wenigstens eine Seite hierher, während die zweite und wichtigere erst weiter unten zur Sprache kommt. — Liebe zu etwas trägt unverkennbar das Gepräge einer Vorliebe für etwas, und der nun können ebenso ausgeprägte Abneigungen zur Seite treten, manchmal gesteigert und verbesondert zu den sogenannten Idiosynkrasien.

Since ”love of neighbor” demands an inclination from mere respect, thus eliminating the rights of natural loving (Liebhabens)—which is a preferring, higher esteeming, liking better of its object— it becomes understandable that within that concept the completely twisted (”perverse”) concept of ”love of one’s enemy” (Feindesliebe) came about. Considering further that the, if not founder, then certainly instigator of Christianity, in the most definite contrast to any pagan demon or hero, according to the testimony of the Gospels, used to call himself ”the Son of Man,” and thereby deified man to the same extent that he humanized the ”Father in Heaven,” we recognize for the core of the Pauline ”love of God” (Gottesliebe) the sanctification of man as the bearer of mere personhood, in relation to which all of nature can henceforth only claim the rank of booty or an instrument of his ”will to power.” —

Geschwisterliebe unterscheidet sich durch schwerbeschreibliche Eigenzüge von der Freundesliebe, Männerfreundschaft von Frauenfreundschaft, Kameradschaft wiederum von der Freundschaft überhaupt. Liebe der Eltern zu den Kindern ist weit etwas anderes als die Liebe der Kinder zu den Eltern. „Anders liebt der Gesell, anders der Meister den Meister“, äußert in der „Morgenröte“ Nietzsche.

Equally different from ”love for the thing” (Liebe zur Sache) as from the always dispositionally conceived ”love of humanity” (Menschenliebe) is love as a choice-determining inclination of the heart (Herzensneigung), we mean in the literal sense of the name inclination (inclination, gravitation, propensity), covered by the foreign word ”sympathy” (Sympathie). According to the type of preferred objects, several species again stand out among the inclinations.

Kaum braucht es gesagt zu werden, daß jede wahlbestimmende Herzensneigung eine Wahlverwandischaft zu begründen geeignet ist und darum als ursprüngliches Bindemittel zwischen den Menschen erscheint. Wo immer die bloße Interessengemeinschaft noch nicht jedes Naturband zerrissen hat, und selbst innerhalb der Interessenverbände entspringt das Zusammengehörigkeitsbewußtsein weiterer oder engerer Gruppen aus Sympathiegefühlen. Im Zusammenspiel mit der Macht der Gewohnheit erwächst daraus die natürliche Änhänglichkeit, die jemand an die Familie, die heimatliche Scholle, den Kreis der Berufsgenossen bekunden mag, und es gründet ferner darin das keineswegs nur sittliche Pathos des altertümlichen Treuebegriffes. Wir gingen z. B. fehl, wenn wir die altgermanische „Mannentreue‘ oder die skythische „Freundestreue‘“ mit ihren uns heute überschwänglich anmutenden Forderungen für das Erzeugnis bloß einer besonderen Sittlichkeit hielten und nicht darin zu erkennen [p. 17] vermöchten den zur Gesinnung verdichteten Niederschlag einer sympathetischen Lebensstimmung.

Inclination to specific things or their perceptible properties and characters: love for the sea, for the mountains, for the forest, for the heath, for the stars, clouds, bodies of water, for flowers, animals (dogs, cats, horses, birds), for stones, for the south, for the sun, for spring, summer, autumn and winter, for specific sounds, colors, scents, for flute playing, for a specific architectural style, for paintings, for objects of art of any kind. At least one aspect of ”love of homeland” (Heimatliebe) also belongs here, while its second and more important aspect will be discussed further below. –

Ferner gibt es, wie gesagt, eine Liebe zu anschaulichen Teilen und Eigenschaften des Menschen: zu schönen oder besonderen Händen, Füßen, Körperhaltungen, Nackenformen, Nasen, Hautfarben, Ausdüftungen. Die Vorliebe für blonde oder dunkle Haare, blaue oder braune Augen ist Ja als wahlbestimmend bekannt und gibt zugleich einen Fingerzeig, daß die Neigungsbeschaffenheit eines Menschen zum Teil aus Rasseeigentümlichkeiten gespeist wird. — Uhnterscheiden wir endlich nach Graden, so eröffnet sich eine beliebig unterteilbare Reihe von der bloßen Gewohnheitsneigung bis zu leidenschaftlicher Hin- _ gezogenheit. Keine obiger Neigungsarten, die nicht ebensowohl auf der Temperaturstufe der Lauheit verbliebe als sich auf die der Wärme und endlich der Glut erhöbe!

Love for something bears the unmistakable stamp of a preference (Vorliebe) for something, and to this, equally pronounced aversions (Abneigungen) can stand side by side, sometimes intensified and particularized into the so-called idiosyncrasies.

War in allen bis hierher betrachteten Fällen „Liebe“ mit „Neigung“ vertauschbar, so fassen wir nunmehr solche ins Auge, wo der Name unleugbar Triebe bezeichnet. Da es vielleicht den Anschein hat, Triebe und Neigungen seien dasselbe, so erörtern wir vorweg die uns vorschwebende Verschiedenheit beider.

Furthermore, inclination to specific people or to their perceptible parts and properties. Here again there are numerous subspecies: love of friends (Freundesliebe), love of parents (Elternliebe), love of children (Kindesliebe), love of siblings (Geschwisterliebe), love of family (Familienliebe), comradely love (kameradschaftliche Liebe), love for one’s superior, for the ruler, for subordinates, for the people, etc. Each type of inclination has its own distinct emotional tone. Thus, in the love for the ruler, the partial tone of veneration swings more strongly; in that for the subordinate or the people, that of compassion swings more strongly. Sibling love is distinguished from friendship love by indescribable peculiarities; male friendship from female friendship; comradeship, in turn, from friendship in general. The love of parents for children is something far different from the love of children for parents. ”The journeyman loves differently, the master loves the master differently,” Nietzsche remarks in The Dawn (Morgenröte).

(Man beachte an der Redewendung „ein gefallenes Mädchen“, daß sie den sogenannten Fehltritt als die Folge einer „Neigung“, eines „Hanges“ — daneben allerdings auch wertend — beurteilt!) Wenige Belege mögen es deutlich machen, daß damit vom gängigen Sprachgebrauch der Kerngehalt herausgeschält wurde.

It scarcely needs to be said that every choice-determining inclination of the heart is capable of establishing an elective affinity (Wahlverwandtschaft) and therefore appears as the original binding agent between people. Wherever the mere community of interest has not yet torn every natural bond, and even within interest associations, the consciousness of belonging together of wider or narrower groups springs from feelings of sympathy. In interplay with the power of habit, the natural attachment grows from this, which someone may show to the family, the native soil, the circle of professional colleagues, and furthermore, the by no means only moral pathos of the ancient concept of fidelity is founded in it. We would be mistaken, for example, if we considered the Old Germanic ”fidelity of the retainer” (Mannentreue) or the Scythian ”fidelity of friends” (Freundestreue) with their demands that seem exorbitant to us today as the product merely of a particular morality and were unable to recognize in them the precipitate, condensed into a disposition, of a sympathetic life-movement.

l. Vereinigungstrieb schlechthin.

If in all cases considered up to this point ”love” was interchangeable with ”inclination,” we now turn our attention to those where the name undeniably denotes drives (Triebe). Since it might appear that drives and inclinations are the same, we will first discuss the difference between them as we conceive Nothing prevents us from understanding the word inclination broadly enough that it includes drive, and the word drive broadly enough that it implies inclination. But if we did that, we would nevertheless notice in the effect of the psychic impulse, call it drive or inclination, two different sides, one of which could only be understood from the feeling of inclination, the other only from the experience of drive. For as certainly as ”inclination,” synonymous and replaceable by ”propensity” (Hang), already by its very name demonstrates the being-drawn of the bearer of inclination to the object of the inclination-choice, so certainly can every inclination retain the form of a mere state of feeling, without therefore fading or otherwise losing significance. In contrast, as if the attractive force could act on yet another organ of the soul, the feeling of the drive for union (Vereinigungstrieb) irresistibly impels its bearer into movements that actually result in union, and would, if that were impossible, inevitably be experienced as a more or less considerable disturbance. Consequently, we hold apart: proper inclinations and proper drives. ”Drive” is a directional given that from within inevitably ”drives”; ”inclination” is a directional given according to which one would sink or fall, provided the circumstances allowed it. (Note in the phrase ”a fallen girl” (ein gefallenes Mädchen) that it judges the so-called misstep as the consequence of an ”inclination,” a ”propensity” – albeit also evaluatively!) A few examples may make it clear that thereby the core content has been extracted from common usage.

Triebhafte Liebe ist immer Vereinigungstrieb und Vereinigungstrieb immer körperlicher Vereinigungstrieb. Da nun zwei Sonderwesen körperlich einszuwerden nicht in der Lage sind, so scheint die Urform der Erfüllung des Triebes jedes Höchstmaß körperlicher Nähe zu sein oder: die körperliche Berührung, für deren Vollendungszeichen eine ausgebreitete Völkergruppe die zugleich symbolische Handlung des Kusses nimmt. Fahnden wir demgemäß nach dem Sinn dieser Liebe im Ausdrucksbereich der Zärtlichkeit, so tritt uns als ihre Grundform entgegen die Mutterliebe.

While all types of inclination can also be called sympathies, it would sound to us partly too weak, partly simply inaccurate for genuine drives for union. Furthermore, we involuntarily denote the degrees of intensity of a drive with different adjectives than those of an inclination. We speak less often of ”violent inclinations” (heftige Neigungen) than of ”violent drives” (heftige Triebe), and we never choose the adjective ”deep” (tief) for the strength of a drive, whereas we believe we have most aptly described that of an inclination with: ”deep inclination,” ”deep sympathy.” For we consider the drive violent insofar as we want to emphasize with it the feeling by which it is a cause of movement; in contrast, we consider the inclination deep insofar as we have in mind a πάθημα (pathēma), a state. Finally, if we place beside aversion or antipathy the corresponding negative drive, it becomes clear that the latter effects an action, the former at most an omission. Out of deep aversion one will avoid its object; from the drive-experience of violent hate, anger, or envy one will seek it out in order to harm it. One speaks of a drive for revenge (Rachetrieb) and certainly not of an inclination for revenge (Racheneigung). – After the difference has been somewhat clarified, we list three linguistically distinguishable types of drive-like love. 1. Drive for union pure and simple (Vereinigungstrieb schlechthin).

Diese ist Neigungssache, jene Sache des Triebes. Denn einmal gehört das schon zu den natürlichen Folgen eines Triebes, der in der zärtlichen Betastung seine Erfüllung findet; sodann aber gilt die fragliche Fürsorge ursprünglich durchaus dem Körper des Kindes. Ein Fürsorgetrieb für sich besteht auch auf seiten des Vaters, nicht aber der auf Berührung zielende, körperlich spendende Fürsorgetrieb. Was gerade körperliche Nähe im Rahmen der Mutterliebe bedeutet, sehen wir klar, sobald wir bedenken, welches Maß von triebhaftem Widerstande die Mutter der Trennung von ihrem Kinde entgegensetzt. Am Trennungsschmerz einer Mutter verblassen die Trennungsschmerzen zweier sich trennenden Freunde, Geschwister, Ehegatten; nicht weil deren gegenseitige Liebe geringer wäre, sondern weil sie — soweit nicht durch lange Gewohnheit des Zusammenlebens verwandelt — weit weniger der körperlichen Nähe ihres Objektes bedarf als die Mutter der Körpernähe des Kindes. Die altchinesische Bildzeichenschrift drückt den Begriff der Liebe aus, indem sie neben die Gestalt einer Frau die eines Kindes stellt.

Drive-like love is always a drive for union, and a drive for union is always a drive for physical union. Since two separate beings are not in a position to become physically one, the primal form of the fulfillment of the drive seems to be every maximum of physical proximity or: physical touch, for whose sign of completion an extensive group of peoples takes the simultaneously symbolic action of the kiss. If we accordingly search for the meaning of this love in the expressive realm of tenderness (Zärtlichkeit), its basic form confronts us as mother love (Mutterliebe).

Haben wir in der Mutterliebe den ‚Vereinigungstrieb schlechthin‘ erkannt, so wird es uns nicht mehr wundern, in der Zärtlichkeit überhaupt stets auch einen Zusatz von Bemutterungstrieb zu finden. Die Sprache hat nun zwar für die zahllosen Arten des außergeschlechtlichen Vereinigungstriebes nicht ebenso viele derartiger Kuppelwörter gebildet, wie sie es für dessen Grundart mit der „Mutterliebe‘ tat, läßt aber allein schon durch die stehende [p. 22] Wendung „zärtliche Liebe‘ erkennen, daß sie die Zärtlichkeit für das Wesenskennzeichen der auf Vereinigung abzielenden Liebe nehme. Wir finden den größten Reichtum dahingehöriger Triebe in der Kindheit (dem Gegenstück zur Mütterlichkeit), deren Tierliebe etwa — stark verschieden von der durchschnittlichen Tierliebe Erwachsener — jeweils alsbald in ein Streicheln, Hegen und Herzen des geliebten Tieres übergeht.

We use the example of mother love to once again illuminate the essential difference between inclination and drive. – The mother love of a mother for her children can be precisely distinguished from her sympathy for one or the other child. The latter is a matter of inclination, the former a matter of drive. The always personal sympathy shows many variants in degree and character; the quasi-impersonal mother love of one mother always looks confusingly similar to that of a second, and similarly the same mother’s mother love for one child to that for the other! Like every drive, something purely animal, mother love certainly has psychic depth, but by no means spiritual height, and belongs no less to the animal mother than to the human mother. From its foreignness to spirit it immediately follows why the mother fundamentally knows and acknowledges no other excitatory value in her own child than its filial belonging to her. As a mother, she will cling to the ”prodigal son,” even if she should condemn his behavior, no less than to the model son. She need not be blind to her children’s weaknesses; but these do not touch her maternal drive for tenderness at all. And this does not apply equally to the love of the father, who rather, as a father, shows selective inclinations towards his children! And so language is right when it does not place beside mother love an essentially related ”father love”! (The not infrequently observable drive-relationship between fathers and daughters belongs to the realm of sexuality, which we have not yet dealt with in a single word.) – Finally, the circumstance that mother love culminates in care (Fürsorge) for the beloved also does not argue against its drive-like nature. For one thing, that already belongs to the natural consequences of a drive that finds its fulfillment in tender touching; but then, the care in question originally pertains entirely to the body of the child. A drive for care exists also on the part of the father, but not the drive for care aimed at touch, physically giving. What physical proximity means precisely within the framework of mother love, we see clearly as soon as we consider the measure of drive-like resistance the mother opposes to separation from her child. The separation pain of a mother overshadows the separation pains of two parting friends, siblings, spouses; not because their mutual love is lesser, but because – insofar as it is not transformed by long habit of living together – it far less needs the physical proximity of its object than the mother needs the bodily proximity of the child. The ancient Chinese pictographic script expresses the concept of love by placing next to each other the figure of a woman and that of a child! If we have recognized in mother love the ”drive for union pure and simple,” it will no longer surprise us to find always also an admixture of a drive to mother (Bemutterungstrieb) in tenderness in general. Language, it is true, has not formed for the innumerable kinds of extra-sexual drive for union as many such compound words as it did for its basic kind with ”mother love,” but it shows already by the standard phrase ”tender love” (zärtliche Liebe) that it takes tenderness to be the essential characteristic of love aiming at union. We find the greatest richness of drives belonging here in childhood (the counterpart to motherliness), whose love for animals (Tierliebe), for instance – strongly different from the average love for animals of adults – almost immediately transitions into a stroking, nurturing, and embracing of the beloved animal.

Die „zärtliche Liebe‘, wofür wir in Anbetracht ihrer Natur eines Triebes auch Zärtlichkeitsbedürfnis zu sagen berechtigt sind, zieht sich, wenn auch verschiedensten Grades, durch das ganze Leben des Menschen, ist durchweg größer bei Frauen als bei Männern, erreicht für jeden ein besonderes Höchstmaß und kann sich auf alle nur möglichen Dinge erstrecken. So gibt es eine Liebe zu Pelzen, zum Sammet, zu Vogelfedern, schwellenden Pfühlen, weichen Rasenplätzen, zum Diwan, ja sogar zu bildsamem Ion, formbarer Erde, kurz, zu den verschiedensten sei es weichen, sei es federnden Gegenständen, die durchaus im Bedürfnis nach zärtlicher Betastung wurzelt. Ja, selbst die Vorliebe für das Baden in lauen Gewässern, die im Altertum den Wärmegrad einer volkstümlichen Passion erstieg, führt zum größeren Teil ebenhierauf zurück. Wie vom Kinde schon dargetan, kann auch die Liebhaberei für bestimmte Tiere (etwa die Katze) mitbestimmt sein durch die schwellend weiche Form des Objektes und ist alsdann gar wohl zu unterscheiden von der weitaus gegenständlicher zugeschnittenen Tierliebe, deren wir früher Erwähnung getan. — Endlich erleidet es keinen Zweifel, daß jene ganz ursprüngliche [p. 23] Heimatliebe, die im Trennungsfalle ein „Heimweh“ von (mindestens bei Naturvölkern) lebensgefährlicher Heftigkeit zeitigt, aus einem Triebe gespeist wird, den. nur die körperliche Nähe von Haus und Heim befriedigt. Livingstone hat es zuerst, nämlich von den Randbewohnern des Lualabaflusses berichtet, daß sie nach Trennung von ihrer Heimat nicht selten in kurzer Zeit an „gebrochenem Herzen starben. Bei ebendiesen und vielen anderen „Naturvölkern“ ist die Verschwendung von Zärtlichkeiten an Pflanzen und Bäume, Arbeitsgerät oder Waffen noch etwas Gewöhnliches. Im alten Germanien wünschte man dem Baum „Guten Morgen‘ und sagte ihm feierlich den Tod des Hausherrn an. Manche Afrikaner weckten durch "Trommelwirbel ihre Kähne und sangen sie nach geschehener Fahrt mit Schlummerweisen wieder in Schlaf. In diesem Zusammenhange verstehen wir es, daß der Heimwehkranke plötzlich genesen könne, dem von seiner Heimat ein körperliches Stück, etwa ein dort gebackener Brotlaib, zuteil wird (wobei denn freilich dessen erst später zu erwägende EAßbarkeit auch noch in Frage steht). Auch mag uns hier in neuem Lichte der symbolische Gehalt der Sage vom Antäos erscheinen, der ‘seine Kraft verliert, wenn man ihn der körperlichen Berührung mit der Erde beraubt!

The ”tender love,” for which, considering its nature as a drive, we are also justified in saying need for tenderness (Zärtlichkeitsbedürfnis), runs through the whole life of a human being, albeit of the most varied degrees, is generally greater in women than in men, reaches a particular maximum for each individual, and can extend to all possible things. Thus there is a love for furs, for velvet, for bird feathers, swelling cushions, soft lawns, for the divan, indeed even for malleable clay, formable earth, in short, for the most diverse objects, be they soft or springy, which is rooted entirely in the need for tender touch. Indeed, even the preference for bathing in lukewarm waters, which in antiquity reached the temperature level of a popular passion, leads back for the greater part precisely to this. As already demonstrated by the child, the fondness for certain animals (e.g., the cat) can also be co-determined by the swellingly soft form of the object and is then quite distinguishable from the far more object-specific love for animals we mentioned earlier. – Finally, it admits of no doubt that that very original love of homeland (Heimatliebe), which in case of separation produces a ”homesickness” (Heimweh) of (at least among natural peoples) life-threatening intensity, is nourished by a drive that only the physical proximity of house and home satisfies. Livingstone first reported it, namely from the inhabitants on the banks of the Lualaba River, that after separation from their homeland they not infrequently died within a short time of a ”broken heart.” Among these very peoples and many other ”natural peoples,” the lavish spending of tendernesses on plants and trees, work tools or weapons is still something common. In ancient Germania, one wished the tree ”Good Morning” and solemnly announced to it the death of the master of the house. Many Africans woke their canoes with drum rolls and sang them back to sleep with lullabies after the journey was done. In this context, we understand that the homesick person could suddenly recover if a physical piece of his homeland, say a loaf of bread baked there, is given to him (though its edibility, which must be considered later, also still comes into question). Here too the symbolic content of the legend of Antaeus, who loses his strength when deprived of physical contact with the earth, may appear to us in a new light! In doing so – linking the end to the beginning – we once again arrive at the warmly encompassing motherliness, this time of the earth, and we immediately perceive a close kinship of ”tender love” and ”intimate love” (innige Liebe) and want to emphasize in advance that the bungling psychology which undertakes to derive tenderness, intimacy, motherliness, in short the ”drive for union pure and simple” from the sexual drive, has contributed like nothing else to making the already dulled sense of the ”educated” completely blind to the essence of – Eros. 2. Drive for incorporation/consumption (Verschlingungstrieb).

Indem wir damit — das Ende an den Anfang knüpfend — wiederum zur warm umfangenden Mütterlichkeit, diesmal aber der Erde, gelangt sind, nehmen wir sogleich eine enge Verwandtschaft wahr von „zärtlicher Liebe“ und ‚„inniger Liebe‘‘ und wollen vorgreifend unterstreichen, daß die Pfuscherpsychologie, die [p. 24] Zärtlichkeit, Innigkeit, Mütterlichkeit, kurz den „Vereinigungstrieb schlechthin“ aus dem Geschlechtstriebe abzuleiten unternimmt, wie nichts anderes dazu beigetragen hat, den schon abgestumpften Sinn der „Gebildeten“ vollends erblinden zu machen für das Wesen des — Eros.

We will see later what an extraordinary role the drive for incorporation plays in the realm of Eros. Here, however, where we are first elucidating the concept of love, one will doubt whether common usage offers any support for the inclusion of the drive for incorporation, even if one concedes that the intake of solid or liquid nourishment could after all be considered from the viewpoint of a union that even far surpasses mere touch in ”intimacy” (Innigkeit). But now, one does not deny the possibility of a pleasure in eating, a pleasure in drinking, and would thereby have already agreed to the possibility of love for food, love for drink. But each of the two drives (for hunger and thirst are two of them!) shows a great variety of selective inclinations: thus the love for drinking water differs from that for wine or milk, indeed the love for Bordeaux from that for Rhine wine or Tokay. We are not yet asking how this comes about, and only point out that the pleasure in food, the pleasure in drink, also has its climax and finds its fulfillment only in the incorporation (Verschlingen) of the pleasure-arousing object.

2. Verschlingungstrieb.

But if the experience of eating or drinking always also contains specific (= qualitative) traits, then one must agree when we even consider hungering and thirsting from the viewpoint of a drive for union, namely for the purpose of incorporation, and, even with regard to the needaspect, most decidedly reject the attempt to derive them from a ”drive for nutrition” (Ernährungstrieb)! Nutrition is a consequence of food intake, first recognized by man and hence conscious only to him, but not a component of the drive itself! One falsifies, and hopelessly so, the entire doctrine of drives if one believes one may read off the essence of drives, which can only be discovered in the experience, from observed effects of their activity! How much better informed the life-wisdom of the ancients was in this may be shown by the circumstance that even such a late and already quite professorial thinker as Aristotle defined hunger as a drive for contact with the dry, thirst as for contact with the fluid.

Welche außerordentliche Rolle der Verschlingungstrieb im Reiche des Eros spiele, werden wir später sehen. Hier dagegen, wo wir vorerst den Begriff der Liebe erläutern, wird man Zweifel hegen, ob der Sprachgebrauch der Einbeziehung des Verschlingungstriebes eine Stütze biete, gesetzt selbst, man gäbe zu, daß die Aufnahme fester oder flüssiger Speise immerhin für eine Vereinigung gelten könne, die an „Innigkeit‘‘ sogar noch bei weitem hinausgehe über die bloße Berührung. Allein nun leugnet man nicht die Möglichkeit einer Lust am Essen, einer Lust am Trinken und hätte denn damit schon eingewilligt in die Möglichkeit der Liebe zur Speise, der Liebe zum Trank. Es weist aber jeder der beiden Triebe (denn Hunger und Durst sind deren zwei!) einen großen Spielartenreichtum wählerischer Neigungen auf: so unterscheidet sich die Trinkliebe zum Wasser von der zum Wein oder zur Milch, ja die Liebe zum Bordeaux von der zum Rheinwein oder Tokayer. Wir fragen noch nicht, wie das zugehe, und weisen nur darauf hin, daß auch die Lust an der Speise, die Lust am Getränk ihren Höhepunkt habe und ihre Erfüllung finde erst im Verschlingen des lusterregenden Gegenstandes.

3. Sexual drive or ”Sexus.” While in the previous case a special explanation was needed to make visible the nominal relation of love to the drive for incorporation, now precisely the opposite would be necessary to demonstrate that love means anything else at all than – sexual drive, if we had begun with this side of the matter! To spare us that, we have placed the sexual drive at the end. It is therefore sufficient to note that ”love” often simply means sexual drive and ”to love” (lieben) means the satisfaction of the sexual drive.

p. 25

Zum erstenmal sind wir auf eine Gattung von Trieben [p. 25] gestoßen, die zur Ausführung von Bewegungen nicht nur drängen, sondern sogar sie erzwingen und somit als eigentlichste, weil unwiderstehliche, Bedürfnisse auftreten, deren Steigerungsgrade statt Leidenschaft oder Begeisterung vielmehr Begierden heißen: „gieriger Hunger“, „gieriger Durst“. Allein, wenn schon die wählerische Natur des Verschlingungstriebes neben der nicht zu verkennenden Bedürfnisseite auch eine Neigungsseite an ihm zu bemerken gestattet, so wird es vollends niemanden geben, der nicht der besonderen Lüste, der unvergleichlichen Genüsse zu gedenken vermöchte, die ihm durch Essen oder durch Trinken irgendwann einmal zuteil geworden. Liegen aber im Erlebnis des Essens oder des Trinkens immer auch artliche (qualitative) Züge, so wird man beipflichten müssen, wenn wir sogar das Hungern und Dürsten aus dem Gesichtspunkt eines Vereinigungstriebes, behufs Verschlingung nämlich, betrachten und selbst im Hinblick auf die Bedürfnisseite aufs entschiedenste den Versuch verwerfen, sie herzuleiten aus einem „Ernährungstriebe“‘! Die Ernährung ist eine erst vom Menschen erkannte und mithin nur ihm bewußte Folge der Nahrungsaufnahme, nicht aber ein Bestandstück des Triebes selbst! Man verfälscht und zwar hoffnungslos die gesamte Trieblehre, wenn man das nur im Erlebnis zu entdeckende Wesen der Triebe ablesen zu dürfen vermeint aus beobachteten Wirkungen ihrer Betätigung! Wie sehr die Lebensweisheit der Alten darin besser unterrichtet war, mag uns der Umstand zeigen, daß selbst noch ein so später und schon ganz professoraler Denker wie Aristoteles den Hunger als einen Trieb zur Berührung mit [p. 26] dem Trockenen, den Durst als mit dem Flüssigen bestimmt.

Since today by ”sexual drive” one more precisely means the bi-sexual drive for copulation (Begattungstrieb), and hopes to gain from it an understanding both for other types of drive for copulation and for other types of sexual activity (which in relation to that then all become so-called perversities), so linguistically, under this category of love would belong: homosexual love, pederastic love (Knabenliebe), lesbian love, sexual love for animals, solitary love (masturbation); furthermore, love for causing pain (”sadism”) and love for suffering pain (”masochism”); furthermore, sexual exhibitionistic love (geschlechtliche Zeigeliebe), as one might Germanize ”exhibitionism” with its innumerable sub- and side-species; finally, every kind of sexual love for human body parts, bodily products, and objects of use, for which the completely inaccurate name ”fetishism” has been adopted.

3. Geschlechtstrieb oder „Sexus“.

We will show later, insofar as the illumination of Eros requires it, why the mode of consideration starting from the bi-sexual drive for copulation is insufficient. On the other hand, let it be especially emphasized here for the sexual drive, what we already cited for the distinction between hunger and ”drive for nutrition”: that it is fundamentally wrong and an intentional falsification to call it a ”drive for propagation” (Fortpflanzungstrieb). For again, propagation is certainly a possible consequence of sexual activity, but it does not lie as an aim within the experience of sexual excitation. The animal does not know of it, but exclusively the human being. Accordingly, it is not the animal in man, which alone has the sexual drive, that is driven to copulation for reasons of propagation; but rather his spirit, which does not have it, may accompany the drive-activity with the thought of propagation and with wishes directed towards it.

Während es im vorigen Falle einer besonderen Erläuterung bedurfte, um die Namensbeziehung der Liebe zum Verschlingungstriebe sichtbar zu machen, so wäre nun gerade umgekehrt eine besondere Erläuterung nötig, um darzutun, daß Liebe überhaupt noch etwas anderes als — Geschlechtstrieb bedeute, wofern wir mit dieser Seite der Sache begonnen hätten! Um dessen überhoben zu sein, haben wir den Geschlechtstrieb an den Schluß gestellt. So genügt es denn zu bemerken, daß „Liebe“ vielfach schlechtweg für Geschlechtstrieb und „lieben“ für die Befriedigung des Geschlechtstriebes steht.

Now, wishes can certainly also assume a drive-like form. However, then it is firstly something completely different from the sexual drive, which in turn would sink to the level of merely a means to an end in relation to it, and it is furthermore not a drive at all, but an ”interest,” a direction of the will, a motive (Triebfeder) of a personally very varied nature. Thus, for instance, a man sometimes places his sexual drive in the service of the interest of preserving the lineage, and that would now be his ”drive for propagation.” In contrast, if a woman wishes for children, perhaps even a whole troop, she has regularly subordinated the sexual drive to her drive to mother (Bemutterungstrieb), and that would now be her ”drive for propagation”: it has not the slightest similarity with the lineage-preservation wishes of the man! This example shows irrefutably into which abysses of absurdity one falls who confuses genuine natural drives with human interests and imagines he may deduce the former from consequences thought up and added to their activity.

p. 27

Da man heute mit „Geschlechtstrieb‘‘ genauer den zwiegeschlechtlichen Begattungstrieb meint und von ihm aus das Verständnis zu gewinnen hofft sowohl für sonstige Arten von Begattungstrieb als auch für sonstige Arten von Geschlechtsbetätigung (die im Verhältnis zu jenem dann sämtlich zu sogenannten Perversitäten werden), so würde also sprachgebräuchlich unter diese Abteilung der Liebe gehören: homosexuelle Liebe, Knabenliebe, lesbische Liebe, geschlechtliche Tierliebe, einsame Liebe; ferner Liebe zum Schmerzbereiten (,Sadismus“) und Liebe zum Schmerzerleiden (,„Masochismus“); ferner geschlechtliche Zeigeliebe, wie man den „Exhibitionismus“ mit seinen zahllosen Unter- und Nebenarten verdeutschen dürfte; endlich jede Art von geschlechtlicher Liebe zu menschlichen Körperteilen, Körpererzeugnissen und [p. 27] Gebrauchsgegenständen, wofür der unzutreffende Name des „Fetischismus‘“ Verwendung findet.

For all drive-like kinds of love, what we already had to emphasize with inclinations applies in a special measure: that their stronger degrees are called either ”passions” (Leidenschaften) or – when their object is evaluatively disparaged – ”addictions” (Süchte). For reasons not yet to be explained here, love passion contains three things: depth of inclination, violence of the drive, and the exclusivity of choice, completely unknown to the animal and early humanity. If one wants to emphasize more only the violence of the drive, one speaks of desire (Begierde) or of rut (Brunst): ”rutting love” (brünstige Liebe), ”love’s rut” (Liebesbrunst); (yet ”fervor/ardor” (Inbrunst) again implies depth simultaneously). Finally, if the object of the drive is to be debased, then one chooses ”addiction” (Sucht).

Wir zeigen später, soweit es die Beleuchtung des Eros erheischt, warum die vom zwiegeschlechtlichen Begattungstriebe ausgehende Betrachtungsweise nicht ausreicht. Dagegen sei hier für den Geschlechtstrieb noch besonders betont, was wir schon angeführt zur Scheidung von Hunger und „Nahrungstrieb“: daß es grundverkehrt und eine absichtliche Verfälschung wäre, ihn einen „Fortpflanzungstrieb“ zu nennen. Wiederum nämlich ist die Fortpflanzung zwar eine mögliche Folge sexueller Betätigung, liegt aber nicht als Zielvorstellung im Erlebnis geschlechtlicher Erregung darin. Es weiß davon nicht das Tier, sondern ausschließlich der Mensch. Demgemäß nicht das Tier im Menschen, das doch allein den Geschlechtstrieb hat, wird aus Fortpflanzungsgründen zur Begattung getrieben; wohl aber mag sein Geist, der ihn nicht hat, die Triebbetätigung mit dem Gedanken der Fortpflanzung und mit darauf gerichteten Wünschen begleiten. Nun kann allerdings auch der Wunsch eine triebhafte Form annehmen. Allein, dann ist er einmal etwas gänzlich anderes als der Geschlechtstrieb, der seinerseits ihm gegenüber auf die Stufe bloß eines Mittels zum Zwecke sänke, und er ist ferner überhaupt kein Trieb, sondern ein „Interesse‘, eine Willensrichtung, eine Triebfeder von persönlich sehr verschiedener Beschaffenheit. So stellt etwa der Mann seinen Geschlechtstrieb bisweilen in den Dienst des Interesses der Stammeserhaltung, und das wäre nun sein „„Fortpflanzungstrieb“. Dahingegen, wenn ein Weib sich Kinder wünscht, vielleicht selbst eine ganze Schar, [p. 28] so hat sie den Geschlechtstrieb regelmäßig untergeordnet ihrem Bemutterungstriebe, und das wäre nun ihr „Fortpflanzungstrieb‘“: er hat mit den Stammeserhaltungswünschen des Mannes gar keine Ähnlichkeit! An diesem Beispiel tritt es unwidersprechlich zutage, in welche Abgründe von Widersinn wir geraten, wenn wir echte Naturtriebe mit menschlichen Wünschen verwechseln und jene erschließen zu dürfen meinen aus hinzugedachten Folgen ihrer Betätigung.

Thus, from the ”love of play” (Liebe zum Spiel) soon becomes ”passion for play” (Spielleidenschaft or Spielpassion), but also ”gambling addiction” (Spielsucht); from the ”love of drink” (Liebe zum Trunk), ”drunkenness/addiction to drink” (Trunksucht); from the ”love of eating” (Liebe zum Essen), ”gluttony” (Freßsucht); likewise ”morphine addiction” (Morphiumsucht), ”opium addiction” (Opiumsucht), and so on. Since the word Sucht originally meant diseases, the psychic addictiveness of every kind enters into the closest relation with ”viciousness” (Lasterhaftigkeit), whence it becomes understandable that particularly many love habits fall under the genus of ”vices” (Laster).

Für alle triebhaften Liebesarten gilt in besonderem Maße, was wir schon bei den Neigungen hervorzuheben hatten, daß ihre stärkeren Grade entweder „Leidenschaften‘ oder — bei abschätziger Wertung ihres Gegenstandes — „Süchte“ heißen. Aus hier noch nicht auseinanderzulegenden Gründen steckt in der Liebesleidenschaft dreierlei: Tiefe der Neigung, Heftigkeit des Triebes und die dem Tier wie der frühen Menschheit völlig unbekannte Ausschließlichkeit der Wahl. Will man mehr nur die Heftigkeit des Triebes betonen, so spricht man von Begierde oder von Brunst: „brünstige Liebe‘, „Liebesbrunst‘‘; (doch liegt in der „Inbrunst‘‘ wieder zugleich die Tiefe). Soll endlich der Gegenstand des Triebes herabgewürdigt werden, dann wählt man „Sucht“. So wird aus der „Liebe zum Spiel“ bald die „Spielleidenschaft“ oder „Spielpassion“, bald aber auch die „Spielsucht“, aus der „Liebe zum Trunk“ die „Trunksucht“, aus der „Liebe zum Essen“ die „Freßsucht“, ebensa „Morphiumsucht“, „Opiumsucht‘“ und so fort. Da das Wort Sucht ursprünglich Krankheiten meinte, so tritt die seelische Süchtigkeit jeder Art in engste Beziehung zur [p. 29] „Lasterhaftigkeit“, woraus es verständlich wird, daß zumal wieder viele Liebesgewohnheiten unter die Gattung der „Laster“ fallen.

Having reached the end of this by no means exhaustive overview of the occurrences of the word love in contemporary usage, we string together some of the enumerated love concepts and seek to make their extraordinary diversity palpable by designating their essential sense in advance, each time with a word that does not contain ”love”: thing-interest (Sachinteresse – Love for the thing) – selfishness (Selbstsucht – Self-love) – respect for people (Menschenachtung – Love of neighbor, Love of enemy) – enthusiasm for beauty (Schönheitsbegeisterung – Love of beauty) – friendship (Freundschaft – Love of friends) – motherliness (Mütterlichkeit – Mother love) – tenderness (Zärtlichkeit – Tender love) – passion (Leidenschaft – Violent love) – alcoholism (Trunksucht – Love of drink) – Sexual drive (Geschlechtstrieb – ”Love”). If one keeps in mind that accordingly, with one and the same word, can be covered, among others: selfishness – friendship – thing-interest – sexuality – motherliness, then one understands why it would be a hopeless undertaking to tie an investigation of essence to the name of love, and has gained the standard to correctly assess the bungling behavior that – misled by a word – presumes to want to derive all possible impulses and properties to which the word is occasionally applied from one trait of inwardness and even from one drive and finally from the – sexual drive! One knows the orgies of sexualism from earlier and especially from today’s medicine.

III. Der Elementare Eros — III. The Elementary Eros

Am Ende angelangt dieser noch keineswegs erschöpfenden Übersicht der Vorkommnisse des Wortes Liebe im heutigen Sprachgebrauch, reihen wir einige der aufgezählten Liebesbegriffe aneinander und suchen ihre ganz außerordentliche Verschiedenheit dadurch handgreiflich zu machen, daß wir deren wesentlichen Sinn jeweils vor"weg durch ein „Liebe“ nicht enthaltendes Wort bezeichnen: Sachinteresse (Liebe zur Sache) — Selbstsucht (Eigenliebe) — Menschenachtung (Nächstenliebe, Feindesliebe) — Schönheitsbegeisterung (Schönheitsliebe) -— Freundschaft (Freundesliebe) — Mütterlichkeit (Mutterliebe) — Zärtlichkeit (zärtliche Liebe) — Leidenschaft (heftige Liebe) — Trunksucht (Liebe zum Trunk) — Geschlechtstrieb (,Liebe“). Hält man sich vor Augen, daß darnach mit einem und demselben Worte gedeckt werden können u. a.: Selbstsucht — Freundschaft — Sachinteresse — Sexualität — Mütterlichkeit, so begreift man, weshalb es ein hoffnungsloses Beginnen wäre, eine Wesensuntersuchung an den Namen der Liebe zu knüpfen, und hat den Maßstab gewonnen zur Abschätzung des stümperhaften Gebarens, das — genasführt von einem Worte — sich unterfängt, alle nur möglichen Regungen und Eigenschaften, auf die das Wort gelegentlich Anwendung findet, herleiten zu wollen aus einem Zuge der Innerlichkeit und gar noch aus einem Triebe und endlich aus dem — Geschlechtstrieb! Man kennt die Orgien des Sexualismus aus der früheren und besonders aus der [p. 30] heutigen Medizin. Aber man wird sich nach unseren sprachlichen Aufschlüssen nicht mehr hinreißen lassen, sie nur insoweit ernst zu nehmen, daß man um die in Frage stehende Sache mitstritte; sondern man wird sich zu bemerken erlauben, daß der umstrittene Gegenstand, die sogenannte Liebe nämlich, bloß im Einheit vortäuschenden V erwendungsspielraum eines Namens vorhanden sei, dagegen überhaupt nicht in der — Wirklichkeit! Hieraus erhelle zugleich, wohin die „Wissenschaft“, d. i. die bloße Tatsachenforschung, zu geraten Gefahr läuft, wofern sie es unterläßt, sich zu verständigen mit der „Metaphysik“, d. i. der Wesensforschung!

Wenige Worte genügen, um nun auch den zweiten Grund noch namhaft zu machen, der es uns verböte, statt vom „Eros“ von der „Liebe“ zu handeln. Unter sämtlichen oben zusammengestellten Bedeutungen für das Wort Liebe kommt nämlich keine vor, die aus der Kenntnis des elementaren Eros gewonnen wäre. Wohl werden wir diesen an mehr als einer Liebesregung beteiligt finden; aber ihrer keine wäre geeignet, an seine Stelle zu treten. Wir sind also nicht in der Lage, aus den tausendundeins Bedeutungen, die das Wort Liebe heute besitzt, auch nur irgendeine herauszugreifen, wenn wir vom ursprünglichen Eros handeln; womit der Verzicht auf den Namen der Liebe hinlänglich dürfte begründet sein.

Bevor wir nach dieser Klärung des Liebesbegriffes zum elementaren Eros kommen, müssen wir kurz erörtern, was das auf den Namen des heidnischen Gottes getaufte Erlebnis in der Auffassung der Antike gewesen sei.

But after our linguistic disclosures, one will no longer let oneself be carried away to take them seriously only to the extent of arguing about the matter in question; rather, one will permit oneself to remark that the disputed object, namely the so-called love, exists only in the deceptively unity-suggesting range of application of a name, but not at all in – reality! From this, it simultaneously becomes clear where ”science,” that is to say, mere fact-research, runs the danger of ending up, if it neglects to come to an understanding with ”metaphysics,” that is to say, essence-research! A few words suffice to now also name the second reason that would forbid us to speak of ”love” instead of ”Eros.” Among all the meanings for the word love compiled above, none occurs that was gained from the knowledge of the elementary Eros. To be sure, we will find this Eros involved in more than one loving impulse; but none of them would be suited to take its place.

We are therefore not in a position to pick out even a single one from the thousand-and-one meanings that the word love possesses today, when we speak of the original Eros; whereby the renunciation of the name of love should be sufficiently justified.

Before we come, after this clarification of the concept of love, to the elementary Eros, a brief discussion cannot be avoided concerning what the experience baptized with the name of the pagan god Eros was, not so much in and for itself, but rather in the conception of antiquity.

Two notions typically come to mind in everyday conversation when speaking of the Eros of antiquity: the image of a winged youth with bow, quiver, arrows, wreath, and torch, and alongside it, the idea of “Platonic Eros” or “Platonic love.” The image of the charmingly playful Erotes, however, reached its perfection only in Alexandrian times, belonging to the rococo of antiquity. The Platonic Eros, while tied to something deeper, as we shall demonstrate, represents a deliberate distortion of it.

Eros as a mischievous little boy, punished by his mother Aphrodite with a rod for his naughtiness, as a putto, is a creation of the bucolic poets. In Theocritus’ Adoniazusen, Aphrodite and Adonis recline, and above them:

A throng of Erotes hovers, like boys in their loveliest bloom,

Like the nightingale’s brood, sheltered in the shady tree,

Flitting from branch to branch, testing their youthful wings.

This form, or rather the somewhat richer figure of “Amor,” whose passionate yet fateful love for “Psyche” is recounted in Apuleius’ charming tale, has so displaced the original form of the god in the consciousness of late antiquity that even the educated today often struggle to follow when one speaks of the “cosmogonic” Eros!

It sounds somewhat different when we hear the chorus in Sophocles’ Antigone (translated by Jordan): All-conquering Eros, You choose as your ambush For the leap upon assured prey The rosy cheeks of the maiden.

You stride over the sea

And over the rural plain.

None can escape you,

Neither eternal god

Nor mortal man.

To feel you is to rave.

You lure even the heart of the just

To injustice.

Through you, this time too,

Father and son were inflamed to discord;

For against the mighty voice

Of the primal command

The longing of love arises...

And even more than in the conceptually sharpened verses of the tragedian, we begin to sense the demonic nature of the original Eros when Anacreon, otherwise so playfully jesting about him, declares: Eros strikes him with a mighty hammer and then bathes him, as a smith bathes glowing steel in the torrent.

We are touched, as if by the wingbeats of a long-lost shudder of youth, by the verses of the solemn Ibycus (in free translation):

Like a Thracian north storm, lashed by the lightning

Of Cypris’ scorching flames,

So, with reeling senses, yet with irresistible force,

Eros reigns in my heart since I awoke.

We feel that between these verses and those of Theocritus lies a vast distance, during which the concept of Eros in the minds of thinkers utterly transformed. In step with an increasingly humanized and bounded era, the at least as threatening as it was auspicious form of a demon of elemental natural forces gradually faded, as if drowned in distant clouds, until it finally succumbed to oblivion. By Theocritus’ time, three and a half centuries had passed since Ibycus!

There is no doubt, then, that the further back we go in time, the more vividly that image re-emerges, its superhuman contours coming into focus. The fact that Eros does not appear at all in the theology of the much older Homer should not deter us from assuming an antiquity for the impulses that gave rise to him, extending even beyond Homer. For it is now beyond doubt that among the Ionian Greeks of the Asia Minor coast, the triumph of spiritual and personal gods over chthonic and elemental divine forces occurred much earlier than among the Greeks of the mainland.

We are unlikely to find the most telling signs of primordial times in Homer, but rather in Hesiod and in the legacy of the sects and secret societies that emerged from the struggle of all layers of the Greek soul with the Dionysian tide between the eighth and sixth centuries BCE.

In Hesiod, though he otherwise scarcely mentions Eros, we immediately encounter the “cosmogonic” Eros! Alongside Gaia and Tartarus, he is one of the world-creating divine trinity that emerges from the pre-polar Chaos. He appears particularly prominently in the cosmogonies of Orphism (albeit in the later versions, which connect to older traditions). In the most well-known and significant of these, Chronos, the “never-aging time,” creates from the Aether and the immeasurable abyss, Chaos, the silver world-egg. From it springs the radiant god Eros, himself a demonic trinity of Metis, Erikapaios, and Phanes. He is androgynous and carries within himself the seeds of all gods! Through self-procreation, as we may anticipate, he generates the oldest of the three Greek divine generations: Echidna, Gaia, and Uranus. Similarly, the Zeus of Pherecydes becomes a demiurge only after transforming into Eros. Lucian still speaks of the “cosmogonic” Eros as the “hierophant of the mysteries,” who plunged Chaos into the depths and, with a “radiant torch,” penetrated the forming night. That the Orphics, despite later veering into a doctrine of salvation and fertilizing the ground, so to speak, on which the Pythagorean school and later Platonism would flourish, were nonetheless rooted in ancient cults is no longer in doubt, based on the evidence we possess from recent research.

How little does this align with our image of winged Erotes when we learn that the original Eros of Boeotian Thespiae, in whose honor the Thespians later held their Erotidia festival every five years, was a massive, unhewn stone: akin to the aerolith of the great mother, Cybele, or, bridging the vast expanse of time, akin to that conical stone from Emesa, with which the madness of the Caesar Elagabal (priest of the god of the same name) mocked the Olympian gods of Rome! (Though far less than it seemed! For even in the temple of Jupiter Feretrius lay a “sacred flint,” by which oaths were sworn and solemn treaties concluded!) Similarly, we have knowledge from Laconic Leuctra and from Parion in the Troad—reaching back to Pelasgian prehistory—of temples and festivals dedicated to this same Eros. The transition to the Orphic-Bacchic initiations is clearly mediated by an ancient cult of the “gift-giving” Gaia at Phlya, where, alongside Zeus Ktesios, “Eros Kosmogonos” presided as a mystery god and “redeemer.” In his honor, the Orphic hymns of the Lycomids resounded (which Pausanias, who still knew them, ranked above the Homeric hymns!).

No less than this demon of planetary pregnancy and eternal rebirth differs from the Alexandrian Eros of fleeting passions, so too does the Eros of male love, though later in origin, diverge from the notion we commonly associate with the predominantly Platonically derived concept of Hellenic “pederasty.” There must have been something profoundly serious, even fearsome, about a god who bound the “sacred band” of Thebans together in loyalty unto heroic death for their homeland, or who compelled the Cretans to place their most beautiful youths in the front lines of battle as sacrifices to Eros. And certainly, it was not due to any soft trait that his cult persisted longest in the harsh and archaic Sparta!

From all this, we consider ourselves justified in placing the original Eros among the great mystery gods—Sabazios, Attis, Adonis, Dionysus, Bacchus, Osiris, Serapis, and so forth—who, in turn, form the diverse entourage of the great mother: Cybele, Astarte, Aphrodite, Ma, Anaitis, Berekynthia, Isis, Demeter, Hecate, Persephone, Urania, Luna, Magna Mater, Venus Caelestis, Anna Perennis, Bellona, and so on. While these goddesses only grew more powerful in the divine syncretism of the Roman imperial era, the cult of Eros waned, leaving behind two remnants of the once-demonic figure of ancient rites: the human Eros, glorified in art and playfully sung of by lyric poets, and Eros as a philosophical sub-concept within the degenerated cathartic teachings of secret societies, which later revolved particularly around the figures of Baal, Serapis, Zagreus, Mithras, and Sol Invictus. We will later suggest why the cosmogonic Eros likely met such a fate, but first, we must dedicate a few words to the teachings of the man who decisively shaped the philosophical concept of Eros in antiquity: Plato.

Although Plato’s entire philosophy is more or less permeated by the idea of Eros, the two truly “erotic” dialogues are the Symposium and the Phaedrus. The former perhaps offers more insight into Greek pederasty, while the latter provides far greater clarity regarding the transformation that Plato wrought upon the ancient concept of Eros. Since the content of the Symposium can be assumed to be wellknown, we will limit ourselves to citing a few passages (following Schleiermacher’s translation), to which we will later refer, either affirmatively or critically.

In explaining the irresistible longing of love as the striving of the divided halves of an originally androgynous, spherical being, which the gods split in two to prevent it from becoming too powerful, it is said of lovers that when asked by Hephaestus whether he should weld them into one, they believe they have heard exactly what they most deeply desired: “to become one from two through closeness and fusion with the beloved.” From the later characterization of Eros by the Platonic Socrates, famously presented as a revelation from the woman Diotima, we need only recall the essential point that Eros is neither a god nor a human but a “daemon” partaking of both, stemming from his divine father Poros (resourcefulness, abundance) and his non-divine mother Penia (poverty, need). This accounts for his possessing just enough perfection to compel him to strive for higher perfection!

As such, he stands as a mediator between gods and humans, and through this daemonic mediation, all divination and enchantment occur:

“For a god does not mingle with humans; rather, all interaction and discourse between gods and humans takes place through this, whether in waking or in sleep. Whoever understands this is a daemonic man, while whoever focuses only on other things... is merely common.”

Thus, the state of erotic arousal, which, as the above statement suggests, we must understand far more broadly than modern usage allows, is grounded in a peculiar need that drives the needy toward the object of their need as a complementary pole that grants fulfillment:

“If love is always this,” Diotima continues, “in what way... do those pursue it whom we would properly call ‘lovers’?” And when Socrates cannot answer, she replies: “I will tell you. It is a bringing forth in the beautiful, both in body and in soul. For all humans are pregnant... and when they reach a certain age, our nature strives to create. And it cannot create in the ugly, but only in the beautiful... Beauty, then, is the birth-assisting goddess for creation.”

The desire for physical procreation is distinguished from the desire for spiritual procreation. The former is found in animals and in the animalistic part of humans, while the latter exists only in the spiritual part of humans. From this spiritual procreation arise the works of artists and poets, the deeds of heroes (to secure “eternal fame” in the memory of posterity), and true love relationships— naturally, between man and youth!—aimed at engendering the highest wisdom in the soul of the beloved and jointly recovering a heavenly bliss! How the lover ultimately achieves the goal of Eros, the telete (initiation), is better illustrated with a glance at Plato’s “theory of Forms” through the more illuminating Phaedrus.

Plato’s view is this: when someone loves a beautiful or excellent person, they do not love the perishable individual, inevitably flawed, but rather the beauty, excellence, goodness, justice, or other virtues that appear lovable and love-inspiring in them. The individual person is never beauty, goodness, or excellence itself but merely represents these qualities, as do many others besides them. Many individuals partake in the same singular beauty, goodness, or excellence, and it is their participation in these impersonal, imperishable, and flawless “Forms” that makes them lovable. But how do we come to know of these “Forms” when no perceptible individual is ever identical to any “Form”? How do we know of something that cannot be seen in any way?

The Platonic answer is: through recollection (anamnesis)! In a pre-birth, “better,” “higher” existence, when our soul—or rather its immortal part—was not yet mingled with corporeality, we were capable of beholding the “Forms” or archetypes. But then—apparently due to a kind of metaphysical fall— the immortal soul sank into corporeality, and with it came the forgetting of its entire pre-birth state and, consequently, the “Forms” it once beheld. Eros consists in this: upon encountering certain individuals endowed with an extraordinary share in those “Forms,” the memory of what was once seen suddenly flares up in us, so that—discovering the sensually invisible beauty, goodness, or excellence in their perishable image—we are seized by love for them.

“For a human must comprehend what is expressed in kinds, which arises as a unity from many sensory perceptions grasped by reason. This is precisely the recollection of what our soul once saw when it wandered with a god, looking beyond what we now call real and rising to what truly is.” “But beauty alone has received this fate, to be the most evident and enchanting.” The initiate, “who has already seen much of that realm, will shudder at first when he beholds a godlike face... that excellently mirrors beauty. Then he will feel a sacred awe, as before a god, and if he did not fear being deemed utterly mad, he would offer sacrifices to the beloved youth as to a divine image.”

There follows the struggle between the “evil and the good horse” of the soul, that is, between the “earthly and heavenly love” invented by Plato (in the Symposium as Aphrodite Pandemos and Urania), or in plain terms: between sensual inclination and abstract spiritual enthusiasm. Depending on the victory of “heavenly love” over “earthly love,” the longing of love sublimates, and in its place arises the desire to cultivate the spiritual essence of the beloved to its highest bloom, making them resemble the god that the flash of recollection revealed as latent within them. Moreover, each lover—through a kind of elective affinity—shapes the beloved after the god that guides them. Those led by Zeus seek whether the beloved’s soul is Zeus-like and destined to be a philosopher or supreme leader; those from Hera’s retinue seek a soul suited for kingship, and so forth.

“And since they regard the beloved as the cause of this, they are even more delighted with him and... like Bacchantes, they pour it into the soul of the beloved, making him as similar as possible to their god.”

Through such love, the soul gradually regains the “wings” it lost when it fell from the realm of archetypes into the world of perishable images, approaching even in this life the boundary where it becomes capable of beholding, instead of the ever-changing, “never constant” world, “that colorless, formless, and substanceless essence that truly exists.”

After death, the lovers reach the “super-celestial place,” which “no poet has ever worthily sung.” For they are: “winged and light, having triumphed in one of the three truly Olympian contests, and neither moderation nor divine madness can grant humans a greater good than this.”

Here we have the philosophical seed of the tale of “Amor and Psyche,” and we see the greatest concern of the ancient soul, Eros, pressed with the subtlest arts of seduction into the service of a world-fleeing metaphysics that veils its hostility to the senses with promises of otherworldly bliss and glosses over its true aim: hatred and war against desires. We will later address the meaning of the so-called “Forms” and for now only unveil the life-denying aim of this thinking with regard to the experience of love through a simple and easily understandable consideration.

We ask: is it true that when one person loves another, they love the beauty, goodness, or excellence in them? Everyone knows that a strong and deep love least of all seeks reasons for its existence; everyone also knows that the lover may discover many flaws and weaknesses in the beloved but does not wish to remove them, loving the beloved with all their imperfections, “skin and bones” included! Nevertheless, it remains possible that experienced goodness, beauty, or excellence forms the secret source of love. Yet, by virtue of every merit we can name, as well as every nameable flaw, the beloved surely aligns with all other beings of whom the same merits or flaws can be claimed. Is there anyone who, upon honest self-examination, would admit to having loved a person for traits they share with others? Or would they not rather notice that, as a beloved, that person differed from all other conceivable beings! Let us recall the great passions recounted by history and legend! For Kriemhild, there is only one Siegfried; for Tristan, only one Isolde; for Ophelia, only one Hamlet; for Heloise, only one Abelard; for Dante, only one Beatrice; for Julia, only one Romeo; for Hölderlin, only one Diotima. If this one perishes or is otherwise taken away, everything perishes for the lover, everything is taken away. The thought of countless other men or women of equal excellence does not in the least replace the one woman or man with whom, unpredictably, love was bound! As we noted earlier, antiquity indeed knows no “eternal” passions but only those of limited duration. Yet even an “eternal” passion can fade, and a second or third person may step into the beam of Eros’ light; but the one standing in it at that moment is, for that reason, utterly incomparable to the rest of the world.

This holds true without distinction for ancient love and medieval passion. And it is not incomparable because the lover believes it to be more excellent than all other beings, but because it is this one and no other. Thus, whoever claims that a being becomes lovable to another due to its (general) beauty or excellence lies. We say they lie because this falsifies the living state of Eros with the intellectual state of knowing and valuing! It is clear what secret purpose this falsification serves. By adorning the word “Eros” with an interest of the intellect and thereby legitimizing only an Eros whose object enjoys certain “virtues,” it displaces and destroys the true Eros. Just as the Christian rhetoric of love served only to supplant genuine love—which selects and deifies—with the leveling demand of universal respectability (for the “neighbor,” meaning, in effect, the rabble!), so too does the Platonic Eros (in Nietzsche’s words, “pre-existent Christianity”) attempt to substitute the soul’s erotic arousal with an affection of reason, according to which we are supposed to love conceptual phantoms guided by scholastic thinking! We scarcely need add that this proves the utter illusoriness of the “shudder” in which this Eros is supposed to find fulfillment.

[p. 51] Wollen wir uns über das Wesen des elementaren Eros, über seinen Sinn im Zusammenhange der Wirklichkeit, über die Bedingungen seines Auftretens verständigen, so müssen wir zuvörderst zu umschreiben versuchen, was er denn eigentlich sei. Und das wiederum kann nur dadurch geschehen, daß wir den Zustand schildern, der im Geiste unserer Auffassung ein elementarerotischer wäre. Sofern nun solche Kennzeichnung nur dem etwas sagen kann, der sie aus sich selbst zu erfüllen vermag mit der Erinnerung an mindestens irgendein Erlebnis ähnlicher Art, ist schon unsere allererste Betrachtung auch ein erster Schritt in „esoterische‘‘ Wissenschaft. Allein in der Beziehung ist die Seelenforschung zwar nicht besser, aber auch nicht wesentlich schlechter daran als die gewöhnliche Tatsachenforschung. Wir können dem Blindgeborenen die Gesetze der Optik vermitteln: der Vorstellung der Farbe Blau kommt er dadurch nicht näher, als wenn er niemals von Optik vernommen hätte. Wie wir die Fähigkeit des Sehens bei unserem Hörer voraussetzen müssen, wann wir ihm von Farben sprechen, so wenigstens ein bescheidenes Ausmaß der Fähigkeit elementar-erotischen Erlebens, wenn wir vom Eros sprechen. Und wie man ferner mit Vorteil das Mittel der Gegenüberstellung wählt, um dem Sehfähigen darzubieten [p. 52] die Eigenschaften der Farbe, so wählen wir ebendasselbe Mittel zur Entfaltung der Eigenschaften des Eros für den ihn der Möglichkeit nach Erlebenden. Es scheint aber keine Gegenüberstellung besser geeignet, um sofort über ihn ein helles Licht zu verbreiten, als die mit dem Geschlechtstriebe.

[p. 51] If we want to understand the nature of the elementary Eros, its meaning within the context of reality, and the conditions of its appearance, we must first attempt to define what it actually is. This, in turn, can only happen by describing the state that, in the spirit of our conception, would be an elementary-erotic one. Insofar as such a characterization can only mean something to someone who is able to fulfill it out of themselves with the memory of at least some experience of a similar kind, our very first observation is already a first step into "esoteric" science. However, in this respect, the study of the soul is no better off, but also not significantly worse off, than ordinary factual research. We can convey the laws of optics to a person born blind; yet they come no closer to the conception of the color blue than if they had never heard of optics. Just as we must presuppose the faculty of sight in our listener when we speak to them of colors, so we must presuppose at least a modest degree of the faculty for elementary-erotic experience when we speak of Eros. And furthermore, just as one advantageously chooses the method of juxtaposition to present [p. 52] the qualities of color to someone capable of sight, we choose this very same method to unfold the qualities of Eros for someone capable of experiencing it. Yet no juxtaposition seems better suited to shed a bright light upon it immediately than that with the sexual drive.

Eros ist nicht Geschlechtstrieb, und Geschlechtstrieb ist nicht Eros. Wir beschreiben den Eros, indem wir die wichtigsten Punkte bezeichnen, wodurch er vom Geschlechtstriebe abweicht. Dazu empfiehlt es sich jedoch, zuvor erst einmal nachzuforschen, worin denn beide übereinzustimmen scheinen und warum sie konnten verwechselt werden. Wir bitten aber, im Auge zu behalten, daß wir jetzt vom echten Geschlechtstriebe und nicht von einem „Iriebe zur Fortpflanzung‘ reden.

Eros is not the sexual drive, and the sexual drive is not Eros. We describe Eros by designating the most important points in which it diverges from the sexual drive. To this end, however, it is advisable first to investigate wherein both seem to agree and why they could be confused. But we ask the reader to bear in mind that we are now speaking of the genuine sexual drive and not of an "instinct for procreation."

Sollten wir ein wenigstens niemals fehlendes Merkmal des erotischen Zustandes mit einem einzigen Worte geben, so hätte unser Bescheid zu lauten: die Wollust. Erfände man daher in Analogie zum Geschlechtstriebe einen erotischen Trieb, so wäre es kurz: der Trieb zur Wollust. Nun bemerkt man aber sofort, daß, was gemeinhin unter Wollust verstanden wird, auch im Geschlechtstriebe vorkommt, nämlich bei Gelegenheit seiner Befriedigung; Ja, daß jene nicht näher beschreibliche Gefühlswallung, die mit der Empfindung geschlechtlicher Akte und ihrer Ersatzvorgänge einhergeht, eben dasjenige ist, woran man ganz vorzugsweise beim Worte Wollust zu denken pflegt. Indessen, der Sprachgebrauch selber kann uns belehren, daß hier eine Verengung des Wortsinnes vorliegt und zwar, wie wir sehen werden, genau entsprechend [p. 53] derjenigen Verengung des Erlebens, welcher zufolge von der erotischen Wollust nurmehr die geschlechtliche Wollust zurückblieb. Verstößt es doch durchaus nicht gegen ihn, wenn man etwa von einer Wollust des Sieges, des Triumphes oder der Grausamkeit redet, das Wort also auf Vorkommnisse anwendet, die mindestens keine geschlechtlichen Akte sind! Immerhin, da es schwerlich Zufall ist, daß die geschlechtliche Wollust für den Namensgebrauch den Ton angab, so stellen wir fest, es müsse in der Wollust der geschlechtlichen Befriedigung etwas gelegen sein, was sie mit dem erotischen Zustande gemein habe und wodurch es geschehen konnte, daß beide verwechselt wurden.

If we were to give at least one never-failing characteristic of the erotic state in a single word, our answer would have to be: voluptuousness (Wollust). Therefore, if one were to invent an erotic drive by analogy to the sexual drive, it would be, in short: the drive for voluptuousness. Now, however, one notices immediately that what is commonly understood by voluptuousness also occurs in the sexual drive, namely on the occasion of its satisfaction; indeed, that that indescribable surge of feeling accompanying the sensation of sexual acts and their surrogate processes is precisely what people tend to think of most preferentially when hearing the word voluptuousness. Meanwhile, linguistic usage itself can teach us that a narrowing of the word's meaning has occurred here—and indeed, as we shall see, exactly corresponding [p. 53] to that narrowing of experience by virtue of which only sexual voluptuousness remained of erotic voluptuousness. For it by no means violates usage if one speaks, for instance, of a voluptuousness of victory, of triumph, or of cruelty, thus applying the word to occurrences that are at least not sexual acts! Nonetheless, since it is hardly an accident that sexual voluptuousness set the tone for linguistic usage, we establish that there must be something inherent in the voluptuousness of sexual satisfaction which it shares with the erotic state, and by means of which it could happen that both were confused.

Darnach merken wir aber vorweg eine wesentliche Verschiedenheit an. Der Sexus ist fraglos ein tierischer T’rieb von ausgeprägtem Bedürfnischarakter mit dem Ziele einer einzigartigen und vorwiegend verhältnismäßig kurzen Berührungsnähe zweier geschlechtsverschiedenen Eigenwe‚sen, mit dessen Erreichung nun allerdings auch Wollust einhergeht. Das will aber sagen: er ist an und für sich kein Trieb zur Wollust! Es kann sich im Menschen zu ihm hinzugesellen ein Wunsch nach Wollust (wie ferner ja auch der wieder ganz andersartige Wunsch nach Fortpflanzung). Dahingegen das Tier in uns — genau wie jedes andere Tier — erlebt zwar Wollust, wann es seinen Geschlechtstrieb befriedigt; aber es wird im Zustande geschlechtlicher Erregung nicht zur Wollust, sondern zum gegengeschlechtlichen Wesen gezogen, mit dem es sich zu verbinden trachtet! Im Gegensatz dazu ist der Eros zunächst überhaupt kein Trieb, sondern ein völliger [p. 54] Zustand; sodann erst recht nicht ein Zustand der Bedürftigkeit, sondern der Erfüllung „an sich“, nämlich der vollkommenen Wollust! — Wir nehmen die Gelegenheit wahr zu einem erläuternden Rückblick.

Accordingly, however, we note in advance an essential difference. Sexus is questionably an animal drive of a pronounced character of need, aiming at a unique and predominantly relatively brief physical proximity of two distinct beings of different sexes, with the attainment of which, to be sure, voluptuousness is also associated. But that is to say: in and of itself, it is not a drive for voluptuousness! A desire for voluptuousness can join it in humans (just as, furthermore, the again quite different desire for procreation). On the other hand, the animal within us—exactly like any other animal—experiences voluptuousness, to be sure, when it satisfies its sexual drive; but in the state of sexual excitation, it is drawn not toward voluptuousness, but toward the being of the opposite sex with which it seeks to unite! In contrast to this, Eros is initially not a drive at all, but a total [p. 54] state; and furthermore, certainly not a state of neediness, but of fulfillment "in itself," namely of perfect voluptuousness!—We take this opportunity for an explanatory retrospective.

Zuvörderst hat es hiermit seine Begründung gefunden, warum wir keine der früher aufgezählten Bedeutungen des Wortes Liebe verwerten konnten zu unmißverständlicher Unterscheidung des Eros. Nirgends nämlich, weder unter den Liebesneigungen noch unter den Liebestrieben, kommt etwas von der Art vor, das auch nur einem Triebe zur Wollust gliche, geschweige dem Zustand der Wollust selbst. Auch die ihm nächststehenden Liebesbegriffe meinen immer bloße Gefühlszustände, und in jedem Gefühl liegt irgendein Streben. Der elementare Eros hingegen betrifft einen Zustand, der alles Streben als schon erfüllt weit hinter sich läßt und, wie wir vorgreifend andeuten dürfen, noch anderen Mächten angehört, als welche allein im stets nur menschlichen (oder denn tierischen) „Gefühl“ erscheinen. Er ist eine Art der von den Alten so genannten „Ekstasis“, des „Außersichseins“, nicht Ekstasis überhaupt, aber wollüstig-selige Ekstasis. — Vergleichen wir ferner mit dem bis hierher Festgesetzten die Bestimmungen Platons, so erkennen wir sogleich, daß er den erotischen Zustand fehlgreifend dargestellt habe in Gemäßheit des Zustandes geschlechtlicher Bedürftigkeit. Sein Eros ist „dämonisch‘‘, sofern er ein Streben ist, ein Streben nun freilich, dem die eigentliche Liebesbeziehung nur als Mittel dient zur Erreichung einer geistigen Vollkommenheit, von der uns versichert wird, daß es höhere Glückseligkeit als ihren Besitz für den [p. 55] Menschen nicht gebe. Allein, wie immer es mit der geistigen Glückseligkeit bestellt sein möge, eines ist gewiß: sie hat den Charakter der Befriedigung im Verhältnis zu einem ihr voraufgegangenen Streben nicht anders, wie die sinnliche Wollust des Begattungsaktes zugleich den voraufgegangenen Vereinigungstrieb „befriedigt“. Und wir behaupten nun, keine Glückseligkeit, möge sie sinnlich, möge sie geistig heißen, trage den Charakter der echten Teletä, wofern in ihr als Bestandsstück darinliegt die Befriedigung, sei es eines Triebes, sei es eines Wunsches! Die Vorstellung des Glückes aus dem Gesichtspunkt der Befriedigung eines Strebens ansetzen, heißt nämlich: das Leben selbst aus dem Gesichtspunkt eines wesenhaft unbefriedigten Willens deuten! Schopenhauers falsche Willensmetaphysik, auf die wir später zurückkommen, hat wenigstens das eine Verdienst, unwiderleglich gezeigt zu haben, daß jede Willensbefriedigung durchaus nur im flüchtigen Augenblick des Überganges vom Zustand des Nochnichtbesitzens in den des Besitzens liege, worauf unausbleiblich ein Zustand der Langeweile eintreten müsse, wofern nicht aus neuer Bedürftigkeit sich neuerdings Willensstrebungen geltend machten. Wer das Glück — ım Vollsinn des Wortes — als Befriedigung mißversteht, hat die Erlebnisart in ihm ausgelöscht, um an deren Stelle zu setzen das farblose Gradgefühl des im Brechen von Widerständen sich erfüllenden Wollens. Wie es gar nicht anders sein kann, führt daher Platons Weg aus dem immer farbenvollen Glück der Sinnlichkeit an den „überhimmlischen‘, meint raumlosen, Ort der blutleeren Selbstgerechtigkeit des Erfolges. — Und nun nehmen wir mit [p. 56] einem Sprunge voraus: die geschlechtliche Befriedigung ist insoweit Wollust, als auch sie noch teilhat am Zustand des erotischen Rausches, und die geschlechtliche Wollust ermangelt insoweit der Vollkommenheit des Eros, als sie bloß Befriedigung eines tierischen Triebes oder gar nur eines menschlichen Wunsches ist.

First of all, this establishes the reason why we could not utilize any of the previously enumerated meanings of the word love for an unambiguous differentiation of Eros. For nowhere, neither among loving inclinations nor among loving drives, does anything occur that even resembles a drive for voluptuousness, let alone the state of voluptuousness itself. Even the concepts of love closest to it always mean mere emotional states, and in every emotion lies some striving. The elementary Eros, on the other hand, concerns a state that leaves all striving far behind as already fulfilled and, as we may indicate by anticipating, belongs to powers other than those which appear solely in the always merely human (or else animal) "feeling." It is a kind of what the ancients called ekstasis, of "being outside oneself"—not ekstasis in general, but voluptuous-blissful ekstasis. —Furthermore, if we compare the definitions of Plato with what has been established up to this point, we recognize immediately that he mistakenly depicted the erotic state in accordance with the state of sexual neediness. His Eros is "daemonic" insofar as it is a striving—a striving, to be sure, for which the actual love relationship serves only as a means to achieve a spiritual perfection, of which we are assured that there is no higher bliss for [p. 55] man than its possession. However, however it may stand with spiritual bliss, one thing is certain: it has the character of satisfaction in relation to a prior striving, no differently than the sensual voluptuousness of the act of copulation simultaneously "satisfies" the preceding drive for union. And we now assert that no bliss, whether it be called sensual or spiritual, bears the character of the genuine telete [initiation/consummation] if it contains within itself as a component part the satisfaction of either a drive or a desire! To establish the conception of happiness from the standpoint of the satisfaction of a striving means: to interpret life itself from the standpoint of an essentially unsatisfied will! Schopenhauer’s false metaphysics of the will, to which we shall return later, has at least the one merit of having irrefutably shown that every satisfaction of the will lies entirely within the fleeting moment of transition from the state of not-yet-possessing into that of possessing, whereupon a state of boredom must inevitably ensue, unless new strivings of the will assert themselves out of a new neediness. Anyone who misunderstands happiness—in the full sense of the word—as satisfaction has extinguished the mode of experience within it, replacing it with the colorless degree-feeling of a willing that fulfills itself in the breaking of resistances. As it cannot be otherwise, Plato’s path therefore leads from the always colorful happiness of sensuousness to the "supracelestial"—meaning spaceless—place of the bloodless self-righteousness of success. —And now we anticipate with [p. 56] a leap: sexual satisfaction is voluptuousness to the extent that it still participates in the state of erotic intoxication, and sexual voluptuousness lacks the perfection of Eros to the extent that it is merely the satisfaction of an animal drive or even just a human desire.

Er kann sich vollenden beim bloßen Anblick eines geliebten Wesens, und das mag ein gegengeschlechtliches Wesen, aber auch. ein gleichgeschlechtliches, es mag ein Tier, eine Pflanze sein; und er kann sich nicht minder vollenden beim Riechen eines Duftes, Schmecken eines Weines, Hören eines Klanges, Betasten eines tropfenden Zweiges. Er kann im Wachen erwachen wie im betäubendsten Traum. Er feiert seine Orgien unter dem Wehen der Frühlingsstürme, angesichts des sternbesäten Firmamentes, im Schloßenschauer, auf flammendem Bergesgrat, im Toben der Meeresbrandung, unter dem Wetterleuchten der „ersten Liebe“, aber nicht weniger in der Umarmung des Schicksals, das seinen Träger zermalmt. Er ist Wollust zugleich des Aufgangs und Unterganges; Wollust, der Sterben und Tod zur wehvoll seligen Wandlung wird! Im Ewigkeitsaugenblick seiner Vollkommenheit liegt: enifesseltes [p. 57] Rasen oder kristallene Entrücktheit. — Und damit haben wir denn schon vorbedeutet, was an zweiter Stelle zu sagen wäre. So wenig gleicht er dem Zustand irgendeiner Bedürftigkeit, daß wir, was Drang in ihm, zu kennzeichnen haben als Drang des Überströmens, der strahlenden Ergießung, des maßlosen Sichverschenkens. Nicht Bedürftigkeit ist er und Mangel, sondern Überschwang quellendster Fülle, goldstreuende Flamme und weltenträchtige Schwangerschaft. Darum, worauf sein Strahl fällt, das erglüht in namenloser Schönheit, wohin er den Fuß setzt, da wuchert ein Blütendickicht, und seine Umarmung befreit aus Dingen und Menschen den eingekerkerten Gott! x Das ist die allgemeinste Beschaffenheit jenes Zustandes, der in der Symbolsprache des seherischen Geistes der Vorzeit der Eros Kosmogonos heißt. — Wie geriet die Begehrlichkeit des Geschlechtstriebes in seine Nähe — warum, wie es scheint, blieb nur noch im Begattungsakt von ihm ein auch für Menschen faßlicher Rest zurück — und wie verhält sich zu ihm die echte, tiefe, „todüber„windende“ Liebe?!

Returning thereby to the comparison of both, we mark two fundamentally separating differences of content between them. —While the satisfying voluptuousness of the sexual drive accompanies the sexual union of two beings—strictly speaking, even two beings of opposite sexes—the occasional prompts for the onset of erotic intoxication cannot even conjecturally be limited. It can achieve fulfillment at the mere sight of a beloved being, and that may be a being of the opposite sex, but also one of the same sex; it may be an animal, a plant; and it can no less achieve fulfillment in the smelling of a scent, the tasting of a wine, the hearing of a sound, the touching of a dripping branch. It can awaken in waking life as in the most numbing dream. It celebrates its orgies under the blowing of spring storms, in the face of the star-strewn firmament, in a shower of hail, on a flaming mountain ridge, in the raging of the ocean surf, beneath the heat lightning of "first love," but no less in the embrace of fate that crushes its bearer. It is voluptuousness simultaneously of the rising and the setting; voluptuousness to which dying and death become a painfully blissful transformation! In the eternal moment of its perfection lies: unleashed [p. 57] frenzy or crystalline rapture. —And with this, we have already foreshadowed what would have to be said in the second place. So little does it resemble the state of any neediness that what is an urge within it must be characterized as the urge of overflowing, of radiant effusion, of measureless self-giving. It is not neediness and lack, but the exuberance of the most welling fullness, a gold-scattering flame, and a pregnancy pregnant with worlds. Therefore, whatever its ray falls upon glows in nameless beauty; wherever it sets its foot, a thicket of blossoms runs wild; and its embrace releases the imprisoned god from things and men! This is the most general quality of that state which, in the symbolic language of the visionary spirit of antiquity, is called Eros Kosmogonos. —How did the covetousness of the sexual drive get into its vicinity—why, as it seems, did only a remnant of it, still comprehensible even to humans, remain behind in the act of copulation—and how does genuine, deep, "death-conquering" love relate to it?!

Beide stimmen nämlich allerdings überein sowohl in der ekstatischen Seelenverfassung als auch zumal in jenem umsichgreifenden Überwallen, das (mit einer Wendung Nietzsches aus Schopenhauer) die Schranke der „Individuation“ durchbricht und das Sonderleben zurückversenkt in das Leben der Elemente. Kaum brauchen wir darnach noch einzuschalten, durch was die Wahl solcher Worte wie „kosmischer Eros“, „kosmogonischer Eros‘, „elementarer Eros‘‘ bestimmt worden sei. Der Eros heißt elementar oder kosmisch, sofern das von ihm ergriffene Einzelwesen sich erlebt als durchpulst und durchflutet von einem gleichsam elektrischen Strom, der wesensähnlich dem Magnetismus unbekümmert um ihre Schranken einander fernste Seelen im verbindenden Zug sich gegenseitig erspüren läßt, das Mittel selber alles Geschehens, welches die Körper trennt, den Raum und die Zeit, in das allgegenwärtige Element eines tragenden und umspülenden Ozeans wandelt und dergestalt unbeschadet ihrer nie zu mindernden Verschiedenheit zusammenknüpft die Pole der Welt. Und er heißt kosmogonisch, weil er ein Zustand sich ergießender Fülle ist, welchem gemäß das Innere — sofort sich auch ausgebärend — augenblicklich ein Äußeres, Welt und erscheinende Wirklichkeit wird. Sehr verschieden vom bloßen Gefühl [p. 59] ist er zugleich das unaufhörliche Offenbarwerden dessen, was unaufhörlich aus verborgenster Seele quillt. Würden wir das Hegelsche Wahrwort: „Nichts ist wesentlich, was nicht erscheint“, unbesorgt um die Meinung des Prägers ergänzen: „Und nur als vom Eros entzündet, tritt aus gestaltloser Hehl in den Glanz des Erscheinens das Wesen,‘ so hätten wir am elementaren Rausch schon kenntlich gemacht, wodurch er sogar über jeden bloßen Zustand hinaus der in ihm schwingenden Seele die Bürgschaft ihrer unmittelbaren Teilhaberschaft am schöpferischen Geschehen sei. In doing so, he even [p. 58] adduces Schiller’s youthful Hymn to Joy to bring it closer to us that the wave of Bacchic enthusiasm, in unbounding and dissolving what it touches, also binds it by that very means. And without question, one might also call the erotic state a Dionysian one, even if one cannot with quite the same right call the Dionysian an erotic one. For both certainly agree both in the ecstatic state of soul and especially in that all-enveloping overflowing which (to use a turn of phrase from Nietzsche via Schopenhauer) breaks through the barrier of "individuation" and sinks the separate life back into the life of the elements. Accordingly, we hardly need to interject what determined the choice of such words as "cosmic Eros," "cosmogonic Eros," "elementary Eros." Eros is called elementary or cosmic insofar as the individual being seized by it experiences itself as pulsed and flooded through by a virtually electric current which, akin in nature to magnetism and regardless of their barriers, lets distant souls mutually sense each other in a binding pull, transforms the very medium of all events that separates bodies—space and time—into the omnipresent element of a bearing and washing ocean, and in such a manner, without prejudice to their never-to-be-diminished difference, links together the poles of the world. And it is called cosmogonic because it is a state of pouring fullness, according to which the interior—immediately giving birth to itself—instantaneously becomes an exterior, world and appearing reality. Very different from mere feeling, [p. 59] it is at the same time the unceasing manifestation of that which unceasingly wells up from the most hidden soul. If we were to supplement Hegel's true saying: "Nothing is essential that does not appear," unconcerned with the intention of its author, by adding: "And only when ignited by Eros does essence step out of shapeless concealment into the radiance of appearance," we would have already made recognizable in elementary intoxication how it is, even beyond any mere state, the guarantee to the soul vibrating within it of its immediate participation in the creative process. At the very least, however, it would be disclosed in this way to what extent it alone bestows that quite exuberant happiness which "like the sun in the evening, continually gives away from its inexhaustible riches and pours into the sea, and like it, only feels richest when even the poorest fisherman still rows with a golden oar" (Nietzsche in The Gay Science). If we are now to attempt to define even more sharply within the Dionysian that by which it specializes into elementary-erotic voluptuousness, we must above all consider the underlying ecstasy more precisely than was done even by Nietzsche. Since it would far exceed the limits of a mere sketch if we intended even to touch upon the history of the conception of ekstasis, we content ourselves with the dogmatic rendering of our own research results. The following three questions may guide us in this: I. What is it that frees itself in ecstasy? II. From what does it free itself? III. What does the self-freeing entity gain through its becoming free?

We preface the responses to the initial two inquiries to our subsequent contemplation: it is not, as was presumed, the spirit of humanity that emancipates itself, but rather the soul; and it emancipates itself not, as was presumed, from the corporeal form, but precisely from the spirit!

The cosmos thrives, and all existence is polarized betwixt soul (Psyche) and body (Soma). Wherever vital body resides, there too dwells the soul; wherever soul abides, there too exists vital body. The soul constitutes the essence of the body, the image of the body the manifestation of the soul. Whatsoever appears possesses an essence; and every essence reveals itself through appearance. The essence is inwardly experienced, the manifestation outwardly perceived. The former must transmute into image to convey itself, and the image must once more become inward to exert its influence. These, without allegory, are the poles of reality. – Should we now comprehensively and thoughtfully survey the world from without, it shall unveil to us everywhere the Pelasgian triad of poles articulated within the unity of the whole. Space is the body of time, time the soul of space. The soul and essence of night is day, the maternal womb of light is night. Winter and summer, slumber and vigilance, demise and genesis align in sequence. The feminine embodies the corporeal and maternal aspect of the soul, the masculine the revealed and revealing essence of the mother’s womb. Blood and nerve, solar plexus and cerebrum, “heart” and “head”, mouth and eye, left and right stand in equivalent relation. Sever any one of these elements from its counterpart, and the world is rent asunder.

The annals of humanity reveal to us in man—and solely in man—the mortal combat betwixt the omnipresent life and a power transcending space and time, which endeavors to sunder the poles and thereby annihilate them, to disembody the body, to desoul the soul: this is denominated the spirit (Logos, Pneuma, Nous). In harmony with the dual nature of our essence as well, it manifests: through discerning reflection (Noesis) and through purposive volition (Boul¯esis). The shared fulcrum of both, which in us has evolved into the eccentric nucleus of life, is termed: I or Self. As conduits of life, we are akin to all life-bearers individuals (that is, indivisible entities), as conduits of spirit we are furthermore Egos or Selves. “Person,” deriving from personare—to resound through, and originally signifying the mime’s mask through which a daemon speaks, has long metamorphosed into spirit-violated life, life in servitude to the role decreed by the spirit’s mask! We endure solely in the obligation to cogitate and to will; only through the sentiment of self do we yet discern the voices of the All, from which we have been severed, and this mask has ingrown into our flesh and ingrains itself more firmly with each century.

Upon the prehistoric humanity of the sovereign soul—permit this interpolation here—succeeded the historical era of the sovereign spirit. Yet succeeding it shall be the posthistorical humanity of the now merely seemingly vital larva: we presently witness its ascent. Nonetheless, whether we poison, incinerate, atomize life within and around us: from the cadaver of the slain mother inexorably arises the “vengeance of the Erinys.” In the retribution for the sullied and profaned life, humanity shall perish in inconceivable horror at the precise instant it celebrates the ultimate unbounded triumph of the larva, the Golem: thus prophesies Cassandra, whose destiny is to be derided as a fool by those who must remain blind for the calamity she foretells to manifest. —

Whilst every extrahuman living entity, though distinct and possessed of its own inwardness, pulsates in the rhythm of cosmic life, the edict of spirit has estranged man from this. What appears to him as the bearer of self-consciousness in the light of superiority through anticipatory thought over the world, that to the metaphysician—if he delves su!ciently deep—appears in the light of life’s subjugation beneath the yoke of concepts! To emancipate life anew from it, both soulfully and bodily, is the concealed propensity of all mystics and narcotists, whether acknowledged or disavowed; and it culminates in ecstasy. The evidences thereof would not conclude in a hundred pages. Here, a glance at the most palpably crude shall su!ce.

That solely man possesses self-consciousness, and necessarily from partake in the eternal spirit, several eminent modern thinkers have posited; hence it is deemed already substantiated.

If ecstasy is thus soul-de-spiritualization, it must also be: soul-de-selfization. This is compellingly confirmed, among other things, by language, and especially the German tongue. Literally translated, ecstasy does not mean “displaced,” but “beside oneself” (—outside the self). The inebriated or intoxicated, whether in rapture or due to narcotic poisons, is no longer “with himself,” has “lost himself,” risks “forgetting himself,” and sobered, “comes to himself again.”

No less validly, however, does one employ the identical phrases to denote the overwhelming e!cacy of an instinct. For in that the spirit is momentarily disempowered, the merely instinctual arousal wholly concurs with the ecstatic. From sudden rage too one can “forget oneself,” “lose one’s temper,” and after the instinctual act of a homicide committed in utmost fury, one “comes to himself again.” Yet, whereas in ecstasy the self is transiently forsaken, in the moment of ordinary surge it is merely under the dominion of a somatic instinct; and if the instinct indeed topples the barrier of volition, it does not equally topple the barrier of individuality. In instinct, the spirit is enfeebled by the preponderance of own-life; in ecstasy, it is enfeebled—though mediated by the former—by the vital force of the world.

Instinct manifests the animal, ecstasy reveals the elemental life. The former is evinced by the violence of the seizing surge, the latter by its depth.

Among the Indo-European languages, there is scarcely one that does not describe the depth and potency of emotions as affliction, suffering, surrender! “Pathos” (´), “Passion” (passio), “Leidenschaft”: thrice “suffering” for the supreme intensification of emotion erupting from soul-depths! Had one but posed the obvious query: what precisely is the suffering entity and what the other that causes suffering, the answer would not have been missed: passive, suffering, surrendering is our self, and it surrenders to the triumphant might of life. Whensoever we will or think, we say: I think, I will, I do, and we emphasize the I the more decisively the more emphatically we think or will. But when we experienced great feeling, it seems pale and insipid to say: I felt the following; instead it is: it gripped me, shook me, seized me, overwhelmed me, enraptured me! What enraptures us? Life! And what is enraptured? The self! – And hereto joins the long chain of linguistic testimonies to the self-otherness, nay self-alienness of that which possesses the wholly surrendered. The mystic was, in Greek conception, “Entheos,” meaning filled with god or daemon. From this remain the concepts of possession, enthusiasm, and inspiration. In the latter word lurks not the spirit with which one judges, but a “spirit,” an extrahuman entity of another kind, as also in “ghostly apparition.” Furthermore, what the mystic brings from ecstasy into diurnal vigilance is “inspiration,” “inspiration” (= in-breathing), “illumination,” thus something not generated from the self, but overtaking the self as from without. – And to the linguistic evidences finally adjoin complementarily many utterances from the mouths of genuine ecstatics, depicting the selfsame with convincing primordiality. Jalaluddin Rumi, the greatest disciple of the Sufi order, proclaims (in Rückert’s translation):

Well ends death life’s need,

Yet life shudders before death.

So shudders a heart before love,

As if threatened by death. For where love awakens, dies

The self, the dark despot.

And: “Who knows the power of the round dance, dwells in God. For he alone knows how love kills!” – No doubt: ecstasy is not the soul’s disembodiment, but de-selfization and thus de-spiritualization.

If it be so, then every ecstasy of a spirit-afflicted being shall traverse two phases: the phase in which the self perishes, and the phase in which life resurrects. The former is preparatory, the latter fulfilling, the Teletae of the secret initiations, whose incommunicability Aristotle (per Synesius’ report) aptly interprets: not through “mathein,” i.e., learning, but through “pathein,” i.e., suffering, does the one suited to receive the mystery consummate. The path to life thus leads through the death of the self, and since death exists solely in the foreknowledge of death (for one can no more experience it than one ever experiences the moment of falling asleep!), we may say more concisely: for a spiritual being, life is purchasable only at the price of death. Death is the Pandora-gift that, for its “dominion over nature,” the Promethean man alone among all beings redeemed from nature itself; wherefore Nietzsche poignantly, from their truest commonality, named humanity a “brotherhood of death”! An animal does not die, because it knows naught of dying, but merely ends; man however dies, and not once, but a thousand times, for step by step and day by day the consciousness of impending end accompanies him!

Of death-horror we shall speak later, but now of that death-longing whose secret attraction draws from the will to existence and into the spirit-consuming fire.

Tell it to none, only the wise,

For the crowd forthwith mocks:

The living I shall praise, That longs for flame-death.

For the “wise” as knower of ecstasy’s secret feels in flame-death not loss of life, but what alone opens the portal to “life”: the demise of the self! But this is experienced in twain manners, alternating with eras as with individuals, imparting exceedingly diverse character to the observances leading to fulfillment: the self dies in intoxication by rending or by dissolving, and beside the ecstasy of inner explosion stands the ecstasy of inner melting. If maenadic frenzies testify to its explosive force, the melting force is a!rmed by the circumstance that all intoxication gods, foremost Dionysos, Bakchos, and Eros, bear the epithets “Lysios,” “Lysimel¯es,” thus “loosing,” “limb-loosing,” (whence, with mingling of both aspects, in our language the phrase of wine as “care-breaker” persists!). And as the explosion recalls bodily death, so the melting recalls the bodily similitude of death, the “limb-loosing” slumber. That both are but diverse modes of the selfsame process is evident from the unquestionable aptitude of narcotic poisons to induce both explosive and melting ecstasy. There was perhaps never a true ecstatic who was not occasionally also a narcotist; and not by chance do orgiastic customs cling to the grape-harvest festival unto the latest time. Indeed, the daemon of Bacchic rites is also a god of wine, and even today—Bacchus and Venus rhyme!

Yes, we must emphatically assert that the propensity widespread at all times and among all peoples of the earth for the enjoyment of anesthetics but manifests in folk form the ecstatic death-longing. No guild scholar has been able to explain this phenomenon! All that has been pedantically advanced thereon misses the mark and but exposes the spiritual arrogance of a life-alienated morality. Certainly, the “drunkard” is a degeneration phenomenon; but not because he “drinks,” but because in him has become dull habit what to the living is festival and celebration! And does man degenerate only in the enjoyment of intoxicants? Is the soulless today, which daily enslaves armies of factory slaves for a dozen hours in the service of lifeless machines and condemns the vast majority of humans altogether through the compulsion of necessity into restless busyness, not rather a vice, in comparison to whose debilitating terror the vice of drinking still preserves a festive shimmer!

As long as one is sober,

The bad pleases;

Once one has drunk,

One knows the right...

And does it not represent the far worse, because irreplaceable, anesthesia when one sings the praise of the life-devouring “work”?! If one comes to us with the trite phrases of “harmful” and “beneficial,” it is to be noted that utility concepts have no place in the doctrine of life! Not in existence and assertion of existence does life reveal itself, but solely in those moments of overflowing fullness, which would not be too dearly bought even with destruction of the body! Who exists ninety years need not have lived two minutes; and who dies young is not thereby deprived of eternity-moments, of which in shorter span he may have been graced richer share!

And as long as you have not this,

This: Die and become!

You are but a gloomy guest

On the dark earth.

The psychological exploration of ecstasy requires supplementation by a science of intoxicants. Opium, hashish, coca, alcohol, ethereal oils, incense, laurel, the Solanaceae poisons, even nicotine, caffeine, theine have variously served the de-selfing urge of visionaries, and we might expect the greatest insights into the nature of intoxication from a science of “signatures,” as the Renaissance mysticism sketched in fresco style. In view of contemporary medicine’s failure, for now only the path of inner dissection remains open to us.

At least of the melting form of de-selfing, linguistic expressions attest that it leads into dreamlike in the “self-forgetfulness” of a rare states. We all know from memory that we lived as if in a dream: happiness, or when wholly “lost” in the sight of the setting sun, entirely “absorbed” in the alluring song of a flute, deeply “immersed” in the distant image of bygone youth days. In explosion, the readiness of the unleashed soul for “sallying forth,” “swarming,” “roaming” announces itself; the inner melting prepares the magic of its “rapture.” Though the perfect bliss of Eros fundamentally can be linked as well with explosive as with melting de-selfing, yet there exists a palpable elective a!nity betwixt this and it, such that we can no better depict the sweetness of erotic shudder than by emphasizing the melting overflow, the surrender of the soul. But holding this as an essential state-feature of erotic bliss, we shall readily recognize its share, once in all moments of “pleasure” in the stilling of bodily desires (be it eating and drinking, be it embrace), on the other in every kind of genuine happiness, of “beatitude,” “felicity,” “rapture,” “beatification,” “enchantment,” as even still in the already spiritualized elevations of “transport” and “transfiguration.” —

In the Orphic and Hesiodic theogonies, the emanation of Eros precedes the Chaos. This assumption rests upon an ecstatic experience. In the transitional segment of every ecstasy from explosive or melting de-selfing into the as yet unconsidered fulfillment, a chaos of all emotions erupts from the soul’s depths, which circumstance alone would forbid classifying ecstatic arousal in the same genus as the more or less always determinate emotions. What in mere emotion diverges into extreme opposites, as pleasure and displeasure, joy and sorrow, anticipation and recollection, hope and fear, love and wrathful glow, triumph and anxiety, sweetness and bitterness, here finds knotted into an inseparable whole. “From the highest joy resounds the cry of horror...” (Nietzsche.) Who has never experienced this, rely on transitional “mixed emotions”: perchance the longing sweetness of melancholy, the beatifying oppression of sublime shudder, the painful ardor of luxuriating melancholies. Or think of Goethe’s revelatory stanza:

Yet in rigidity I seek not my salvation.

Shudder is humanity’s best part.

How dearly the world makes him pay the feeling,

Gripped, he deeply feels the monstrous.

But it is the chaotic soul-underworld that imparts to ecstatic intoxication the orgiastic trait. (Hence in torch-illumined night the frenzied whirl-dance of “swarming” Bacchae amid brazen clash of timpani and the “madness-luring harmony” of Phrygian flutes!) In it, as already intimated, the poles lie yet undifferentiated. And what, cosmically grasped, is the emergence of the cosmos, that is, the articulated All, from the yet unarticulated Chaos, that is, soulfully grasped: in the pregnant darkness of the Dionysian vortex flashes the luminary of Eros-Phanes: binding the now differentiated poles in the glowing ring of incessantly renewed embrace.

Since we have advanced thus far, let one of our profoundest findings be mentioned in passing. – Suppose a thinker has convinced himself that the widespread sanctity of the number three—Poseidon’s trident, Pythia’s tripod, trinity of divine realms, Christian Trinity, triad of Norns and more—is the symbolic expression of a triadic articulation of reality, then he shall, if the Orphic Eros himself was an experienced, also seek behind the tri-form of his appearance in the theogonic myth the reality of an experienced. The cosmic intoxication as the higher stage of chaotic intoxication must then admit three particular forms. More than twenty years ago, we succeeded in discovering and incontrovertibly proving these three fundamental forms, namely the heroic, the erotic, and the magical form. – What the erotic form, the middle member of the triad, signifies, we shall see below. – In the magical intoxication-form predominates the in essence unitary dual relation to the distance of the nocturnal firmament and to the realm of the dead.

Its historical zeniths lie in Median Magism and ancient Egyptian tomb-cult, and its perhaps strongest conceptual precipitate is Chaldaean astrology. The heroic-tragic intoxication, predominantly supported on explosive de-selfing and circling in the extreme revolution of the universe, bestowed upon an entire era of late-Pelasgian humanity the name Heroic Age and evidently lawfully generated among the four heroic peoples we know the poetic art-form of the epic.

Its most striking distinguishing mark lies therein that the self-death here always passes through the warrior-death of the body, and its grandest manifestation offers among the heroic peoples the Germanic, in which alone it also wholly absorbed the other two intoxication-forms. The warrior destined for Einherjar experiences battle-death as the Valkyrie’s kiss and awakens from torment-drunken darkness of downfall in—the death-realm of Valhalla!

If the cosmos, as we have seen, is polarly articulated, then in every ecstasy, when it attains the cosmic stage, must lie a duality of inner poles. We treat not yet of this, but of the particular duality that distinguishes the erotic intoxication both from the pre-natal soul-chaos and from its co-births, the heroic and the magical intoxication. The words we deliberately prefer like intoxication, drunkenness, voluptuousness, beatitude, rapture certainly mean states of a living being, but in no way indicate whether such states might encompass several beings. There is not only solitary intoxication, solitary drunkenness, solitary beatitude, but ecstatic fulfillment is primordially insofar always perfect solitude as it is perfect filledness, which—elevated above all halfness—bears the complementary pole in itself. The Gamos even of erotic intoxication is inner Gamos or self-begetting of a being expanded to world, hence begetting and receiving and precisely therefore pregnant. In and of itself, accordingly, every ecstasy bears not so much pathic as idiopathic traits. We must not be deterred therefrom by the circumstance that the bearer of intoxication often finds the crowning fulfillment only on occasion of clamorous folk-festivals or nocturnal celebrations amidst a similarly seized crowd. That would mean exchanging the occasion of the matter with the matter itself!

The Bacchae, for instance, were idiopathic enthusiasts. They commenced the invocation of the god in throngs; but the more the daemon carried them away, the more they dispersed, “roamed out,” until many a one collapsed wholly isolated in pathless wilderness of nocturnal mountains, now first dissolved in the “hieromanic” stream! The same holds for the raging Hieroduli of Syrian Ma, Phrygian Cybele, Phoenician Astarte, Babylonian Beltis and Mylitta, Carian Hecate, Indian Kali, Egyptian Fachet; further for similar phenomena of the Islamic cultural sphere, like the whirling dances of the Dervishes or the bloody flagellant festivals with which the sects of Aissawa and Sheikh Ruffai honor the memory of their saints, finally for all ecstatic observances that we can still occasionally observe today among “nature peoples,” such as especially Lapps, Samoyeds, Tunguses, Buryats, Korchins, Indians, and numerous Malay tribes. – But also true poets—all and sundry laggards and belated “disciples of the god Dionysus”—are as poets always idiopathic, may some of them require for inducing “inspiration” among other things even infatuation! Not infatuation dictates, as has been falsely opined, but on occasion perchance of infatuation, provided the infatuated is a poet, the cosmogonic Eros guesting only in him! The two perhaps most Dionysianly gifted poets of the nineteenth century, Lenau and Conrad Ferdinand Meyer, led—the one buried in books, the other with tobacco and violin—a life wholly closed outwardly; and the though less deep yet artistically superior Keller does not lag behind them therein.

We must thus strictly distinguish the content of intoxication and the conditions of its emergence. These can lie in the unity of a human crowd for the purpose of festive sociability or cultic customs, while nonetheless the former belongs to the genus of own-intoxications. Apart from deeper grounds, the semblance of contradiction is already dispelled by the consideration that every intoxication by its nature as an overflowing life-wave possesses the gift of encompassing the bystander and spectator, yea we may unhesitatingly say, of psychic contagion. In an orgiastically aroused crowd, doubtless a psychic contagion is diffused that threatens to permeate even the cooler observer and ultimately overwhelm him. The thus seized certainly stands with each of the other seized in a common medium; but what churns him, crushes him, raptures him, he shares least with the crushings of the others! Of his intoxication holds, and may it glow in the purple of elemental voluptuousness, wherewith a witty Frenchman contested the coital act’s usual claim to love by describing the copulation process as egoisme à deux (in the sense of our conception, as understood, not at all depreciating the satisfaction-bliss, but only describing it as idiopathic, which indeed for the majority of cases applies on the male side, not equally on the female!).

But if we have thereby come to know “drunkenness in general” as an idiopathic state, we would never find the way to the particularity precisely of Eros, if we remained at this stage of its operation. We must ascend a third and final stage of intoxication to ascertain what transforms “intoxication in general” into fulfilled beatitude of Eros.

The blossom is not merely the uppermost tip of that whereof the root is the lowermost; but the root also reaches up into the blossom, the blossom down into the root:

in the former sticks—only transformed—the root, in the latter no less already—yet unfolded—the blossom! But to learn thereof, the root must have released the stem and the blossom must have burst from the crown. Similarly, we mean when we say the soul-chaos releases from itself the tri-stem of cosmic intoxications; but only the middle one ultimately bears the blossom of Eros; whence we must explore this to discern wherein lies the peculiarity and significance of the stem. – Also the three stages of intoxication can ultimately be distinguished purely statally. In chaos lie inextricably interwoven horror and beatitude; in cosmos this stands in that, contending or allied, like sparkling star in surging darkness; in sovereign Eros the intoxication bears the ray-crown (= wreath of the initiate), which sinks the transfiguring radiance down into the abyss of night (= arrow of Eros). If however anything can illuminate the emotion-alien nature of intoxication-content, it would be the endeavor to depict their kinds and layers with aid of words, each signifying an—emotion! For characterizing the erotic blossom of intoxication, however, we fortunately have another means, from which after this insertion we need no longer fear misinterpretation from inadequate love-concepts.

The erotically consummating intoxication is never idiopathic, but invariably sympathetic; and hence every intoxication partakes of the particularity of the erotic in the strictest sense insofar as its content is sympathetic. What do we understand by a sympathetic surge simpliciter? This: that to the arousal of one being inseparably belongs its commonality with the similar arousal of at least one second being. Sympathetically aroused would be e.g. two persons who not only felt indignation from the same occasion, but also knew themselves allied by the commonality of their wrath and precisely therefrom drew the pathos of their indignation. There is perhaps no human emotion that could not occasionally enter sympathetic contexts, and especially the formally negating emotions by no means exclude sympathetic pathos; as participants in a national liberation struggle can be bound by the stormy elevation of deep commonality of their flaming wrath. Even if this rose to intoxicating height—the emotional tone of great revolutions—, it would yet, as doubtless no one misrecognizes, be something wholly distinct from the orgiasm of the Bacchae.

The pathos of wrath, “formally” negating, can be the response of ardent love for life to outrage upon life.

If however we incorporate the negating drive already in the fount of emotions, the sympathetic nature thereof wanes in equal measure. Namely, if the drive determining me is directed against life, it aims also against the elevations of other beings and henceforth finds itself impossible to be heightened by the knowledge that these share the same happiness. Because life-envy glances askance at every foreign enthusiasm, it inexorably excludes even itself from the pathos of co-enthusiasm among confederates; hence the sympathetic hue unclouded adheres only to a!rming surges and may most luminously overlay reciprocal love-inclination.

According to the predominance now of idiopathic, now of sympathetic arousal-character, whole races, peoples, and especially life-ages differ. The enigmatic homesickness that sometimes overcomes the adult for his childhood rests upon the depth and fullness of life-shudders precisely in earliest youth. It is the mystical gleam of unbroken idiopathic beatitude that especially separates the intoxications of boy-soul from the almost always mood-burdened wave-crests of later age and for that brings them closer to the enduring state of some “primitives.” That summer-sky unknowing of itself of a solitude wholly self-su!cing, which no tenderest veil of intimation clouds from the painful inclination to twoness, overspans world-wide only the enchanted garden of childhood years. Not bliss lacks child-happiness, it lacks only sympathetic bliss, without in the least needing it. When children play together, Jean Paul in “Levana” expresses, “two fantasies like two flames play beside and into one another unknit.” The puberty initiations of maturing youths widespread among almost all “nature peoples,” from which the church borrowed the custom of general confirmation, are in our view the cultic expression for the transition of the idiopathic into the sympathetic life-age.

If in the longhouses and men’s associations of “nature peoples” we see most decidedly prefigured the thought that the church later placed in service of abstinence, the thought of monastic unisexuality of those sympathetic unions, this may anew warn us against conflating Eros with sexual dimorphism and the love-inclination igniting thereon. On the whole and essentially, Sexus and Eros rather stand in the relation of mutual disturbance, such that it has ever been one of humanity’s most di!cult life-questions—never solved—to create a balance betwixt both. The attempt to infuse the copulatory drive with erotic essence almost always ended with the tragic downfall of the lovers; the opposite attempt, to rend both asunder, led and leads contrariwise to the poisoning and ultimately death of Eros, whereto we shall return. Here we emphasize that the sympathetic shudder tends to prevail stronger, purer, and deeper betwixt beings of the same sex than of diverse. Its eternal emblem is not the duality of man and woman, but of the Dioscuri, and its highest festivals have never been celebrated by amour, but always only by love-imbued friendship! (Or yet sometimes amour and festivals even of more shudderful beatitude?! Perhaps! But then followed by that wild-painful tragic which—celebrated by poets with trembling ardor, unriddled by no sage—like a bloody fire-glow, captivating and terrifying alike, hangs over the dust-enveloped battlefield of historical humanity; ever repeating Shakespeare’s most passionate drama, which concludes with the words: “For never was a story of more woe – Than this of Juliet and her Romeo.”) From this vantage we regard Germanic man-loyalty; but also the original “man-love” of the Greeks, which has very little in common with so-called homosexuality and only from evil entanglement with the dimorphic union-drive degenerated into shallow “boy-love.” Whom in youth did not enthuse the tale of Damon and Phintias, or the relation of Hagen and Volker, or the world-widespread brother-fairy tale! Who would misrecognize that in fairy tale altogether the erotic bond with maternal tenderness embraces all beings, humans, animals, plants, rocks and especially all utensils! Recall perchance the touching friendship of dog and sparrow and the terrible retribution the sparrow exacts on the carter because he ran over his “friend dog”! The Eros of the Occident stands under the sign of “blood-brotherhood,” of which the “sacred band” of Thebans is a world-historical example. It created the poetry of guilds and work-communities, of landsknechts and vagrants, and acts unto our days into the fraternization customs of student unions, whose song-rich symposia until a generation ago managed to preserve enough of it to make many a “philistine” lifelong wistfully recall the “old burschen-glory”! Page upon page could be filled if we traced its tracks through all crystal-formations of a humanity not yet servile to the coercive law of the allegedly sole beatifying family. Therefore we break off and take the next step to illumination of the cosmogonic Eros by proving the incompatibility of mere satisfaction-voluptuousness with its sympathetic nature.

Hitherto we treated of divided emotions, yet not of their transition into the voluptuousness of intoxication.

If this too be one doubled by division, its essential content can never be found in the voluptuousness of bodily union. The Platonic Aristophanes has the lovers assure that it corresponds to their innermost wish “to become one from two.” In whatever measure the bodily embrace grants such wish at least momentary fulfillment, this much is certain, that insofar as it occurs through it, precisely thereby the bond of Eros would be torn! For with what I became one, that is no longer existent for me; its and my reality have flowed together into one reality, in which loving and loved no longer diverge. But if the harmony rests precisely thereon that in each’s own-arousal the foreign-arousal is heighteningly present, it collapses when the “Other” itself extinguishes for me as merged in the unarticulated reality of voluptuous oneness. – Earlier we showed that the sexual drive does not coincide with Eros because it is a drive, a need, a striving, whereas Eros is consummation; now we have shown that also as voluptuousness grasped, satisfaction-voluptuousness must diverge from erotic voluptuousness, insofar as it could transiently escape the twoness whereof Eros, to be, inescapably requires. Not only is the erotic state instead of needy rather bestowing and overflowing, but also erotic embrace is never mingling: it links the poles, but without annulling them!

Precisely because he knew this as the great eroticist he undoubtedly was, Plato could combat lusts from the opposition of Eros to the satisfaction-voluptuousness of mere desires! The twoness of enthusiasm was the bridge on which he enticed the cosmic Eros across to the “supercelestial place” of his “uranic” Eros. Not the desiring, but the bestowing lover became the archetype of all “savior-figures.” For the “redeemer” and “healer” shares with the creatively loving that he wills not to possess the disciple’s body, but to “transfigure” it, albeit at the price of the far worse subjugation of life itself by the parasitic Logos! – Yet what in the distortions of a Plato remained predominantly dialectical forgery, that in the vilifications of the fanatic Paul stems already from that narrow-minded malice which can unveil to us the poisonous fount of all asceticism. Here some samples thereof (per Weizsäcker’s translation):

“I see however another law in my members, which wars against the law of my mind and takes me captive in the law of sin that is in my members.”

“Therefore God gave them up to shameful passions: their women as well have perverted the natural practice into the unnatural, as also the men have abandoned the natural intercourse with woman and inflamed in wild desires one another, man with man in shameless act.”

“It is good for the man to touch no woman. Yet for the sake of fornications let each have his wife and each woman her husband.”

“But to the married I command, rather not I but the Lord: that the wife not separate from her husband..., likewise the husband not dismiss his wife.”

And also shamelessly revealing the ulterior motive:

“What the world holds for foolish, God has chosen to shame the wise; and what the world holds for weak, God has chosen to shame the strong; and what the world holds for base and despised, God has chosen; what is nothing, to nullify what is something: that all flesh be deprived of glory before God.”

No commentary on the sanctity of marriage and the defamation of Eros, on pagan and Christian love, could surpass the impact of such sentences, which could be supplemented and expanded by hundreds similar. From this we discern: Whoever condemns Eros because, for whatever reasons, he deems it necessary to reject sexuality, thereby betrays that Eros is unknown to him and voluptuousness familiar only as intoxication-less titillation! World-hostility there and a furiously begetting Sexus here are accordingly but different sides of the same coin, and agitating asceticism was never anything but inverted lechery! It is the devouring “will to power” of a de-eroticized sexuality, which equally demands abstinence and sacramental marriage; as indeed every society founded on legal monogamy has besides brought to most luxuriant proliferation a venal prostitution! “Christianity gave Eros poison to drink; he did not die of it, but degenerated to vice” (Nietzsche).

If for the ancients the phallic stone was the symbol of the cosmogonic Eros, then for us, sexuality may serve as an essential indicator of the purity as well as the impairment of life’s processes. Accordingly, world-hatred, sin-delusion, and devaluation of the moment— in short, all forms of enmity against lusts—are the sign of a nature that has fallen prey to a maddened sexual drive, because wholly severed from the uniting stream of Eros! Thus, from the “love” of the sexes grows the “death-hatred” and battle of the sexes, wherever one becomes to the other merely a means for realizing the most selfish purposes. This is the occidental form of enmity between the life-poles through the principle of spirit. Finally, if we ponder what great interest spirituality has in naked sexual rut, to confound erotic voluptuousness with intoxication-less lechery, we shall no longer wonder that in prevailing linguistic usage, the joys of the copulatory act became the model for the concept of voluptuousness generally. – But now steering back to the erotic surge, we pose the content-heavy question: how can it remain sympathetic at all, when it becomes from mere emotion to genuine ecstasy? Has the ecstatic not only forsaken his self, but even the barriers of his individuality, and is now he himself become the triad in which the poles of the world have internalized—where then in his intoxication would there still be room for the co-intoxication of another being? That seems impossible, and if it nonetheless became event, then it is the soul’s deepest and ultimate secret. We shall not lift the veil, but lead only to the threshold; beforehand, however, let what follows from the dual nature of erotic intoxication be brought to conclusion.

We had presupposed as possible that sexual union brings transitory oneness in the souls of the lovers and lets twoness drown in the darkness of intoxication. But if we experimentally made the assumption ourselves that it too is experiencable sympathetically, it would always remain an experience only of one part, without guarantee that in the same moment it would also be experience of the other part! Quite unquestioningly, of two embracing, one can experience as bestowing love what the other experiences merely as satisfaction of a taking and possessing drive. But then the condition would no longer be fulfilled in which we found the essential mark of Eros: the polar reciprocity of being gripped. This leads us forthwith to the second point. Even if the mingling-voluptuousness had sprung from a bestowing overflow and devoted love, it yet tears into the loosing whirl of a simpliciter unarticulated darkness, wherein twoness appears as no longer existent. But from this darkness there is now no awakening that does not break the spell! “Come to oneself again,” each of the lovers finds itself as what it was before, as separated own-being, unredeemed from for-itself-being in a world that, along with the beloved, remains an eternal outside! The sexual union has not helped the lovers to the “crown of life,” has not carried them from the transience of singleness into the “eternity of life,” has not bestowed upon them the mystical consecration! In relation to this, the copulatory drive is adiaphoron and the satisfaction-intoxication an illusion, though beatifying. Contrariwise, even in the shudder of highest fulfillment, Eros remains an Eros of distance, and the intoxicated to the co-intoxicated a never mingling second of unfathomable depth, an eye of the All gazing at him from purpurean night! To surrender oneself to this means not: to desire it; to embrace this not: to become one with it; and to sink into it means: to awaken! The solution of the so-called riddle of the world is the ecstatic inwardness of the mystery of the world. Why are the questions of the Sphinx lethal for man—why does the sight of the Gorgon petrify to stone—why does lifting the veil of Isis bring ruin? Better than the answer with sober sentences could, may the ancient fate-question itself be to us the knowledge-bestowing symbol, that with rapturous beatitude only an eternally distant bestows.

True to our intent, to link even the remotest, insofar as possible, not with everyday and habitual, yet with the memorable, we draw for confirmation of the said an experience hereto, of which in the age of “poeting and aspiring” doubtless everyone once partook, whether he long forgot it in the age of deed. – The immediately convincing utterance of Stendhal, that the beauty of the artistically consummated artwork is a “happiness-promise,” perhaps in the finder’s spirit really meant what Nietzsche imputes to it to support his own conception: that art has the determination to persuade the beholder to a!rmation of life at any price. But the questionable phrase teaches us yet other things, if we ponder that the promise harbored by the sung pain, the poeted love, the chiseled grace, the painted storm, the stone-eternalized star-kinship perchance of the Porta nigra, would find fulfillment nowhere in diurnal existence. Whom even once the heart moved to quicker beating by the cloud-shadowed sea-distance that Böcklin’s brush awakened in the painful drunkenness of his Triton-gaze, how could he hope to see the more than human longing-shudder die somewhere in a satiation ending him! Who, spellbound by the sucking afterglow of irrevocably dead millennia, strides along the grave-monuments of the Via Appia, how could he deem the unpossessable become his own, if transported back into imperial Rome! Whom in lost Volga-songs the nameless melancholy of the steppe draws, how could he think, wandering, to find the place where that draw would be at goal! And so rather holds for the life-secret itself what the Stendhalian utterance elucidates at a humanly mediated life-expression! To nearness belongs as its counterpole the essentially never attainable distance. Every glance, surrendered even only to the expanse of space, promises and lures; but to what it lures, that we would not find if we set out and strove “into the distance”: the horizon flees before us, and no wanderer yet traversed the sunset. – Thus prepared, we announce the unassuming experience that shall awaken echo in many a memory.

In lilac-scented summer night, by uncertain flickering light-beam, met us with unspeakable happiness-promise from moist eyes a shimmering ray, transpierced and transformed us the mysterious smile, encompassed with magnetic charm the crackling loveliness of the floating-by form: a stream of rapture dissolved us, and the flame of love flaring touched the circling firmament. But woe to us, as long as unwise we took this moment of fulfillment for naught but a promise, exchanged the charm of the appearance with its bodily bearer, and let ourselves be seduced by the mystic hint into a possessing love-relation. It will not redeem the promise that the unbound minute seemed to give, and we shall too soon, tormentfully disillusioned, find instead of the gods-form that illumined us a finite, a limited, a measurable being! What we in human sense held for mere promise was rather a full draught from the beaker of the Eros of distance, which raptures out of the graspable world of things into the never to be touched reality of images!

But the image is a mysterious deception of distance, which gladly cloaks for the soul most ruinous in alluring sheens. What it raptures becomes beautiful, and were it the site of decay and horrors! It is life’s inwrought riddle— Is the with twilight-colors through the All Written question of the living after its origin. Sparkling shimmer crowns The unattained and extinguishes as soon as We grasp after it. For distance is, Where never we are; yet secretest urge Wills it into nearness us and own transform.

Already the next chapter shall wholly illuminate the fact. But here we place, countering and a!rming alike, alongside the just heard truth-word of the poet: who pursues the ever-eluding image is like the child that seeks to catch the rainbow and carries away naught but—wet fingers! We contest not the uniquely beatifying of the intimate cohabitation of two devoted one another lovers; but we contest the repeatability of the overwhelming shudder of first moments and we maintain the proposition that to the world-secret only first moments unlock the gate, not the humanly yet so warm enjoyments of secret nearness. – The so-called Romanticism stood like no historically known time before in the spell of the Eros of distance (as the present conversely in the spell of sexuality). And so read it more immediately from a revelatory stanza of Eichendorff’s that the supposed promise is only the humanly interpreted concomitant feeling of the painfully-sweet fulfillment:

All stars sparkle upon me

With glowing love-gaze.

Drunken speaks the distance

As of future, great happiness. —

Unnoticed we have thereby already initiated the answering of the last of those three questions that determine the course of the investigation. The first was, what liberates itself in ecstasy. We answered: the soul. The second, from what it liberates itself. From the spirit. The third, what the self-liberating gains through the liberation. To give thereon an equally round answer, we must provisionally leave the erotic intoxication-form out of consideration and first once for ecstasy as such characterize what manner consequences the soul-de-selfing has. It will after the last expounded then require only fewer words to show the particularity of the success for the case of elemental Eros, insofar as that within the frame of the limits we drew can occur at all. But since it would further complicate the discussion and impair its transparency if we held strictly within the bounds of a mere state-description, we answer the third question from the outset in the sense of a characterization no longer of the constitution of intoxication, but of its essence.

Three traits, despite otherwise significant di!erences in the primal experience of great ecstatics, always recur: the (supposed or real) rapture of the soul into distant regions, if not indeed into an “other world” – the insensitivity to painful impressions, whose expression is invulnerability – and the reception of life-determining revelations. Only the third concerns us initially for answering the question of what the experience is like, whose value of certainty overwhelms the persuasive power of all profane truths. If we were to rely for this purpose on the statements, especially those of the initiates from the mystery cults of all times and peoples, we might either come away empty-handed due to a duty of silence of the “knowing ones” – or we would get entangled in the confusion of those “visions” from which the holy myth springs (where on the one hand we would encounter meteoric wonders like clouds of light, pillars of fire, stars, and on the other the most manifold apparitions of spirits, now the satyrs of Dionysus, now devils and sorcerers, now angels and saints) – or we would learn of many symbols, which themselves would require complete interpretation, like the mystical rose, the halo, the holy grail – or we would be informed of the thoroughly physical accompanying feelings of those seized by ecstasy, from which we could at least gather that they not only saw something but simultaneously also heard, smelled, tasted, touched it; (the sweetness of the tasted God plays the greatest role in the Christian mysteries: “Gustate et videte, quoniam suavis est Dominus!”) – or finally we would be instructed about the faith convictions that had established themselves in the mystic as a result of the experience and which, apart from the ever-recurring “immortality,” diverged to the utmost! Indeed, the faith of the Godblessed “bride of heaven” stands in the sharpest contrast to the confession of the witch, who sealed her consecration covenant through fornication with the devil! In this manner, we would run the risk of confusing the mystical experience with a hallucination that draws its material from the wishful notions of the prevailing cults, and of considering the initiates themselves to be hysterical fools, in which the “modern age” has indeed done its part. However, whoever proceeded thus would have from the outset taken a path that never leads to the goal.

To understand faith convictions, one must know the myth from which they grew; to understand the myth, the symbols that produced it; but to understand the symbols, one must know the nature of an experience that can never be communicated in judgments. Although it seems we must actually renounce this, we are nevertheless still permitted to ask whether, despite so many visions and doctrines of faith, there is not something common in the motivating reasons of that certainty of knowledge, which is logically neither acquired nor shaken, and which, though long no longer attached solely to them, certainly originally belonged to them first! And here we initially encounter in the mystery cults the following ever-recurring fact: the mystical certainty originates from the experience of “epopteia,” i.e., the vision – the vision relates to the appearance of the god (epiphany, parousia) – and this in turn occurs on the occasion of the symbolic representation of his death with subsequent rebirth from a “holy wedding.” We anticipate the final result: the mystic experiences the consecration of fulfillment by being united with a god, himself deified (communion, unio mystica, hierogamos), and he unites with him by seeing him. Here are some pieces of evidence from the realm of antiquity.

“Happy is he among men on earth who has seen this,” it says at the end of the Hymn to Demeter. “O thrice happiest of mortals, who go to Hades after having beheld this,” in Sophocles. “Happy is he who, after having seen that, goes under the hollow earth; he knows the completion of life, he the God-given beginning of life,” in Pindar. “Those participating in the Bacchic and Corybantic actions work themselves into ecstasy until they see what is expected,” reports Philo in his work “On the Contemplative Life.” But the mystical union with God – placing it, according to the hopes of the initiates, into the new life beyond physical death for postmortal bliss – is also proclaimed by one of the Orphic gold tablets: “O happiest and most blessed, you will become a god instead of a pitiable man!” On another, the soul of the initiate rejoices before the gods: “I boast of being of your blessed race!”

The consecration rites are something regulated, established by a human founder, which chained many generations to a life community and preserved for the rhythm of the universe the power to loosen again the coercive bonds in which the spirit wants to alienate those already bound by it from life. But the experience that created them and whose renewal they wish to facilitate, the original ecstasy, once bore all works of man in the age of Pelasgianism, whose fragments and ruins we gather today among dying “primitive peoples,” and it still bears today (or did so until very recently) genuine poetry and genuine art; whence it comes that their content, too, absolutely cannot be transferred into judgment-stating speech. The expression familiar to us, at least for a group of arts, “visual arts,” reminds us, however, that every formative action aims at the objectification of images, and lets us suspect: the event that forces the spirit to formative e!orts is somehow connected with the flashing up of an “image.” We therefore take the, if not yet decisive, then all-further-pre-determining step for understanding the essence of ecstasis, if we investigate how the certainly only su!erable vision of occurring “images” di!ers from the willable act of perception, which gives us things.

I cannot take a beautiful landscape that I have enjoyed with me; but I can form a “representation” of it, namely on the basis of the image of it living within me. While the objective landscape remains the same thing, where it is, its image travels with the thousands of experiencing beings who partook of its sight, and indeed both already di!erent in each one in and of itself, and again di!erent depending on the lighting colors, noises, movements of the beheld archetype. If we subtract from the representation the mental activity of the one representing, if we thus grant the image the sovereign majesty of a dream vision, which encounters the dreamer like a reality, and if we finally think of this as conditioned not so much by the soul of the recipient as grounded in the archetype itself, then we have in it the representational form of all happening, which ruled in the twilight consciousness of the ecstatic and in the consciousness generally of primordial man over the world of facts.

This premised, we formulate the following contrasts: the image has presence only in the moment of its being experienced; the thing is “established” once and for all – the image flows with the ever-flowing experience; the thing persists, endures, stands in a life-alien indivisibility – the image is only there in the experience of the experiencer; the thing in any act of perception by anyone – I can certainly remember the image, but I cannot make it present in a judgment; the thing, because it is now the same as then, I can refer to in thought at any time and make it the identical reference point for all perceivers by communicating my referring judgment – the image, immersed in the stream of time, transforms itself, as everything transforms, including the experiencing soul; the thing, because outside of time, falls prey to destruction in relation to it – the image is received by the soul; the thing is accomplished on the basis of what is received through the judging act of the spirit – the image has reality independent of consciousness (for it remains completely untouched by whether I remember it afterwards or not); the thing is thought into the world by consciousness and exists only for an inwardness of personal beings. Therefore: whoever shatters personhood in ecstasy, for him the world of facts perishes in the same moment, and the world of images rises for him with a reality-power that displaces everything. The envisioning soul is its inner pole, the envisioned reality its outer pole. The former is now connected with the latter (= incessant Gamos), but it never coincides with it into one (= incessant vision). From the polar contact of inside and outside, the itself ensouled image is incessantly born (= incessant emanation). The outside begets, the inside receives, and from the embrace of both bursts forth the singing fire-stream of the images of the universe, the “dancing star” of chaos articulated into cosmos. The visionary sentence of Novalis: “The external is an internal raised to a state of mystery,” expresses with e!ortless grace the meaning of ecstasy and the ground of all envisioning certainty.

We pause for a moment to protect the vision of the archetypes from the devastating misunderstanding that has threatened it since Schopenhauer’s further interpretation of the Platonic “Ideas.” We cannot demonstrate the nihilism of that “doctrine of Ideas” more convincingly than by the seductive reflection that the mirror of Schopenhauerian thought shows us of it. – In the thinking state, he instructs us, we grasp individual facts, which further form the object of scientific research. In the contemplative state, on the other hand, we grasp intuitively and immediately in the individual object its imperishable “archetype.” Poets and artists are such personalities to whom it is granted more often and to a higher degree than to the ordinary man to behave contemplatively towards the world-picture and consequently to gain an immediate intuition of its archetypal reality. Connected with this is the strongly Buddhist-colored view that the thinking state is simultaneously “interested” in the broadest sense of the word. Commonly, the interest consists of a very personal inclination or disinclination towards the thing; but it could exceptionally also be that general interest of the researcher, yet it remains nonetheless a kind of interest or excitement of the “will” inherent in all appearances. According to this, the contemplative state presents itself as a temporary liberation from the neediness of willing, and the beheld archetype becomes the counter-image of a both supra-personal and supra-individual “knowing.” We cannot refrain from quoting a few passages from Schopenhauer, because we first want to make the extraordinarily appealing aspect of his view emphatically clear before demonstrating its erroneousness.

“No attained object of willing can give lasting, non-receding satisfaction: rather, it is always only like the alms thrown to the beggar, which sustains his life today to prolong his torment until tomorrow. Therefore, as long as our consciousness is filled by our will, as long as we are given over to the pressure of desires with its constant hoping and fearing, as long as we are the subject of willing, never will lasting happiness or peace be granted to us... Thus the subject of willing lies constantly on the turning wheel of Ixion, always draws water in the sieve of the Danaids, is the eternally languishing Tantalus. – But when external occasion or inner mood suddenly lifts us out of the endless stream of willing, tears knowledge from the slave-service of the will, when attention is no longer directed to the motives of willing but apprehends things free from their relation to the will, thus without interest, without subjectivity, considers them purely objectively, completely given over to them, insofar as they are mere representations, not insofar as they are motives: then the peace always sought on that first path of willing, but always fleeing, has suddenly come of itself, and we are completely well. It is the painless state that Epicurus praised as the highest good and as the state of the gods: for we are for that moment relieved of the base pressure of will, we celebrate the Sabbath of the penitentiary labor of willing, the wheel of Ixion stands still. – This state is precisely the one I described above as required for the knowledge of the Idea, as pure contemplation, sinking into intuition, losing oneself in the object, forgetting all individuality, abolishing the mode of knowledge following the principle of sufficient reason and grasping only relations, whereby simultaneously and inseparably the intuited individual thing rises to the Idea of its species, the knowing individual to the pure subject of will-less knowing, and now both, as such, no longer stand in the stream of time and all other relations. It is then all the same whether one sees the sun set from a dungeon or from a palace.” – And: “That liberation of knowledge lifts us out of all that as much and as completely as sleep and dream: happiness and unhappiness have vanished: we are no longer the individual, it is forgotten, but only the pure subject of knowledge: we are only there as the one world-eye, which looks out from all knowing beings, but in man alone can become completely free from the service of the will, whereby all di!erence of individuality so completely disappears that it is then all the same whether the seeing eye belongs to a powerful king or a tormented beggar.”

Hearing this, should one not think it is pretty much the same as what we just spoke of as the essential characteristic of the ecstatic state, since we too assume that it enables us to see through the world of things into the reality of the I-independent images! Schopenhauer too seems to see the precondition of vision in a shattering of the barriers of selfhood and declares war on all subjectivism of world-contemplation just as decidedly as we do! So it seems, but this appearance thoroughly deceives! We must not let it vex us to reproduce one more passage, because it can glaringly reveal that our thinker means rather the complete opposite.

Attempting to explain with some examples what then actually constitutes the content of the Ideas beheld in such a manner, he says, among other things:

“When the clouds drift, the figures they form are not essential to them, are indi!erent to them; but that they, as elastic vapor, compressed by the impact of the wind, are driven away, extended, torn apart, this is their nature, is the essence of the forces objectifying themselves in them, is the Idea: only for the individual observer do the respective figures exist. – For the brook rolling downhill over stones, the eddies, waves, foam formations it shows are indi!erent and inessential; that it follows gravity, behaves as an inelastic, completely displaceable, formless, transparent fluid, this is its essence, this is, when intuitively recognized, the Idea: only for us, as long as we recognize as individuals, do those formations exist. – The ice on the windowpane shoots forth according to the laws of crystallization, which reveal the essence of the natural force emerging here, represent the Idea; but the trees and flowers it forms in the process are inessential and only there for us.”

This passage shows us with almost frightening clarity that the so-called archetypes or Ideas, which Schopenhauer, and indeed in full agreement with Plato, has in mind, are not images at all, but one and all nothing but concepts! What he here designates as the essence of the clouds, the essence of the brook, the essence of the ice crystals, is absolutely that concept of the general nature of vapor, water, and freezing which, as no one can fail to recognize, forms the object of “natural research”; so that it is not accidental that on occasion of his description the keyword “law” also slips from him. He is in fact talking about nothing other than the law-governed nature of the world and is in the gravest error if he nevertheless believes that such natural laws are intuited! Let us try to mentally place ourselves in the state of someone who, at the sight of the ice flowers and crystal trees on the windowpanes, really “loses himself in the object,” who forgets himself and the rest of the world and completely merges into the image of these ice flowers: how could a thought about the laws of crystallization and freezing come to him in that very moment, how could the law of ice formation become intuitively visible to him in the mysteriously softly growing ice flowers that winter conjures on his panes! No, he will feel as removed from all concepts of crystallizing, evaporating, freezing as he feels removed from his person; he will believe himself dreamily transported into the ensouled world of those ice formations, to partake in the inwardness of their blooming and growing! Schopenhauer’s, and exactly likewise Plato’s, “Ideas” are not counter-images for the envisioning soul, but products of the judging spirit and di!er from the concepts of ordinary human reason, and further from science, solely through their claim to metaphysical reality. But through this claim, the intention of Platonic-Schopenhauerian thinking proves to be directed against archetypal vision; for it undertakes to put reified concepts and absolutized natural laws in place of the real archetypes! Plato, seeing deeply in this, was therefore a declared enemy of art; Schopenhauer, however, as ultimately a modern man, attempts, admittedly without knowing it himself, to veil the opposition by having his invented intuitive knowing, which in reality is intellectual knowledge once again, erupt from a state of contemplative rapture. But with this he falsifies ecstasy, whence it follows that its genuine discharges, which we find in myth and in the symbols, must be rejected by him as much as by Plato, who consistently even rejected art. And that is indeed the case!

“Whoever has well grasped this and knows how to distinguish the will from the Idea and this from its appearance, to him world events will only have significance insofar as they are letters from which the Idea ... can be read, but not in and for themselves. He will not believe with people that time produces something really new and significant, that through it or in it something absolutely real comes into existence... Therefore, he will as little appoint a whole Olympus full of gods to direct those time events with Homer, as hold the figures of the clouds for individual beings with Ossian, since, as said, both have (i.e., equally little) significance in relation to the Idea appearing in them.”

We see: The world of gods as well as every world of poetic images that approaches the mythical is sacrificed to the conceptual world. – In contrast now to the universality of the meaning-content of mere concepts, however one may doll it up with the name “Idea,” the real archetype is at all times absolutely momentary and therefore unrepeatably unique. But we must consider carefully what follows from this alone.

Whether it concerns people, animals, plants, forest or starry sky, we speak of each of the things so designated with regard to the circumstance that it is a selfsame in the temporal streaming of the world. The forest I perceive from my window is today the same forest it was yesterday, and will be tomorrow the same it is today. I thus arrived at the concept of this forest by looking away from the temporality of all happening and particularly of my experiencing, and subsuming a permanently selfsame under the respectively present impression. In relation to the former, this is a universal, as becomes most sharply clear as soon as we consider that we can return to it at any time. Nothing would be changed about that even if one were to clear-cut the forest; for that would not take from me the consciousness that it, this one and same forest, once stood there earlier. In terms of possibility, any thing can be destroyed; but not even the omnipotence of a god could e!ect that it had not been. Or: What has happened cannot be unhappened. Accordingly, what we have in mind when we speak of things or processes, indeed simply of objects, has, in relation to the uniqueness of the image living only in momentary vision, the nature of universality or conceptuality. But then no archetype can be the same as any thing!

Let us imagine two sympathetically connected people, while their love is, so to speak, in bloom; nevertheless, it will not happen that within a period of, say, several weeks they feel the same degree of rapture at every arbitrary hour or minute.

In the innermost of the love-flame lives A kind of wick and snu!, which dampens it; And nothing remains in constant goodness always. For goodness, which becomes full-blooded, dies In its own excess. (Shakespeare.)

They will live through common hours or minutes of exuberant bliss, indeed of rapture, and extinguished hours will follow, where each of them longs in vain for the blissful shudder and seeks in vain for the reasons for its absence. For the experience of the lover, even if he scarcely knows it, refers not to the enduring person of the beloved, but to his image streaming with the time-stream. Therefore, it is the great and tragic fate precisely of the great lovers to indulge the error that the state of Eros also guarantees its duration. From the young girl becomes a woman, from the woman an old woman; from the youth becomes a man, from the man an old man: each time it is the same person wandering through all these appearances, but the appearances themselves are widely di!erent from each other; and all Eros hangs only on the image, not on the bliss of a particular thing! If Schopenhauer said one must disregard the appearance to arrive at the Idea, we say rather the reverse: one must disregard the conceptual content, i.e., the object, to come to the image. And this holds not only for the erotic impulse, but it holds for the vision of images . A hundred times I may have seen the forest before my window without experiencing anything other than just the thing, that same thing the botanist also means; but once, while it flames in the glows of the evening sun, the sight is able to tear me from my I; and then my soul suddenly beholds what I had never seen before, perhaps only for a minute, indeed perhaps only for seconds; however, whether long or short, what was beheld now was the archetype of the forest, and this image returns neither for me nor for anyone else ever again. – Perhaps a really significant objection will be raised here. If the forest is a conceptual content, one could say, then he who beheld it archetypally should no longer perceive a forest; if that hardly happens, how can one claim that he is free from the fetters of the I, thus of the spirit and all conceptuality! – We answer by now also trying to describe the archetype positively.

Here we see first that even every content of intuition is incomparably more manifold than the thing to be extracted from it. Thus, the object meant by the concept of the forest, even of a very specific forest, contains nothing of its lighting colors. At the moment of my vision, however, the forest flamed in the lights of the evening sun, and this shimmer was absolutely inseparable from the image I experienced. Another time the same forest may sway, shrouded in fog, in the storm, and I may be graced anew to behold its archetype: then, however, this is a di!erent one from the previous one. Compared to the expressible object of perception, the impression-image proves to be richer by numerous traits, which can never be completely divided up into again expressible properties. – However, we hear the objection that this is due to the peculiarity of (necessarily) abstracting thinking and possibly language, but is not suitable for distinguishing the act of perception from the event of vision; for if one replaced the former with the light-sensitive plate, it would be quite capable of capturing the momentary impression of the perceptual object and, what it captured, of preserving pictorially. However, with the contrast of the momentary impression to the enduring thing, we only wanted to recall why the di!erence between the two does not and cannot consist in the fact that for the ecstatic receiver of the image, the thing to be called “forest” had now been replaced by another thing! As just indicated, the expressible thing is extracted from the impression-image; and that is now indeed the only similarity between it and the content of the de-selfed vision. For the “archetype” – appearance of the essence of the world and therefore the most real reality – nevertheless never stands there as a being and something to be found, but is first born from the polar contact of a receiving soul with an e!ective daemon, and of such origin the sign and seal is the luminous shudder that surrounds it in the moment of becoming. The archetypes are richer by the nimbus than even the impression-occasion that the spirit is able to grasp in them. It would be futile to want to analyze the nimbus and distribute it to concepts; against this, we can try to awaken the memory of it by pointing to its formative consequences.

In ancient Chinese lyric poetry, it is said of the king’s bride that she is “a tree in silken leaf-garment.” In the episode of Nala and Damayanti (from the Indian Mahabharata), the half-undressed hero is painted with the words: “Who is that beauty-flame enveloped in smoke.” A sonnet by Eichendor! on the dead beloved ends with the stanza:

But your image has become a star to me, Which points homeward with silent glance, That piously the sailor may strive with the winds.

The delight, the melancholy, the nocturnal sorrow of the singer transforms for him the loveilluminated figure, moves it into distant connections of cosmic depth and lets us behold in the individual section of the appearance what no daylight glance of consciousness would find: the extinguishing or inflaming, the approaching or fleeing, the weeping or rejoicing soul of the world. Lenau’s dark melancholy appears the adorable image in the lightning features of a surging firmament:

How storm-clear I think I see you And your long hair Wave free in the storm.

Mörike in his poem on “Peregrina” conjures the magic of the beloved in the symbol of gold:

The mirror of these faithful, brown eyes Is like a reflection of inner gold. Deep from the bosom it seems to suck it. There such gold may thrive in holy grief.

Similes, we are told. Far from it! If we descend to the level of the folk primal song, we see, by the magical power of the word, feature by feature happening what the god-inspired mouth of the seer proclaims! In a Russian song, the aged wandering singer finds no ship at the sea to ferry him across. Then however:

Sang so beautiful a song the old man, So powerful a song the aged one, That the sea began to listen, That the blue waves listened, That the foaming billows listened And the deep streams listened And even the shores themselves listened; Sang so beautiful a song the old man, So powerful a song the aged one, That the yellow shores inclined The one to the other, So inclining towards each other, That no little space remained between; So inclining towards each other, That no gap was to be seen; So inclining towards each other, That they were closely connected: And they were firmly united, Left shore with the right, Right shore with the left, Forming a visible bridge.

A Finnish song tells of the rune-singer:

All jubilate and rejoice, All sing, all spring, All hop and delight themselves, And all larks trill, And all salmon leap up, And all crickets chirp, And all bees hum, And all beetles whirr, Even the mountain winds dance, Even the sound’s waves hop, Even the firs bend, Even the pines bow, Even the birches curve, Even the willows stoop, Even the flowers sway, Even the grasses rock, Even all ships rustle, Yes, even the stones roll, And the dune trembles, And all smooth pebbles whirr At the salmon-catching places, At the beautiful whitefish bays: Sings a good rune-singer, Sings a brave rune-singer.

But also of the mystical temple, before which, compelled by Faust’s journey to the Mothers, the abduction of Helen is to play out anew, it says in Goethe:

The column’s shaft, even the triglyph rings. I rather think, the whole temple sings.

The image of man dissolves into the meteorilia, the meteorilia congeal into the image of man. What was to the day’s eye something individual under something individual, stands surrounded by telluric and cosmic streams. Colors, forms, sounds, noises, fragrances seem fused in it into a pandemonium of all image-elements; and yet it shines shimmering and super-clear, resembling a now threatening, now promising countenance. Not objectively was the reality changed, in contact with which the image was born, but rather the barriers receded that separate from reality itself the – object! Whatever entered the ray of archetypal vision, it is no longer one thing among other things, but it became the midpoint of the world. What Mephisto grants the adept holds true for it:

Throw yourself into the sea, where it rages most wildly, And scarcely you tread the pearl-rich ground, So billowing forms a splendid round; You see up and down light-green swaying waves With purple hem swell to the fairest dwelling Around you, the center. With every step Wherever you go, the palaces go with you.

In the spark jumping between daemon and soul, this soul itself becomes the daemonic primal source of the images, living, of which the revelation in the word can never be thought: the world-beginning renewing itself again and again in the event of vision! We recall the fire-wrought stanza of Schuler in the “Corybantic Dithyramb” of his “Cosmogony”:

I am the light that sucks you from the night,

I am the eye that feigns the radiance for you, I am the pearl that fills the shell, I am the intoxication that rejuvenates this world, I am life. – The world-destiny stands present in the illumined moment; into the distances of space and into the distances of time, everything that ever happened and happens has its light and its meaning from the, however quickly, fading image.

Any chance thing, Novalis thinks, can “become our world-organ. A sight, a star, a region, an old tree can make an epoch in our interior. This is the great realism of fetish worship.” Indeed, whatever may seem strange to us today about cult actions, holy festivals, magic customs, taboos, divination arts, it is grounded without exception in the predominance of the envisioning inwardness over the merely perception-capable faculty of impression and in the interweaving of the always only mechanically moved world of objects into the daemonically-living reality of images. But as these immediately shaped themselves in the whole way of life of prehistoric man, as they crystallized, as it were, in temples, idols, and grave monuments, as they still settled late in the blown-after shower of blossoms of poetry, so too a lore of signs remained from it, which we must not disdain to call upon for help where we have to lead over from the, because extra-conscious, non-communicable content of ecstasy to those logically incomprehensible certainties that the Pelasgian lived, which the man of historical antiquity at least spiritually regained in the secret initiations. For if there was a humanity for whom it was still something ordinary to enter into connection with the world around them through fertilizing vision, then they had to strive in the intermediate states of sobriety to capture what had now passed in a similar way through signs for consciousness, as the rational man preserves his knowledge of things with the help of a conceptual language. Now – these signs are the symbols. They are glyphs of ecstatically beheld images, thus true hieroglyphs, just as the word of communicative language usage is the glyph that holds fast the objects in concepts. If conceptual language serves the communication of judgment, then symbolic language serves the reawakening of vision; and if the concept forms the starting point of scientific research, then the symbol forms the origin of myth. – Incalculable is the lore of signs of the envisioning state of consciousness, as we shall henceforth briefly call it, immeasurable the gain in knowledge if one knows how to read from it! For our purposes, it may suffice to pick out a single page from the abundance of its sediments and customs, for whose understanding, however, we must first deepen our insight into the essence of images.

We transitioned from the state to the essence of ecstasis by emphasizing the distance of the image in relation to the nearness of the perceptual thing. To the scarcely avoidable objection that distant things are also perceived, and in particular the pattern for any possible distance is the perceivable spatial distance, we would have only to reply that no perception takes place without (even if remaining unconscious) vision, not even of the spatially near, but that the perception of the spatially distant has a peculiar “timbre,” in which one accustomed to listening hears the voice of the awakening soul; and precisely in this the already beginning change of state is faintly announced. Compared to one who notices the beetle on his hand, the viewer of blue-forested mountain ranges stands under otherwise similar circumstances essentially closer to the “dreamer” or “absorbed one.” The distinction-willing observer even treats the distant as if it were something near, and sacrifices the intuitive image to a sequence of points that he measures one after the other and thus separately; whereas the gaze of one sunk in contemplation, even of a near object, is purpose-released, captivated by the image of the object, and that means at least a form that was not closed by boundary-setting, but by the entirety of surrounding neighbor-images. Not so much the distance of the object as the kind of observation decides whether it has the characteristic of nearness or of distance; and no one fails to recognize the thing-like quality of the near character, the image-like quality of the character of distance.

However, someone could now object with no small appearance of justification: however much that may be correct in and of itself, we would thereby get into an insoluble contradiction with the primitive state of consciousness, which according to our opinion is primarily governed by vision. For even siderical units of intuition of primordial sensing, not to mention the objects of the near space, are clothed with distance for us, who know or presume to know their objective nature, but by no means already for the one experiencing them appearance-wise; and rather precisely in this lies the distinguishing mark of prehistory, that it moved even stellar distances into, as it were, domestic proximity. “The Greek cosmogony,” he might continue, “knew Eros as the creator of the world; but he remained, despite his cosmic nature, an Eros of nearness. It has a deeper meaning than modern astronomy could decipher, that it let the stars nourish themselves from the vapors of the earth; however, it also denotes besides the relation of nearness, which the eye-measure of Hellenic sensuality demands between the celestial and the landscape of the earth. Their, in core elemental, symbolism buys the charm of architectural formdefiniteness with a narrowing of the soul’s visual circle, which admittedly made possible above all the unique beauty-magic of their human-form art, but no less accelerated the silting up of the cultic into merely logical thinking. There is perhaps no more persuasively convincing testimony for the dependence of the faculty of judgment on the ‘seer’s glance’ of experience than the Copernican rediscovery of the wandering flight of the earth! The thought of heliocentrism – known early to the Greeks (Philolaus, Aristarchus) – was for antiquity an improbable ‘view,’ which withered beside the Ptolemaic scheme, because it never experienced the truly dizzying pathos that alone, as it were, lends it voice and fills a seemingly merely astronomical formula with the significance of the most unheard-of revolution that had until then broken in over the image of space in the mind of men! – The Greek hylist stands with wide eyes on the ‘well-founded enduring earth.’ Grove, rock, and surf-battered seacoast resound as in an articulated hymeneal, enclosed and clearly bounded by the radiant bell of the overarching ‘aether.’ The flame whirls up into the more related spaces, the falling rain fertilizes the receiving earth, animating wind, murmuring from fate-knowing treetops, connects nearness with distance, below with above, and the sky with all stars ‘grazes its countenance’ in the splendor of the waters. Everything moves and wanders, announces itself, unfolds, and vanishes, and thus it lives; drives and wishes, desire for satiation or mating, war and confraternity clump the airy, dissolve the solid, draw the celestial fire into the embrace of the swamps, send the nourishing moisture as mist into the sun’s zone of fire. The belief in all-livingness, in pammixia and incessant transformation permeates the myth as well as the systems of the earliest thinkers. But, although these removed it from the barriers of perception and even taught the circuit-like recurrence of polar reality-phases: one would nevertheless falsely supplement their state of consciousness from today’s if one supposed they had ever left the geocentric stage. Their vision of space opposed that! One should take the Occidental-antique space from the myths, symbols, wisdom teachings, the Homeric epics, Aeolic and Pindaric lyric, the choral songs of the tragedians, the temples and sculptures: it always has the form of the closing sphere, whose visible half stands like a time-roof or dome-like on the spread-out plane of the earthly landscape. The life-feeling of antiquity is cosmic, but cellular and takes the macrocosmos for an enlarged microcosmos. It remains deaf to the discovery of the Aristarchians, because its space is the macrocosmic cell that holds all happening like the creating jug holds the water-pour or which places the images of the sky with the images of the earth under the roof of the same house. The space of antiquity was the near-space of the oikos; and is it any di!erent with the world-space of the ‘savages’? Many Polynesian peoples express their firm conviction that the sky reaches down to the earth at the horizon and holds it thus enclosed, by calling foreigners ‘Papalangi,’ i.e., ‘sky-breakers,’ as those who broke into this world from another world! But what would speak more strongly for the near-character of the originally experienced, and how does that square with a doctrine of the distance of the image? –“ We would have to answer: the distance of the image is not a spatial distance, but a distance of inwardness. The image is not distant because it is far away, but because it is ensouled. The soul of the image is the daemon. The daemon is the soul of the image. The daemon is the ensouling power of the image.

We have taken the above consideration from an as yet unpublished work on the origin of the Copernican world system, which elaborates in detail what we can only communicate here in the form of an answer in its most indispensable points. – If one defines the concept of distance from the consciousness of one for whom that oikos of the universe was shattered by the “infinity” of Copernican space, one could comparatively indeed quite well call the Eros of antiquity an “Eros of Nearness.” But now, as will be shown immediately, we are justified, indeed obliged, for the purpose of distinguishing envisioning consciousness from object-consciousness, to take the expression “Eros of Distance” in an extended meaning, which permits its application to the cultic stage without exception of all peoples and times. Beforehand, however, we interject: what the above objection asserts concerns far less the di!erence between early and late times than that of two racial constitutions, more precisely the di!erence of the Greco-Roman group of peoples (with which many – not all – “primitive peoples” agree) from numerous Oriental tribes and especially the prehistoric Germans here, the Indians there. Many hymns of the oldest Vedas, for example, though unfamiliar with Copernican space, are irradiated by a distant gleam of images, to which not even Aeolic lyric poetry, let alone Homer and the tragedians, sets anything similar; and Indian architecture anticipates by more than a full millennium the dissolving trait of landscape romanticism in the late Baroque. But also in Occidental antiquity there were again and again individuals who broke through the Oikos thought, like the already mentioned Aristarchus of Samos, and above all Heraclitus with his uprooting of the stasis of thinking through the downright counter-Greek doctrine of the reality of eternal flux. But this by the way and now back to the “Eros of Distance” in the extended sense.

If the object, as moved into the distance, can at least become an image of intuition, as it were the reflection of the real image, then no less conversely does it hold that from the “infinity” of boundless world-space the distant image can be moved closer and ever closer and thus, as one might think, risk congealing into objective isolation. If we now asked whether there is a marker suitable for distinguishing the near-image from the even more distant object, we would find ourselves referred first of all to the negative sign of the untouchability of all images. To be sure, we heard above that the mystic often also tends to hear, smell, touch, taste something; however, in the next chapter we will explain, especially using the example of tasting, that it is not the touching sense of touch that comes into play, but a capacity for vision inherent even in tasting and touching! Now, strictly speaking, of course, everything only slightly objectively distant from the touching body is not touchable; but if we disregard the firmamental appearances, there is no objectively however distant thing that could not come into touchable proximity, be it through movement towards the observer, be it through his movement towards the object. If accordingly it belongs to the nature of the earthly body , the distant as well as the near, to be touchable, then the essential characteristic of distance does not apply to it, but it does truly to the images of the heavens and finally to everything temporally past.

Nearness and distance are the complementary poles not only of space, but equally of time. We make temporal distance present by means of the intuitive image of spatial distance; but what places the spatially distant into (untouchable) “distance” is – temporal distance. This appears in spatial distance; or: that which appears in the distance of space is distance of time. More briefly said (as already above): time is the soul of space. – It seems as if we might exceptionally call “natural science” to our aid; does not astronomy teach us that the light from Sirius, the nearest fixed star, already takes several years to reach us; therefore, beholding it, we would have “present” in the act of perception something that had actually already passed away by just as long a time! However, even if we did not believe ourselves certain that the science of tomorrow will reject this nowadays popular opinion as erroneous, nevertheless everything would be confused if we exchanged the measurable distance of the Sirius body from the earth planet with the distant character of the Sirius image! We are not talking about the mile-length or even light-year length of body-separating distances, but about that distance (so to speak distance “in itself”), which is immediately (and thus pre-conceptually) co-beheld in the vision of the image.

But imagine resting on the flat shore of the sea and given over without secondary thoughts to the utmost edge of the horizon, marked by the dissolving smoke-cloud of a steamer already vanished, and now place side by side in the mirror of recollection the accompanying feeling of the experienced spatial depth and the absorption in the memory-images of an irrecoverable youth; and whoever has the gift of finding in the realm of the interior will find both confusingly similar. What fills the heart with yearning as the unattainable distant scent of the horizon, painfully exciting and sweetly soothing at once, is the no longer touchable reality of the past; and what sets o! the long-past as far receded in the inner image of what just happened and is still present in the near-time, is the distanceblue of the depth of space, comparable to nothing else in the world. Cloud-walking over mountain firns and with the “deceptively remote” star-sparkle, eternity taking leave, eternity the prehistoric passes before the gaze of the one unintentionally captured by those images! Let us recall again the mystical revelation: the external is an internal raised to a state of mystery.

But now the new objection already arises: even if temporal and spatial nearness, temporal and spatial distance are each essentially one, why should the spatially distant in the light appear solely as the past and not equally as the future? According to language, the past lies behind us, the future before us; the evening glow may remind us of the former, the morning glow of the latter, and did not Eichendor! express in song that distance speaks of future happiness! – It would lead us away from the main subject and we must content ourselves with hints as to why the distance of past time is thought of as lying behind us; but we hope nevertheless to convince the reader that there is no such thing as a distant future in – reality!

Space and time, belonging together polarly, have this in common, that each is stretched between the poles nearness and distance. Now, as certainly as spatial nearness is only one (and indeed essentially = loadability), regardless of where I linger, and conversely spatial distance is only one, regardless of whether I have a view to east or west, north or south, so certainly in relation to the one and same temporal nearness can there be only one temporal distance! If there were two of them, namely besides the distance of the past yet a distance of the future, then the distant character of the vanishing point lying before me would be somehow specifically opposed to the distant character of the vanishing point lying behind me. If now undeniably the opposite is the case, then the duality of temporal distance was invented, and one of the two must be a – figment of the imagination! But we must consider the future as such for the following reason. If I remember the past, I remember a reality that was; if, on the other hand, I think of a future thing, I think something unreal, more precisely, a state of a!airs that has existence only in being thought! If all thinking beings vanished in an instant, what it was before, the really occurred past, would nevertheless remain exactly the same; whereas the name “future” would absolutely lose its meaning as soon as there were a lack of beings with the thought of the future! Future relates to past not like a distance of time to an opposite distance of time, but like a mere concept to reality or: future is not a property of real time!

Perhaps one objects: only the present happening is real. But as this was co-determined by the past happening, so too does it itself again form the precondition of future happenings. Don’t numerous provisioning drives leave no doubt that even the animal state of life is just as intimately connected with the future as with the past; (example perhaps: nest-building of birds for mating season!). Therefore, whoever grants reality to the past should not want to deny it to the future. To this we would have to reply: one thing is the necessity in the progression of happening and another the mental anticipation of a not yet existing present; and again this is by no means on the same level as the thought through which I refer to something really past! Without weighing certainty values against each other, however, everyone must recognize and acknowledge: there is no event set in the future of which it could not be thought that it might not occur instead of occurring! If the astronomer predicts the lunar eclipse to the fraction of the second, we do not doubt, it will occur and precisely at the predetermined point in time; but it would not be unthinkable that, before it happened, the entire visible universe for us shattered into atoms! Unthinkable, on the other hand, would it be that the lunar eclipse that took place yesterday simultaneously also did not take place. Whoever this does not yet find clear from physical examples, let him choose some from the realm of life. We can hope our friend might survive a dangerous undertaking, we can fear he will not; but we have nothing more to hope and nothing to fear for him after he perished in it. But if accordingly the future is populated by the shadow-play of human fears and wishes, then it is itself something unreal, merely thought, a – figment of the imagination. If, conversely, even the most passionate wishes must powerlessly shatter against what has irrevocably happened, then the past proves itself filled with reality to the brim; past and present and not past and future are the poles of time and thus temporal distance coincides entirely with past-distance. “Nature is purely past” (Novalis). Linguistically, then, “fern” (cognate with “firn” [ancient]) was originally used solely for the past and downright synonymous with “formerly, once, old”!

The Promethean humanity, which immediately preceded the historical one, elevated the future to the same level of reality as the past, to which we will return; the Heraclitean one of “world history” slays and slays with the figment “future” the reality of what has been, cores out the moment by annihilating its content of pastness and tears apart the fertilizing connection of nearness with distance, to put in its place the Ahasverus-like driving relatedness of the present to that specter of distance called future. It will in the end have completely lost its past, but in the same moment also itself annihilated, because made un-present; for only in images and that is to say in images of what has been does time realize itself and appear. The “Eschatologies” and “Apocalypses” give the most terrible expression to the insanity calling itself “History.” But of that the indispensable later and here only a few more words about the “before and behind” us mentioned above.

Reality is eternal and real time the pulsation of eternity, according to which every present throws to the just approaching moment a just slipping away one of the past. In relation to the bridging arch of the Now hovering over it, time becomes a stream with the direction out of the future and into the past. If we turn to that side, every moment seems to us to be arriving – “künftig” [future] is linguistically the same as “kommend” [coming] –, but if to this side, then to be departing. The vital enabling ground of the concept-pair past-future lies in the fact that the each momentary present can be experienced both as arriving and as slipping away, departing, passing time. Since we normally do not walk backwards and never do so in the long run, the extension forward is the direction in which we stride or would stride, the extension backward the direction in which we stride away. If, striding, we fix any distant point before us in our eye, it comes closer and closer to us, whereas in equal measure the distance grows between us and the distant point behind us. The former is an arriving, the latter a disappearing, finally passing state of a!airs, the former the symbol of the approaching, the latter of the slipping away moment; (and likewise, as everyone sees, the sunrise relates to the sunset). But the distance remains thereby one and always the same. For it is not it we reached when we reached the distant point, which through being reached rather immediately became nearness, utmost nearness. Because we cannot finally reverse the direction of time and from what just happened unremittingly remove ourselves ever further, while every moment truthfully thought as future at some point arrives and becomes present, so in the real distance-light can stand only what has been. It only remains to explain why the future too can sometimes present itself with the appearance of distance, as in that Eichendor! stanza.

The answer is: the soul of the beheld distant image is always past-distance; but as thinking beings we are capable of carrying this into the extension of space lying before us: future is past projected forward! This holds even for the (actually timeless) futureconcept of the natural sciences. No future calculation whose entire material does not flow from conceptual interpretation of the past! If the astronomer predetermines the time of the lunar eclipse exactly, he does this not by virtue of a mere rule of probability, which would rely on recorded time intervals of previous lunar eclipses; but the forces and orbital elements and revolution periods that he uses as calculation units are one and all constructed from the observation of already occurred movements of the stars. And enough of this.

To ascertain the essence of ecstasy, we had to ascertain the essence of vision and for that purpose most definitely distinguish the distant character of the seeable image from the near character of the perceptual thing. But everything said about it would have remained misunderstood fragmentariness without metaphysical enlightenment about the essence of distance, which we have now discovered to be in itself pastness, even if it appears in the distance of space. Accordingly, the archetypes are images of what has been (whence the thought of anamnesis flowed into Platonic erotics), and ecstatic vision is directed, in contrast to the act of perception, towards the absolutely untouchable reality of the past! Whether the space-image is geocentric or “infinite”: therein proves itself as genuine the Eros of Distance, that it lights up in the envisioning of the prehistoric world! – In the mirror of the doctrine of essence, the near is sober and sobering, only the distance is intoxicating, the present is near, only the past is distant, and of the state of vision the extra-rational sense: that it “raptures” into the “not to be entered,” into the motherworld of what has been or else brings back the “spirits” of those long since departed! No Plato, no Plotinus, no Schopenhauer fathomed this! Seduced by the will-o’-the-wisp of the Logos, the philosophers exchanged the eternal with the timeless limit of time or else with the delusional thought of a never-ending duration, hoped to extort with the understanding what unasked gives itself alone to the sacrificially pious mind, and sought, transgressing against the wonder-image of a universe that also bore them, beyond it, as if it were a second world of objects, but more comfortably furnished, that of which only the appearance shines for the seer, namely in the night-blue of past-distance. But among the poets, many knew it, so too Goethe.

Sink then! I could also say: rise! It’s all the same. Flee what has originated Into the spaces unbound by formations! Delight in what is long no longer present. What once was, in all splendor and shine It stirs there; for it wants to be eternal. And you distribute it, almighty powers To the tent of day, to the vault of nights.

And whoever now reached “into the unbesought, not to be entreated,” be it through “grace,” be it by magical power, who, self-escaped, transformed became one with the pulsebeat of eternity, he accomplishes and completes perhaps even the venture of necromancy:

And should I not, most longing power, Draw into life the most unique form?

Even if he, like Faust, also had to confess:

I scarcely breathe, my word trembles, halts; It is a dream, vanished day and place.

According to the essential identity of temporal and spatial nearness (and accordingly also of Here and Now), “Gegenwart” [present] formerly meant “Anwesenheit” [presence] and is still used today in the same sense when we say of someone that he is “gegenwärtig” [present]. Now if vision is anyway a kind of making present, but the seeable distance is an absent one, then we characterize the distinguishing essential particularity of the former with “making present of the absent” or still more sharply with “making present of what has been” and thus hold in our hands the key to a death cult once spread over the whole earth, which has completely slipped away from the average consciousness of today. As we find in it first color-saturated completed and distance-intuitively deepened our outline drawing of the state of vision, so in turn this helps us to understand both the original meaning of Pelasgian ancestor worship and especially, by virtue of which confusion the enduring initiations gave the initiate certainty of “immortality.” Not, however, by stringing prehistoric ancestor service customs onto the spun thread of thought, but more immediately do we want to provide evidence for this by considering them from the viewpoint of the Eros for the Formerly; for which we must not fail to adduce many facts that are long familiar to every expert; (for acquaintance with saga research and ethnology is limited in our practical time to a sidelined minority of enthusiasts and scholars!). But let the transition be formed by the reference to a state of a!airs not to be touched upon further, which for the last time illuminates the essence of distance and incidentally, as it were shadow-casting, lights up why the Eros we are dealing with is “cosmic” Eros.

Everything that is spatially distant, we saw, can move into nearness, unconditionally excepted alone the stars! Though our eye with the aid of ground lenses penetrate yet millennia of light deeper into the chasms of space and in the darkroom catch the image of myriads of stars at sky-places where before only lightless emptiness spanned, though we trace exactly to the measurability of their heights the ring-walls of the moon’s craters, chemically analyze the substances of the suns and burning vapors of other worlds: we have as before “present” always only the appearance of the stars, never their (merely inferred) corporeality! Therefore: the stars “one does not desire them,” and their “presence,” if a joke is permitted to illuminate the paradox beyond all surmise, “shines through absence”! – Could accordingly no more convincing symbol of the Formerly be devised than the starry splendor of the nocturnal firmament, then we understand not only the shudder of sublimity that breathes through every world-unlocked beholder at the sight of the sparkling vault, but we also understand why to the original sense the stars were sometimes souls of the departed, sometimes their dwelling place, but always on earth past choirs of the prehistoric world shining in the imperishable, to which “all earthly change remained bound.” As certainly as a Bachofen is right with the remark that the statement of Aristotle in his Metaphysics, “the circuit of the planets produces all appearances,” is a “primal thought of humanity, which completely dominated the mode of intuition of the ancient world”, so certainly but because the distant images of the stars were then powerful daemons and the daemons in turn “spirits” of a primordially ancient prehistoric world. – Their no less archaic underworldliness, according to which they dwell in the interior of the earth, does not contradict this! For apart from the fact that the original “Uranism” stood polarly opposed to “Chthonism” and by no means hostilely, the delusion of spiritualization seized in the beginning not at all the nocturnal stars, but the day and the sun culminating in it; which is why it is still spoken entirely from the inwardness of a humanity that, though already de-deified, still knew itself near to gods, when on one of the Orphic gold tablets the soul of the initiate testifies of itself: “I am a child of Earth and of the star-bearing Heaven.” The examples, which we almost indiscriminately pick out from a multitude partly from antiquity, partly from the “savages,” leave no doubt that star worship everywhere stands under the sign of the Eros for the Formerly, and teach us incontrovertibly how much more strongly the envisioning man was determined by the untouchable distance of images than by the nearness of tangible bodies.

No doctrine of expediency has been able to explain to us why in the customs and sagas of such backward Pelasgian peoples as we undoubtedly have in some of today’s Negro tribes, in the Bushmen, Botocudos, Fuegians, Puris, Proto-Australians, alongside the symbolism of, say, hunting spear, harpoon, awl, cooking stone, gourd bottle, of jackal, owl, toad, fox or bear, of tattooing, incantation, harmful magic, of women’s monthly rule, mating, childbirth and what similar “all-too-human” near occurrences there are, yet without exception the distant images of the stars also play a role far beyond mere fear interests, although not in astronomical outline; and especially the completely disproportionately advanced star lore, in whose possession we find the so-to-speak genius Pelasgian peoples of the Chinese, Akkadians, Assyrians, Egyptians, Incas, Aztecs at the beginning of their historically accessible culture, should – in contrast to the star-stupor of today’s European – instantly assure an unbiased mind that we must once and for all renounce finding the thought-impulses of mythical search in the spirit familiar to us of expanding and preserving self-interest. The chiefs of the former Virginians lived on in the land “beyond the sunset.” Right there in the color-intoxicated air-sea of the evening west lay for the Greeks the “Island of the Blessed.” The Polynesians of Tokelau saw in the moon the dwelling of their deceased kings. Plutarch in his treatise “On the Face Appearing in the Moon-Disc” holds all stars to be “gods appearing in the sky” and especially the moon for the region of Persephone and the “element of souls.” “For they are dissolved into it as the bodies of the dead are into the earth.” On the earth-averted and sky-turned side lay the “Elysian plain.” The North American Winnebagos called the Milky Way the “Path of the Dead.” In the sky the souls of the Greenlanders pitch their tents, there where the rainbow arches. According to the belief of the Eskimos, all stars were in grey prehistory people or animals. In Orion’s belt they saw a band of seal hunters who missed their way home. The natives of Van Diemen’s Land used to show the stranger in clear nights their Dioscuri pair of two stars that brought fire to their ancestors. The Pharaohs, the Sassanid kings, the Roman Caesars called themselves “Brothers of the Sun and the Moon” and returned after their death to the starry sky whence they had descended. A comet at Caesar’s death was interpreted as his soul; as indeed with almost all peoples of the earth unexpectedly appearing stars and especially shooting stars are threatening or felicitous souls. Pliny speaks of the societas coeli nobiscum [the fellowship of the sky with us]!

The Eros for the Formerly is Eros of Distance. But the latter, because kindled on incorporeal images, puts the seer in connection with the untouchable world of the stars, thus pulsating through the earthly life of those still present in the light with the rhythm of the universe; therefore its bearers were literally “cosmic” beings and what they called themselves in their highest initiates: Sons of the Sun. The reflection of this in the consciousness of the knowing ones shines towards us from a late grave inscription of antiquity which – though it already announces the poles split and the turning away of the upper world from the lower – nevertheless with the closing verse radiantly expresses the ancient blissful shudder of that star kinship foreign to us:

But in two hosts are separated the souls of the dead, One, which restlessly roves about on the earth, the other, Which begins the round dance with the luminous heavenly stars. To this host am I joined, for the god was my guide.

Become foreign to us; but again and again flashing up in the high moments of creative ingeniums, as long as there still are such in a withering humanity! The same state of consciousness that formed the Orphic saying let a Nietzsche devise the word of “love for the most distant” and gave him the verses:

Predestined for the star’s course, What matters to you, star, the darkness?

“Stellar courses” cost more today than formerly! They are the courses of those who, a sacrifice, fall, a sacrifice on unknown altars and hopeless. So too fell Nietzsche, doubly judged: by a humanity that expelled him (though it outwardly courted him) and by his own participation in it. Nonetheless: he too went to the stars, a comet probably never returning.

The modern ethnologist is inclined to derive the soul-cult of primordial times from a fear of ghosts, thus making it understandable why it had to vanish the more the belief in "spirits" faded before the "light" of reason. But now, unquestionably, erotic practices stand alongside the fear-based customs of the death cult, and they shine forth all the more brightly the closer we approach its oldest layer. We, too, still know the wreath as the crown (which indeed derives its name from it), and to crown someone, even for us, means to confer the highest consecration upon him. Wreath = crown is the ancient symbol of completion, of the Teletä, of achieved perfection. Every crown is a "crown of life" or a sign of the Gamos of the poles of life, the consummated marriage of a crowned soul with the soul of the universe. Therefore, the wreath or the fillet adorns the initiate (Myste), therefore the victor, the ruler, the priest, therefore the bride! But now we know that the Mycenaean also adorned his dead with wreath and fillet! The dead had thus attained the highest consecration for him; he had become for him a Myste, a hero, indeed a demon, and the process of dying coincided with the consummation of the completing Gamos! Highly revered as "Tritopatoren" and themselves bestowing blessings, the ancestral spirits are present at every wedding. Libations are o!ered to them; the physical wedding of the bridal pair is placed under the protection of their consecrating power. Romulus, the deified progenitor of the Roman Empire, was the protector of infants and made weak children healthy. In exactly the same way, still in Christian times, Saints Cosmas and Damian, after their death, performed the o"ce of protecting and healing children as two ancestral spirits and heirs of Aesculapius. Could there be stronger testimonies that the source of the death cult was Eros and the underlying "belief" the intimately felt certainty of their still present proximity! Or: we, too, still know the color purple as the symbol of the highest dignity. We do not ask here what the symbolism of purple is based on, and only point out that the Mycenaean loved to wrap his dead in purple cloth and bed them on olive, myrtle, and poplar leaves for their final rest. The fear-based customs are only a late side-phenomenon of the sympathetic customs spread throughout all of Pelasgian culture.

Nothing occupies the man of the Pelasgian stage more than the solemnity of burial and the care for the corpse. The most humanly moving tragedy of all antiquity celebrates the self-sacrifice of Antigone in the service of the sacred customs for the corpse of her fallen brother, a motif without equal if we measure it against the poetry of modern times! The original interment most probably took place in the house under the hearth, furthermore in the midst of the village, before the walls, in the city gate, in the marketplace, in the Prytaneum, on the festival grounds! Thus, at Olympia, the tomb of Pelops was next to the great ash-altar of Zeus, and the temples were simultaneously burial sites of a demon. (Example: the Delphic Apollo temple over the grave of the earth-god Python.) Christendom, too, formerly used to bury the dead of noble families in the cathedrals and minsters, and still today in villages gathers their graves in the protected space of the churches. The mightiest structures of the Egyptians, the pyramids, are places of the dead, as are the rock temples of the Lycians and Rome’s catacombs.

"Greater care," reports Diodorus of the Egyptians (according to Bachofen’s translation), "is devoted to the dwellings of the dead than to those of the living; the latter they regard as an inn for a short, passing sojourn, the graves alone as the true and lasting abodes for eternal times." Sacred groves, the sanctified mountains of so many peoples, the Manitou stones of the Indians, the pagodas and stupas of the Indians were no less seats of the dead. The soul of the dead flutters and hovers around the grave, dwells there in serpent form, resides as a genius loci, as an Agathodaimon, blessing the house of the living! The entire Roman cult originates from the veneration of domestic ancestral spirits, the Lares, as does the Shintoism of the Japanese. The tribes of antiquity and likewise all "savages" were and are fond of naming themselves after a mythical progenitor. In honor of the death of celebrated heroes of the past arose the Greek Agon, symbolizing the cycle of becoming and passing away! Who could fail to recognize that here, not ghosts are feared and warded o!, but that it is the loving veneration of the living that courts the reciprocal love of elevated beings of the past, "drawing them into life" again and again with customs, partly of immediate comprehensibility, partly of mysterious depth of meaning! What finally could assure us more irrefutably of this than the moving custom of late Greek times to have the heroized dead appear on grave reliefs in the very form of Eros himself, sometimes sleeping and with lowered torch, sometimes as if gently wearied from the festival of life, crowned, reclining on a resting couch and with the bowl from the drinking party! But not "immortality" had the dead of prehistory gained in such a way, but, as not dead at all, he was present, transformed!

Only the spiritual mode of apprehension, for which dying means the annihilation of existence, has produced a formerly incomprehensible horror of death and with it the passionate desire for immortality. Primordial man, on the other hand, for whom the merely existing world still lay shadowily concealed behind the soul’s far more vivid images, knew neither of the two. He was still far too little a person for the thought of existence to have been able to enslave him, even in relation to his own self, nearly as it does modern man, and to determine his customs. But as certainly as everything, be it thing or human, for him only "existed" as it were incidentally, so certainly for him not only the human lived, but also the so-called dead thing, indeed the "matter" of which it consists a word which, one should not forget, comes from mater mother, thus the symbol of the primal source! For him, too, dying was certainly a mighty event, only not an annihilation of existence, but a liberating transformation of life. One did not "die" then in our sense, because there was still nothing essentially dead in our sense!

To understand the connection that exists between the state of vision and the perpetuation of life, one must first have deeply immersed oneself in the unbridgeable chasm that separates the death conception of primordial times from the death conception of the present. Therefore, we will show the latter once in the light of the ancient world. Every genuine adherent of the spiritual religion of Christianity believes in a personal immortality of the soul. But if now a peasant from his congregation were to come to a Protestant or Catholic pastor to tell him that it was haunted at his grandfather’s grave, that his soul was walking there, the "pastor" would instruct him that the grandfather’s soul was in the "beyond" or in "heaven" and thus could impossibly still be walking about anywhere. Thus, the belief in immortality pushes the souls out of the world, makes them from "mighty souls" into "poor souls" and devalues the entirely opposite soul-belief into "superstition." The Catholic preserves in the last rites, the reading of masses, and the feast of All Souls remnants of ancient care for the dead. For the Protestant, i.e., the perfected Christian, dust returns to dust, spirit to spirit, while the soul loses the Here along with the There and finally the last foot of homeland. Located neither on earth nor on one of the myriad stars, it does not dwell in space at all, but in the Not-Here, at the "supracelestial place" in the Beyond. Consequently, what stood at the center of the soul-service of the ancient world becomes arbitrarily a matter of a soft "piety": the care for the corpse, for the grave, for the ashes! The art and culture of the ancient world thrived on the soil of grave cults because they knew no immortality of the soul, but instead its enduring presence; the entirety of immortality-believing Christendom sees in the corpse merely a carcass, in the grave a place that is plowed again after thirty to forty years for useful purposes, in the once-existent ancestor a completely powerless, because dead, being, which no longer interferes in any way in the interest struggles of the living. To relegate the soul as immortal into the beyond means to rob it of its feminine homeland, to make it a "departed" and thus null and void soul! Certainly, then, it was not the Christian in him, but the pagan Myste, who dictated these verses to the poet’s pen:

We dead, we dead are greater hosts

Than you on earth, than you on the seas!

We plowed the field with patient deeds,

You swing the sickles and cut the seeds,

And what you completed and what we began,

It still fills the rushing fountains up there,

And all our loving and hating and strife,

It still pulses up there in mortal veins,

And what we found of valid truths,

On that all earthly change remains bound,

And our tones, forms, poems

Win the laurel in mortal light,

We still seek human goals

Therefore honor and sacrifice! For we are many!

"That African chieftain," says a meritorious ethnologist, Lippert, "who removed himself from the company of a traveler because a headache reminded him that he had fallen behind in the care of his father’s soul, expresses the view of an entire primordial age." The fact is correct, only unfortunately our researcher omits to interpret it. He is concerned with nothing but the circumstance that that chieftain must have feared the spirit of his father. We ask, however: how could his thought leap suddenly from the pain in his head to a not-at-all-present ancestral soul? Would it not have been far more natural to think of the rays of the equatorial sun, which were presumably burning on his crown, and to see in them the cause of the headache? Yes, must not such a procedure be followed by a being so believing in the present as our chieftain certainly was? Mere thoughts, no doubt, can certainly determine a modern European, but not at all a sensual Negro. If he nevertheless leaped over the sensual present and sought the source of his pain in the spectral ancestor, this means that the ancestor had become more palpably feelable to him through gripping presence than even the piercing sun rays! Not the "remembrance" of an ancestral soul to be venerated, but its phantasma took possession of his mind. His animal feeling of fear and his primitive will to knowledge require no explanation: the rationally inaccessible How of his interpretation shows, on the other hand, incontrovertibly that he, even waking, was connected with a reality of dream-formed images. One cannot avoid admitting this, even if one fundamentally denies any claim to reality to a phantasma.

Saga researchers who derive the entire soul-belief of the ancient world from dream life are, after all, a little closer to the truth than the representatives of the opinion of its origin from the perception of dying. For we, who are not poets, can of course only try to reflect on this by contemplating our dreams: that for primordial man souls were sensually present just as perceptible things are for us. Whoever cannot even comprehend that should renounce interpreting early times! But we would again err if we thought to understand the state of consciousness of prehistory from the assumption of richer dreams and a more vivid thinking-along. It is not so much about nocturnal dreams, but rather that the wakefulness of man of yore can be compared far more to our dreaming than to our waking, without, however, coinciding with the state of sleep.

We have repeatedly expressed that vision transforms the visionary; which obviously indicates the extreme contrast to the act of perception, which separates the perceiver from the perceived thing and first truly assures him of limited being-for-itself! We now reproduce from the fragment of our work on "dream consciousness" published years ago some sentences that attempt to convey from common dream experience the positive essential characteristic of images, namely their transformability, and are thereby apt to lead over unawares into the reality-consciousness of the visionary, which usually bewilders us to the point of speechlessness. It was prefaced that there are also dreamy moods in waking, and that one of their characteristics consists in the feeling of the fleetingness of what is experienced. Thereupon it says: "Connecting with the third point, the drawing fleetingness of the real for the dreamily disposed, we come upon a property of the dream that has been underlined by all observers, but, as it seems to us, has not been measured to the end: the restless mutability of all dream formations. The street on which I was just driving in the dream has transformed in the next moment into a canal, the carriage into a ship, and already the house walls have receded and I am sailing among many ships in the harbor; and I am not astonished and have no feeling of a lack of connection in the sequence of events. If we earlier derived from the fleetingness of the dreamily real the preference of mythical thinking for the cloud-like plastic atmospharilien, we now find the basis for this mood-moment in the ability of every dream phenomenon to transform itself into any other, and for the mythos the motive of its selection in the compulsion to know gods, demons, fairies, yews and other spirit beings equipped with the same gift. For it is common to all of them to both exchange their own shape and to transform enemy or friend, things, animals or humans. Ovid grasped with a lucky stroke the core of the whole realm of myth and fairy tale when he chose the stopping points of his poetic traversal of it from the viewpoint of the ‘Metamorphoses,’ although he did not succeed in transferring the basic phenomenon into the language of literature. We also understand at least one element of the generating state of a!airs from the feeling of transitoriness (namely of the dreamily disposed person) in the experience of the dreamer, suddenly believing himself transformed, and we see in this a support for our assumption of the relation between the dream event and precisely the ‘soul.’ For what the rulers of the dream, the gods, are capable of, that must be able to befall the dreaming soul, willingly or unwillingly: to swarm out into the entire multiformity of things and, dwelling within, to become one with stones, plants, animals, humans, and tools. Especially according to this logic of dream-thinking, which in many places condensed into the dogma of the transmigration of souls, the dream-bound spirit knows in principle nothing inanimate and merely mechanical, animals, plants and ‘dead’ things ‘speak’ to it, it shares at every moment the fate of the world surrounding it! And again it is the compulsion to choose also the materially changeable that determined the typical forms of the soul: the billowing breath, the flickering light and the licking flame (in German legend as fire-men, will-o’-the-wisps, witch torches), the water of springs and rivers, from which prophesying voices now murmur, the glimmering mote of sunlight; among animals, however, the flying or silently slipping away, thus especially the bird, the fish and the snake, the fluttering butterfly, the restless bee, the hunting wolf, the nimble weasel, the hurrying mouse, which so often runs out of the mouth of the sleeper when the soul sets out on its migration. To bring the principle of transformation and with it the souls into the power of the day-wake spirit is the ancient goal of all theurgists, spirit-conjurers and necromancers and one of the proto-ethnic roots of mysticism, whose primal form, untainted by any swindle of spiritualization, we can grasp in view of, for instance, the self-transformation of Germanic heroes into werewolves and berserkers, of the Ceylonese devil dancers into demons, of the ancient Thracian Sabos mystes into the swarming thiasos of the raging bull-god. Greek mythos and not it alone has itself seen it again in the form of a god and, as is understood, a water demon, namely that of the seal-friendly sea-old man Proteus, of whom the Odyssey tells that he, seized by Menelaus and his fur-clad companions, immediately transformed into a lion, and as they did not let go, in turn into a leopard, dragon, boar, into flowing water, into a tree towering cloud-high, before, defeated, he divulged his infallible foreknowledge of the future. After him, as the classical demon of mythical transformation art, we aptly attribute protean character to the reality of the dream."

If we compared the waking state of the visionary man to our dream state above, the preceding sentences already let us guess much of the tremendous change in apprehension which thereby his entire view of life, his holding-for-true, thinking and acting underwent. "It hardly allows any comparison with what we understand by ‘belief,’ which is a belief in tangible facts, if, for example, the Vedic hymn thinks of the fire demon Agni as originating from heaven, from the water, in the forests, in the stone, in the plant, through the Gamos of the fire-sticks, in the ‘womb of butter’; yet again lets him be fetched down from on high by a prehistoric hero, like the Promethean fire; if it calls him now a bull, a light-red steed, now the ‘shining tongue of the gods’ or the mouth with which they consume the burnt o!ering. One is tempted to consider it nothing but wild nature poetry when the thunderstorm spirits, the Maruts, chatteringly rush along, milk the heavenly udder and fill the earth with milk, blow the pipe of the storm, plunder the sea of clouds like the caterpillar the tree, break the forests in elephantine fashion and approach with ‘lion’s thunder,’ so that the earth reels like a ship. Now we have certainly already considered what is involved with genuine poetry. Meanwhile we do not forget that all spirit beings are not only praised but also venerated with abundant sacrifices, appeased and drawn near by a ritual that spans the whole of human life with ceremonies and demands, and that the meaning of the seemingly only decorative, in truth unfolding images largely recurs in widespread magical practices, all of which would obviously be senseless without the belief in their reality. In vain one attempts to understand the surges of the dream-sense from the perspective of waking consciousness, for which one’s own sensing can congeal into an event, while this event evaporates in sounding images." Even in the fairy tale, the childish o!shoot of visionary life, whether it stems from India or Arabia, from Oceania or from Germany, the event is completely removed from the factual realm of our peculiar wakefulness by the secret momentum inherent in all objects and beings for lightning-fast exchange of forms; according to which fortunate gold comes from rotten leaves, a handsome prince from the ugly frog, and the murdered man rises more beautifully anew from the defleshed bones! The Indian would never call his totem animal, say the wolf, the "first man," had he not been able to experience, thanks to ecstatic vision, and really experienced at some time, that a wolf transforms into a man or a man transforms into a wolf! The heart-nerve of visionary thinking is the experienced certainty of the transformability of the world of appearance.

Under the concept of a transformation falls, accordingly, also dying. And indeed, following the dream logic of the primal state to the end, we can easily determine what role the dead plays in relation to the living. Whoever was graced to "travel out" into the inviolable distance by means of ecstatic vision, experiences, having come to himself again, only now as a soul-fetter the eternal hereness of the tangible body. His soul, flown away like a bird, was pulled back by invisible threads to the fettering place and into the bounded nearness. If it tore those threads, then instead of limited freedom and revocable transformation, the certainly deeply problematic fate of released wandering and unrestricted transformability would be its lot, promising cosmic fullness of life as much as threatening with pathless errancy. But they really tear in death, since then undoubtedly the soul left the corpse; delivered up to infinite possibility of self-transformation in the totality of space. Herein lies primarily its demonic power; from this, however, as we will see, also grows its danger of losing itself through alienation. Utilizing the turn of phrase of the philosopher Plotinus about matter as the "receptacle of images," we can formulate: the soul of the presently living is an image bound to matter, the dead soul is the image released from matter. Having become thoroughly image, it has entirely the reality-form of the dream phenomenon, transforms itself, comes and vanishes, unhindered by material barriers.

Who indeed appears as an avenging spirit to the transgressor Don Juan? The statue of the murdered governor! In this, too, we hear the echo of the belief of an entire ancient world! And thus Pindar, powerful in speech, proclaims it with immortal word: "The body follows death, the all-powerful; but the image of the living remains alive; it sleeps, however (this Eidolon), when the limbs are active; but to the sleeper in the dream it often shows the future." That is the key to the soul-service! That avenging statue had many like-minded ones in antiquity. Thus we are told that the statue of Theagenes, which a mocker in arrogance had dared to whip, bided its time; and when once the transgressor passed by in closest proximity, it fell over and killed him. Far from being torn from the world, into the invisibility of the beyond, the dead soul is rather a demonically living image, now of a star, stone, tree or animal, now of a spring, a river, the passing air current, then again the likeness of its formerly corporeal bearer. The belief of ancient times does not read: there are invisible souls or even a personal continued existence; rather it reads: the images of things are alive, and something lived all the more free of fetters, it assumed all the more the character of the magically transformable soul, the more it became image and nothing but this!

Only in passing we remind: not only Greeks and Romans used the word for "shadow" (Greek skia, Latin umbra: "tumulum circumvolat umbra"!) as furthermore (we heard of it already) also eidolon conceptually identical with "soul," but countless "savages" still do so today: thus the (now certainly extinct) Tasmanians, thus the Algonquin Indians, Quiché, Arawaks, Abipones, Zulus, Basutos, Greenlanders and so forth. The thousandfold magic of the mirror, the conjuring and divinatory arts by means of reflecting surfaces, the world-wide harmful magic using symbolically conceived objects, the doctrine of signatures, astrology, all that becomes understandable only from the ancient essential equation of soul and image. The Macusi Indians in Guiana say that after the decay of the body the "man in our eyes" remains. The Fiji Islanders distinguish the "dark spirit" and the "light spirit": the former is the shadow of the man, the latter his image in the water. According to European folk belief, the sick person was bewitched or doomed to death if the image in the pupil (= little doll) disappeared. Likewise, whoever had lost his shadow fell to the subterranean powers; for only the soul-images stripped of matter are shadowless!

And here we reflect on what the temples originally were! Not, like the Christian cathedrals, spaces of devotion for the congregation, but houses of the gods and accordingly such places where reverent piety was able to behold them and really beheld them at the sight of their "idols"! So much was for the pagan the essence of demonic powers identical with the ability to appear, that the most popular etymology derived (Theion), the divine, from (Theaton), the visible! Atheoi, despisers of gods, he therefore called the Jews and Christians, and indeed undeniably with right: for they had elevated to the god-denying god the spirit, the invisible principle of the image-hating will! Early Christianity knew exactly about this, as the following passage from the declamatory zealot Augustine from his book on the City of God proves: "Thus the demons, as shown by magicians, philosophers and by Plato, are enclosed by consecration in statues or images and receive through inspiration a power equal to that of a present deity, at times inspiring priests, inhabiting temples, animating the fibers of the entrails, directing the flight of birds, guiding the fall of lots and giving oracles ... And these are the madmen whom you see raging in the public squares; and even the priests become mad from it outside the temples, rage and whirl in circles ..." But what holds for the souls of the gods, holds to a lesser degree for the nearer souls of departed friends and relatives; for the gods are themselves only ancestral souls, from another round of humanity to be sure, and thanks to the nature of vision, all the more radiantly outshining the present, the more the distance of the past from which they shine seems to grow. In his "Remains of Arabic Paganism," Wellhausen reports from the Orient how images were made of several deceased, so that now each could visit his brother, cousin or father; in the following generation the veneration of the images rose still higher, the third, however, worshipped them!

In the words of Augustine, to which numerous similar ones could be added, already resonates that only too understandable doubt which in modern times condensed for ethnology into the still unsolved problem of whether statue, idol and "fetish" are themselves demonic beings or only their seats. They are in reality both simultaneously: as tangible bodies, seats of the soul, as only visible images, the souls themselves. The images, flashing up in the event of vision, come and go, transform themselves, can never be grasped and held. The corporeally touchable place is in relation to them at best their site. They can leave it, because mobile beyond all concepts; and they can be present at it without the human necessarily noticing them. Not everyone recognizes the secret signs of their presence, and only the ecstatically enraptured receives their breath, overflowing with a rippling shudder. But when sacrificing piety vitalizes the local symbol with captivating essence, then it was opened to the settling soul, which momentarily transforms the afterimage, dissolving it in the appearance of the god.

As already indicated above, danger threatens the wandering soul of being driven about from expanse to expanse, from transformation to transformation, finally of derealization through loss of the capacity for taking form. "Journey of shapes" was what the Icelanders called the journeying forth of souls. It gained as homeland the space spanning cosmic distances, but forfeited the homeland of the place, the intimately and warmly uniting nearness. Now space and place, distance and nearness, heaven and earth, wandering and clinging, periphery and center belong to each other polarly. Separated from the revolution of the firmaments, the telluric withers; unanchored in the core of the earth, the sidereal evaporates. If the former for its flora needs the fertilizing storm of past images, reverent fervor and gift-willing sacrificial sense of the corporeally present in the light preserve the dream-demonic ancestral souls from dissipating into nocturnal gulfs of space. Therefore spoken in parable like thirsty wanderers, the ones who were yearn for a flower-embowered and preserved as holy site, and those, in turn, ignited by the Eros of distance, prepare for them and seal with recalling signs grave or temple, grove or pillar, tree or stone pyramid, urn or cave, statue or Lar figure: that it may be the favorite seat and secret resting place of the soul, which is indeed not banished from space, but without the bedding love of those still protected by the body is released to place-less wandering. If perhaps a warrior had fallen in the distance or someone had su!ered shipwreck at sea, his relatives erected on land a "cenotaph," an empty grave: the protected site where the image of the dead was implored to enter! Indissolubly the web of Eros spins between the images of the past and the genii of the present.

All death customs, symbols and soul legends unanimously point to the fact that in the Pelasgian age there was still widespread among the people what a later time attained primarily by way of initiations: ecstatic vision, through which the soul, freed from the fetters of the body, unites with the divine. The ecstatic vision is not a mere hallucination but a profound experience of the reality of images, where the soul of the living merges with the soul of the dead, the human with the divine, in a mystical marriage. The act of sacrifice, central to these customs, is not a mere o!ering of material goods but a symbolic act of communion, where the image of the divine is received into the soul of the sacrificer. But now one will object anyway that the custom of god-eating points precisely to the thoroughly material basis of the guiding conception. However striking it may remain that one considered the image of the real god made of dough or even only a piece of bread su"cient to become partaker of his soul by means of it, yet the taking possession happens through the process of eating, thus through incorporation of a body! To this we would have to reply: the concept of matter appears in a di!erent light if we see in it the substrate of material processes, and again in another if we see the support and the site of images, and the incorporation of matter has a di!erent sense, depending on whether it happens for the purpose of receiving the image adhering to it or for the purpose of nourishment. But we would, as already explained earlier, miss the character even of animal eating by deriving it from a nutritive drive. The hunger drive of the animal presupposes two things: the dependence of the animal body on the world in general and a reciprocal relation between the animal and a specifically determined segment of the world. The impression of the world-segment that stills hunger works on the life-bearer stimulatingly, enticingly, and the animal unites with it through the process of eating, for which the indication is a physical well-being. Insofar as something smellable, tastable, touchable always also has species-specific properties, besides the bodily sensory process that mediates the contact, smelling, tasting, touching also involve the psychic seeing; and it may be added with regard to earlier discussions that qualities cannot be touched at all, not even those we experience on the occasion of touching. Now if there is a particularity of connection between animal impressionability and edible substances, by virtue of which every species strives only for the nourishment appropriate to it, this can only be based on polar tension between the soul of the animal and the image of the substances, thus building the bridge to the state of a!airs emphasized at the outset, that every more highly developed individual being even shows choosy inclinations in eating and drinking, to the fulfillment of which more soulful joys are tied than the mere stilling of hunger could provide! And these can intensify to such an extent that the feeding appears as an expressive component of the marriage of the soul with the image of the food! Replace the purposive thought of food intake with the experience of the voluptuousness of tasting, and one is at least on the way to the conception of feeding as an aspect of the appearance of Eros. Whoever goes to the end of it will know of a kind of feeding that is experienced as polar connection of the feeder with the food and accordingly happens under ecstasy-like shudders! In such a case, however, not so much an edible substance would be devoured as rather, with the help of the substance, its image, its soul, its essence united with the feeder’s own body-soul, and the act of eating merely accompanied a rapturous surrender of the feeder to the food. "Thus we enjoy," says Novalis, "the genius of nature every day, and thus every meal becomes a memorial meal ..., a mysterious means of a transfiguration and deification on earth, a living intercourse with the absolutely living." Is this confirmed by cultic customs? We need only place side by side some well-known ones from ancient traditions of the Eleusinian, Orphic, Egyptian and related initiations to immediately recognize that in the epoptic ceremonies marriage-symbolic customs and the mystical feeding run interchangeably. We put the former first.

Concerning the Eleusinian mysteries, Bishop Asterius reveals: "Is there not the dark descent and the solemn coming together of the Hierophant and the Priestess, between him and her alone? Are not the torches extinguished and does not an innumerable multitude believe for their salvation what is performed in the darkness by the two?" According to other reports from Christian opponents, the Hierophant proclaimed: "A holy boy, Brimos, the sublime Brimo has borne, the Strong the Strong." De Jong points out that the nine-day mystery celebration of a secret society of the Hopi Indians in Arizona likewise concludes with the underground wedding between the "snake hero" and the "snake maiden." According to Clement, the phallus was hidden in the sacred chest in the Cabiric mysteries. That the soul was the receiving part and the deity the generating part is evident, among other things, from the declaration of the (blessed) Mystes on one of the gold tablets: "I have gone under the womb of the underworld goddess." While such rites and symbols leave no doubt about the nuptial nature of the vision, the following examples show us the same experience in the symbol of feeding. From the Attis cult, Firmicus Maternus testifies to the mystery formula: "I have eaten from the tympanum, drunk from the cymbal, I have carried the kernos (vessel with first fruits of the fields); I have learned the secrets; I have entered the bridal chamber and become a Mystes of Attis." Porphyry, in his work "On Abstinence," quotes from a lost work of Euripides the following words, addressed by the chorus to Minos: "I lead a holy life since I have become a Mystes of the Idaean Zeus and a herdsman of the nocturnal Zagreus. And after holding the torch high for the mountain mother Rhea and completing the meal of raw flesh, I have become a Kouretes, a Bakchios!" The so-called Eleusinian formula reads: "I have fasted, I have drunk the mixed drink, I have taken from the chest, and after performing that, I have placed it in the basket and back into the chest." (The god in the chest here is the snake.) Furthermore: "I, the young goat, have come to the milk." The significance of drinking from the mixing vessel, an essential initiation rite of by far most mystery services, is further illuminated for the Eleusinian mysteries by the fact of its transition into the cult legend: Demeter, mourning in Eleusis, refuses all nourishment; finally made to laugh by the jokes of a slave woman, she drinks the mixed drink of barley-grass, water, and mint. Likewise, in the Mithras cult, the initiation stages are distinguished precisely by the foods which the Mystes, as we may now well say, embraces on each. On the fourth stage, that of the "Lioness," he receives honey for the first time; on the fifth, that of "Perseus," he is fed with milk and honey. "Drink the milk and eat the honey before the sun rises, then you will behold the deity." Such evidence, undoubtedly suited to show mystical eating and drinking in the light of a marriage, however, lets us guess nothing of the inner shudders through which it would first be what it claims to be: conception of the god followed by image-birth. To become certain of this too, we turn from the priestly ordered to the orgiastic mysteries. The theme recurs multifariously in the Orphic god-poems: the primal deity, torn apart by a tragic deed, distributed itself into the world to finally arise anew from infinite fragmentation. Thus we hear of the scattering of the primal being Phanes; a remaining portion was appropriated by Zeus, who then, in serpent form, with Persephoneia, engendered Dionysos-Zagreus. Of him, it is further said that as a child he was outwitted by the Titans. They gave him the mirror in hand so that he would sink into his own sight; and now they fall upon him; he transforms into a lion, a dragon, finally a bull: this they tear apart and devour. The lightning of Zeus burns them to ashes: the human race that arose from this is thus partly Dionysian, partly Titanic in origin. The heart of the child, however, is saved by Athena. Zeus takes it into himself and with Semele begets the new Dionysos.

From such admittedly rather artificially composed sagas, scholarly science prefers to extract only what was unmistakably theological intention and, as it were, leads on winding paths into those (always spiritual) pantheisms which for the first time encouraged rational thinking in its battle of annihilation with the world of the senses and thus brought about the unique event of Greek philosophy. For the soul-constitution of prehistory, however, the motifs used therein are far more revealing: the transformation magic already discussed; furthermore, the self-contemplation, which we also encounter in the Narcissus saga and was a custom, among others, of the Corybantiasis; furthermore, the much-discussed mystery idea of rebirth; finally, the tearing apart of a demonic being, which immediately reminds us of the sagas of Orpheus, Osiris, Har, and many similar ones, including the repeated devouring in a twofold form. They, and only they, point back to experienced realities! And indeed, underlying the tearing apart and devouring in fact is the action of the orgiastic tearing apart of the god, as attested by pictorial representations and sober reports for the entire sphere of cultic excitation festivals. From the Greek Bacchic celebrations we know, and find it depicted on beautiful ancient reliefs, that snake-entwined Maenads tear apart goats. The community of Cretan Dionysian mystai tears apart with their teeth and devours, still bloody, an actual bull, raves about, and carries o! the heart in a chest. The holy Nilus (d. 430) reports to us of Bedouin tribes who, between the rising of the morning star and that of the sun, in orgiastic frenzy tear apart a camel and consume it still bloody. The tearing apart of at least a lamb is said to still be observed in Algeria on high holidays today! That the sacrificial animal, however, appeared to the ecstatically enraptured one as an image of the god himself, even the theogony we cited would reveal, even if it were not evident from original myths and especially from the circumstance that in the form of exactly the same images the, as we know, always male god unites with the woman, as the symbol of the surrendered human soul! In bull form, Bacchus strides before Pentheus transformed into a Maenad. Amid trumpet blasts, the Elean women call the god with the ox-foot from the waves:

! (axis toure! - ‘Hail, bull!’). Pasiphae, through Daedalian art, unites with the Poseidonic bull and gives birth to the Minotaur. Zeus, as a bull, abducts Europa. When the ray of the androgynous moon falls on a calving cow, the Apis bull, i.e., Apis himself, is born to the Egyptians. Precisely thanks to their gruesome aberration into the grossly tangible and grotesquely exaggerated, the ancient Mexican sacrificial customs finally express the guiding thought of the entire process with glaring clarity. For the greatest sacrificial festival of the Aztecs, the human destined for it was often selected a whole year in advance. The closer the time of his demise approached, the greater honors were shown to him, until finally he was worshipped as that very deity for which he was destined as a sacrifice. The participants in the likewise orgiastically proceeding sacrificial meal were again god-devourers, and their eating too is the expression of the entering of a god-soul into the thereby self-deifying mystai.

To the ecstatically seized one, the demon appears in the symbol of the goat, the bull, the serpent, a mixed being perhaps of man and bull, or also in human form; and the violent tearing apart and devouring is only the most unbridled way of his merging with him: taking him into himself, he simultaneously gives himself to him. Henceforth he goes, pregnant with the god, he is "Entheos" (, , numine afflatus), filled with or "possessed" by the god; is himself bull or goat; has become one with the soul of the universe, which, in glowing images of exuberant fullness, incessantly renews itself from the darkness of death. On the side of the Mystes, the devouring means reception of the god or conception; on the side of the demon, it means his outpouring into the souls of the mystai. With the tearing apart of Dionysos-Zagreus by the Titans in the Orphic doctrine of the gods and the subsequent rebirth, what in the Mystes are two pulses of his experience are distributed onto two mythical events: upon the god-eating as the god-conception follows the birth of the ensouled and ensouling image. We recall the verses of Goethe:

"Spread out to all regions

From this holy feast!

Inspired, tear yourselves through the nearest zones

Into the All and fill it out."

We were therefore right to bring the devouring drive to bear among the love drives in the first chapter, and it should shine forth from our examples how little, under the signs of the ecstatic wedding, physical embrace enjoys a preferential position!

Our side glance at the orgies, however, now seems to call for a counter-question!

If the league novice, through the sober-seeming action of drinking from the mixed drink, breaking bread, and similar things, indeed seemed to lack those dissolving shudders from which alone he could arise as "in aeternum renatus" (reborn forever), then conversely, the ecstatic tearing apart, whether performed on a human or even only on an animal, gives the impression of excess and terrifying wildness, and we involuntarily ask ourselves whether in such acts of destruction an experience must inevitably express itself, whose fulfilling sense we recognized as the vision of the reality of images. Could not for the quite elemental man the "ordinary" eating and drinking in the intoxication of the celebration already give the consecration of union? The answer is: certainly it does not have to be so. The vehemence of the expressions accompanies only one type of ecstasy and not necessarily self-surrender in general. Rather, whenever this occurs with such terrible convulsions, it had to struggle with an obstacle and could only occur after its removal. We touch here a fundamental state of a!airs in other respects as well.

What a person accomplishes outwardly, whether willed or involuntary, is never anything other than a process of inner life that has become objective in the recoil against the world.

If the Mystes must tear the god apart in order to enter into polar connection with that very god, it means he comes to self-emptying only after violent self-laceration. The manifold examples of self-flagellations, self-woundings, indeed self-destructions in orgiastic cults give us the key to the ultimate meaning of ecstatic tearing apart in general as the outwardly breaking appearance of the most violent inner explosions. But these are needed only by a soul already detached from the primal image, because locked in the prison of selfhood, which nevertheless still succumbs to the call of the god. Intoxicating drinks, sound of brass basins, bagpipes, pipes, magic drum: they are so many means to stupefy the thinking-capable consciousness and to help the soul, fallen into volcanic bubbling, to break through, not we repeat it from the fetters of the body, but of the I, the will, the reason. The spirit of the Mystes, instead of melting away as in a dream, is, as it were, shattered in the surf of the blood; and the immediate expression of this is the actual tearing apart of a living body!

Every bloody orgy thus shimmers in an enigmatic double light, which albeit wrongly gave cause to fundamentally separate it from the devout observances of quieter mysteries, as supposedly those of Eleusis or of Isis were: delivered up to storming spirit-choirs in extreme self-crushing, the Maenadic ecstasy yet seems again to be a rearing Titanism, by virtue of which its bearer, as if from demonic self-power, falls into a frenzy of destruction, soon as a Bacchically blinded Agave murdering her own son, soon as a Nero, fate-intoxicated, setting fire to the "eternal city" and singing dithyrambs into the sea of flames. What may have changed in the heart of the soul, and did a change begin here that could end with a reversal of the meaning of ecstasy?

It had begun since there were such frenzies, since there were bloody sacrifices, but also since there were secret leagues and mysteries. In the decomposition of body and soul, beginning already in prehistory, the accumulation of bloody sacrifices denotes the defensive pressure directed against the soul, the increasingly pronounced cathartic attitude that directed against the body. However unfamiliar it may sound: ancient Mexican cannibalism and child-roasting Moloch service on the one hand, and an Orphism striving toward Platonism on the other, are only di!erently directed expressions of the same feud between the poles of life. This the bloodless-Promethean wrong path leads into the most desensualized rational belief in the reality of conceptual contents; that the bloody-Heraclean leads to the privileging of purposes over grounds and will end, after gruesome slaughters, in complete atomization; as indeed, in the number of human sacrifices o!ered to it, the will-religion of Christianity far surpasses Aztec cannibalism! Following the Greek saga of the all-devouring flood, we have called the original humanity by the mythical name of the annihilated, the "Pelasgian," and in the Deucalionian we pursued exclusively those traits through which it is connected with the former in its early time. But now a few words about the most important properties in which even the preparatory time of "history" and even more so the culture of antiquity deviates from genuine Pelasgianism, or rather about their di!erently directed emanation from a single point of radiation! We will connect this with Eleusis.

According to the usual view of classical studies, everything we illustrated with the help of the Eleusinian mysteries would be absolutely inapplicable in relation to them; for it was neither about what we called the "eternity of life" nor even about immortality, but about the guarantee of a blessed life after death! For the Homeric Hymn to Demeter says:

Blessed is he among men on earth who has seen these things!

But he who is uninitiated in the rites, who has no part in them,

Unequal lot he has also in the murky darkness of death!

Immortality is thus already presupposed and it only depends on the manner of continued existence! An Eleusinian gravestone from the second century AD even proclaims:

Truly, a beautiful mystery the blessed Gods proclaim:

For mortals, death is not a curse, but a blessing!

To this, one would first have to say: whenever and to whatever extent the idea of immortality took hold, the passionate desire of people unfailingly and to the same degree aims at a pleasant immortality; and whenever a mystery league takes into account the immortality hopes of its participants, it must inevitably promise a future bliss! The bliss, in other words, is self-evident as soon as it is a future life that forms the attractive force of the mysteries. Furthermore, we interject: the mediation of the certainty of bliss happened through the awakening of vision, while the projection of bliss into a future life happened through that misleading of spirits which we must now characterize in extreme brevity.

Ponder for a few minutes the following opposites and you will grasp with your hands that they negate each other: immortality wishes and the eternalization of life. Care for the dead preserves something that really was; belief in immortality promises something never and nowhere existing; the former surrenders itself to the past; the latter even alienates from the present, is completely fallen away with the past, and sacrifices the only eternal thing that exists, the moment ablaze with life, to a future only graspable in concept by it; the former, blossoming from the love of the corporeally living for the dark-bright images of life, places its bearers in the restlessly transforming "circle of necessity"; with the latter, the selfish hubris of uprooted individuals flees from the horror of having-to-end into the faith-lie of the supra-temporal continuance of separate existence. Summarized and glaringly illuminated: the mentality of death cult asks: what became of those who were, and how can their lot be beautified; the immortality-believing asks: what will become of me when I am no more, and with what do I purchase a joyful continuance?

Belief in immortality thus stems from selfishness, and that is to say: the I has taken the place of the primal image and consequently the will-to-existence has taken the place of life. Will-to-existence is hostility to images and aims at disembodiment. ("For everything must decay into nothing If it wishes to persist in being!") The further it takes hold, the more the meaning of ecstatic experience changes, before it finally dies out:

from self-emptying becomes life-emptying, from union with the All through breaking the barriers of objects becomes flight from the All through bursting the bonds of the body. And thus, from the inner rupture, Orphism fled into the ascetic delusion of the body as the tomb of the soul, and this alone, as is understandable, has the spirit-betrayed philosophy spun into those gigantic systems, whose thoroughly self-contradictory structure could be aptly compared to the hopeless undertaking of a great architect who, though he devised the most ingenious plans, had unfortunately set himself the condition of using no earthly material for his buildings! But this is not the place to speak of that.

So much for the repression of the body. For the understanding of the complementary repression of the soul, we consider the following. From the essence-full wellspring of the past, no bridge leads into the essence-empty nothingness of the future. Turning towards the future, however, coincides with turning away from the past; and whoever became a hoper, became to the same degree a fearer. If one hopes to gain heaven after his death, he also fears hell! Preserving his I in hope and fear, he has turned away from the ever-flowing primal source, which only lent him life (note the phrase "lending of life"!), and thus the maternal Eumenide changes for him into the avenging Erinys. The souls of the dead, no longer nourished by love-congealed self-interest, become bloodthirsty vampires, whom one strives in vain to appease with hecatombs of animals and people! The heart-nerve of the present of all who succumbed to the madness of future-belief is anxiety: anxiety about death, anxiety about the morrow, anxiety in general, because anxiety about the next minute, anxiety from impious neglect of life, which, even if all belief in ghosts long since died, only grows all the more corrodingly as "bad conscience".

That was I once, ere I in darkness sought it,

With impious words myself and world beshrouded.

Now is the air so full of such a spook,

That no one knows how he its way may brook.

Though one day laughs on us clear and rational,

In dream-entanglement involves us night’s pall;

We come back glad from fields of youthful hue,

A bird croaks; what croaks he? Misery.

By superstition early late ensnared

It fits, it shows itself, it is declared

And so intimidated, stand we alone ...

The counterpart to "heavenly bliss" is hell on earth, and the worst form of such hell on earth: Ahasverian slumber-deprived wakefulness of the eternally insatiable willing and its self-stupefaction through Mammon service! The application of these disclosures to many things mentioned in detail above and finally to the innumerable distinguishing characters of "historical" humanity must be left to the reader.

We would have nothing further to add, if we did not fear that one doubt, probably felt from the beginning when reading this chapter, might still not be entirely removed. Namely, to what extent, it might still be asked now, is prehistoric soul-care not the manifestation of a belief in immortality? Even if the Pelasgian saw the psychic not in things but in images, his grave-care customs nevertheless show that for what the soul meant to him, he claimed a continuance beyond the sensory body of man. We ask for our reasons against the correctness of this view to be given particular attention, because they might serve more than all other explanations to show our doctrine of the reality of images in the brightest light.

In the first place, it is clear: if images are something incessantly flowing, the "existential judgment" fits them only figuratively. Images transformations; but transformations not = "existences". The reality of images the most real or rather the only one that exists is eternal coming and going, growing and withering, flaring up and extinguishing again. In contrast to the time-withdrawn rigidity of objective being, it is not thought through concepts, but through symbols, of which we have come to know many. Things and persons exist, but images and they alone live, and the reality of being-alive is fundamentally and essentially di!erent from the thought-ness of existing.

Secondly: The receiver of the existing thing is the spirit thinking it, which belongs to all thinking-capable beings and performs the same acts in arbitrary repetition; the receiver of the image is an eigen-living soul, whose de-spirited vision never repeats itself in exactly the same way. The world of images thus only glowed in the viewing Eros of those experiencing them. The so-called continued existence, in fact however living presence, which the Pelasgian demands for the souls of his dead, blossoms from moment to moment from the elemental-erotic connection of those who have been with the corporeally living. The image-soul dies when it dies in the souls of those who remember it! Here lies the deepest root of that otherwise incomprehensible soul-care. Finally, we deduce from this, thirdly, that the transformations of the soul essentially depend also on the transformations of its cult. The "ancestor" and "guardian spirit" of the house gains strength from the fullness and depth of living adoration which its corporeal inhabitants pay to it, wastes away and vanishes, forgotten by them. If humanity as a whole has lost the gift of vision, the last stragglers will unfailingly hear the shattering cry of woe: "The great Pan is dead!"

Looking back, we summarize: in the ecstatic surge, life aims at liberation from the spirit the perfection consists in the awakening of the soul and the awakening of the soul is vision but it sees the reality of the primal images primal images are appearing past-souls to appear they need connection with the blood of the corporeally living this happens in the event of vision, which is therefore a mystical wedding between the surrendered, receptive soul of the viewer and the generating demon "come to himself again," the ecstatic knows, if he is otherwise able to recollect: that the world of facts is merely a hard-to-tear web of brain-spun illusion, the world of bodies a world of symbols, but absolutely real are the progeny of vision fertilized by the primal world, in the face of which passing away and dying means transformation.

One can now well recognize why no other philosophy was just as suited to attract and seduce enthusiasts and poets as the Platonic doctrine of Ideas! Grown out of Orphic theology, it seems to o!er its innermost meaning in the language of higher reflection. For according to it too, the lover partakes of recollecting vision, indeed even in communion with the beloved (!); he too gains back the "eternal life" of original divinity precisely through this; and here too the discourse is adorned with the simile of inner procreation and o!spring! However, already the one sentence: that the beheld is that "colorless and intangible and formless essence, which in truth has Being," reveals to the knowledgeable that here the reality of always momentary images was substituted by the timelessly constant illusory world of reified concepts, according to which the purported vision evaporates into the dialectical act of the understanding!

And now we have only one more thing to add, but let it be preceded by something long forgotten today. We know associations based on interest, and these are without exception founded on a life-hostile "will to power"; we know associations based on the feelings of individuals: from friendship, from comradeship, from working communities, from a!airs, from sexual subjugation; we know finally, as something noblest attainable to us, associations based on shared conviction [Gesinnungsverbände] from a common enthusiasm for the suprapersonal cause, which enthusiasm in turn stems from a kinship of life [Lebensverwandtschaft] between characters. But even the most sacrificially willing form of a community of conviction, racial consciousness fed from the blood of ancient tribal feelings, reveals itself upon sharper inspection to be a"icted with the degenerative trait of a propensity for the abstract and has, wherever it appears, always already taken the transitional path towards partisanship of mere doctrines, convictions, moral formulas; which is why a nearly common usage of language not unjustly understands the most vital connection we still know of nevertheless as "idealism"! For, to remain protected from this, within its adherents would have had to have occurred what in the earliest Eleusis was yearned for and intuited, but most probably even there was no longer fully accomplished: the brotherhood of blood would have had to renew itself through the union of its bearers in the mystery of Eros. For that, as we now finally impart, would be the true cosmogonic Eros.

Let one recall the experience of vision [Schauens], as we explicated it, and add to it that two people su!ered it together: then both would be sympathetically connected through the event which bears the name of the cosmogonic Eros! That such a thing would never be accomplished through the coupling of their bodies needs no further word; but how it could nonetheless take place, that might, without committing sacrilege, only be revealed by one whom the miracle had transformed, rapturing him among the gods. If this unheard-of event, however, were to happen even only between two out of hundreds of millions, then the curse-power of the spirit would be broken, the terrible nightmare of "world history" would dissolve, and "awakening would bloom in streams of light".

Conversely, we can now clarify with a few words what is the nature of that "death-overcoming" love which antiquity did not yet know. To the initiate [Epopten], we said, physicality is a symbol of the image-streaming soul of the world. Well, he who is not merely, so to speak, in love, but possessed in his innermost being by the deepest passion think of Solveig’s love for Peer is, in relation to the object of his love, likewise a mystic with the eye of the world-creating god, before whom the shell bursts and the bodily husk begins to glow with the translucence of the elemental soul.

Ignited indeed by the uniqueness of her being, he penetrates the humanity of the beloved with the ray of a gaze in which her demon stands. This common to the Eros of antiquity and the Germanic Middle Ages does not preclude that the magic semblance mirroring the archetype, wandering, touches person after person. Even the shortest duration of the erotic union would not contradict the most fulfilled inwardness, and precisely the most giving exuberance would be compatible without contradiction with the abandon even of the hetaera.

The more closely, however, the I [das Ich] fused with the soul in the object of love, the essence hardening into a closed-o! "character", the more the danger also grows that the lightning bolt of vision [Schauung] is captured and bound by the tormenting riddle of a personality. The symbol is exchanged and the person is elevated to god in place of the god. This is in truth "idolatry" [Abgötterei] and with it begins the tragedy of Eros. Destined to flow together with the flame of the image, it now seeks it in vain in the human self of the beloved and wants to attach to a spiritual being and preserve in the durability of existence that which lives only in the unfettered and unfettering blaze of the eternity of the unpossessable moment. Thus arises the pathos of the "great passion", which forces the lovers to assure themselves of fidelity "until death", even beyond death, and which, metaphysically considered, always means doom [Verhängnis]. For:

if it leads to the illusory fulfillment of physical possession, it will sooner or later consume itself (Faust and Gretchen!); but if the lovers are torn apart, betrayed, sold, they indeed give their lives for their love without hesitation; however, no sacrifice, however heroic, overcomes all-powerful death! In all its di!erence, related therein to "love of God" which it nevertheless surpasses through its deeper participation in the consecration of Eros the "great passion" also builds a bridge to the belief in immortality: what was not fulfilled "here", for that it must hope for fulfillment "over there". But "immortality" is only the illusory fulfillment [Wahnerfüllung] of unfulfillable wishes, and the destiny of the world, for which this entire humanity is merely a drop in the ocean of time, strides on relentlessly, crushing even the irredeemably loving. The sublime nimbus that rightly surrounds them must not blind us to the fact that they are cursed to lose life without escaping the self. And so we turn back from the anxious image of tortured human greatness to the world-breathing brightness of the gods, as the poet thus invoked:

What fiery wonder transfigures the waves,

Which shatter sparkling against each other?

Thus it shines and sways and brightens forth:

The bodies, they glow on the nocturnal path,

And all around is embraced by fire;

So let Eros, who began it all, reign!

[L]et us direct our attention to certain phenomena arising from the affective stratum of life that have long been ignored by investigators. For instance, we all recognize those numerous associations that have, without exception, one interest in common, namely the employment of the group’s energy to connect up with that “will to power” whose inner essence is intransigently hostile to life itself. Likewise, we have encountered those people whose connection inheres in the emotional bonds that enable individuals to participate in the sense of community that may flourish, say, in the workplace; or we may recall those ties that arise in friendly affection, as well as those bonds that are tied when sexual attraction draws two persons into more intimate connection. We finally draw attention to that which, at first glance, certainly seems to be one of the noblest attainable species of human association; namely, that intensely close union which transpires when a certain group’s shared enthusiasm inspires its members to work collaboratively for the ultimate triumph of a particular enterprise, although the mission to which they have given their allegiance utterly transcends the personal, or selfish, interests of the people involved. This species of shared enthusiasm arises almost always in situations where those involved share a profound characterological affinity. Nevertheless, even this most intensely self-sacrificial form of human association, which can exist only when an ancient tribal sense of racial consciousness is intense enough to make the call of the blood potentiate the living bond of the associated persons, reveals itself, on closer scrutiny, to be dependent upon an obviously degenerate obsession with abstractions. Wherever this degenerate trend is discovered at work, as a matter of fact, the project will evolve in very short order into an entity that is soon seething with the least worthy species of partisan spirit, at which point the members become the creatures of their hollow doctrines, the most zealous missionaries preaching the most tedious ethical formalism imaginable. Utilizing the more colloquial, more “popular” lexical expression for that which had once seemed the most vital connection conceivable, we must conclude that these individuals have bogged themselves down in the phenomenon justly known as “idealism”! Since one remains protected when one stands before it, inwardly one must confess to one’s belief in that which has been yearned for and anticipated in the earliest days of Eleusis, although apparently even in that place, the deepest experience could never have been brought unto its consummation: the renewal of the blood-brotherhood as it was fortified within the bearer of the mystery of Eros.

Suppose one were sought out by a person who wished to question us as to our personal recollections of a visionary revelation to which we happened to have been an eyewitness (in order to acquire additional details about the scene) — if, suddenly, we should learn there was a second witness to that very vision, we are bound by that event in a sympathetic connection with that other person. To that connection, we give the name of the cosmogonic Eros! That such an event has nothing to do with the physical expression of bodies goes without saying; but it may, however, come to pass that such an inoffensive connection might well result in a person’s experience of so transformative a miracle that he feels himself transported among the gods. Events about which no one hears since they happened to have transpired between two individuals out of hundreds of millions, might actually break the fleeting power of the spirit, the destructive nightmare of “world history” might be shattered, and we might awaken in a world “blossoming with streams of light.”

We wish now to clarify, in a few brief words, just what it is that inheres in the notion of a love that is “faithful beyond death,” a species of passion, as it happens, about which the ancient world knew precisely nothing. The Epoptes, in fact, regarded the very condition of “bodiliness” as a potent symbol that enabled man to participate in the stream of images that constitutes the soul of the world. Let us consider the case of one who is not “merely” loved, but rather that of the person whose innermost passion is to possess another utterly and to the deepest levels of that person’s life — we might recall Solveig’s love for Peer (Gynt — translator’s note) — he is necessarily viewing the object of his obsessive passion as if through a fog that renders his gaze identical in essence with the eye of the “worldcreating” God, before whom the surface of the Earth is pulverized as the mortal coil of the flesh begins to glow with the penetrating radiance of the elemental soul. As if he were truly becoming a man on fire, he probes the humanity of his beloved, but with the ray of light that reveals merely the presence of his own demon. In this case — whose lineaments are common to the vision of Eros that was characteristic not only of Greek Antiquity but of the Germanic Middle Ages as well — it is nonetheless only to state that this standpoint did not exclude the possibility that a magical image might transmit its reflections while the image wandered from person to person. The least enduring experience of an erotic connection in itself cannot keep pace with the most fulfilling inwardness, and it is precisely in the midst of the most freely bestowed and most overflowing sexual experience that, without the slightest contradiction, transpires the payment of money to a member of the class of hetairas…

Ever more constricting, however, becomes this passion now that his ego consumes the object of love with its soul, until the nature of the “character” of the person so obsessed with this passion-as-exclusivity becomes more and more rigid and unyielding, so that in time there may even arise a serious danger that the lightning bolt of vision itself becomes increasingly constricted, until the only thing on the lover’s mind is the tormenting riddle of an individual’s personality. The person severs his vital bonds with the sensuous world of images, all of which are stripped away until the human being believes that the beloved has become a god to be worshipped in the place of a god. This mode of passion has now obviously metamorphosed into idolatry, and with its advent begins the ultimate tragedy of Eros. Whereas once the lovers were filled with rapture when they formed one interfused flame of vital imagery, the person now seeks the essence of love in the human “self” of the beloved, so that he may raise his beloved to the stature of a spiritual being whom he seizes fast in the dimension of duration, whereas only in the releasing of the beloved from all such chains may he live an eternity in an instant of time. Thus there transpires the pathos of a “grand passion,” which constrains the lover to secure for himself that passionate “loyalty unto death,” which even seeks to extend its domain beyond the borders of the tomb, and which, scrutinized from the standpoint of metaphysics, can only, and always, result in misfortune for both persons. Then, inevitably, the lover is demanding the pseudo-fulfillment of his passion as he commands that he be granted the sole access to the body of his beloved, so that, sooner or later, disaster occurs (Faust and Gretchen!); but no matter how much the lover torments her, betrays her, ridicules her, this same lover swears that he would, without hesitation, give up his life for his beloved; nevertheless, no matter how heroic is the deed of the martyr, by itself the martyrdom avails him nothing in the face of all-powerful death! In every modality in which we encounter the “love unto God” there is always present a very deep share in the sacrament of Eros; but in the “grand passion” there is always an impulsive undercurrent that is seeking to build a bridge to the faith in immortality: whatever wishes of ours cannot attain to fulfillment “here” must be fulfilled to the limit of our hopes in the transcendent “yonder.”

However, “immortality” is only a pathological yearning for the fulfillment of unfulfillable wishes, and the entire history of our cosmos, in comparison with which the whole chronicle of mankind is merely a drop in the ocean of time, cries unrelentingly that even the beloved is doomed to be a thing of corruption and dissolution. The nimbus surrounding that wish, whose loftiness is perpetually rewoven — and quite righly so — should not, however, blind us to the fact that this is a wish that life cannot fulfill without dissolving that self to nothingness in the very process.

And now we turn back from the melancholy image of the tormented greatness of man to the breathing brightness of the world of gods, as they once promised the following to the poet:

What fiery wonder transpires when the waves transfigure us, As they shatter, one upon the other, in their coruscating radiance? They glow and shift and shimmer ever onwards: And the stars and planets are gleaming on their nocturnal paths, And everything is ringed around by the fire; This is the all-ruling Eros, begetter of everything that lives!
