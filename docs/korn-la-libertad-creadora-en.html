§1

I do not write for those who still suffer from naïve realism.2 It is difficult to emancipate oneself from this congenital error. It is as ingrained as the geocentric error and the anthropomorphic conception of divinity once were, and as countless prejudices of which we are not even aware remain, being tacit assumptions of our reasoning.3 Neither Descartes’s methodical doubt nor Bacon’s repudiation of idols nor, finally, Kant’s critique managed to dispel all presuppositions. In the great philosophical systems, one always finds traces of the naïve convictions of the era. If they were to be eliminated through a logical purification, they would simply resurface as instinctive impulses, as expressions of latent feelings,4 or as dogmatic assessments.

p. 20

Picture the astonishment of Copernicus’s contemporaries upon being told that the planet rotates, as if it were a top, on its own axis and in an extensive orbit around the sun. Children now hear this lesson on the benches of elementary schools, and people today accept these ideas without hesitation. But what a secular, slow, and painful effort was necessary to transform the paradox into a trivial truth! Copernicus’s work was published in 1543. Galileo was tortured in 1633. Father Feijóo, taking appropriate caution, dared to disseminate the novel theory [p. 20] in Spain in 1750. And it wasn’t until 1855 that the book was removed from the Index. Even in a spirit as elevated as Hegel’s, vestiges of the geocentric conception can similarly be discovered.

With how much greater tenacity must naïve realism, whose pragmatic value is proven in every moment of life, impose itself. The Eleatics already condemned it. All moderately enlightened criteria reject it. Yet, it has not been able to be completely eradicated. To dispel it is, nonetheless, the precondition of all philosophy.

And, evidently, dispelling it is not difficult. Few reflections suffice to show that this visible and tangible universe, which is spatially extended and develops in time, is only known5 as a mental phenomenon.6 How many, though, after conceding this fact, proceed to disregard it, setting it aside as something bothersome and thus continue their dealings7 without taking it into account? This reproach is not directed at the common folk,8 who are unaware of the first of philosophical notions. There are cultured spirits who, though knowing the error of naïve realism, fail to accomplish the intimate task necessary to escape the suggestion of habit. Moreover, there are refractory mentalities9 incapable of shedding off their naivety. With spirits so disposed, one should not speak of philosophy, just as one does not speak of music to the deaf. Although they may otherwise be good people worthy of appreciation and respect, and not lacking in common sense, they lack philosophical sense.

We must bear in mind, then, that the external world is not a known reality,10 but a problem that, for now, when it exists, only exists in a consciousness.11

§2

It is not enough to emancipate oneself from naïve realism up to the point of understanding12 the totality of things as a mental phenomenon. This is the crudest part of the initiation. It is necessary to pursue naïve realism in all its hideouts, especially where it conceals itself in larval forms.

Space and time, the two magnitudes in which the cosmic process is framed, are also only known as elements of consciousness, and their real existence outside of it is not a proven fact.

Meanwhile, the habit of contemplating reality in spatial or temporal relations takes hold of us13 even more than the supposed externality of things. We can at least suppress the externality of reality in mente, but it is not possible for us to evict space and time from our representation.14 Such a psychological disposition finds its natural support in a language constructed on the mold of naïve realism, so much so that it places spatial and even temporal relations where they only have a figurative meaning.15 It takes effort to overcome these suggestions to realize that, in addition to the corporeal world, we also only know the existence of space and time as a fact of consciousness.16

Not satisfied with populating the external world with sensible images,17 we also add to them creations of our reasoning.18 Through a known psychological process, we abstract general concepts that — initially almost concrete — amplify and superimpose themselves and end up taking on exceptional subtlety. We then expel these logical children of consciousness to [p. 22] place them in space or, perchance, beyond. We begin by baptizing them, by giving them a name, and, in a continuous act, they become escaped specters, as if having forgotten their genesis.

Matter is the concept of what is extended; ether is that of the medium;19 energy and cause are that of action; etc. This progeny of the beings of reason20 settles like a swarm upon things or incorporates itself into them, thereby allowing us to construct our conception of the cosmos.

We give such progeny the mission of ordering, distributing, and concretizing things, as well as of establishing a nexus between successive events.21 In truth, though, their home is in consciousness, and they have not the slightest thought of abandoning it, for it is there that they exercise their office.

§4

Let us see if it is even possible to conceive22 of something outside of consciousness. In the very utterance of the word “conceive,” we have already set consciousness in motion. We can anticipate that the result of our attempt will be but another conception.

Think of the most remote beginning of time. Launch your thought into the unfathomable places that lie beyond the Milky Way. Wander through the multidimensional spaces. Never will we be able to escape the boundaries of consciousness. Not even the most audacious imagination can cross such boundaries.

Nevertheless, we want there to be23 something foreign to the process of consciousness, something that is its negation. This we call that-which-is-not-in-consciousness.24 And, well, we have once again created a conception. That-which-is-not-in-consciousness itself does not exist except insofar as we think it, and, in the very act of thinking it, it no longer is that-which-is-not-in-consciousness.

§5

But if the sensible and intelligible orders only exist in consciousness, does this universe, despite its apparent solidity, amount to nothing more than a fiction? Not at all. Whether we consider the cosmos as a material or ideal process, in both cases it will be real. What varies is only the interpretation we have given it.

In giving the sun a different position in the planetary system, we haven’t thereby ridded it of its functions. For both realists and idealists, it illuminates today as it did before Copernicus. Indeed, we haven’t even modified the common expressions with which we refer to its rising or setting. We can say that the diurnal movement is a deception, but certainly not that the sun is a deception. What is deceptive is rather our way of seeing it.

Similarly, whether we see reality as material, energetic, or psychical, reality is not thereby altered. We prefer the latter because it is the only way we can know it. The other ways of seeing reality are hypothetical.

§6

That everything only exists in a consciousness does not, by itself, imply that reality itself is merely a mental phenomenon. It only means that reality presents itself to us in this form and in no other. However, our knowledge could very well be the reflection of a reality that is independent of us. We can, in the abstract, distinguish between the modus cognoscendi from the modus essendi. To affirm the identity of both amounts to identifying thinking with being.

p. 24

This position is that of absolute idealism. Its opposite is extreme realism, which considers consciousness to be an epiphenomenon of external activities. In the first case, consciousness would be the center of the irradiation of the cosmos, [p. 24] a power that gives being to its own conception of the world. In the second case, consciousness would be an accidental efflorescence, one whose presence or absence neither modifies nor alters the development of the universal mechanism. In the first case, consciousness would not only be active, but it would be the only existing activity. In the second case, consciousness would not only be receptive, but also completely passive.

All intermediate positions, of course, fall within these two extremes: dualistic compromises, eclectic reconciliations, transfigured realism, mitigated idealism, and critical analysis — multiple ingenious attempts to delineate the domain of consciousness and that of things.

§7

We will attempt, in due course, to take a position. If we explore the content of consciousness, we discover the concept of an entity that, unlike all others, we do not try to expel. On the contrary, we try to withdraw it to our most intimate and personal space. We detach it even from the physical organism. We place it in opposition to the mental process itself, and, in our eagerness25 to abstract it from everything and in every manner,26 we lodge it, perhaps, in the hollowness27 of a mathematical point. This is the concept of the I.28

It is the persistent and stable unity that we postulate and to which we refer the successive moments of the changing psychic process. Beyond all doubt, it exists only in consciousness. As does the series of facts that we attribute to it, the I: affective states, volitions, and judgments.

But in no way do we attribute to it all the content of consciousness, for consciousness also includes the representation of a world that the I conceptualizes as foreign and separates [from itself], as internal from external. Yet, though this world is outside the I, it is not outside consciousness. The sensations [p. 25] that are its constitutive elements are psychic facts, and we have no other sign of this world’s existence.

According to realism, in accordance with common opinion, for the portion of consciousness opposed to the I, there exists a duplicate of another order; or, better, an original, whose more or less faithful reproduction is all that is cognizable.29

The proof of that problematic world is arduous. We cannot escape consciousness, and any such effort is futile. There is no other recourse but to resort to argumentation, which itself reduces to considering the content of consciousness as an effect that must have a cause outside of it, without noting that such a cause is unknown, inaccessible, a pure noumenon. And, for now, we shall not ask with what right the concept of cause is employed, a concept which is nothing more than an element of our reasoning.30

The existence of this hypothetical world, situated outside the horizon encompassed by our knowledge, does not have, in the literal sense of the word, a reason to be.31 The affirmation of its reality is but an act of faith,32 an irrational residue of naïve realism.

§8

However, in seeking to convert objective reality to a manifestation of the I — which, as we have seen, is not consciousness but rather that which integrates consciousness — the adversaries of realism only manage to contribute to maintaining the fiction of an external world. This egocentric error characterizes subjective idealism and, strictly speaking, it leads to solipsism.

If realism ends up turning the I into an offspring of the physical world, subjective idealism inverts this order and assumes the world to be a creation of the I. In this, the subjectivist is mistaken in more or less the same way as Rostand’s rooster, who believes that if he were not to crow the sun would not rise.

p. 26

[p. 26] No sophistry can suppress the fundamental distinction between the I and the not-I, between the subjective and the objective order. The Greeks never conceived of such an absurdity, even though they exhausted almost all possible philosophical positions.

It was Descartes who, in identifying thinking with the I, inoculated modern philosophy with this pernicious germ that later prospered in the monstruous systems of the German Idealists. They confuse consciousness with my consciousness; they take the part for the whole; and, thus, they arrive at the conclusion that “life is a dream,” that the world is nothing but “the veil of Maya,” or “the cinematographic reel” passing through our consciousness.

It is the merit of realist schools to have opposed this fallacious conception. In demonstrating the independence of subject and object, they celebrate their greatest triumph because they draw support from an indisputable fact of consciousness. Unfortunately, they hasten to undermine their triumph in their desire to subject the I to a noumenal world. The objective world is certainly outside the I, but it is not outside consciousness. In counting something as external, we refer to the I and not to an unknowable reality. Why should we substitute the known reality with an imagined one?

In truth, consciousness bifurcates itself33 into an objective and subjective order. We cannot say more than what we know. And this we know in an immediate and definitive manner.

§9

The subject34 does not maintain a contemplative attitude before the world. The subject is by no means a disinterested spectator. Consciousness is the theater of conflicts and harmonies between the subject that feels, judges, and wills and the object that conforms or resists.

p. 27

[p. 27] The mutual relations are established by means of mental forms, which constitute an intermediary zone between internal and external reality.

Sensations would birth chaos if they were not coordinated and made concrete in a determinate object. It is necessary to unite some sensations and separate others; to assign them, most importantly, to their respective places in space and time, so that they constitute unities that then stand in relations to one another. The same must also be done with the multiple manifestations of the activity of the subject. To this end, the countless isolated facts must be classified and linked.

This task is carried out through concepts abstracted from both the subjective and objective orders. Although secondary and derived from intuited facts,35 concepts are as necessary as these for constructing our conception of the cosmos.

§10

The destiny of concepts usually varies. While some always retain the mark of their origin and do not stray from their sources, others become independent and acquire their own jurisdiction, especially if they are denoted by a noun,36 which almost crystallizes them. Most concepts encompass only a particular, more or less limited domain, while others are so broad that they encompass the entire subjective or objective orders; or, sometimes, they encompass both with a universal meaning.37 Some always possess a certain concrete content, while others are completely abstract and lack any possible representation.

p. 28

Finally, concepts are born or die when they have completed their task. Their life — sometimes ephemeral — responds to a temporary need and barely leaves the trace of a term in the vocabulary. Many endure — as modest instruments of daily labor — and some stand out as dominant and emancipate [p. 28] themselves. Like rebellious slaves, instead of obeying, they claim to govern consciousness and tenaciously resist their displacement. The history of philosophy is the history of these rebellious concepts. Human consciousness has bowed for centuries before the idols incubated in its bosom, like savages38 before the charms fabricated by their own hands.

§11

There are, however, concepts whose tyranny is difficult to evade. They form a select group, a kind of aristocracy, and they seem so indispensable that they have been regarded as having an origin distinct from the mass of empirical concepts, a plebeian mass sometimes denigrated with the label of pseudo-concepts. Those select concepts have been called innate ideas, a priori forms, categories, or, at the very least, they have been recognized as having a distinct lineage.39 They certainly perform an important role.

If we suppress concepts like space, cause, and energy, then the entire cosmos would collapse and disappear. If we suppress the concept of time, then the very process of consciousness stops and extinguishes. This is because such concepts are universal, applicable not just to a more or less broad or restricted group of facts, but to all of them, without exception. They serve especially to coordinate and establish a nexus between facts. It is as if they were immanent in each singular case. The validity of particular or general concepts depends on them. For this reason, they have been considered to be necessary.

p. 29

The practical necessity of their use as forms of knowledge is imposed on us, but this doesn’t go all the way to making such concepts irreplaceable. Let us respect them without exaggerating our devotion. Some members of this oligarchy have already experienced a capitis diminutio. Thus, the concept of an unchanging substance,40 with all its repercussions41 for bodies and souls, is in full decline. [p. 29] And nothing less than the old, almost untouchable, concept of cause is being replaced by that of function. Space and time, in a more abstract language like mathematics, may also suffer some degradation. And even the multiple categories on whose number philosophers never managed to agree can be reduced to a single one — relation — which expresses the relativity and reciprocal dependence of all elements that constitute a state of consciousness.

In virtue of being constant elements in the logical process, and in virtue of their dialectical development being an abstract reflection of facts, these concepts are used to systematize the data of experience. However, like all other concepts, they are empty42 without the intuitive content to which they apply. To operate with concepts in place of intuitions is to invert hierarchies and to subordinate43 the primary to the secondary. It is the river, the active force, that carves the riverbed, not the riverbed that engenders the river, even if the riverbed contains the river when it doesn’t overflow. Thinking presupposes intuiting, as Croce says: “Pressupposto dell’attività logica sono le rappresentazioni o intuizioni.”

Of course, we cannot think except in44 concepts. But let us not take the logical scaffolding45 for the essence. Let us not imitate the example of natural sciences, which frame46 facts in schemata and subsequently confuse the schemata with reality. General ideas, like schemata, are indispensable, but let us remain on guard, since conceptualism is the first step toward verbalism.

The point of departure of knowing47 is intuition.48 I understand by intuition the evident fact, the spontaneous and immediate knowledge49 constituted as a unity50 by [p. 30] synthetic apperception. I do not add “without discursive elements” because this ideal condition is never realized. Pure intuition does not exist. Analysis always discovers its complexity, but criticism cannot be taken to the extreme of denying the intuitive basis of knowledge without falling into nihilism and committing suicide through absurdity. However, even though it deals with experience, I avoid employing the term “empirical” because this sensualist concept assumes a simplicity that does not withstand even the most cursory examination, and it tends to convert the act of knowledge into a passive affair.51

Likewise, to avoid misunderstandings, I exclude from my concept of intuition the psychological phenomenon called intellectual intuition, for which I reserve, in a figurative sense, the historical name of vision.52

p. 31

Sometimes words56 — the Word57 — have been claimed to possess a reality and action of their own. Magical formulas, many of which persist in religious rites and popular superstitions, claim there to be a mystical power in words. Kabbalistic doctrines attribute such a power to numbers, and the same tends to be insinuated, albeit in an algebraic form, in contemporary logistics,58 the latest budding of Pythagoreanism. Well, we ought to have convinced ourselves that verbal expressions neither crown nor dethrone kings, that number is but a quantitative abstraction. But is it perhaps more difficult to understand59 that the instrument of thinking, the concept, is not also an entity, as the rationalist systems have believed? The old Kant [p. 31] saw this well in his affirmation that concepts without intuitions are empty. This doesn’t mean we should stop speaking, calculating, and thinking.

Even a philosophy based on intuition cannot develop through luminous projections but must necessarily60 employ words and concepts. However, it must employ them with an understood meaning. We continue saying that the sun rises and sets without deceiving ourselves about the real fact, and that is how we shall employ concepts, without granting them any character other than that of a symbolic abstraction, devoid of their own reality.

On the other hand, a slight reflection suffices to convince oneself that all universal concepts, when hypostasized, become self-contradictory and absurd, e.g., time, space, first cause, etc. Concepts, like words, are symbols. The action that we endure or exercise is no longer a symbol. It is a fact.61 The Logos, the immanent principle, ceased to be word long ago. One does not continue considering it a rational concept because, really, it is efficient action,62 will,63 and energy.

§14

The relation between concepts in opposition64 is rather strange. Formal logic, according to the principle of identity, considers this relation contradictory, without realizing that this principle only governs things.65 Yes, this hammer is not those pliers. But I use both tools accordingly, without them excluding each other. Both are useful to me, and the use of one does not prohibit me from using the other. The choice depends on the circumstances and my aims, just as their effectiveness depends on the [desired] practical result. So, too, with the use of concepts that are mere instruments of logical operations.66

Affirmation and negation, despite being concepts in opposition, are not, for that, contradictory, and neither are they linked by some mystical bond that fuses them into a coincidentia oppositorum. [p. 32] I affirm this and negate that. I affirm today what I negated yesterday, in accordance with the concrete case under consideration. The rapid and fleeting process in which life carries death in its bosom — in which perishing is the condition of birth and contrary tendencies come apart and then together — presents a good occasion to use both abstractions. In the living conflict of consciousness, what takes place is not a game of pale abstractions, but the clash of antagonistic forces, ones which we experience and do not dream. The synthesis of opposites occurs67 in the concrete, singular, and determinate act.68

§15

When concepts in opposition have both been abstracted from real facts, they both retain an intrinsic meaning.69 But if one of the terms is merely a speculative or grammatical construction, it thereby lacks any possible content and becomes an irredeemable vacuity.

The verbal possibility of being able to oppose a negative complement to every positive concept induces us to create unreal phantoms that stand in relations to true concepts, as the centaur does to the horse.

Thus, the negation of the limit does not constitute a new fact. It is merely the mental subtraction of an attribute inherent in things. We know only limited and finite objects. The infinite is a poetic creation. A realized infinity70 is an absurdity.

p. 33

In contrast, the applicability of concepts is unlimited,71 as they can be used again in each concrete case. If we were to use the simile of the tools without forgetting that, like all its counterparts, it falters, we would say: this hammer, indeed, is not infinite, but the series of blows it can [p. 33] deliver is infinite. It is a futile endeavor to attempt to reach the origin of things with a concept like cause, because no matter how far we carry mental regression or empirical investigation, it will always be applicable again. A first cause is inconceivable,72 for a cause is not a thing.

In light of these considerations, we can appreciate Einstein’s doctrine when he tells us that space is finite but unlimited. That is, that any space subject to our study is measurable, but we will never lack a space in which to locate a fact. Through very different paths from those of philosophical reflection, as a postulate of the exact sciences, Einstein offers us here an analogous conclusion, for space is a concept and not a reality.

§16

Apparently, the capacity for cognition73 should precede knowledge,74 but knowledge in fact also cannot be conceived without the cognizable.75 Knowledge consists precisely in the act of knowing,76 and it cannot precede itself. The isolated subject or isolated object are abstractions. One does not exist without the other. When the activity of consciousness77 polarizes itself, it sets one pole against the other,78 without thereby ceasing to maintain them united through mutual relations, which necessarily participate in the subjective and objective. Here there is neither an a priori nor an a posteriori. There is a confluence and concordance, a simultaneous, common action that even the most subtle abstraction cannot pinpoint.79

p. 34

In virtue of this, reasoning,80 with equally valid arguments, can deduce the necessary concepts of81 both the subjective and objective orders. Here, as always, the egocentric error goes wrong82 in that it considers knowledge to be a function of the I, instead of recognizing that knowledge is equivalent to the content of consciousness in its totality. Hence the sterile disquisitions [p. 34] of realism and subjective idealism. As evident as it is that being is identical to thinking, it is also evident that thinking is not exclusively subjective.

The exact boundary83 between both orders, the subjective and the objective, is an interesting psychological question, though a satisfactory solution remains quite problematic. We know well what falls grosso modo on one side or the other: sensations on one hand; affects, volitions, and judgments on the other. To distinguish between what in knowledge is form and what is matter, and then, on that basis, to attribute form to the subject is, however, hazardous. Form is as necessary a part of the object as its matter. To put it in Kant’s idiom (while contradicting him), we would say: both matter and form are given to us.

The subject distinguishes what is its own from what is foreign, and the subject does not attribute to itself the function of giving form to knowledge in the same way that it attributes, for instance, attention to itself. This is not a naïve impression that could be corrected, for we never possess immediate consciousness of such a form-giving capacity. It seems to me that the matter of knowledge is nothing more than a shadow of the material matter84 of the realistic dualism.85 Pure sensations do not exist.

The old scholastic distinction between material and formal elements of knowing reduces to the abstraction of primary elements from secondary ones. But both, in their unity, constitute the objective order that stands in opposition to the I. The psychic process develops in its forms, not through the intervention of the subject, but necessarily86: indeed, in spite of the subject. Spatiality characterizes the objective order and distinguishes it from the subjective.

p. 35

Until now, we have been working with a mistaken concept that we ought to abandon. The term “reality” comes from the Latin res=thing, and it involves the idea of stability. But there is [p. 35] nothing that is stable. In consciousness, we only observe a process, an action, a becoming, a continuous flowing87 and flowing-together.88 If we do not keep this fact in mind, we run the risk of again postulating things and entities89 where there are only acts.90 Subject and object are but synthetic operations in which the complex of mental states or the bundle of perceptions91 is unified. As for the substratum we suppose for them — matter or spirit — it is but a concept, not a thing.

Instead of a reality, we have, then, an actuality.92 This is the correct word that Aristotle taught us. Facts actualize themselves. They do not realize themselves. Consciousness itself is not an entity, but an action.93 And it is not even an abstract action. It is a concrete one. A pure consciousness would be one without content, that is, an action without activity,94 a perfect example of an absurdity. We must cure ourselves of all real-ism, not just naïve realism.

In philosophy, reality is an obsolete concept; that is, it is a superstition. Let us keep the word in accordance with its commonly understood meaning,95 especially to distinguish facts that are certain from those that are imagined or desired. A philosophical treatise, to be logical, should be written only with verbs, without employing a single noun.

The rigidity of names, too solid and massive, does not lend itself to conveying the notion of a dynamic process that is movement, vibration, and the development of energies and rhythms. Around the two poles of consciousness — themselves unstable and movable — swirl and boil currents that are parallel or opposing, that reconcile or resist each other, and which, in each instant, create a new fact that never was nor will again be. The need to systematize the accumulation of facts obliges us to isolate them, to abstract them, to compartmentalize them, and, in doing so, we strip the psychic process of its very synthetic life, in which each element is a function of the others. The intuition of the reader should continuously maintain the unity and correlation that the retelling96 destroys because analysis, by necessity, [p. 36] converts the active unity of that which thinks97 into the disintegrated series of that which is thought,98 and substitutes past phantoms for living reality.

§18

If the only difference between objective and subjective activity lay in spatiality, then one of the two could — though not without great difficulty — be subordinated to the other, as has been attempted with so much perseverance in the monistic systems. In truth, there is a much more fundamental difference between these two opposed currents of conscious activity.

The objective world obeys necessary rules,99 namely, laws. The subjective world lacks laws. It is free. In the objective world, a series of necessary facts100 develops mechanistically, in such a way that each fact can be foreseen. In the subjective world, the will is what acts. It wills101 what it fancies in such a way that its decisions102 cannot be foreseen. The objective obeys causes lost in the past. The subjective obeys ends103 projected into the future. Against the mechanism of the physical stands the autonomy of the I. Forgive the redundancy — autos signifies nothing but the I itself. The autonomy of the I is autonomy par excellence.

The physical order actualizes itself, inexorably chains effects to causes — amoral and insensitive, without purpose104 and without ends.105 The subject, meanwhile, feels shaken by pains or joys; it affirms or denies, creates purposes, forges ideals, establishes values, and subordinates its conduct to the ends106 it pursues.

Long before Darwin identified “the struggle for existence” as the reason behind biological evolution,137 Schopenhauer already believed that he had found the noumenal principle of the universe in the “will to live.”

A century after the Critique of Pure Reason, it should no longer be necessary to demonstrate the impossibility of metaphysics as a science.
