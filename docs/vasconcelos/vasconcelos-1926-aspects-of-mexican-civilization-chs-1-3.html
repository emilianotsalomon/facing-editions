The Latin-American Basis of Mexican Civilization

I. Similarity and Contrast

From Aspects of Mexican Civilization: Lectures on the Harris Foundation, 1926

José Vasconcelos — 1926

p. 3

Many travelers have recorded the same impression; even casual observers have seen that Mexico's most striking feature is the extreme difference in the appearance of the inhabitants, in the nature of the soil, in the climate of nearby sections, abysmal differences in the thought of the people and in the landscape of the country. Perhaps there is no other nation on earth where you can find, in the same accentuated form, a coexistence of human types separated by centuries and even by epochs of ethnographical development—people different in blood, race, tradition, and habits. And such plurality of types is not a new condition that might be attributed to the Spanish invasion alone, because long before the Spaniards landed, a mixture of peoples existed, and the Spaniards only contributed one more race-wave in the already mixed waters of our national plasma. Remember that archaeologists tell us there is no connecting link between the race that built the Teotihuacan pyramids and the Aztecs of the day of Montezuma. The Aztecs themselves were invaders and comparative newcomers [p. 3] who had overwhelmed the Chichimecs, and then the Chichimec on his part, had received practically no inheritance, at least no direct influence, from the builders of the ancient palaces. Also there is no known relation between any of the successive civilizations of the plateau and the wonder-builders of the Maya-Quiché hot countries of Guatemala, Yucatan, and Tabasco—the race that has left such a magnificent testimony of its genius in the monuments whose silent messages have not yet been deciphered.

p. 4

All countries, you may say, all old countries, show the landmarks of this uninterrupted work of history through which races arise, prosper, and then decay only to be overpowered and replaced by newcomers; but the one fact I wish to emphasize is the fact that Mexico is a land where the process of history has been one of continuous destruction and substitution of cultures instead of the regular growing and evolving of one period into the other. With constantly alternated and disconnected efforts of enlightenment, instead of a continuous cumulative progression, the Mexico of today still shows trace of the multiplied experience of our history. Independent and even conflicting manners of culture have developed in our land and sometimes have become enrooted in it; but suddenly they are driven [p. 4] away, or they disappear through decay, before they are able to evolve into a higher civilization; they are like a stem giving out a beautiful flower only to die without leaving a seed. And thus the soil remains barren until another accidental flora, independent of the preceding one, comes in turn to life. Instead of an evolutionary series of events, as you may find for instance in the history of Europe, the history of Mexico will show you a number of glorious but lonely, disconnected efforts like sparks in the darkness, and in the background a constantly recurring tragedy of destruction and anarchy. In this manner even the wise of every period find themselves unable to take advantage of the cultural conquests of the preceding epoch; and each new period through the destruction of the past finds itself in the need of building anew the whole fabric of social life. Such an overwhelming undertaking consumes, of course, the national energy and postpones the realization of truly transcendent plans.

p. 5

A series of layers composed of materials that do not mix—such is the sketch of our history. A compound of races that have not yet become thoroughly combined—such is the social condition of Mexico, notwithstanding that four hundred years [p. 5] ago, the Spaniards, although introducing themselves as a new element of complexity, still brought forth the first organized effort to blend the different peoples into one faith, one rule, one purpose. The blending process is yet unfinished either because human nature opposes such tendencies as are imposed upon it through force or because the conflicting elements are so deeply differentiated that even religion has found the task a hard and lengthy one.

p. 6

Let us now set aside for a moment the racial question in order to consider the nature of the soil. A glance at our physical structure will only add to the puzzle of the Mexican problem. A sort of elongated, immensely vast pyramid, cut at the top end, then sloping toward the two seas. On the top a temperate climate fit for the pine, the wheat, and the corn, and as you go down, all the varieties of plants and of animals, together with the unexplored unlimited wealth of the tropical jungle. All climates and all resources, just as amongst the people you will find all sorts of faces—the white man's face represented by the gallego and the asturiano of modern Spain and by the descendants of the old Castilian and the gay Andalusian—the old-time gentlemen and landowners who are gradually absorbed in blood and in temperament by the ascending indigenous wave of the mestizo and the [p. 6] Indian. Standards of living also multiply and coexist, from wealth and refinement to absolute destitution and want. A country of caste and oppositions of every sort, a country of worry and of wonder. Even in respect to moral temperament our lot varies from the saintly deeds of the missionaries to the wretched cruelties of the conquerors, and in latter days from the sternness and enlightenment of a Juarez or a Madero to the brutality of military chieftains who impose political murder as the rule of government. Such is our Mexico, a country of hope and despair; the rhythm of its very life is the rhythm of contrast.

p. 7

The nation where contrast is the rule instead of the exception—such is, I believe, the main trait of our national being. Perhaps the reason of the social contrast lies in the varied physical, temperamental, and historical factors that intervene in the making of our soul. Progress in other lands seems to be a comparatively easy affair, instead of a permanent rhythm of contradictions; a well-regulated series of efforts; an addition of similar elements in order to constitute progress; a constant flow of cumulations instead of the repeated subtractions in which we lose ourselves. For instance the original United States, with its level, rich soil, all about the same [p. 7] for cultivating purposes from New England to Georgia and to the Middle West; a land open to the flow of civilization; the tool fit for one place, being simply moved along in increased rate of production. Compare that with Mexico where the climate and the soil and the kind of the tillage change from north to south, from east to west, and within only a few miles around any given spot. Compare also the homogeneous racial stock at the beginning of this country, the unmixed European of the original Eastern colonies, developing in a virgin land; compare that with the hundred different stocks that inhabit the Mexican territory, and you will then understand why it is that our country has not been able to give a crop—it has not had the time necessary to ripen its fruit.

p. 8

But let us say from the beginning that the case of Mexico is not an isolated fact, a national situation; the case of Mexico is a portion of a larger continental situation. The case of Mexico is exactly the same as the case of Bolivia and Peru, of Chile and Venezuela and Central America. In every one of these nations physical conditions and traditions combine to produce exactly the same results. In order to be more exact in our views it will then be necessary to abandon for a moment the nationalistic pattern, which is of a very superficial mold, in order to study the Latin-American civilization in accordance with such natural subdivisions [p. 8] as spring to sight as soon as we reflect on the question. Take, for instance, the type of culture that is to be found in Peru, Colombia, or Mexico and compare it with the more simple social manner of Argentina or Uruguay and you will have a clear start in the judgment of our problems.

p. 9

Let us speak, then, of a certain whole composed of the high plateau section that comprehends Mexico, Central America, Colombia, Venezuela, Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, and Chile. Let us also imagine as one continuous portion all of the lowland occupied by Argentina, Uruguay, and Brazil. The moment we grasp the contrast of these two geographically defined sections of our continent we are in a position to judge from a point of view of reality far more significant than by reference to the political subdivisions. Let us insist upon the outline of these two natural divisions of our America, and let us connect them with a view of the northern section of the New World. We shall have, then, in the west the long highland country extending from [p. 9] New Mexico to Chile; then at both ends of the continent, and more or less in all of its eastern section, notice the expanse of the lowland countries that comprehends the larger part of Canada, the United States in the north, and Argentine, Uruguay, and Brazil in the south.

p. 10

In order to get a more accurate picture let us imagine a provisional map of the continent: a three-color map corresponding to the threefold topographical sections of our continent, or rather to the three sections made by history out of the two large divisions of lowland and highland, the lowland being inhabited by the Anglo-Saxons in the north and by the Latins in the south. We could give color then to our map by spreading, say for instance, a golden yellow over all the section of the highland, considering its closeness to the sun—a few miles of altitude, of nearness to, or of distance from, the sun meaning so much in the schedules of our planet. After covering up with yellow all this [p. 10] highland section, we could take a green color to indicate the fertile plains of the Amazonas and the Pampas, and then we could put a vivid pink upon the surface of Northeastern America, from the edge of the California-Colorado plateau, way down to Florida and up to Quebec.

Before going any farther we shall remark that there is a complete similarity between the green and the pink areas, especially in the northern and southern ends. The Canada-United States northern section has practically the same sort of soil as the Argentine and Southern Brazil territory. Both are lowland countries of temperate climate, level surface, and unlimited possibilities for cultivation. The natural problems of both countries are practically the same. Take, for instance, the wheat-growing industry; after you have succeeded in applying the motor plow and the chemical fertilizer, the only thing left is to extend your system and multiply it indefinitely from the Kansas plain to the Argentine pampa. In other words, the technique of civilization will not have to change in going through the enormous surface of the lowland continent.

p. 11

Between the northern and the southern sections there is only one factor of difference, a factor large enough though to justify the different shadow of color in our map. As everybody knows it is the Anglo-Saxon race that has created the astounding North American civilization that is changing the axis of history from the Old World to the New. On the other hand, we have in the south a curiously similar sociological situation, developing under identical natural circumstances, but with the Latin instead of the Anglo-Saxon stamina. For it is European races, a mixture of European stocks, that have been building Southern Brazil and the Pampas empire; but they are predominantly Southern Europeans instead of Northern Europeans, and the results thus obtained in the south can very well be compared with those that were [p. 11] accomplished in a similar period in the north. If you consider, for instance, the rapid development of territories like San Paulo of Brazil, if you study the figures of town and factory increase, you immediately realize that the fabulous rise of the American Middle West is being matched both agriculturally and industrially by the Latin inhabitants of Brazil, who are working under conditions similar in nature to the North American conditions. Only the fact that the Latin spirit predominates in the lowland of the south while the Anglo-Saxon spirit rules the north will justify our provisional three-colored division of the map of our continent.

p. 12

The threefold division is in reality only a provisional one, because we have in the center of the continent, or rather in the center of the lowlands of the continent, a huge area that falls in the realm of what we know as the tropical country—the hot country, practically unconquered yet by man. In fact the distinction is so important that the whole question of civilized life in the tropics should be undertaken as a separate problem. We shall have to point out in our map and consequently we shall have to imagine a fiery color spread over the surface of the whole section composed of the coast of [p. 12] Mexico and all of the Antilles, plus the coast of Central America, Venezuela, and all of the Amazonas territory.

Although we shall not go very deeply into the discussion of the tropical problem, let us say in passing that it is probably the most vital problem for the future of our whole continent—perhaps even the most vital for the future of humanity at large. The mere idea of the possibility of mastering the tropical climate and resources opens before our imagination something like the eve of a new epoch in human culture.

p. 13

A period is bound to come in which humanity, duly provided with a powerful technique, will undertake the conquest and exploitation of the torrid zone. With a highly developed equipment, man will start in the south a task similar to what was done thousands of years ago when, through the invention of fire and the use of combustion, the wild hunter succeeded in mastering and adapting for civilization the snow-covered plains of the cold and the temperate zones. The complete outline of our map will have to be redrawn; the whole process of history will change on the same day that we shall become capable of handling cold waves and chemical processes, and able to enter into the jungles of Brazil and Colombia and Mexico to impose there the rule of man and the purpose of man. But [p. 13] it would take us too far away if we stop to consider the possibilities of this sort of task. Let us only mention the fact of the existence of these four natural sections in our continent, insisting a little further upon the problem of the nations located in what we have called the highland as compared with the future of the nations living in the lowlands of the two ends of the New World and which constitute two undisputed successes—the colossal success of the United States and the Canadian Empire and the rapidly increasing success of the Brazil-Argentina Latin civilization.

First we shall try to compare the northern type of homogeneous culture with the southern type of homogeneous culture, and after that we will pass judgment on the heterogeneous, composite type of culture represented by the highland and the tropical nations.

p. 14

It is easy to notice from the start, that the differences between Anglo-Saxon North America and Latin South America are only racial, temperamental, spiritual differences. The material problems of both civilizations are very similar if not the same. Both civilizations are governed by the normal law of associated kindred efforts that combine to build a great nation. None of the two lowland territories has suffered from the tragic rhythm of contrast [p. 14] which we have pointed out as being the rule of the highland type of civilization. This alone explains why the Latin and the Anglo-Saxon, although so different in temperament, have yet succeeded in creating, each one in its own area, that wonder of the New World—the possibility of populations and cities developing overnight. The truth is that there is nothing extraordinary in this sort of achievement. Take any moderately capable branch of the human family, give it fertile plains and rivers to carry its boats and its crops and you will be repeating the old-time story of every regular normal civilization. If to these favorable surroundings you add a certain number of good seaports, then sooner or later there will arise one of those outgrowing cultures that have created the fabric of modern life. There has never been any considerable doubt expressed as to the future of Argentina, just as nobody doubted fifty years ago that the Yankees were bound to create a strong modern nation. Where the problem was to enlarge and multiply the prairies of England and the herds of Scotland, all that was required was a sort of cumulative effort, one generation adding naturally to the work of the other generation and everybody contributing to the increase of the unlimited figures of a prosperity that could be calculated beforehand. It is [p. 15] very easy to collect results from a territory of such a sort. It is very easy to be a prophet where all the elements of a national growth are given as fixed normal elements. The same can be said of temperate Argentina. Humanity has tried once and again, and always with a fair measure of success, all of this cumulative process of civilization where nothing is left to chance or to fate. But the task changes in scope and in nature the moment you leave the trodden road to enter into the trackless unknown of uncontrollable natural elements. It is then, perhaps, not so much a question of race capacities as it is mainly the nature of the territory and the peculiar task of every given period of history that one should have in mind in order to judge fairly on a given situation. To make this point even more clear, allow me to give you an instance taken from the present condition of our own American continent.

p. 16

It very often has been said that the Spanish system of colonization has proved to be a failure because none of the old Spanish-American colonies has been able to compete with the astounding [p. 16] progress of the United States. For some reason or other almost everybody accepts the belief that the Spanish system of colonization and culture cannot compare with its rival, the English system of colonization in America. But when you start to investigate the reasons of such supposed failure, immediately not one but a hundred facts and circumstances spring up to demonstrate that the Spanish encountered and solved difficulties such as never appeared before the English invader. We shall not undertake to consider this historical question, but judging the problem even by our present standards there is a very significant question that I have once before submitted to a learned audience. The question is the following: Why is it that the [p. 17] English, the Dutch, and the French, three first-class races, have had such slight success in their sleepy old colonies of the three Guianas? What have these three powerful races done in their South American lot? They have done considerably less than what the Spaniards did in the similar surrounding territories of Venezuela and Yucatan and the coast of Mexico. Why is it then that the three all-gifted nations of Northern Europe have failed in the same territories where Spain was able to achieve a considerable amount of success? Does this instance not show at least that perhaps we have been too quick to underestimate the civilizing effort of the Spaniard in tropical America, instead of realizing that our civilization as a whole is not yet prepared to meet the obstacles of heat and big [p. 18] rivers and unfavorable elements? And notice further that the Guianas are a virgin country while Mexico and the rest of the highland country is not a virgin territory, not a barren, empty zone, but a land more complicated precisely because it is filled with residues of past civilizations, haunted with phantasms of noble failures that call to our will for a new opportunity of development.

Again coming back to the lowland type of civilization represented by the Anglo-Saxon and the Latin nations at opposite ends of the continent, let us say also that, although both have in common the temperate climate and the consequent homogeneous type of civilization, there is still between them a difference in spiritual nature which is bound to give different, though in each case very admirable, results.

p. 19

It is hard to define any social subject with any amount of fairness because every definition implies a more or less arbitrary selection of certain features at the expense and to the exclusion of others. It would take volumes, simply to try to point out what we mean when we speak of the Latin temperament transplanted to America and what we mean by the Anglo-Saxon of the New World. But taking for granted that our statements will have to be incomplete and sometimes overdrawn, we are bound [p. 19] to repeat that there exists a deep, important difference between the people of Latin America and the people of Anglo-Saxon America. The difference is so much more important because it is a difference of a spiritual sort. Perhaps it lies in the manner of feeling and in the manner of expressing our feelings of life. It is in the type of our higher desires and in the manner we go about our pleasures that we can best be distinguished one from the other. The physiological, the elementary material needs of our nature are practically the same in all the human family; we all need food and shelter, but the moment the practical need is quieted through satisfaction or control, and as soon as we conquer leisure, a series of different longings and desires will arise. What is a pleasure to me, may not be a pleasure to others; and such things as delight a certain kind of people may displease or embarrass another.

p. 20

In the manner of enjoying life—there is where the thinker should search; where the artist should discover those traits that enable him to portray the soul of a nation or a period. And when one speaks of pleasure, one naturally has to take up the literature, the art, the emotion, the soul-lifting expressions of a country and its epoch. In the manner of enjoying life and in the manner of expressing the [p. 20] higher purposes of life the Latin and the Anglo-Saxon of America are different, just as the two parental races are different in the old continent. And it is only natural that this should be so, because we are a sort of readaptation of the Mediterranean standards and manners of life while the North Americans are a sort of enlarged Baltic civilization. And the fact that European civilization is a solidly inspired forward movement of humanity, notwithstanding the internal differences of Europe—indeed exactly on account of those internal differences—should teach us in America that it is possible to build a strong powerful civilization of the New World without trying to impose one type of civilization upon the other, without trying even to argue which is the better, whether the Latin or the Anglo-Saxon; for both are useful, and perhaps both are indispensable for the present and the future power and glory of the continent. It is not by suppressing differences that progress opens its way; it is through differences—deeply inspired, sincere differences—that the human soul has made all of its conquests. And the explanation is a very simple one: it is only material differences that create bitterness and rivalry among men. Spiritual differences on the contrary tend to the tightening of human bonds, because we find pleasure in every [p. 21] difference of the taste; and every peculiarity enriches the wealth of the species. In itself spiritual wealth is such that everybody can enjoy it without using it up, but on the contrary the more you make use of it the richer in value it becomes.

The fact that this continent is divided among Anglo-Saxons and Latins should be looked upon then as a blessing; because we all long for a higher, richer spiritual world, and it is only through the work of singularly gifted groups of people that a true all-comprehending type of civilization may come to life. The bigoted patriot who may dream of an all-Yankee America extending its shops from Patagonia to Canada is just as potent a foe to the true ends of mankind as the blind Latin-American chauvinist who may dream of a time of decay in which the Yankee standards of life are to be substituted by the newer southern manners of life. The one comforting fact is that differences of temperament and of soul tend to create sympathy and friendliness rather than opposition and scorn, and this should be borne in mind whenever any conflict of a material character may arise among our nations. The other evident fact is the oneness of the temperamental type of the people living south of the Tropic of Cancer.

p. 22

In the south we are one, morally and racially, [p. 22] and this unity is not destroyed through our threefold geographic, ethnographic map, just as the Colorado Mountains do not break the Yankee spirit of the northern country. Fortunately some peculiarities and modes of expression of the soul do not follow slavishly the boundaries and varieties of the soil. The highland has its own problems, and the tropic has its own problems; but above this a similar cultural tradition maintains a perfect unity between the people of the Antilles and those of the Mexican Plateau, who constitute an almost identical type with the inhabitants of Argentina, Chile, or Peru. In other words we might again present the problem by saying that the Latin culture is represented in America by the three geographical working zones we have been defining: the peculiar highland, the tropical land, and the southern lowland inhabited by Latins. If we were to draft a soul-map of the continent instead of the working map we have been mentioning, then we should have to use just one color from the Rio Grande to the Tierra del Fuego, including the Antilles, and another color for the United States and Canada; and thus we would have before us the two forms of civilization, the two manners of interpreting life, that we must try to combine in a brotherly manner if our purpose is as it should be, to create in this [p. 23] hemisphere a new cycle of history—the cycle of liberty, understanding, and love amongst all races and nations.

But again, as soon as we start to consider what Latin America really is we fall fatally into the three subdivisions already outlined—subdivisions that very plainly indicate to us the threefold task of our culture. Thus we have, as a map suited to working purposes, the four distinctive zones, the four cultural zones of the New World: (1) the North American, Anglo-Saxon zone of the United States and Canadian lowlands; (2) the highland or Sierra Madre, Andean Plateau of temperate climate and mixed Indian-Spanish populations; (3) the torrid, or tropical, zone consisting of Cuba, Santo Domingo, and Puerto Rico, plus the coast of Mexico and Central America and the hot low countries of Venezuela and Colombia, with the immense unexplored zone of the Amazonas and the Orinoco; (4) and farther south, the Latin lowlands of Argentina and Brazil.

p. 24

I have called this fourfold subdivision a working subdivision, and neither the name nor the classifications are arbitrary; on the contrary they both correspond exactly to living undeniable realities. We all know that the richest, vastest, most promising soil of the New World lies in the Torrid Zone, [p. 24] a place where nobody has yet been able to live and to prosper. This presents a task that will come into execution in some future time, perhaps close to our day, perhaps still distant; but let us have in mind that the race that shall conquer the tropics will be the master of the future. In regard to Latin-American problems we must consider that one of Latin-America's greatest handicaps has been this inheritance of the tropical zone—an inheritance that may develop tomorrow into untold wonders, but that today stands as a weight on our shoulders and as a barrier to our effort.

p. 25

It is time now to consider the problem of the highland as it appears, for instance, in Mexico. We find that Mexico, as we have already pointed out, is a mixture of climates, of races, and cultures; but still more perplexing it happens that Mexico is the zone where nature has put, one opposing the other, the two most powerful but least controllable elements: the tropical heat and the mountain. From whatever way you approach the problem of civilization in Mexico you will find the strength of these two formidable barriers of human enterprise: excessive heat in certain sections, impassable mountains in other places. We have already stated that the tropical heat may be converted in some near future into an advantage instead of a handicap. [p. 25]

Can we say the same of the mountain? I believe we can; but, of course, not under the present almost elementary condition of human technique.

Mexico is one of the most mountainous countries in the world; wherever you go, excepting only the northern end of the plateau, you encounter the sierra; you meet with the mountain in all its grandeur and its charm, with all its majesty and its might. Hard indeed it seems to establish any degree of civilized standards in the midst of a nature that seems adapted for the bird rather than for man. Building roads in such a territory, as well as tunnels, means such a terrific effort that the whole system of man's labor seems checked and postponed the very moment that you meet with the masses of the Cordillera. The best proof that we have not entirely overcome, even with our minds, all of this awe and power of the mountain, can be found in the emotion with which we read today historical accounts of the crossing of the Alps by Hannibal, or the crossing of the Andes by Bolivar's armies.

p. 26

If the occasional feat of transporting through the mountains is still considered an admirable enterprise, imagine what it means to be forced to establish a regular road-system through the ravine and the summit. Imagine what it means to be [p. 26] forced to plant and cultivate grain on the slope of the hills, under the menace of ravaging storms.

If we consider then that Mexico, like Peru and Bolivia and Colombia, is a sort of meeting place of all of the most powerful and yet uncontrolled elements of nature—the sierra and the heat, the flood and the earthquake—and if we add to these natural handicaps to the ways of man the other conflicting factors of race and history, we shall readily understand the reason for the continued and colossal failures of civilized effort in our country. And after reflecting upon these circumstances, will there be anybody bold enough to condemn a race or a series of races that have been struggling under such exceptionally difficult conditions? Are we not, on the other hand, authorized to state that we are working under the pressure of a series of obstacles and hardships such as no other people have ever encountered?

p. 27

At least we must seek in the existence of all of these handicaps the reason for many of our national traits, both the bad and the good traits. Take, for instance, our individual, our national psychology. We have been raised in the mountains; we have been living out of the mountains—that is to say, we have been depending on mining for our living and our prosperity. From Potosí to Zacatecas and [p. 27] Guanajuato the highland of America, together with the northern end of California, has been supplying the world with silver and gold. Our forefathers, after completing the conquest, devoted themselves to the extraction of the ore from the vein. They did this under the same risky, bold circumstances with which they had carried on the conquest of the land. Mining at that time was an adventure as doubtful as the expeditions and combats for the discovery of the Eldorados. Conquerors, discoverers, explorers of the unknown surfaces of the continent, and later miners who explored the unknown underground in search of Aladdin's wealth—all of them were adventurous heroes with uncertain tasks to fulfil. It is only natural that such ways of living should have affected the feeling of the race toward life. People that get into the habit of adventure necessarily develop traits of imagination and occasional daring followed by long periods of relaxation—traits such as are necessary for their particular form of living. In some respects the national character becomes spoiled; we become accustomed to await everything from the unexpected. Our psychology as a rule is the psychology of the gambler; we gamble with our wealth and with our chances in life; in fact, we even gamble with [p. 28] our lives. A popular saying is often applied whenever we hesitate before unknown, possibly unexpected, developments: A ver que sale, "Let us see what comes out of it." By it we mean not fatalism but a sort of a lottery sentiment, a decision to try fate and to force it to give out its secret and its treasure. The A ver que sale really means: "Let us put faith in the outcome; after we have done our best let the uncontrollable do its caprice." Such is our normal attitude toward life; such was the attitude of the explorer of the wilderness and the attitude of the miner of the latter day. Such is the attitude imposed on man by the mountain and by all uncontrolled forces of nature. A similar psychology might be traced perhaps even in the Yankee miner of the first epoch because nature imposed its influence upon man in this wild mysterious highland of the New World. There is a sort of sentiment which one might call the bonanza sentiment, which pervades our soul with all the strength of enthusiasm but also with the deteriorating effect of dismay of the present and doubt about the future.

p. 29

All the effects of the mountain upon man are far-reaching. The civilizations of the plains go along increasing through centuries in an added enjoyment of wealth that is only interrupted by war and jealousy of less fortunate neighbors. But a [p. 29] civilization like the civilization of our highland which has been dependent on the mining industry cannot be permanent, cannot be more permanent than the industry to which it owes its breath. Even the city that the miner builds is a temporary city. Placed as a rule on the slopes of the hills that surround the mining camp, the city has to bring from afar and has to pay dearly for its food and its water. It does not matter when the times are prosperous, but a collapse becomes inevitable the very moment the vein is exhausted. And after the vein is exhausted no attachment can be maintained with the soil, the mining area as a rule being unfit for cultivation. There is then nothing left to do but to emigrate, or at least to come down to the more hospitable valley, there to start anew, undergoing poverty after the days of lavish expenditure and splendor. Even the only remaining testimony of good times is condemned to become a ruin. I refer to the gorgeous churches, to the handsome palaces that fill such old towns as Guanajuato of Mexico and Quito of Ecuador, so many marks of the height of the bonanza wave. The overflow of gold, after spreading all over the world, finds its level in the safes of the calculating lowland agricultural and industrial people, who had the patience necessary to create savings and to organize banks. Only in [p. 30] architecture, the most noble of arts, has the miner been able to leave the permanent trace of his wandering but princely masterful soul. If, for instance, California was saved the fate of Guanajuato or Potosí, it is only due to the fact that California instead of being surrounded by unsolved riddles of the tropic found itself inside the permanent civilization of the Middle West; its present agricultural wealth being only an extension of the neighboring highly developed lowland standard of work. Left alone it would have probably fallen into the curse of the highland, into the irregular, perplexing rhythm of the brightness and shadow of ardent hopes and painful despair—the law of contrasts that constitutes the rule of life in the plateau.

What is, then, the conclusion that we should draw from such circumstances? Should we state that the soil of Mexico and the soil of the highland are unfit for a true civilization, for a powerful civilization, and are consequently bound to the tragedy of flashing prospects and then irrevocable decay?

p. 31

How could we account then for the fact that Mexico and Peru, during the seventeenth and the early eighteenth centuries, were the centers of the only civilization then existing on the continent—a civilization that created an architectural [p. 31] style like the Mexican churrigueresque that today is giving inspiration to the most important development of the present-day North American architecture; a civilization that printed the first books and issued the first newspapers of the continent; that built roads through the desert extending from the Mexican Plateau to the wild immense plains of Arizona and New Mexico; a civilization and a national wealth that had the power to overflow its territory in order to contribute to the building of forts and palaces in such distant places as the island of Puerto Rico and the missions of California? How could such an effort come to naught by break and fall? And, first of all, how did it happen to achieve what it did achieve?

All of these perplexing questions are easily understood by those who have been in Mexico, by those who have felt the fascination of the highland power. The explanation lies in that same law of contrast and irregularity, in that rhythm of the unexpected possibilities and calamities—the law to which I have been referring as peculiar to the civilization of the highland.

p. 31

It is not only the bonanza that intervenes to create among us periods of renascence and florescence and periods of decay; it is all of the factors of nature combined, that lead in Mexico to this con- [p. 31] stant uncertainty and to the corresponding periodical offspring of the higher capacities of life. The mountain and the climate constitute a handicap to [p. 32] steady, regulated progress, but the mountain and the climate also constitute a treasure. The highland succeeded when it devoted itself to its proper task, the task of mining; but when it was found necessary through the exhaustion of the mine or through changed conditions in the world-market to change from the mine industry to the agricultural or the manufacturing industry, it was found that we lacked in the highland most of the essential equipment. We had no irrigation works to protect our planted fields from the irregularities or the scarcity of rainfall; we had no combustible, no coal, to generate the steam that moved factories. For the moment—and in the life of nations centuries are like mere moments—for a moment we found ourselves in the midst of a world that had changed its tools without giving us notice. Absorbed as we were, and even infatuated with our wealth in silver and gold, we did not realize that an era was developing in which coal and steel would become more valuable than gold and would succeed in putting to its service all the gold of our mines and of the world's mines. And perhaps the main reason for the evident decay we have shown in this last cen- [p. 32] tury comes from the fact that we did not have [p. 33] at hand the gold of our times—steel and steam working together for the new period of world-culture. Our mining industry in such a situation became nothing but a luxury; and it is always the powerful, the vitally powerful, that makes the terms for the value of any luxury. Since then we have not found our way economically. It takes time and it takes suffering to change the mode of living of any people, and it is always harder to imitate than to invent. Even the ability for inventing goes along with economic demand and success. At the time of the supreme importance of the precious-metal industry, we had in Mexico the most advanced technical methods of the time. The best methods for assaying and smelting ores were local Mexican inventions. In later days how could we be expected to invent in mechanics, if we hardly knew the modern machines except through pictures in books and magazines? It is not then so much a question of race ability as a question of the world-importance of the task that every nation is undertaking in a certain given time. In the period of the adaptation of motor power to human industry we have had to be spectators. But the question now is: What is this new tool in the hands of man going to mean for us? May it not [p. 33] possibly happen that such tools, though not origi- [p. 34] nated in our territory, are still apt to render a large profit in our lands, larger even than in the place of their origin? It is true that our case might have appeared to be hopeless when machinery was moved only by steam power. We had neither the stuff out of which a mechanical civilization is made nor the necessary useful combustible. But the very instant that modern industry began to utilize electrical power instead of steam power, the whole aspect of the future changed to our benefit. Because what other country in the world can rival Mexico in the lavish, immeasurable amounts of utilizable energy that today goes to waste in the annual, natural rhythm of the fogs that rise from the sea in order to make the clouds, and the wind that takes the clouds over the mountain tops in order to cover the plateau with a rainfall that comes rushing down, every season, through the narrow passes of the ravines and through the saltos of the thousand grades of levels? And every one of these changes in level is like an inexhaustible coal mine—a perpetual source of energy, that will allow us to build, some day, one of the most potent manufacturing centers of history. So in this way, in our land of mystery and contrast, the mountain that has been a handicap is apt to become, tomorrow, [p. 34] just the treasure that is needed to give comfort [p. 35] and power to a great wonderful epoch.

Plans for the industrial electrification of countries are now discussed everywhere, but there is probably no other section of the world that is more fit for this sort of advancement than Mexico; and notice, if you will, that this advantage comes to us precisely in the combination of the two disadvantages that appeared to be fatal—the heat and the mountain. For if it were not for the heat, the clouds could not carry mountains of water to be dropped over the mountain of rock, producing thus the electrical current that is there waiting for the mastery of man.

p. 35

It is enough to consider the unlimited possibilities of a development of the sort just outlined in order to understand that Mexico is not a hopeless backward country, but a wonderful promise—more than that, a certainty that cannot be delayed very much longer. The hour of Mexico has not yet sounded, but it is there already marked in the clock of progress. The hour of the whole of the highland will be a wonderful hour. So long as electricity remains the governing power of human industry, the highland will be able to retain the leadership among nations. It would take endless space and thought to fol- [p. 35] low all the different factors that contribute to the [p. 36] distinction of the highland and the lowland, to describe what civilization means in a country like Mexico and what it means in a flat country like Argentina or Western United States; all that we can do, for the present, is to bring the main characteristics to the attention of the hearer. There are many other elements that contribute to mark the differences between the highland and the lowland, between Mexico and, for instance, Argentina and Eastern United States. The most important of these elements after the physical characteristics already discussed is the racial element. The racial element of the highland is different from the racial element of the lowland. We have already referred to the fact that the United States is racially a mixture of Northern European bloods while Argentina and Southern Brazil are composed of Southern European stock. The highland is more complex on account of the Indians, or rather on account of the different races of Indians that inhabit it; and also on account of the mestizo, who as everybody knows came into existence through the mixture of the Spanish and the Indian bloods.

p. 36

It has often been repeated that the reason for the slow progress of Bolivia and of Mexico, for instance, is due to the large Indian population of [p. 36] both of these highland countries. [p. 37] Such a statement involves never-ending questions of race-aptitude and biological, anthropological theories. One cannot deny the fact that the Indian lives in a backward condition from the economical point of view. But whenever one compares sincerely the mental aptitude or the moral qualities of the Indian with the other races of the world one is convinced that the Indian is the possessor of as many good qualities as any other living race. You will easily realize that, if you apply truly superior standards of judgment. I know the extent of race prejudice, but is it not the duty of the mind to study dispassionately and draw its conclusions in accordance with a sense of the higher meaning of life? And is not the race question, however you approach it, one in which almost any statement receives credence? A thing that happens only because we know little about it!

p. 37

Observe, though, the handicraft of the Indian; observe it in the architecture of the prehistoric times; and watch it at work in the present-day small industry of the mountain-section villages. A nation that is capable of exercising such complicated and artistic workmanship is also capable of producing any sort of mechanical, industrial manufacture. And when such a race will get the ma- [p. 37] chinery necessary for large-scale production, we [p. 38] may be sure that the native instinct for refinement and beauty will leave its imprint even in the rude results of the steel production, in the same manner that today it takes a refined worker, an artist, to complete and polish the pieces of the most powerful delicate air-motor.

p. 38

There is not one side of the question in which different views cannot be advanced. Take, for instance, a question apparently trivial—the question of good looks. The main reason why most people retain their race prejudices is because a certain aesthetical instinct leads us to search for beauty in our fellow-beings. The white race, the predominant race of today, often finds that the other racial types are ugly. The other racial types may not always find that the white man is a sort of god descended from the sun; but still, as a rule, they allow themselves to be fascinated by the white man's standards of beauty. You find almost in every one of our Indian or mestizo poets, dark of skin themselves, the ardent eulogy of the white hands and the pale cheeks of the amada. This aspiration toward the white standard of beauty may be only one of the consequences of the fact that the white race is predominant in the present era of history. To be sure the blond-pink type of beauty, popular [p. 38] today, was not known nor hardly admired by the [p. 39] pale Egyptian, whose skin, stained by the sun, had in its shade something of the shimmer of alabaster before a light. The standards of beauty change with the times and the people; but there is one thing probably certain and it is that physical beauty is closely related to comfort and peace of mind. In other words, a race of slaves cannot be beautiful because hard work and misery tend to leave their imprint on the body. There is not in all Europe a prettier stock than that Celtic Portuguese race that produces some of the best flavored wines of the world. And yet I have seen thousands of really ugly peasant women, whose faces, although enlightened with the Minervan green eyes of the most aristocratic of races, have fallen in true degradation through hard constant toil in the open field. It is no wonder, then, that a large proportion of the Indian population is decidedly plain looking. Where there is no comfort at all, the human body turns back to the beasts. Leisure and wealth develop beauty in any racial stock.

p. 39

Among the Indian races one often finds, under the common trait of a more or less dark brown skin, all the varieties of expression from the oddest and ugliest to most perfect Caucasian lines. Many a voyager knows the fascination of the Tehuana In- [p. 39] dian, whose charm resembles the effect of a heavy [p. 40] odorous wine.

p. 40

The racial problems we are mentioning in a passing way are accentuated in the highland but are not entirely peculiar to the highland. Argentina and Brazil reach and take into their bosom extensive provinces like Tucuman and El Chaco or Parana and Misiones in which racial conditions are very similar to the Mexican or the Bolivian conditions. It would then be absurd to think that the purely Latin European people of the Argentine coast were going to constitute a caste apart from the mestizo and the Indian of the interior. Such a thing would be as ignoble as to become Argentinian in order to persist as a porteño, a citizen of Buenos Aires. The process there, just as in the rest of Latin America, is of amalgamation with the native stock. The descendants of the Italian and the Pole in one or two generations become criollos, and thus fall into the common type of the continental Latin-American civilization. In this way, gradually, and notwithstanding nature's differences in soil and climate, we are becoming more and more identical. We are bound together by a common past, a common tongue, a common culture, and even our institutions and our problems are the same from the moral and spiritual point of view. [p. 40]

p. 41

With the obstacles of nature we have always count- [p. 41] ed, but at the same time the very principle of our faith was defined when Bolivar, referring to the Latin American union, stated:

If nature opposes we shall conquer nature. The time is nearing when the tropic will be mastered and when the intricacies of the highland will be converted into usefulness. We have a world-mission to fulfil. The task involves such a tremendous effort that we do not pretend that we are going to develop, alone, all this new era of history. We know that today more than ever the work of civilization requires the concerted effort, the collaboration of every human being no matter from whence he comes. Our standard then should be a free open standard—free not only from the point of view of public rights but also free from prejudice, free through its wide power of brotherly emotion—a free heart, strong, and always ready for all sorts of love.

p. 41

II. Democracy in Latin America

In order to sum up the nature of our progress as compared with that of the United States let us state that North America has developed in accordance with a law of similarity of races and efforts and conditions while Latin America is struggling along according to a sort of varying rhythm of radical changes and contrasts. The law of similarity and the law of contrast—which one is more rich in truly lofty human achievement is a question that only the future, only a comparatively remote future, can tell. [p. 41]

⁂

p. 43

A vital question in Latin America, and one that is often debated is this: Should all the Latin-American states remain loyal to their written constitutions dating from the beginnings of the nineteenth century that establish the sovereignty of the people expressed by the vote, or is it better to look for more native standards such as military dictatorships in the name of the republic or in the name of socialism? Let us say from the start that the question is only debated in such places as have to suffer from the military dictators and from those people that wish to please the military dictators. It would not be fair to state that Argentina or Brazil have any sort of sympathy for military rule; both of them are too civilized to compare even in theory the imperfect rule of democracy—imperfect though as it is—with the troglodyte methods of our military dictators. But if Argentina and Brazil and Colombia and Cuba and Costa Rica succeeded in getting rid, fifty years ago, of personal leadership and dictatorship of the type we call "caudillism"—the rule of the military—there is another large group of na- [p. 43] tions where local petty caesarism is constantly reasserting itself and maintaining hold, through ignorance, through cowardice, through lies, and through murder and theft.

And as the question of passing judgment on such methods of government, when applied to our lands, is not only discussed by local talent but also very often advocated by enlightened foreigners, I wish to take advantage of the hospitality of this University of Chicago—a hospitality generously granted to truth and the good cause of human understanding—in order to present a few suggestions to contradict the opinion of those that are constantly talking of the iron hand and the strong one-man rule that is supposed to achieve success in our more backward Latin-American countries.

p. 44

The struggle between democratic methods and dictatorial methods of government is not a new one in our continent. The Indians of pre-Cortesian times were slaves to god-like dictators, that governed the state in a manner very similar to the manner of modern military caudillos. The monarch of old represented the state; the caudillo of the times of Santa Anna represented liberalism or centralism; and the caudillo and dictator of today represents order, for instance, in Venezuela, or radicalism in Mexico. At bottom they all represent the [p. 44] one-man savage rule of the Oriental type of despotisms of yesterday and of today. In fact, there is very little difference between a Turkish pasha of Asia and a soldier-president of Latin-American history—the difference consisting in that the people of Latin America have never tolerated a long dictatorship and have always succeeded in punishing the dictator, through liberating triumphant revolutions. But revolution again brings into power the victorious revolutionist chieftain, and again we fall into the old ways and the same abuses; and so democracy, that in many cases has not even been tried, is scolded and ridiculed, only to serve better the shameful interests of one-man rule.

Such has been the development of events, but the one thing we should try to rescue from all this tragedy is the validity and the usefulness of a certain number of principles without which no nation can survive; and among these principles the democratic form of government is one of the most essential.

p. 45

One of the puzzles of old Mexican history and of old Peruvian history is the facility with which a small group of adventurers succeeded in overturning and subduing the all-powerful empires of the Aztecs and the Incas. And yet if we give the matter a moment's attention we shall find the simple les- [p. 45] son that history once and again repeats for us in its pages—the lesson which says that a despotism can never survive in a clash with a democracy. All of you remember the classical story of Sparta and Athens defeating the huge armies of despotism under Darius, the incarnate state and God of the Persians. A free parliament appointing generals that commanded free men; citizens of small republics, but subject to no man—these were enough to destroy the most powerful empire of the time.

p. 46

The Spaniards that came to conquer America were a wonderful sort of men, brigands if you like, but free brigands, independent and haughty in their own land. So when they arrived in Mexico and observed the poor Indian, trembling before the mere name of his emperor, the all-powerful Montezuma, they must have experienced a gay temptation to show the poor Indian what the god-like Montezuma was really worth. And they did so, and as soon as Montezuma was captured, the whole empire crumbled, because one-man countries and one-man situations follow the fate of the idol and pay for the sin of man-worship. The Spanish conquerors were the remnants of the army that fought the Arabs under Isabella, and Isabella was the chief of a group of more or less independent kingdoms and towns that had their own fuero, their own mu- [p. 46] nicipal liberties. At the time of Isabella it was yet true that every Spaniard felt he was as good as the king, and any two of them, better than the king, just as the famous saying puts it. So it is evident that the clash between the Indian and the Spaniard in the days of Cortes and Pizarro was a clash between the democracy of the time—one of the most genuine democracies of Europe—and despotism—one of the crudest despotisms of history, the despotism of Montezuma and the Inca. I doubt very much that the Spaniards of the time of Philip II would have been able to repeat the enterprise of conquering a world. When a nation is afraid of its own government, very little can be done against a foreign enemy or in a foreign enterprise.

p. 47

And if the feat of these bands of freemen conquering kingdoms of enslaved people is amazing, the way they organized things after the conquest is not less admirable. Every new settlement and every old town was always organized, not in accordance with the indigenous habit of dependence to a certain chief, but in the good Spanish way of organizing a city council. The free and ardent proselytism of the missionary was another civilizing and democratic influence. The religious theory of the Christian, in making us all brothers in God, is the most powerful ratification of democratic doc- [p. 47] trines. And it was only in the later days, in the days of decay, in the days of emperors that had made no conquests, emperors by chance like Charles V and monsters like Philip II, it was only then that the king's envoy, and the judge of the inquisition, began to degrade and corrupt the wonderful original organization.

Still, the undoubted fruition of Spanish culture in the America of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was due to the comparatively democratic methods of government and to the fair-minded and liberal policies of such viceroys as Hurtado de Mendoza and the Revillagigedos, who were men with the modern concept and vision of government, according to whom government is a service and a duty toward the community and not a privilege and a joy of the despot.

But with Spanish decay and with Spanish despotism at home, the colonies gradually came down to the same low political standard: the King and the Church—the King represented by the soldier, and the Church represented by the Inquisition. Both, closely allied, became supreme and governed unopposed to the misfortune of the people.

p. 48

And when the War of Independence broke out, it was the spirit of the old Spanish fuero that revived in the race; we wanted to be free as our fore- [p. 48] fathers had been free in the old communes of Castilla and Zaragoza; communes where even the rule of the Roman emperor had been very careful not to offend the sentiments of citizenship. Many of our liberators were men of purely Spanish blood, liberty-loving souls, with the old Spanish haughtiness. Many others of our liberators were mestizos who had in their souls the love of a past that was, at least in part, their own inheritance. And the pure Indians that fought our War of Independence were also men that had adapted and adopted the European standard of social life—the Christian standard of social arrangement, according to which one man is, or can be, just as worthy as the other and is entitled not only to the same rights but even to a certain amount of protection if he happens to be weak or destitute.

p. 49

Our War of Independence from Spain was then a huge democratic upheaval of the mestizo and the Indian race of America. And it shows that the Spanish civilizer had succeeded in changing the social feelings of the native. When the situation in Spain became such that people had to choose between the degenerate Fernando and Cortes de Cadiz, a Congress of Patriots, all of Spanish America, aligned itself with the men of Cadiz that wanted to re-establish the democratic traditions in Spain. [p. 49]

When these patriots were defeated in Spain, the colonies of America decided to go on with the struggle for themselves; and many of the patriots of Cadiz, Spanish though they were, truly patriotic Spanish, came over to New Spain and to Argentina to create liberated new countries, democratic countries.

p. 50

And our forefathers succeeded in liberating Spanish America from the rule of the Hapsburg-Bourbon degenerate caste—but they did not succeed in establishing working democracies in our nations. The liberators did their task on the battlefield and accomplished their duty through the promulgation of constitutions copied more or less from the democratic standard of the North American Constitution. But revolutions contain in their own dynamics a sort of fatal element of decay and reaction. Very seldom do the men that think and organize and fight for a revolution actually come into power. The struggle eats up the life, or at least the energies, of the initiator and the true revolutionist; and at the end, in the hour of victory, it is the man with greater shrewdness, the one that took less chances, the one that took best care of himself, that usually is found in the possession of the larger armed group; and then, it is he, the unknown of the struggling period, perhaps the enemy of the original revolu- [p. 50] tionists, who comes into power and naturally turns all the reforming spirit of the revolution back again into reaction and pre-war oppression. The instance of Iturbide becoming the head of the Mexican government at the triumph of our War of Independence, after, and notwithstanding, that Iturbide had fought the rebels during ten years and only turned to the rebel banner when the case of Spain was lost, simply demonstrates this rhythm of revolutions in our territory. The present-day Revolution is no exception, as the situation in Mexico to a large extent remains in the hands of people who in many cases antagonized Madero, or at least did not take any part in Madero's uprising. Many of them came into the Revolution, only after Madero had demonstrated that it was a comparatively easy job to overthrow despots; and it is only natural that such people who came to the Revolution, only after it was proved that revolutions were good business, should be trying to imitate Porfirio Diaz's methods of one-man rule rather than Madero's democratic doctrines.

p. 51

But the irrepressible self-asserting revolutionary spirit of the Latin-American nations is enough to show that we shall have no enduring peace on this continent so long as there are one-man governments and dictatorships in our nations. The strug- [p. 51] gle of the Latin-American revolutionist is the struggle of democratic European ideas to impose themselves upon the Oriental indigenous type of despotism and upon the decayed soldier-rule of the days of Ferdinand the Coward. All the petty Napoleons of our history, all the Caesars that "have never known defeat," are nothing but incubators of revolutions; and so long as they exist peace cannot exist, because the Latin-American nations are pledged in soul to the democratic ideal of liberty and equality, which is the only basis of any truly civilized form of social arrangement.

p. 52

The first man who really understood the problem of our social development was the Argentine statesman and writer Sarmiento. His views were very clearly and dramatically exposed in a book that should be read carefully by any person who wishes to understand the Latin-American sociology. The book is a novel called Facundo; the hero of the novel, Facundo, is the embodiment of that type of leader that comes when the revolution is over, claiming to be the revolution but only putting in practice, for his own benefit, the same abusive methods of the past. Facundo is the military chieftain who imposes his will through terrorism and does away with the law and with the sense of human sympathy. The only policy of Facundo is the [p. 52] blood-stained policy of the firing squad: whoever is not with him is against him, and whoever is against him should be shot as enemy of the country or as an enemy of the revolution. With the times, the name of the program changes; but the method does not change. In the time of Facundo the enemies of Facundo were classed as federalistas—defenders of the Argentine federation—and thus were shot. In latter days the enemies of a despot are called enemies of public order and shot, such was the case of the Diaz régime; in later days the enemies of the military dictator are classified as "reactionaries" and are poisoned or shot; but the system is the same—the system is despotism in one of its more brutal and degraded forms.

p. 53

Sarmiento devoted all his life to eradicate this militarism, this caudillism, from the Argentine soil. And after a long, noble struggle Sarmiento succeeded in gaining control, first, of the educational movement of his country, later, of full power by becoming the president of Argentina. Then Sarmiento, as president, managed to carry along the policy outlined in his novel years before: the policy of Europeanization of Latin America; the policy of democratization of Latin America, as against the policy of the caudillos that are constantly struggling for personal rule. In the ancestral struggle [p. 53] between democracy and dictatorship, the government of Sarmiento is the first notable success of our continent, and from the success of Sarmiento is derived the present prosperity and greatness of the greatest of the Spanish-American nations, our sister-republic of Argentina. Had the caudillos won, over the intellectual Sarmiento, the situation of Argentina would not be today any better than that of Bolivia; but after the enlightened rule of Sarmiento, the reappearance of the ignorant caudillo was made impossible.

p. 54

The unquestionable success of Argentina has very often been attributed to its temperate climate, to European immigration, to the scarcity of Indian population, etc.; but I believe that the main factor of Argentina's progress is to be found in the victory that Argentina was able to win over the military chieftain, over the caudillo that has ravaged the rest of Spanish America. And my reasons for supporting this opinion are as follows: Argentina herself was unable to take advantage of its temperate climate, wealth, etc., during the long sterile years of the Rosas dictatorship. And it was only when the democratic revolution overthrew the dictatorship that Argentina started to become a nation. Then, if we take in another section of the continent another case of success, perhaps the only case of [p. 54] permanent success this side of the equator, we find that Costa Rica never has had this type of murderous caudillo that has dishonored the history of other countries. You do not find generals in the annals of Costa Rican history, but since the early times you find, over there, fairly good schools and a general desire for progress and for civilized human manners of living.

Jumping again to the south we find that Chile, a nation that has had no caudillos, no military chieftains in power, has at the same time maintained a very fair standard of progress and a fairly civilized system of government, although in recent years the weight of the reigning oligarchy upon the working classes has become pitifully dangerous. And of course with oppression and backwardness military dictatorship is there, at present, a latent when not an actual calamity. The origin of Chile's greatness lies in the statesmanship of men of the type of Bello, an educator, a grammarian, a man of letters, in power.

p. 55

In Mexico we have always had a fine type of man: the learned man fully prepared with all the advantages of a college career and all the insight of patriotism and even of genius. We had, in the first years of our independent life, a wandering genius—Servando de Mier; but the glorious military [p. 55] caudillos of the time, true ancestors to the present glorious generals of today, kept him out of Mexico off and on for thirty years and succeeded in keeping him away from power. He was undoubtedly unfit to subscribe to the policies of the firing squad and to grab all such property as belongs to the enemies of the "cause," which means the personal enemies of the caudillo of the hour.

p. 56

The only case in which an intellectual group actually came into power in Mexico, in all the history of Mexico, is, at the same time, the only decent period of the history of Mexico and the only progressive period. In fact, it was the time when Mexico was able to write pages into the history of the world; the drama of old-time autocracy and militarism represented by Maximilian, struggling against democracy and right represented by Juarez, has become one of the subjects of the world's literature. The only real progress ever made in Mexico comes also from the time of democracy initiated by Juarez and continued by Lerdo. It was then (remember, this was in the sixties) that the first and the best railroad of the country was built, the first railroads, the first public schools; and, together with material advantages, a complete reform of the social order—a democratic, liberal reform—the organization of public life outside the bonds of the [p. 56] church; the drafting of educational plans; the establishment of compulsory primary teaching; the moralization of the personnel, and the methods of handling public funds. It has always been noted that the generation of politicians and statesmen that accompanied Juarez in his task after years of public service did not accumulate wealth and never produced the type of the revolutionist hacendado and financier. They did produce splendid financiers like Dublan and Matias Romero, men who reorganized the fiscal systems of the country and brought money into the national treasury after covering all public needs; but they never produced any financier such as military governments develop—the financier who dismisses clerks because he cannot pay the government services and closes schools because it is necessary to economize, but who himself is able to emerge from the absolute destitution of his prepolitical obscurity to the position of millionaire and nouveau riche of an impoverished nation.

p. 57

Most people believe, particularly outside of Mexico, that the most progressive period of our history was due to the iron rule of Porfirio Diaz. I do not deny some of the strong qualities of Porfirio Diaz, particularly after one observes his imitators succeeding by copying all of his vices and none [p. 57] of his virtues. The main virtue of Porfirio Diaz was personal honesty and decency regarding money affairs; he never used his political power for accumulating wealth; in fact he was a poor man all his life, and only in the later years, during the decay of his rule, did he become wealthy through the presents of subject politicians. But he would not have been able to retain power for such a length of time if he had suffered from that base passion and greed of his later disciples.

p. 58

Admitting then that Porfirio Diaz was probably the best possible type of a despot, yet we shall have to condemn him and his system the moment we stop to think that any despotism of a personal character is limited to the period of life of the despot. And the despot leaving no political inheritors—such as only monarchs can leave—is always unable to take care of the future. So I feel entitled to say to all those defenders of the iron rule, to all the believers in personal rule and caudillism: If you do not want to see another cycle of revolution and chaos, such as the one we are now passing through in Mexico, do not lend any assistance to the personal aggrandizement of any Porfirio Diaz whether spurious or authentic, because every Porfirio Diaz is bound to bring behind him a new era of hate and destruction and chaos. [p. 58]

It is only through democracy that all of our problems will be solved; no other system of government has proved successful anywhere else. The fact that so many of the Latin nations are falling today into the hands of dictators constitutes only a sad sign of decay. Fortunately, we can still turn for comfort toward the most advanced of the Latin nations, to nations like France and Argentina and Brazil. May their example help us and may it be sufficient to restore the confidence of those who believe that we are capable of rising to the standards of free government.

One of the most dangerous factors that we have to deal with is the hypocrisy with which we make use of certain names. I prefer the frank dictator who defies public opinion to hypocritical leaders who claim to have respect for certain principles but who go along exercising power from a hiding place and through the hand of some ally or accomplice. The case is beginning to be so frequent in our America, that it is necessary that we should define what is to be understood by democratic government and what is meant by autocratic, militaristic, dictatorial government.

p. 59

The answer is very easy in the case of most of our dictatorships. Our dictatorships distinguish themselves from other countries' dictatorships by [p. 59] their hypocritical character. Almost all of our dictatorships disguise themselves under the mask of republicanism or under the mask of social reform and radicalism. But the one unequivocal test to differentiate the dictator from the democratic ruler is found in what we call the exercise of facultades extraordinarias—"extraordinary powers." The exercise of facultades extraordinarias is equivalent to the decreeing of court-martial proceedings throughout the nation, and, more than that, because the extraordinary powers in the field of war and human life are always extended to the use of extraordinary powers in finance, which means that any tax can be levied, any confiscation can be made, through the order of the president-dictator alone.

p. 60

It is of no account then that the constitution may guarantee property and life. The constitutional rights are suspended, in order to save public order sometimes, in order to save the revolution on other occasions, in reality only in order to save the personal rule of the despot. Based on this constitutional suspension and on these extraordinary powers in war and finance, citizens are dragged out from their homes by order of a president, to be sent into exile or to face the firing squad. Political parties are dissolved through imprisonment and murder. The press is silenced, the bigger newspa- [p. 60] pers go on publishing stories about happenings in Japan and Roumania, but are very careful not to touch on the events of the hour. And then as the crowning victory of the military régime, one day the papers announce that all the properties of the enemies are confiscated in order to pay for the expense of putting down the rebellion or the expense of the prosecution. This last expense is never very considerable as the firing squad is under a regular salary and subject to permanent appointment, as part of an army that is the basis of the caudillo conception of the state.

Treaties, international treaties, are concerted with the foreign powers, in which the despot makes exception to the same principles he is supposed to be defending, provided these treaties are accompanied by a wave of persecution of the political enemies that have sought refuge in the foreign land.

p. 61

Large fortunes can be made by the caudillo and his friends through the facultades extraordinarias. How? I shall give you an instance: The facultades extraordinarias in hacienda—in finance—means that the government can change the tariff from one day to another according to "public needs." With the tariff at his command, on twenty-four hours' notice the caudillo becomes a business man. And take for [p. 61] instance grain, one of those products that are sold at large in the foreign market, in the United States market: when the time of the crop comes, the caudillo decrees a high defensive tariff, a large tax on exportation, "in order to protect the local needs" he will always be careful to state. Naturally the prices of the foodstuff are considerably lowered. It is, then, that the caudillo through some representative, or personally, goes around to the dismayed producers; offers them a little more than the ruinous market price; poses as a protector of their interests; and, as soon as he has got hold of all of the crop, communicates with the finance department. One day, all of a sudden, the public and the producers learn that the tax on exportation of the cereal has been eliminated, in order to protect prices that had become too low, and for the good of the local producer and the public good, as the corresponding decree will always be careful to say. Such things occur repeatedly in the caudillo countries, and they explain by themselves the causes of this fall of production that always accompanies the military rule. It also explains the rapid increase of wealth of the caudillo clan. Men, who in private life had not been able to exceed the level of a second-rate salaried clerk, men who had arrived at the forties without showing any special business [p. 62] ability suddenly develop what to uninformed observers is a sort of unexpected genius, making millions out of poverty, out of the impoverishment of the producing class. But, of course, there being no free press, public opinion being entirely scared by the regular, pitiless, man-target services of the firing squad, there is nothing left but the abject admiration of a subdued population, hungry and enslaved.

Every once in a while, there is in any caudillo country a talk of revolution. As a rule, such talk is very useful to the governing group because it gives it an excuse for a sort of razzia on the enemies' lives and properties. A general confiscation is decreed of the properties of the enemies of public order. Even the savings of widows have in many instances been taken over. The natural consequence of such a system is that nobody cares to invest in the country; even bank deposits flock to foreign safes, going to the United States or to Canada for protection.

p. 63

Take for instance the land question. The caudillo promises to give the land (he usually gives other people's lands to his closer associates) and a good deal of talk goes on about all sorts of agrarian theories, but this is only temporary. As soon as the caudillo and his clan have a chance to buy, at the lowered price brought about by condi- [p. 63] tions, some of the best lands of the country, then the caudillo issues manifestos protesting that he is not a bolshevist. In fact, he has become landed proprietor and lord, the possessor of the best seeds, of the best breeds; the promoter of roads and irrigation canals; the creator of a hybrid capitalism that naturally is subject to all the contingencies of revolution and revenge.

If you compare this brief statement of conditions brought about in modern times by the caudillo rule with the conditions described in the book of Sarmiento, which I have already referred to, you will see that the Facundo of sixty years ago is still alive in the caudillo countries, in the countries of military régime, and that only the phraseology of despotism has changed—instead of republicanism and liberalism, socialism and promises of enrichment. The essentials of the caudillo régime have not varied.

p. 64

The long protracted struggle between the military form of government, between the Oriental forms of government, through one man's will, and the European, democratic form of government is still going on in many of our countries. We shall never eradicate revolution from our countries so long as we do not do away with personal government. We shall never be great nations until we follow the [p. 64] example of Sarmiento in Argentina, who did away with the caudillo and the military through the extension of schools and the increase of immigration. But Sarmiento was a learned man, a college man, one of the lights of his century; and it always takes a man like that to reform a country. The unprepared, the semi-illiterate, the men that begin to read when they are in government, can do nothing but perpetuate the ever recurring synchronism of terrorism and anarchy.

p. 65

It has become, in modern times, a sort of a fashion to criticize democracy, especially for the service of personal rule. The defects of democracy are carefully pointed out by the salaried second-rate intellectuals that put themselves at the service of the caudillo, and then the consequence is drawn that there is no use in fighting for democracy and that democracy is a failure. Let us repeat that democracy has proved to be defective, especially because it has found itself helpless in the hands of overambitious capitalism. But, can you compare the results of this defective democracy with the results of the Oriental systems of government, with the personal systems of government? I wish that all the professors of history would answer this question and would give their answer to the public. There is not one case of permanent success under [p. 65] the personal system of government. And even in such limited cases of success, doubtful cases as may be pointed out, it is only such personal systems of government as have succeeded in evolving toward impersonal systems, that can show any amount of contribution to human progress. As a rule it has always been the most democratic form of government that has created the larger, the more long-lived empires of history. And also, naturally, it is only under democracy that the average man's standard of living is raised. Despotism on the contrary, as well as caudillism, tends to perpetuate the caste system. Every new caudillo carries behind him a family clan that robs and sacks the country in order to constitute the new aristocracy, the new god-like caste, the fonctionnaire caste of the period.

p. 66

Let us insist that even if we wish to correct the vices of democracy, we must first of all have democracy, for it is only through democracy that we can achieve social progress. It is only through democracy that the constructive socialism of modern times has been made possible. And through dictatorship you obtain either the Latin-American caudillism or Russian bolshevism, that is to say, the caste system—the military caste of the backward countries of our America and the bureaucratic caste of bolshevist Russia. Both of them represent [p. 66] Orientalism as against European and Christian standards of life. The law of violence against the law of right, or, as you might put it, in venerable words: "Might above right": such is caudillism and such is bolshevism. Yet, I must say that it is unfair to identify bolshevism, which is a theory of government, with caudillism, which is no theory at all, but a personal aggrandizement of the few at the expense of the many. The caudillo has no theory at all. The caudillo does not even see the contradiction between his radical speech and his greedy insatiable hand.

p. 67

All of the political problems of Latin America, and very particularly the political problem of Mexico, can be summed up in these two words: "democracy" or "dictatorship." You will always find among the semi-intellectuals that live on the government, many that in every time of crisis will say, as all slaves always have said: "Let us sacrifice all government principles, let us give up the constitution in order to save the nation." The nation of course means for them their salary and their position and their growing wealth and the person of the caudillo, or the dictator of the hour. Fortunately this sort of people is only a dead weight in the tonnage of the despot's vessel. They actually do not count in a time of strife. [p. 67]

And it is also very fortunate to note that although Latin-American militarism has a perfect resemblance to Oriental despotism in its practices of government, Latin-American life, far stronger than any Oriental stupor, very seldom allows a long reign to the victories of might.

One of the advantages of the Latin temperament is the capacity for sudden all-purifying change. The same stock that produces the Porfirio Diazes is able also to produce the Maderos; with the despot, the liberators. Is not that the perpetual struggle of civilization, particularly in countries that have not yet fully crystallized into being? Perhaps one good sign of the times lies in the fact that modern Porfirio Diazes, although despising principles, do not dare go against them in the same cynical form of the old, but hide behind them and, instead of installing themselves indefinitely in office, put into office dummy personages—human tools that are easier to overturn, although temporarily they do more harm because the true master is able to dictate without the responsibilities of government.

p. 68

A person used to the democratic forms of social organization will probably ask if there is no way to correct this vice of the dictatorial government, other than with the revolution that destroys the [p. 68] person of the despot. Is it not possible to seek redress from the courts? Is there no way in which a man can get protection from the abuses of power? Let us remember that the courts in this sort of régime are at the mercy of the dictator. Our beautiful legal systems empower the judges to issue injunctions, and to stop the effects of every executive order; but it always happens that the order of the supreme court arrives too late—it usually arrives after the prisoner has been executed. Besides, the extraordinary faculties include even the power to overrule the supreme court; and in easier, more normal cases, when only money interests are at stake, if the dictator wishes to have a certain case decided and the judges happen to delay a decision, the pay of the judges is retained on the ground that the necessities of the military campaign have made it necessary to dispose of all of the funds, or with no excuse at all.

p. 69

No liberty, no administration of justice, no guaranty to lives or properties; the powerful of today converted into a pariah by a whim of the dictator, the whole nation depending on the health or the caprice of one man—such was the way of Facundo; such is the way of the iron ruler and the dictator—a system that still has followers in Venezuela and in Mexico, in Peru and Guatemala—a [p. 69] system of government that always ends by putting us at the mercy of a foreign rival; such is the manner of government, that has to be combated and exposed and annihilated by all men who wish to see on this continent the benefits of civilization.

Whoever is the enemy of our countries will stand by the dictators; whoever is friendly will abstain from friendly intercourse with them. And the duty of all of our patriots has once and again been defined by our guides and statesmen, by the Sarmientos and the Montalvos and the Maderos, men who know, that it is only fair to discuss and to criticize democracy after you have won democracy. But before you have it, there is only one way open to any country that wishes to prosper and is deprived of democracy, and that way is the way that leads to the winning of democracy.

p. 70

The fact that some other countries of our race have undergone misfortunes similar to ours and have been able to overcome them in a comparatively short time; the fact that Mexico and Peru have had several periods of honest, able, and free government, should give us confidence and should induce us to try once again for salvation. In order to make democracy a solid practice in our land we only have to follow the example of Sarmiento, the example of Juarez and of Madero. This means two [p. 70] main remedies—one that is slow to show results: public education; the other: political propaganda in favor of civilized forms of government put into effect by honest civilian leaders. You cannot educate, however, until you get a clean government, because moral authority is the main force of the educator, and this moral authority cannot be shown by the agent and the follower of dictatorship. Political propaganda and sacrifice to establish the rights of citizenship ought to be, then, the first means of salvation.

The fact that even England, the modern model of representative government, had its seventeenth century, with a condition similar to some of our Latin-American situations ought to remind us of the fact that any race may fall into chaos but that the action of moral forces, properly organized, is always strong enough to raise any people to the highest standards. The Latin-American people have proved themselves, once and again, capable of moral altitude and sacrifice; we may then feel certain that the sun will rise to dissipate the shadows of night.

p. 71

III. The Race Problem in Latin America

I have tried to show you the inside of the Latin-American dictatorships, which means the obstacles to democracy establishing themselves fully on the continent. My indictments are not particularly di- [p. 71] rected at one man or one country but to all men and countries that may be living in the situation I describe. I feel further bound to say that no matter what the outward attitude of a special leader-dictator may be, you will always find that he has received or is receiving undue support from foreign influences. A dictator cannot last long if he fails to retain foreign help and support. The imperialist and the dictator always find ways to come together, as their end is the same—the enslavement and exploitation of the people for the benefit of a small powerful group. Back of every dictator you will always find a small group of devoted foreign friends that go into their own countries and if necessary criticize their own government in order to support the actions of the great man who is all alone in trying to save a backward nation. The people of such a nation are described as ignorant, illiterate, reactionary, but the despot—the despot is the exception, the genius, the superman. Small sums of money are paid for this sort of childish propaganda, but larger sums are spent in paying for legal services to influential politicians who become busy in defending not the nation that pays them but the ruler who defies public opinion both in his country and outside of his country. The fact that the extraor- [p. 72] dinary powers exist makes it impossible for anyone to find out how much money is spent in this way. The occurrence of such a fact is so frequent that I believe we should devote attention to it, particularly because a good deal of the ill feeling amongst the people of the oppressed nation comes from the fact that the despot actually gets support from the foreign power when a time of crisis develops. The foreign writer, especially the writer friendly to the despotic régime, actually offends the sentiments of the oppressed nation when he speaks with praise of the dictator or his methods, or even when he claims, more or less contemptuously, that such a government is good for such people. There is no thinking American who desires to see a dictator in his own country. Let us then not do unto others what you do not wish to have done unto you. After all we must always go to the gospel for guidance and advice. Not even material benefits can ever be derived from a rule that shows no respect for moral commands and human duty toward our fellow-beings. The despotic rule always fails—even the great Porfirio Diaz failed. Why should we then put any hope in his inferior imitators? The theory that democracy is good for this country but is not fit for such and such another country does not find support in facts. The Argentina of the days of Facundo was as miserable as any [p. 73] of the later caudillo countries, and in only one generation, an enlightened intellectual generation, the whole moral contour of the country was changed. The facility for quick, enlightened change is a Latin-American virtue. Democracy is the only form of government fit for any country of the New World. [p. 74]

p. 75

Many theories have been advanced as to the origin of the old inhabitants of this New World. The discoveries of Professor Hrdlička, relating to strong similarities between a certain Siberian tribe and the North American Red Indian, may be entirely correct, but they do not exclude the possibility of the existence of some more autochthonous stock coming from the very far south, from Patagonia, as the Peruvian legends seem to indicate. Certain similarities that have often been pointed out between the Maya-Quiché architecture and the Egyptian manner of building would tend to confirm the opinion of the believers in the Atlantis of the classic days; and finally, the theory of Professor Wegener regarding the original unity of all continents in one body which has been separated as a result of the earth's rotation would clear away all sorts of difficulties, for it would allow us to state that there is no more mystery attached to the aboriginal American than the mystery that surrounds the whole of the human race. Farther back than five thousand years we do not know how we lived, nor where. [p. 75]

p. 76

We can, however, assert that in the New World, just the same as in the Old, a series of civilizations have developed and decayed. Civilizations come and go, but it is very probable that the human stock, the particular race that creates a certain period of cultures, does not disappear with the disappearance of its constructive power. The social development of a certain people may stop entirely while the race itself goes on reproducing its members. The Greeks of the Roman days had disappeared as a ruling type of civilization but continued to exist as a race and traveled and mixed with other races; and the original stock thus became modified but never totally destroyed. The same thing can be said, I am sure, of the Indian. When Cortes arrived on the continent the Indians of Montezuma did not know themselves who had been the builders of the Teotihuacan pyramids that were lying covered with the dust of centuries a few miles away from the old Tenochtitlan—the Mexico City of today. But there is no doubt that a great number of these Montezuma Indians had in their veins the same blood of the builders of the forgotten monuments. The Aztecs, who were comparatively newcomers, may not have had it, but the strata of the older races inhabiting the valley were no doubt inheritors of the flesh and the soul of the high-type [p. 76] ancestors. The invader never succeeded in destroying all of the subjected population, but had to coexist with them and naturally intermarried and learned from the older inhabitants. In the same manner we can affirm that, although the natives of Yucatan and Guatemala had forgotten all about the history of the builders of the Maya-Quiché palaces, they still have in them the soul of the ancient architects. It is absolutely no wonder that a historical tradition should get lost in a time in which there was no written language; and even such written characters as existed were transmitted only through verbal tradition, with consequent danger of a total loss of the key to the symbols through periods of war, emigrations, and calamities.

p. 77

The fact I am trying to emphasize then is that our race, the Indian races of the tropics at least, do not constitute a primitive stock. Call it, if you will, a decayed stock, but not a primitive stock. The New Mexico Indian may be primitive, although I would rather say that instead of being primitive he is provincial; that is to say, that what remains of his culture is derived from the little he could obtain, placed at such a distance from the higher civilizations of the southern sections of Guatemala and Mexico. I maintain that the rhythm of civil- [p. 77] ization at that time, as well as in the time of the Spaniards and way up into the seventeenth century, the rhythm of civilization in the New World was, and had been, from south to north instead of being, as it is today and as it has been since the Declaration of American Independence, from north to south. Since the beginning of the nineteenth century, the Latin-American countries began to take from the United States their political institutions, their school systems; and later we have taken the railroads, the machinery, and finally the capital for the development of our resources; but in the preceding centuries, during the day of the Indian and during the day of the Spaniard, it was the native of Arizona who had to march down to the south in search of tools for his wool-weaving and in search of inspiration for the drawings that decorate it. In the time of the Spaniard, the mission and the tillage both came from the better architecture and from the more advanced agriculture of Central Mexico, then the mother of Latin-American culture. In South America we find a similar, though inverted, movement. Civilization there centered about the semitropical and about the temperate plateau of Cuzco and scarcely reached the deserted plains of what is today the great growing Argentine nation. But the Aztec and the Inca sections were [p. 78] at the time not only the most densely populated but also the centers of the culture of the New World, the creators of civilization of that period. Our Indians then are not primitive as was the Red Indian, but old, century-tried souls who have known victory and defeat, life and death, and all of the moods of history.

p. 79

I wish to call your attention to the fact that the Indians of Mexico as well as the Indians of Peru represented a certain type of civilization and consequently were not, as the North American Indian, simply tribes of natives, wandering tribes of hunters, because this in itself perhaps explains why the Spaniard had to mix with the Indian, while the Englishman did not mix but simply forced the Indian back. Whenever two civilizations meet, one or the other becomes predominant, but they both undergo a change; they both lose certain traits and win others. What had happened to the Spaniard during the Arab invasion happened to the Indian during the Spanish invasion. There is a difference of course in degree, and, as we shall see later, a very surprising difference in results; but the truth is that the social, the historical process of the Spanish conquest in Mexico was about the same as any of the Asiatic or European invasions by which one race obtained control and predominance and the other [p. 79] was subjected, but both going along living in close contact and modifying each other through that contact.

p. 80

The case of North America, as we all know, was very different. This was not an invasion, at least not a sudden overwhelming invasion, but a long penetration of the territory without conservation of the native stock, and consequently without social contact or any other relation with the Indian. This difference is the origin of the policy and of the practice of what we may call the one-race standard as against the mixed-race standard. By that I mean this undeniable fact, that the civilization of North America is a one-race civilization, a white-race civilization as you insist on calling yourselves, sometimes even to the exclusion of other whites such as, for instance, the Spaniard. A white civilization that may contain, and does contain, millions of other racial stocks such as the Negro, but does not consider such dissimilar stock as a part of itself and does not as a rule intermarry with it. The Negro here, as well as the Indian, is in a world apart socially and is a body that is connected only politically with the white population. Of course nobody can deny the deep influence, and a gradually increasing influence, of the Negro upon the American mind—an influence that gives you [p. 80] the music to which you dance, and the laughter that helps your happiness, two heavenly gifts, lavishly spread by the socially isolated Negro.

p. 81

On the other hand we have, in the south, a civilization that from the beginning accepts a mixed standard of social arrangement not only as a matter of fact but through law, since the Indian, after being baptized, became the equal of the Spaniard and was able to intermarry with the conqueror. The wedding of Cortes—who is accused of having murdered his Spanish wife in order to marry, or at least to live undisturbed with, his Indian love La Malintzin—is symbolical of the new state of affairs and of the whole of the race situation in our country. The example of Cortes in taking an Indian woman for his wife was followed by many others; it went on then and has been going on since; but the first time that the world realized what had happened in America was, I believe, when the first writer of the new race appeared in Peru. About the middle of the sixteenth century the learned public of Spain began to read with curiosity and amazement the works of a celebrated mestizo, surnamed El Inca—El Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, a historian, a man of letters, perhaps the first man of letters of the New World. The writings of El Inca, [p. 81] very entertaining and fascinating reading even today, dealt with childhood memories of the life of his mother, an Indian princess, and his father, a Spanish captain. El Inca also wrote some tales and narratives about the conquest and about the feelings of the defeated natives; he heard and repeated in his writings many old stories taken from the lips of cacique ancestors; he impregnated himself with all the legend and all the sentiment of the dying Indian civilization; and then, while still a young man, he traveled through Spain and learned and adopted Spanish ideas and also learned to love Spain and Spanish European culture. His mind, as his own blood, became then the hyphen, the meeting point of this Spanish-Indian tragedy, all of which through his genius he succeeded in transforming into a new, broader concept of life. And it can be fairly said that in the days of Garcilaso there was no greater mind in this New World, either among the Indians or among the Spaniards, than the mind of this half-breed, who was struggling to make one mind of the two conflicting mentalities of the New World. And with Garcilaso, the spiritual alliance, the spiritual blending, of the Indian and the Spaniard was sealed forever.

p. 82

Since then, more and more every day, the fortunes of the Spanish-American countries have been passing into the hands of men that acknowledge [p. 82] Garcilaso as their spiritual ancestor. We have been at heart Spaniards even when we have had to fight against Spain, and we remain Indian even when our skin accidentally becomes whitened through marriage with the more recent Spanish stock. In this way the half-breed cannot entirely go back to his parents, because he is not exactly as any of his ancestors; and being unable to connect fully with the past, the mestizo is always directed toward the future, is a bridge to the future. No country can show, better than Mexico can, all of the signs and the effects of this peculiar mestizo psychology.

p. 83

Notice the fact that the mestizo represents an entirely new element in history; for if it is true that in all times the conquered and the conqueror have mixed their bloods, it is also unquestionably true that never before had there come together and combined two races as wide apart as the Indian and the Spanish, and never before had the fusing processes of two such different castes been made on such a large scale. History had never witnessed this process of two unrelated breeds intermingling and practically disappearing in order to create a new one. According to some observers, all of our backwardness, all of our difficulties and unfruitful struggles are derived from the fact of this unsound and even contemptible mixture of races. A noted [p. 83] philosopher, who for a time was the master even in our own mestizo universities, the Englishman Spencer, specifically pointed to us as an example of a hopeless hybrid product of the violation of the sacred scientific rules of a purifying, uplifting evolution. At any rate, the English have always stood for the conservation of an original unmixed human stock and have succeeded in maintaining it; and the Spaniards have always disregarded this purely white prejudice and have actually created the millions of the mestizo stock of America and of the Philippines. The unlimited progress of the Anglo-Saxons in civilization in the late centuries has led to the belief in the soundness of their policy. Many even among ourselves felt as though we had to admit that racial hybridism could only produce a low type of humanity, an inferior stock that could only be aided by the constant increase of a renewed wave of white people such as has flooded, for instance, the unpopulated territories of Argentina. Such is still the belief of some Latin-Americans of the purer European stock; and there are many mestizos who feel that all of their vows should favor the elimination of the native strain. [p. 84]

p. 85

To me this last attitude is nothing but a case of cowardice. We are dazzled by the power of the world's civilization at the present moment; and we forget that there is in hybridism something deeper than can be observed by the superficial thinker. It is very easy to agree with success; it is very easy to philosophize with the partial conclusions of a given moment of history. But if we think with our heads and if we search with our own souls into the mysteries of human destiny, we immediately find that the so-called pure-race theory is nothing but the theory of the dominating people of every period of history. And the pure-race theory leads, very often almost fatally, to the parental marriages, to the incestuous marriages of the Pharaohs. If we observe human nature closely we find that hybridism in man, as well as in plants, tends to produce better types and tends to rejuvenate those types that have become static. If we go through history we find that after a period of adaptation the results of the renewal of blood are always beneficial. Even some of the most modern authors on the subject, such as the Frenchman Pittard, conclude that a pure race is a myth because all nations are the result of numerous mixtures; he goes farther and states that "it is only the poor devils" that can claim a nonmixed pedigree, because only the lower classes amongst retired nations marry between themselves, while the powerful of any group always enrich their experience by marrying some of the prettiest or [p. 85] more attractive women of the neighboring tribe. The advantages of a mixture of races has then been generally recognized; race prejudice as it exists today is a comparatively modern feeling and originates perhaps from the necessity for the English colonizer of far-distant territories densely populated by dissimilar races. The Englishman, with his highly developed social instinct, understood that if his colonizers of the distant territory of the Indies began to marry the native women, very soon the European in them would become absorbed and the next generation would become lost for the Empire. The Spaniards were bolder; for the decision to accept the results of intermarrying with the native was not without forethought, but perfectly calculated, as is shown by the long discussion that preceded the papal statement to the effect that the Indians were entitled to receive the sacraments of the church, marriage among them.

p. 86

Now if we compare the results of the English policy in India with the results of the Spanish policy in America, even at a time when the British policy is not yet ripe, not having had the centuries of the Spanish colonization's experience, I believe that we shall be justified in declaring that the cultural results of the Spanish method are superior. The Spanish have succeeded in reproducing their [p. 86] blood in part and their culture in full in twenty nations that are today about as Spanish as Spain itself can be, though independent politically and socially. The English, on the other hand, with their system of not even maintaining social intercourse with the natives of India, are today as completely strangers in India as on the day their ancestors first landed; and it does not seem probable that they will ever succeed in eradicating the Indian, to substitute for him the Islander. We find in India a coexistence, a juxtaposition, of cultures that do not mix, in the same manner that the bodies of the two races remain fatally apart. Which system is ultimately the better, even from the selfish point of view of the conqueror, is something that only the future can answer. We do not pretend to give advice, but we are bound to accept the Spanish method, which in a way has created our nationalities and is the very reason of our existence as a race and a branch of the human family. We cannot condemn the Spanish method without denying ourselves.

p. 87

The founders of the United States were fortunate in not finding in this territory a very large Indian population, and so it was easy for them to push the Indian back; but the importation of the Negro has brought to this nation, as we all know, [p. 87] a problem harder, no doubt, than any known before. And the North Americans, who are the result of a mixture of all the European races, have followed the English system in regard to the Negro, that is to say, the system of strict avoidance of matrimonial relations with the colored race. The Spaniards did not obey this rule of abstention even with the Negro; the population of many of our tropical sections is largely mulatto, a mixture of the Spanish and the Negro; the Portuguese have also created a mulatto population in Brazil; so here again we find the Latin system of assimilation and intermarriage and mixture opposed to the Anglo-Saxon method of matrimonial taboos and pure-race standards.

p. 88

If the tremendous problem involved in this coexistence of two races that live apart in the same home could be considered as finally solved in this country, then there would be a good argument for those who claim that Mexico should try to do away with the mestizo and the Indian population by importing millions of Europeans; but the fact is that you cannot destroy a race, that you cannot change social conditions at will, and that you have to face the problem not only with the brain but also with the heart and the superior instinct of nature. And at least for us in Mexico, it is too late to change our [p. 88] practices. There is nothing left for us to do but to follow the Spanish tradition of eliminating the prejudice of color, the prejudice of race, in all of our social procedures. No matter what our theoretical opinions might be, we have to start from the fact that the mestizo is the predominating element in Mexico.

p. 89

But the mestizo of Mexico does not hold an undisputed reign over the country. Once in a while you still hear in Mexico an echo of the Indian voice that claims a return to the past of the race as a means of obtaining strength and inspiration. The claims of the pure Indian sound sometimes almost as distinct in its vision as the creed of the most ardent advocate of the purity of the white in his own country. And the evidence that this is not merely a theoretical feeling is found in the story of our revolutions, which in some cases have developed purely Indian movements with the tendency to reinstate purely Indian standards. The Indian uprisings of Yucatan, known as the guerra de castas, the "War of Castes"—the pure Indian against the mestizo and the criollo, against the Spanish-speaking, against the Mexican population—is an old but clear example. The Zapata movement of the last revolution clearly contained the seeds of an Indian revival over the whole extent [p. 89] of our country. There was a time when European dress was not allowed in the Zapata territory; and those Mexicans of white Spanish skin that happened to join the Zapata armies had to adopt the dress and the manners of the Indian, in a certain way had to become indianized before they could be accepted. But the weakness of the pure Indian movement lies, of course, in the fact that the Indian has no civilized standards upon which to fall back. He has no language of his own, never had a language common to all of the race. And the advantage of the Spanish method of colonization through assimilation is here again demonstrated, I believe, in the fact that the Spanish spirit is still gaining victories over the native Indian spirit through its language, through its religion, and its social forms of living. Even the more radical leaders of the Zapata revolt, pure Indians like the school-teacher Montaño, who was the brains of the group, were expressing themselves in good Spanish and were basing their economic theory of the distribution of the land, etc., on the terms of the European manner of life. At the same time the mass of the revolted Indians was carrying, as in the days of Hidalgo, the image of the Virgin of Guadalupe as a banner. They were being Spaniards even against their will and their knowledge; and it is only natural [p. 90] that all of this should happen, as they had no other tradition upon which to fall back.

p. 91

On the other hand, the movement, although very conscientiously prepared, was overpowered by the stronger mestizo element of the armies of Carranza, of Obregón, and of Villa, who represented the Mexican rather than the Indian, that is to say, the Spanish American, the Indo-Spaniard who also prevails in Central America, in Peru, and in Bolivia. You may ask if the criollo, the descendant of the pure Spanish blood, has not also tried to gain control in Mexico, and I believe we can answer that the criollo has made efforts to predominate and also has failed in such efforts. I believe that such tendencies as those represented by the group that aligned itself with Maximilian were mainly European, Spanish tendencies. And to defeat them, the mestizo and the Indian joined under the leadership of the Indian Benito Juarez. Since that time I am sure that the alliance between the mestizo and the Indian has become final, and of course, there being no dividing line by reason of color or caste, the criollo also has entirely adapted himself to a national sentiment, or rather to a continental sentiment of Ibero-Americanism, that has as a common indestructible bond the Spanish language and the Spanish type of culture. [p. 91]

p. 92

The truth is then that, whether we like it or not, the mestizo is the dominant element of the Latin-American continent. His characteristics have been pointed out many a time: a great vivacity of mind; quickness of understanding, and at the same time an unsteady temperament; not too much persistence in purpose; a somewhat defective will. It is curious to note that the blending of two different souls through inheritance has produced a broader mental disposition. From a purely intellectual point of view I doubt whether there is a race with less prejudice, more ready to take up almost any mental adventure, more subtle, and more varied than the mestizo, or half-breed. I find in these traits the hope that the mestizo will produce a civilization more universal in its tendency than any other race of the past. Whether it is due to our temperament or to the fact that we do not possess a very strong national tradition, the truth is that our people are keen and are apt to understand and interpret the most contradictory human types. We feel the need of expressing life through many channels, through a thousand channels; we are not addicted to local tradition or to the European, but we desire to know and to try all—the East and the West, the North and the South. A plurality of emotion, an almost mad desire to try all and to live [p. 92] life from every point of view and every manner of sense experience: we are perhaps more truly universal in sentiment than any other people. Notwithstanding, we sometimes appear to be bigoted and patriotically local, but this is a result of the dangerous political position in which we have been placed in recent years. On the other hand we are unstable, and this, I believe, can be easily understood by the biologist, as we are a new product, a new breed, not yet entirely shaped. I believe such weakness can be overcome by obtaining a clear definition of our aims and by devoting ourselves to a definite and a great task.

p. 93

Many of our failings arise from the fact that we do not know exactly what we want. First of all, then, we ought to define our own culture and our own purposes, and educate ourselves to them. No nation has ever risen to true greatness without an ardent faith in some high ideal. Democracy and equal opportunities for every man has been the motto of the great American nation. Broadness, universality of sentiment and thought, in order to fulfil the mission of bringing together all the races of the earth, and with the purpose of creating a new type of civilization, is, I believe, the ideal that would give us in Latin America strength and vision. [p. 93]

p. 94

The goal may seem too ambitious, but it is only great, unlimited ideals that are capable of giving a nation the strength that is required to break the routine of life and to outdo itself. The more ambitious the goal is, the more strenuous the effort becomes. Our mystic temperament demands a task that has, in itself at least, a tendency that is unbounded and almost impossible. A fiery impetus is our only hope if we are to catch up with the world. Our struggle is, in a way, the struggle of the future, because every day mankind will come more and more into contact, and mixtures of all sorts of blood and thought and sentiment will go on increasing, and with them the phenomena and the problems of "mestisage" (of mixture) will become universal. The time and the opportunity of the one-blood, the pure-blood, group is passing away; everywhere the pure-blood groups are being absorbed; and even if they have been masters, they will not stand long before the increasing wave of the technically educated masses of the complex breed. In a way, the world is coming back to the confusion of Babel, and there will come a long period where mixture, or what we call "mestisage," is bound to be the rule. We cannot then dispose of the problem by declaring, as the evolutionists of the Spencer school declared, that the hybrid type was a degenerate type. [p. 94]

Such a statement represents only the short-sighted opinion of prejudice and the view advantageous to the imperialist. Let us remember, though, that all imperialisms have been swallowed and overwhelmed exactly by those same masses they despised. The future will have to be prepared for in a different manner if we wish to see not a repetition of history but a new era in human progress.

p. 95

From our local point of view in Mexico, I have started to preach the gospel of the mestizo by trying to impress on the minds of the new race a consciousness of their mission as builders of entirely new concepts of life. But if the mixed race is going to be able to do anything at all, it is first necessary to give it moral strength and faith in its own ability. The sort of science we have been teaching in our schools was not fit for this purpose; on the contrary it was the science created to justify the aims of the conqueror and the imperialist—the science that came to the help of the strong in their conquest and exploitation of the weak: the aristocracy of the white man and the empire of the white over the world, not only in the name of might but also on the ground of a certain semiscientific theory of the survival and predominance of the fittest, which is popularly known as Nietzschean evolutionism, from Nietzsche, the German. One of the first steps [p. 95] toward our moral regeneration, that I have been advocating in Mexico, is the abandonment of this blind belief in certain hasty deductions from Darwinism and the substitution of Mendelism for Darwinism in our biological philosophy, as we might find more racial hope and more individual strength and faith in the Mendelian hypothesis of life. Modern scientific theories are in many cases like the religious creeds of the old days, simply the intellectual justification of fatalities of conquest and of commercial greed. If all nations then build theories to justify their policies or to strengthen their deeds, let us develop in Mexico our own theories; or at least let us be certain that we choose, among the foreign theories of thought, those that stimulate our growth instead of those that restrain it. And so, instead of taking up, for instance, the Indian problem with the point of view of the ethnologist of the evolutionistic school who starts from the parti pris of his theory, according to which it will take the Indian about five thousand years to develop up to the mentality of the white, we ought to open our eyes to the fact that the Indian five thousand years ago was building monuments that the mentality of the white is using this very day as an inspiration for its new wonder cities of Chicago and New York. The ra- [p. 96] cial theory to which we ought to subscribe then is the theory that the differences among peoples depend more upon ability to do certain things to the exclusion of other things rather than to differences of degree in their total development. Some races chiefly develop artistic ability; other people develop commercial aptitude; and so on. The conclusion of this theory would then be extremely favorable to the mestizo type of culture, as it tends to complement the weaknesses of a particular stock through interchange and assimilation with all the world. In fact, all of the great periods of history have been the work of a mixture of races, of peoples and cultures, rather than the work of any privileged pure-blood nation.

p. 97

The policy of segregation of the races and the policy of educating the Indian or any other race of our Latin-American countries according to separate standards of any sort is not only absurd among us, but it would be fatal. It would be doubly fatal because we do not have that element of pure racial stock that could undertake in our land that supposed leadership that has been taken here by the New Englander. Even if it should be true that this unmixed white leadership has created the greatness of the United States, we do not have in Mexico anything equivalent to this root. Our prog- [p. 97] ress may have been delayed on this account, but there is nothing left for us to do except to go ahead with the situation that nature has made for us; and instead of a blind, shortsighted copying of methods, we should go along creating what is required by our own problem and mission. For us, there is only one sound race policy, and that is the policy of old—the policy of the Spaniard and the Christian who took it for granted that we are all potentially the same and that we are bound to respond differently according to the call that is made upon us, each one bearing a treasure that comes to life at the proper moment in the time of need. The duty of any great culture is, then, to raise human beings as a whole and to call races together so that they can all collaborate in the task of a truly material and spiritual civilization. Do not put a man to a task for which he is not fitted. Place every one in his own vocation. Bring down to social practical life a little of the rule of joy instead of the hard, blind rules of need and of duty, and you will transform the world. But if we are ever going to approach any realization of this sort, we must start first by transforming all of our theories. For it is in the soul where the germ of our disease should be sought, in the mind, and more particularly in the heart. So long as our ideals do not correspond to our intimate [p. 98] wish and desire, our ideals will not be worthwhile. So long as pleasure and joy do not become the rule of life, we must admit that we are lost in a mistaken path. Duty is a means, knowledge is a means, effort is a means; joy and pleasure are the only ends. And there can be no joy in a civilization where races are separated by hatred, prejudice, and misunderstanding.

p. 99

I have said that humanity is going back to Babel, and by this I mean that the day of the isolated civilization is over. In this new coming-together of all the races we ought not to repeat the methods of the past, the methods that transformed Babel into a curse. Babel became a curse because the different peoples did not understand each other and, consequently, instead of concurring in a common purpose, they entered into competition and jealousy that destroyed every one of them. It is time then that we should change our methods in order to make the new period of Babel a prosperous one. The whole theory of the superiority of one stock above the other will have to be changed. The practice of putting the so-called lower races to work for the benefit of the superior will have to be abandoned, or else the dominators will have to suffer from the revenge of the oppressed. This revenge is found in the law of nature itself. Notice [p. 99] the fact that whenever two extremely differentiated groups come in contact, it is, in the long run, the lower group that predominates if the superior does not undertake the task of raising the level of the inferior. The Spanish civilization is perduring today in America, not on account of the pure descendants of the conqueror but because of the mixed race and the Indian race that were educated and assimilated by the Spaniard. If the Spaniard had not mixed his blood with the Indian, there would not be today, on the map, a large area of countries where the Spanish soul is alive and progressive. When, on the contrary, the dominating race stands apart and takes no interest in the life of the inferior, the inferior tends instinctively to increase its numbers in order to compensate through numbers what the dominating race achieves through quality. The more civilized a nation is, the more it reduces its reproduction, the tendency being to obtain an advantage in quality. But the lower, opposed breed, having no control, no hope, goes on multiplying madly; and the weight and the curse of this overpopulation is just as harmful to the elect as it is to the less fortunate. If we are ever going to stop this misery, it is necessary that the superior take pains to educate the inferior and to raise its standard. If we do not wish to be over- [p. 100] whelmed by the wave of the Negro, of the Indian, or of the Asiatic, we shall have to see that the Negro, the Indian, and the Asiatic are raised to the higher standards of life, where reproduction becomes regulated and quality predominates over numbers. Instead of the competitive manner of life advocated by the defenders of the pure-race civilization and by the imperialists and conquerors, we shall have to adopt, then, the co-operative, collaborating manner of interracial organization; instead of the struggle for life and the survival of the fittest, a collaboration of all human efforts for the production of a variety in quality—a salvation brought about, not through the elect of a certain type more or less strong, but through the utilization of every particular aptitude for the purpose of creating superior values. If the mad competition and distrust of the present day can be overcome, every race will then be able to work for the achievement of quality and no race will devote itself to merely creating numbers; thereby the present-day danger of the overpowering of the superior few by the uncivilized many will disappear.

p. 101

If we thus turn selfish competition into far-sighted collaboration, it will not even matter much whether we follow the pure-race, the one-race standard, or the mixed-race standard. The very same dif- [p. 101] ferences of skin and temperament that today seem so distressing may become useful and even pleasing the moment that we learn to develop a task in which every special capacity finds a purpose and a reward. Spiritual affinities and similar fancies of taste and mind will then prevail, and a superior life will become the endeavor of the human family as a whole. [p. 102]
