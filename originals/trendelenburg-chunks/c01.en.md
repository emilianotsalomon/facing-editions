[[0]] # ⟦1⟧ IX. THE END.

[[1]] 1. In the preceding section we have gained ordering concepts which reach as far as the motion from which they arise. There is no greater domain than this; for the domain of motion is the whole world. These categories, which are intelligible to us through our own act, form the thread of Ariadne by which we find our way through the labyrinth of variegated and confused perceptions. By virtue of the mobility innate in them they are able to determine themselves more closely through a varying content. But will they suffice to master the whole of experience?

[[2]] 2. We seek the answer in prominent facts of experience and therefore cast a glance at some significant phenomena. Let the leap we make not seem strange. For a few moments we leave logical derivation and analysis and place ourselves in the midst of the forms of nature. Only there can we judge what means we still lack in order to satisfy knowledge; only there can we learn how far creative motion suffices, with the concepts that spring from it and with the matter taken up by its help. We hold the concepts gained ⟦2⟧ by us from the development against the acquisition and possession of the sciences, the theory against facts to which it is supposed to be equal.

[[3]] Let us consider, as one example in place of all, the highest organ of sense, the sight of man.

[[4]] In the socket of the eye a nerve is deposited in mosaic fashion, alone of all the branches of the nerves receptive to light and colours. The light from without and the nerve from within correspond to one another in a secret understanding, and the nerve is born for the light. Yet there would be nothing in the eye but a bright shimmering and flickering if the light-sensitive nerve alone were to constitute sight. The diverging rays would then stream from all sides upon all points of the retina, and the rays would blur one another. No single image could appear. Nature is clearer and more determinate. The cones of rays which come from *one* point are refracted toward *one* point of the retina. The arched cornea, the aqueous humour, the collecting lens, the thinner vitreous body accomplish the inversion of the cone of rays within the eye, so that the outer points appear again in points and so that shapes thus rise up in what would otherwise be the blurring sea of light of the optic nerve. Thus to the forms of the surface and to the coloured drawing of the world there correspond the transparent collecting media of the eye and the depth of the eyeball proportioned to its refractive power. The image of the outer world now paints itself on a reduced scale in the frame of the eye. Colour and form of things on the one side, and the material and structure of the media of the eye on the other, are there for one another.

[[5]] If the apices of the inverted cones of light are to strike the retina in order to draw the image upon it, different distances of the objects demand a different refraction of the rays. Accordingly there is proportioned to the outer distances that capacity of the eye which, through ⟦3⟧ inner change, namely through the presumably adjustable arching or flattening of the shape of the lens, collects the rays nearer or farther. To the distances of space corresponds the delicate mobility of the inner media of the eye.

[[6]] What these parts intend on the whole becomes clear in this way. But no instrument obeys completely; as soon as the thought is carried out, it surrenders itself to the contingency of matter and, in order to assert itself, must also overcome contingency. Were the walls of the eye white or coloured, they would reflect rays and disturb the distinctness; but a black pigment lines the cavity and sucks up the superfluous light. The spherical lens, if it were used entire, would deflect the rays at its margin, and a circle of dispersion would then blur the image projected by the central parts; but the screen of the movable iris covers the margin of the lens, which would otherwise cloud the truth with a semblance. The lens, in refracting the rays, would at the same time disperse the colours and once more endanger the distinctness of the image; but the collecting media of the eye, of unequal refractive power, of unequal curvature and of unequal chemical constitution, are so balanced against one another that the eye, with correct accommodation, becomes achromatic at the distance of convergence. The necessary defect of the instrument is overcome by creative foresight.

[[7]] Thus the eye is prepared in the darkness of the womb so that, once born, it may be opened to the light. The eye forms itself in the closed workshop of nature; and yet it corresponds to the light, which springs forth at an infinite distance from that workshop, and still more to the changing colour which light conjures forth upon the earth, the dwelling-place of the creature, in its collision with dark matter.

[[8]] Do the above categories suffice here? Here too there is ⟦4⟧ a process of motion; here too matter and form, the intensive and the extensive, force and reciprocal action present themselves in a clear series. But do they hit upon the proper essence of the matter? Light has neither made nor stimulated the eye, and yet the slumbering power of the light-bright nerve yearns for it. The colours and images of the outer world go their way and cannot have brought forth the structure of the collecting media and their transparent material; but the sensitive eye converts the radiating cones of light back into their source, into the points that unite to form the image, and is in this a prelude to the deeper thinking which knows how to concentrate the outflowing effect back into the ground. The distances lie at rest in the world, like geometrical magnitudes, and change nothing in the eye; but sight goes out to meet them or hastens after them. To the outer distances correspond the delicate changes which are laid down in the eye in various ways. The possible deflection of light and the provident diaphragm of the iris, the possible reflection of the rays and the black pigment that prevents it, the possible dispersion of colours and the scarcely calculable achromatism of the eye point profoundly to one another. There is a causality here, but one other than formative motion. Everywhere, in the corresponding oppositions of outer and inner activity, an agreement appears.

[[9]] In the structure of the organ, surely, either light must have overcome and shaped matter, or matter must of itself have become master of the light. So it seems according to the law of the efficient cause, but neither of the two has happened. No glance of light falls into the secluded womb where the eye is formed; light is neither the exciting cause nor the architect of the organ; and still less might inert matter, which is nothing without energetic light, under ⟦5⟧ stand light by itself. And yet light and eye are for one another, and in the marvel of the eye there lies the unveiled consciousness of light. The moving cause with its necessary formation has here entered into a higher service. The end governs the whole and watches over the execution of the parts; and through the end the eye becomes "the light of the body."

[[10]] As the end reveals itself in the instrument of sight, so it repeats itself in a similar way in the receptive organs of the other senses. We leave them and cast a glance, by way of example, at an opposite activity of life.

[[11]] The animal's instruments of locomotion are proportioned to the element in which the animal is to move. In fishes the keel-shaped build of the body, the rapid strokes of the mobile tail, the supporting and bearing fins are, as it were, calculated for the fluid element. The bird, which is to cut through the air, is not merely equipped with the fan-like wing and with strength and firmness for the beating motions; rather, its whole build is airy and light. The bones of birds, filled with air, are lighter, and the air, expanded by the heightened warmth of life, behaves on a smaller scale like the air of a rising balloon. Everything corresponds to the elastic element of air. The higher animals, which are destined for the land, brace their firm bones against the firm ground in order to gain a support for the motion of the legs. How life is designed, on this side too, from *one* thought is recognised likewise in the surprising discoveries that have been made in this domain. We recall the remarkable fact that in man, in the atmosphere in which we live, the head of the femur is held in its exactly fitting socket by the mere pressure of the air and in this position, as if freely suspended, ⟦6⟧ executes its swinging motions. Only when the air grows thin, as on mountains, is this wonderful equilibrium cancelled, by which the full strength of the enclosing muscles is left for the proper functions of locomotion. The innermost structure of the joint and the lower surrounding layers of the atmosphere in which man breathes point to one another. The pre-established harmony which, according to Leibniz, links the realm of nature and the realm of morals meets us at every step in nature itself.

[[12]] What the science of statics and mechanics has laboriously acquired through experiments and inferences as the doctrine of the centre of gravity and the lever lies before our eyes, in one great example, in the instruments of locomotion of the higher animals and especially of man. What could follow as a rule from the laws discovered is found realised here, albeit under a wise limitation by higher considerations; and conversely those laws could be discovered from the study of the organs and especially through the analysis of their reciprocal relations. The structure of the whole body and the centre of gravity conditioned by it, with its mobility, form on the one side a demand which is satisfied on the other in the shiftable point of support and in the compensating motion of the most various limbs.

[[13]] If the steps are to become longer and quicker, it must be possible to carry the two heads of the femur at a lesser height above the ground. To this end, as for the mobility of the centre of gravity that has to be supported, the joints of the knees, feet and toes co-operate. The motion of the one demands, under certain conditions, the motion of the other and, as it were, takes it up into itself for a common effect. Several functions are together subordinated to a higher end and are governed by it.

[[14]] ⟦7⟧ The measure of muscular force demands in the bearing bones a determinate measure of firmness, so that the force may not bend and break the lever-arm. Muscle and joint demand each other. A muscle has no sense where motion is not possible by means of a joint. Joints without muscles would be lame and slack and nothing but obstructing sections in the connection of the limbs. The fact of the organism confirms this thought. Let comparative anatomy bear witness to it. If two bones which in man are movably connected grow together into a single whole in other animals, the corresponding muscles are also not found.

[[15]] The power of the whole reaches still further. The organs of motion are worthless if they do not receive a direction; and direction is possible only if the surrounding space is penetrated by a sense such as sight. Aristotle already called attention to the necessary agreement between forward-looking sight and the moving organs. The gaze of the eyes is directed forward, like the joints of the organs of motion. This intimate unity appears most beautifully in the delicate hand of the draughtsman, which is so governed by the gaze as though the axes of the eyes were themselves drawing with their point of intersection. Motion demands the gaze, and sight demands motion; for what a contradiction would the free gaze be in a motionless body! Through the eye the relations to the outer world pass into the soul; the impulse is excited, and the creature must answer to it through motion.

[[16]] A law of nature is a governing law only when even the apparent exceptions are comprehended from it, and when the ⟦8⟧ disturbances, as in astronomy, not only do not cancel the ground of the rule but confirm it. So it is also with the purposiveness of the organism. In most cases force and lever, where they move the limbs, seem to be disadvantageously arranged. The muscles commonly act on the levers in a very oblique direction, and their insertion lies mostly near the fulcrum and far from the end of the lever. Hence a greater expenditure of force is required than would otherwise be necessary. But the one-sided purposiveness of mechanics yields to a higher purposiveness of the whole organism.¹ Had the laws of the best arrangement of levers been the ultimate norm, the form of the body would have had to become angular and clumsy; the range of motion and the symmetry and harmonious co-operation of the limbs would necessarily have suffered by it. In this supposedly unpurposive arrangement the end appears only the more circumspect.

[[19]] Aristotle, in accordance with his teleological view, attempts to refer the individual activities and parts of animal life to the whole as the determining ground and as it were to design them out of the whole as necessary demands.² Modern science casts similar glances, but more comprehensive and more certain ones. Cuvier, for example, has set forth in fine outlines the intimate connection in which the whole organisation of an animal stands to its food.³ It is important to see, in such an example drawn by a master's hand, how the dependent members proceed from a thought of the whole.

[[20]] Every living being, says Cuvier, forms a whole, a single and closed system, in which all the parts ⟦9⟧ mutually correspond to one another and contribute to the same effect of the end by reciprocal reaction. None of these parts can change without the change of the others, and consequently each part, taken by itself, indicates and gives all the others. If, therefore, the intestines of an animal are so organised that they can digest only flesh, and indeed only fresh flesh, then its jaws too must be constructed for devouring, its claws for seizing and tearing, its teeth for cutting and dividing the prey, the whole system of its organs of motion for pursuing and overtaking it, its sense organs for perceiving it at a distance. There must even lie in its brain the necessary instinct to be able to hide itself and lie in wait cunningly for its victims. The jaw, in order that it may grip, needs a determinate form of the condyle, a determinate ratio between the place of the resistance and the force relative to the point of support, a determinate size of the temporal muscle, and the latter in turn needs a determinate width of the fossa which receives it and a determinate arching of the zygomatic arch under which it runs, and this arch again must have a determinate strength in order to support the masseter muscle. In order that the animal may carry off its prey, it needs a strength of the muscles by which the head is raised; this presupposes a determinate form of the vertebrae, where the muscles originate, and of the occiput, where they are inserted. The teeth, in order to be able to divide the flesh, must be sharp. Their root will have to be the firmer, the more and the stronger the bones they are destined to break, which again has an influence on the development of the parts that serve to move the jaws. In order that the claws may seize the prey, a certain mobility of the toes is needed, a certain strength of the nails, by which determinate forms of all the joints of the foot and the necessary distribution of the muscles and tendons are conditioned; ⟦10⟧ the forearm will have to possess a certain ease of rotation, which presupposes determinate forms of the bones of which it consists; but the bones of the forearm cannot change their form without conditioning changes in the upper arm as well. In short, the form of the tooth entails that of the condyle, the form of the shoulder-blade that of the claws, just as the equation of a curve entails all its properties; and just as, if one took each of its properties by itself as the basis of a particular equation, one would find again both the first equation and all its other properties, so one could, given one of the members of the animal as a beginning, represent the whole animal, provided one had a thorough knowledge of the economy of life. One further sees that animals with hoofs must all be herbivorous; that, since they use their forefeet only to support their body, they need no so powerfully built shoulder, from which the absence of the clavicle and of the acromion and the narrowness of the shoulder-blade are also explained; that, since they also need no rotation of the forearm, the radius can in them be fused with the ulna, or at least be articulated to the upper arm by a ginglymus and not by an arthrodia; that the need of vegetable food requires teeth with a flat crown in order to crush seeds and herbs; that this crown will have to be uneven, and to this end the enamel will have to alternate with bony substance. Since with this kind of crown horizontal grinding too is necessary for trituration, the condyle of the jaw will here not form so compressed a prominence as in the carnivores; it will be flattened and will at the same time correspond to a more or less flat surface on the temporal bone; the temporal fossa, which has only a small muscle to receive, will be of slight width and depth.

[[24]] Thus Cuvier, like an architect of nature, designs from the ⟦11⟧ end of nutrition the means and the fabric of the structure. Wilhelm Tischbein, full of poetry and an intimate of animal life, pursued in his physiognomies of animal heads the same distinction between carnivores and herbivores, and interpreted from the food which they hunt down and outwit or find and take the states of soul and the expression of the animal: the courageous strength or the peaceful calm, the penetrating gaze and sharp understanding, or the startled fancy and duller gaze of an animal's head. Thus here everything further is attached to the manner of self-preservation as the highest end, and on this depend both the outer structure and the innermost stirring of life.

[[25]] If one wishes to continue the analogy and to interpret man in the same way from this outer side, here too the lowly accords with the highest. If man's food is to be flesh, as anatomical comparison already shows, then man lacks that whole apparatus of the sharp claw, that power of the jaws, that cutting force of the lacerating teeth, by which to become master of the prey immediately, as the animals do. If, on the other hand, he is to nourish himself on plants, which offer no resistance and can therefore be grasped without such instruments, then he lacks in turn that greater outlay of animal apparatus which is required for the digestion of vegetable food and which appears most clearly in the fourfold stomach of the ruminants, developed for different functions. Thus from the outset bodily need and bodily equipment stand in contradiction in man; and what in the animal corresponds completely, the end of nutrition and the organs of capture and of inner assimilation, falls apart in man, and he stands there with this end of nature forsaken by nature. But only apparently. Through the physical power he lacks he is directed to the cunning of the understanding, in order to replace the organs that act physically or chemically; and he must make himself the weapon ⟦12⟧ as claw and tooth; and before he takes vegetable food into his mouth, he digests it, as it were, in advance with the help of fire, to a degree which plant food undergoes in animals in the composite structure of the manifold stomach. Cooking takes for him the place of the whole digestive apparatus in the herb-eating ruminants.

[[26]] The most immediate need, that contradiction between food and organs, teaches man to seek the weapon and fire. With fire the human mind, schooled to cunning, then goes on to flourish; with fire it conquers time and space, night and the inhospitable zones; with fire it begins the defiant Promethean work of culture, by which it emancipates itself from nature, or rather the peculiarly human life by which it makes nature serviceable to the human end. Thus even the spur of the first need drives man onto the path of a human development. Blumenbach was therefore right when in his system of natural history he designated the human race by the pregnant character inermis; and Franklin was just as right when he, who with a newly devised weapon "snatched the lightning from the sky," called man the animal instrumentificum. The contradiction which looks out from the needy and yet defenceless condition of man stands in the hand of a higher thought, so that the most glorious germ may not lack the stimulating impulse.

[[27]] In the lower there lies a foreglimpse of the higher, and the whole is designed from *one* thought. What seems to complete itself within itself, as though self-sufficiently closed in itself, serves in turn as a member of a more comprehensive, more significant life. The plant world, great and beautiful in itself, sacrifices its greatness and beauty to the animal world, whose life and preservation demand vegetation as a presupposition.

[[28]] We may in a similar way recall the stages of ⟦13⟧ the life of the soul which Aristotle distinguished and subordinated to one another. Just as the developed figures of geometry, such was his view, the polygons, the circle and so on, are comprehended and measured only from the simplest figure, the triangle, and just as the triangle indeed exists without them but they do not exist without the triangle: so, for example, the stage of nutritive life is found without the sentient, but the sentient not without nutrition. When the end rises higher, it seizes the end already realised as a means.

[[29]] We find a surprising example in the senses which man has in common with the higher animals. In the animals the senses serve only the organism, which seeks its preservation. The sense of touch, which develops most freely in the human hand, is at the lower stages of animal life grown together with the instruments for moving, grasping, defending. The sense here wishes only to serve these functions. The dull nutritive system has received the testing and warning taste, so that only wholesome substances may be admitted for intake. Smell is assigned to breathing, like a sense of the lung, so that the living creature may avoid unwholesome air. Only later does it serve the keen-scented animals for their whole economy of life. Sight, as the sense of space, is demanded together with the disposition to motion, so that motion may receive a direction. For self-preservation the limitation of the eye suffices, since modern physics shows that there are still dark rays which fall outside the colour spectrum. Hearing, which indicates the innermost vibrations and tensions of bodies, serves in the first instance ends of the individual organism. Now it is the watchfully listening sense, for avoiding danger; now the animals perceive through hearing the tension of their vital feelings revealed through sound, and it serves the sexual sense. Thus in the animals the senses are closely bound.

[[30]] But man frees them from the selfish end ⟦14⟧ of the individual natural organism. In man a higher end appears, and in surrendering themselves to it the senses transfigure themselves. Now the sense of touch in the hand mediates the manifold arts; taste recognises chemical differences; smell pursues substance even into the state of volatilisation; through hearing intelligible speech becomes possible, the reciprocal intercourse of the species, the condition of all thinking; and the mobile eye opens up the infinity of the world and of its knowledge. All the senses enter the service of the thinking mind. Even the organs of locomotion are seized by a higher end and mediate the possibility of a science of space, geometry. Thus the organs of life are formed and transformed from within, and the lower is raised up by the higher. But we measure the higher solely by the more universal and more powerful end.

[[31]] We do not wish to pile up facts but to interpret them. Let attention therefore be drawn to only *one* more, which, as it were, gathers all that precedes into one. It is the seed and germ and its development.

[[32]] The seed and moisture, the germ of the plant and the stimuli of the soil, of light, of the atmosphere, and indeed in determinate climatic differences, correspond to one another. They are, as it were, conceived from *one* spirit. In the undifferentiated germ the differences lie hidden, and in the whole course of development the future whole governs every step. That the whole is prior to the parts, as Aristotle puts it, lies visibly before our eyes in the seed and its development. The power of the whole acts before the whole is there, in order that it may come to be. The germ is the future whole in possibility and disposition; through development the members of the whole arise in actuality. What Aristotle distinguished by dynamis and energeia, potentia and actu, are the same stages ⟦15⟧ fixed in logical names. The seed, in changing, does not give itself up. The end of development brings forth the beginning again. In the fruit the seed has multiplied. The organism has produced anew its own possibility, and indeed the same unweakened life in multiplied form. When the organism returns to itself in the formation of seed, it divides itself, as it were, in this return, but it divides itself in such a way that in the individual part the full whole is present again, and the power of life does not decrease but grows. Thus transience is conquered, and in the midst of the physical the metaphysical thought forces itself upon us which Plato already marks in the Symposium and Aristotle in the books on the soul. "An animal begets an animal like itself, a plant a plant, in order that they may partake of the always and the divine so far as they can; for all strive after this, and for its sake all do what they do according to the end of nature; since, then, they cannot partake of the always and the divine in the continuation of life, because no perishable creature can remain one and the same in number, it seeks this communion so far as it can, and remains not itself but like itself, not one in number, but one in genus." Once more the future, and indeed even existence beyond one's own life, reaches into life. This end of the distant future can be no greater paradox to an understanding that measures by human power than the end of distant space, which the plain fact compels us to acknowledge when the eye harmonises with the source of light, removed from the eye by many radii of the earth. If life developed from the germ is, as it were, pervaded by ends and sets instruments over against the outer world for which it is destined, now to appropriate and enjoy it, now to ward it off and preserve itself; if these organs therefore appear as marvels because, apparently brought forth by ⟦16⟧ blind causes, they present a thought which masters the world by seeing through it: then in the seed, from which the whole rises, these marvels crowd together as into the smallest space.

[[33]] The concept of the end, which makes itself known in these examples from present nature, has the testimony of the immemorial past on its side. For as astronomy in Copernicus opened the hitherto closed universe and directed the human mind out into infinite, infinitely filled space, so geology, which reads inquiringly in the earth, a fragment of this universe, today opens backwards the closed history into a perspective of periods measured by millions of years. It shows us wild forces, but already in the most ancient of them the counterpart of life; it shows us the violence of physical destructions and shatterings, but ever again life rising anew and greater above the ruins; it shows us the manifold forms of the races of plants and animals, whose existence, unexplained from physical conditions, indeed opposed to them, is always won from the given ruling forces; and whereas the uniform physical conditions of light and air, of water and soil, would of themselves have to have a uniform effect, it shows at one and the same time manifold stages and forms of life, in motion and sensation of their own, facing these same uniform conditions and drawing from them their preservation and development. In the ascending series of stages of beings the significance of the inner end rises, and, intuited in this compass of its power, it becomes a concept of the world.

In the intuition of motion the productive cause governs; in the examples indicated an unexamined concept clearly confronts it: the end.

[[36]] It would indeed have been easier to fetch this concept from the sphere of the human will; for logic must do justice to this domain too. But the end appears in nature more creative and deeper; and we cannot excuse ourselves from seeking it out precisely where it is most difficult. The further question therefore now arises what it is that appears in these facts as the essence of the end.

[[37]] If we enter analytically into the fact, what lies before us first is division and plurality. Only where this is found is the end found. In the undifferentiated, uniform continuum of space, in the evenly expanding sea of air or in the mass of water striving toward its level, the end does not appear originally and in and for itself. Everything lies there indifferently side by side. One thing penetrates into another; but nothing sets itself off in order to enter again into relation. In this condition no end can arise. Only where there is opposition does the end become possible, which has its essence in this, that the one is for the other and the one is referred to the other, as the way to the goal. This opposition shows itself everywhere in the above and similar facts. The animals and the elements in which they are to live, the eye and light, the lung and air, the digestive instruments and outer food, the movable lever-arms of the hand and the solid that they are to grasp, the speech of the one and the hearing of the other, the great disposition to communication through language, as it were a spiritual function of the whole species, and the individuals who on the basis of a ⟦18⟧ common likeness of kind can receive the thoughts, stand over against one another and point to one another. The division which the end demands speaks most clearly from the two sexes, which, according to the Greek view, like two halves sent into the world from the hand of formative nature at distant places, ceaselessly seek each other in order to restore the originally conceived whole.

[[38]] Kant already showed that all geometrical figures display a manifold purposiveness. They are suited to the solution of many problems according to a single principle. With the straight line and the circle, the two simplest shapes, a great number of problems are constructed. Two lines are to intersect each other, to keep to Kant's example, in such a way that the rectangle formed from the two parts of the one is equal to the rectangle formed from the two parts of the other. The problem is, to all appearance, difficult. But all chords of the circle that intersect anywhere divide one another of themselves in this proportion. The other curves solve other problems. Here there lies before us a purposiveness that rests in the thing itself; but it enters only when the two figures, independent in themselves, are brought to one another. The principle of formation, e.g. in the circle or in the straight line, has nothing to do with this purposiveness. Circle and straight line are there for themselves. But in working together, their purposiveness appears. The same can be shown in arithmetic. If an equation is to be solved, a determinate end governs the method. But all transposing and eliminating, all substituting and completing presupposes separate and combinable numerical magnitudes.

[[39]] As, accordingly, the concept of relation lies in the nature of the end, so the end, in order to be possible at all, demands a plurality of things or elements.

[[41]] ⟦19⟧ What corresponds in the end is thus, on one side, independent; the things set themselves off against one another. Where the efficient cause of motion determines everything, the individual thing appears only as a torn-off piece of the whole. In the domain of the end, however, substance closes itself within itself in order to be able to set itself over against another; and the members of the opposition place themselves under a new whole. The straight line and the circle subsist independently for themselves, but when they come together for the solution of a problem, they form, through the thought that realises itself in it, an articulated whole. Organic life, which wishes to preserve itself, stands only in relative independence over against the life of nature, into which it reaches with its organs; it is a relation of need. Thus the eye strives toward the light; the lung longs for air, etc. The division which the end demands is cancelled again by the end. Plurality for a unity is accordingly the expression of the simple fact.

[[42]] We disregard the new whole into which what was divided joins itself together. In the one member the end is wont to express its architectonic power in particular, while the other, rather the force of the efficient cause, is, as it were, the goal for which work is done. Thus in the opposition of light and eye the organ is pervaded by the end in order to unite itself with the light, while the light keeps still and merely accommodates itself to the active eye, which takes account of its laws. So it happens in the majority of cases. The end appears as the formative cause first in the instrument. In the eye the end of seeing realises itself, in the organs of motion the end of change of place, in the sexual organs propagation, etc.

[[43]] Where the efficient cause produces something, the parts produce the whole. One may indeed say dialectically that the parts are parts only through the whole, and that parts are not ⟦20⟧ distinguished until the whole is there. It is certainly so if we press the relational character of the name and speak of knowledge, not of origination. The blind motion that produces the line drives forth the parts of the line continuously; when the motion stops, the whole is there, and the preceding parts have brought forth the whole. Where the end governs, the relation is reversed. If, to cite the most external example first, we think of a system of a geometrical figure in which a problem has been solved, e.g. those intersecting chords in the circle whose segments give the required equal rectangles, then the whole goes first, inasmuch as it is indicated in the problem, and the parts are brought forth by the whole. This can even be recognised in the way analysis solves problems. The whole is thought as realised, as the problem demands, and the question is then asked: how is the realisation possible? The conditions which thereby result lead only then to the design of the parts. The parts are determined from the whole. The former is before the latter. The mechanics of the instruments of motion stands nearest to the geometrical problem, since it rests essentially upon one. In the eye, the profoundly designed organ, the activity of the whole determined the co-operating parts, so that the clearest image might appear. That architectonics of nature into which Cuvier lets us look in the structure of the carnivores and herbivores gives the outlines of the parts from the fundamental trait of the whole economy of life. Goethe, in the essay on Geoffroi de Saint Hilaire, his scientific swan-song, drew further, in single lines, this mysterious agreement of the parts which in the animal stems from the determining whole. If in this way the whole is ideally before the parts, it shows itself just as really in the seed, which is rightly called the potential whole. ⟦21⟧ The power of the whole is, as it were, compressed in the seed and governs the development throughout its whole course. The whole, as the formative principle, is here grown together with the efficient cause. Hence it comes about that in this domain of organic life the part, as it has proceeded from the whole, subsists only in the life of the whole and, loosed from this bond, dies off. In the body politic there is indeed a greater freedom of the members. But there too the law repeats itself. The individual has subsistence only in the whole. His living activity is extinguished when he tears himself loose. What holds of the members in organic formations holds equally of the members of the members. The eye serves the body and is formed out of the whole as a necessary organ, and again the parts of the eye, cornea, lens, etc., out of the end and whole of sight. The parts live only in the whole just as much as they are demanded and determined by the whole.

[[45]] Aristotle, who transfigures nature with the end and who even in the consideration of the state is an organic physiologist, says at the beginning of his Politics,¹ briefly and characteristically: "And by nature, evidently, the state is prior to the family and to each one of us. For the whole must necessarily be prior to the part. For if the whole is abolished, there will no longer be foot or hand, except in the same name only, as one speaks perhaps of a hand of stone; when the natural hand dies off, it will be of that kind." This is the striking expression of the view of the end. We set over against it, as the one-sided opposite, the dictum of Roscellinus: omnis pars naturaliter prior est suo toto. It is the limited expression of the consistently carried-out view of the ⟦22⟧ efficient cause. Since nominalism in general insists stubbornly on the sensibly individual and present, it must shut its eyes to the end, which gains the ground from the universal and from the future.

[[48]] Accordingly the efficient cause produces the whole from the parts, and conversely the end produces the parts from the whole. We pursue this remarkable opposition further.

[[49]] In the process of the efficient cause we distinguish the cause as the earlier and the effect as the later. When the concept of causality, in which the connection of knowledge rests, had to withstand the storm of scepticism, people frequently took refuge in this distinction as in the last fixed point.¹ In the judgment of the efficient cause, "the rubbing of amber produces electricity," the producing cause precedes in time (the rubbing), and the produced effect (the electricity) follows after it. The process is indeed a continuum, but the distinction comes out clearly if one looks not merely at the end-points of the cause, which are already the beginning of the effect, but grasps the whole course of the cause. Let us compare with this fundamental relation the working of the end. We transform that example into a judgment of the end by saying, perhaps: we rub the amber in order that electricity may arise. The effect is here an end, and this end is in turn a cause. What follows becomes something earlier; the future, which is not yet there, governs the present. The relation of the efficient cause is turned directly around, and the order of time vanishes which is otherwise intuited in causality as the fixed element and extolled as the order of things; for the end becomes the beginning.

[[50]] The above expositions bear this out in facts. The eye has refracting media in order that the bundles of rays proceeding from individual po ⟦23⟧ ints may collect again into individual points. The collecting of the rays is the effect of the process mediated by the structure of the eye. This effect, the later, becomes the determining ground, the earlier. This reversed relation of the efficient cause repeats itself in one and the same organ, and indeed so far that even a possible future, which is not to occur, determines the structure. Nature itself pronounces a negative judgment of the end when, through the margin of the iris covering the lens, it prevents a coloured circle of dispersion from forming on the retina. The possible effect here already intervenes formatively. If, according to another example indicated above, the firmness of the bones accords with the strength of the muscles, as the unbending lever-arm with force and load, then the destination of the bone has built the bone. The bone is so and so strong in order that it may form the firm abutment for this muscle. This effect of firmness is the cause of it. If the seed conceals the secret of development, the whole future of the organism, then it is, as it were, pervaded and bound by this future and has in what is to be, thus in its effect, the ground of its properties and activities. Nature accordingly pronounces it as a simple fact that what, on the side of the efficient cause, is the subsequent and produced becomes, in the end, precisely the antecedent and producing. What in the efficient cause is distinguished as an immutable law of succession is reversed in the end into its opposite, with a boldness that mocks the sequence of time.

[[52]] But how can the effect become the producing cause? Aristotle already indicated simply,¹ how it happens in the analytic problem of geometry. Knowing and producing stand in an opposition. The demand of the problem, the whole that is to come to be, is ⟦24⟧ indeed known first, but is only the conclusion of the construction. On the other hand, the starting-point of the productive design is known precisely last. What is first in knowing becomes last in the formative process, and what is last in the knowing process becomes first in the formative. In a similar way it happens, as Aristotle shows, in free human life. The thought of the end calls forth, as in the mathematical problem, the thought of the conditions and seeks the principle of these conditions in an activity of one's own that is possible. Free thinking, which as such looks into the future and weighs end and conditions, possibilities against possibilities, and finally, decisively, plays the representation over into the deed, is here the presupposition. The properly logical question is indeed not solved in such a consideration. For it is not yet clear in the last ground how thinking, present thinking, gains a power over the future effect. Meanwhile, in this domain of human life the explanation may suffice for the time being.

[[54]] Whoever denied the reality of a causation according to ends, as Spinoza did,¹ sought to transform every end into a mirror-image of human representation, and then let this mirror-image, this semblance of an end, arise through the motion of an efficient cause, e.g. through a natural impulse, so that the end was dissolved into a glimmer of representation and the efficient cause was raised to sole dominion. Consciousness and impulse are here the middle concept that is supposed to remove every difficulty. We will grant the comprehensible explanation if anyone can comprehend consciousness and impulse in their innermost essence without the end. If one penetrates into their grounds, the impossibility soon shows itself. But for the present we prefer to leave this doubtful sphere, merely indicating ⟦25⟧ that in this way the problem is indeed pushed back but not solved; and we turn to the facts before us of unconscious nature, where at least we are nowhere directed by the matter itself to such an explanation. The concepts of the efficient cause confess here that they do not suffice. Where they alone are acknowledged, the greatest works of nature remain an uncomprehended marvel. For according to them it is impossible that the later, which is not yet, should become the earlier, the effect the cause.

[[56]] The everyday does not cease to be a miracle because it is everyday; for if this word is to have a sense, it indicates the mute astonishment which justly befalls thought, deeming itself all-powerful, when the means of comprehending knowledge and the task pressing upon it in the facts stand in contradiction. Miracle is nowadays a word of ill repute and, so people probably think, ought not to occur in logical investigations. One believes one has disposed of it by invoking against it the "immanent laws of nature." But whether these are not themselves the miracle? The miracle is related that five thousand men were fed from seven loaves; and this is easy to deny, because such a report is said to contradict the observed laws of nature. But has one thereby got rid of the miracle? Of this one, certainly; but the same returns, greater, precisely within the laws of nature. Every year five thousand men are fed from seven loaves. Every year the corn is consumed, and there remains for the population of whole countries no more than, say, the corn of the seven loaves; and every year the whole harvest, the full feeding of all, grows again from these remaining crumbs. You, then, who have struck down the *one* miracle with the fact of natural law, acknowledge at least that in that fact the miracles appear all the grander. It needs no demonstration that in this example the ⟦26⟧ law of outer nature and the need of life accord in a way that presupposes a higher end. Here too is that great hysteron proteron, that conversion of the end into the beginning, that overturning of the evident causal nexus. Such an inversion of the natural course was so little to the liking even of great minds, such as Spinoza, that they preferred to deny the end altogether. But it is there; and the only question is to what extent. Historical criticism has its right, and that right shall not be curtailed. We hate only the triumph of a petty understanding which, if only it removes the miracle from the Christian narrative, believes it has a level track through the whole world, like a dead-straight highroad. The ancients were deeper; they derived all philosophising from wonder. For when the mind is astonished before uncomprehended phenomena, astonishment spurs it on to knowing. They were drawn up by the greatness and sublimity of the facts; we prefer to draw these down to ourselves into flat comprehensibility, and set over against the beginning of philosophy, which according to Plato stems from wonder, its consistent consummation, the blunted nil admirari. But for knowing that is the end of all days. Hence we do not shrink from pronouncing something a miracle until it is solved.

[[57]] An unconscious purposiveness is indeed the factum of formative nature, but no more than a factum. If one believes one has already solved the riddle in the word, one has rather only sharpened it; for how can profound purposiveness be thought as unconscious and blind?

[[58]] This question may stand, even after E. von Hartmann has written a Philosophie des Unbewussten (1869), which pursues the end comprehensively and richly in organic nature and in man. The concept of the unconscious, negative in itself, cannot as such contain a principle of possibility for positive facts. If to the ⟦27⟧ unconscious there is ascribed an absolute clairvoyance, to which all data are always and instantaneously at command, then this analogy of clairvoyance is of a pathological nature and has neither been critically established as a fact nor known in its grounds. Hence this theory of unconscious purposiveness still needs further elucidation.

[[59]] The efficient cause, the ordinary point of view of the understanding, shows itself powerless in the whole case before us. Creative nature encloses its workshop so carefully as if it wished, as it were, to cut off the possibility of thinking of an explanation from the efficient cause. Were the eye, for example, turned toward the light while it forms itself, one would presume in the first instance that the touching ray of light prepared this noble organ for itself. One would presume the efficient cause to lie in the power of light. But the eye forms itself in the darkness of the womb, in order, once born, to correspond to the light. So it is also with the other senses. Between light and the eye, between sound and the ear, between the solid and the mechanics of the organs of motion, etc., a predetermined harmony shows itself. For without having had any communion, they suddenly enter into the most intimate communion, and that not while they come to be but after they have come to be. Light has not excited sight, nor sound the ear, nor the element in which the creature is to move the instruments of motion; but the organs are there for these phenomena. The circle reveals itself clearly. The organ, with its activity, falls under the efficient cause; but with its structure, which proclaims an end, under the law of its own effect. The eye sees, but seeing itself has built the eye. The feet walk, but walking itself has shaped the joints of the feet. The organs of the mou ⟦28⟧ th speak, but speech itself, the necessity of uttering thought, formed them mobile from the outset. This circle is the magic circle of the simple fact; and the pre-established harmony seems to point to a power embracing the members, in which thought is the alpha and omega.

[[61]] That thought, as the first, underlies the phenomenon is shown by a simple consideration of the judgment. When we say "the eye sees," the outer activity (the efficient cause) is posited as the first, and the judgment limits itself to reproducing this activity mentally. The outer activity is the original, and no judgment is enveloped in this activity. If, on the other hand, we say "the eye has refracting media in order that it may see," then the judgment (in order that it may see) precedes the activity; the judgment is brought out in the fact itself. The outer phenomenon (the eye has refracting media) itself stands on the basis of the judgment. Where the efficient cause is considered purely and solely by itself, thought is only a copy, only a presentation of an activity foreign to itself. As soon, however, as the end shines in, the efficient cause rather presents a thought. When the circle arises through the motion of the radius, thought is an onlooker, and the efficient cause subsists for itself and determines the apprehending judgment. If, by contrast, two intersecting chords are drawn in the circle in order that the sides of equal rectangles may arise through the segments, then the efficient cause has directed itself according to the judgment of the end. The categories derived from motion as the efficient cause could accordingly be nothing other than the concepts necessary from the original activity and subsequently observed. Thought followed them as they came to be; and thought meets itself in them only in so far as the corresponding activity underlies its own essence. Where in the midst of the efficient forces the ⟦29⟧ predetermined harmony of the end arises, this predetermination, as the fact pervaded by thought proves, cannot possibly be chance. The strict subordination of functions, the vigorous self-preservation, the mysterious propagation, this far-reaching providence goes beyond the impotence of a blind game of dice.

[[62]] It is a simple but significant result that, so far as the end has become real in the world, thought has gone before as ground.

[[63]] Is the antecedent thought, this ideal prius, then sufficient for understanding the fact of the realised end? And how must such a thought be constituted? Bare thought, which has its realm for itself, does not suffice. From it comes nothing but an image, and indeed, if it remains by itself, only an empty and powerless image. We do not yet know it beyond this seclusion of inner motion.
