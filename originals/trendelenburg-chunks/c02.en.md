[[64]] The thought that underlies it is no mute image, like the figure on the blackboard, for it wills something. The eye has refracting media in order that it may see. Particular animals have this determinate structure in order that they may eat flesh. Muscles and joints serve for motion. In all such cases the thought has a determinate direction (seeing, nourishment, motion). The thought is placed in the very midst of things and presupposes them, as they presuppose it. Without division and difference no end is possible. If in the end the thought again completes what has been divided and establishes unity, only the experienced thought does this. Only the gaze that sees through the forces wins something from them. To the thought that built the eye, the nature of light and the means of organic life lay open and transparent. For otherwise it would not have shaped the one so wonderfully to fit the other that the latter now fulfils its longing in the former and receives life. The mechanics of the organs of motion reveals a thought which makes the laws of the solid and rigid serviceable to their own op⟦30⟧posite, living motion. The thought which assigns nourishment to organic life and prepares the organs for the assigned nourishment has penetrated the chemistry of substances and has known how to co-ordinate mechanical contrivances with the chemical process.

[[65]] We do not yet ask here how this circle is to open, if purposive existence rests on the governing thought and the thought in turn first presupposes the things. If the view of the whole is to develop into a philosophical view of the world, it is precisely this unity that forms the deepest point. We do not here lose sight of the beginning and course of the present investigation. The categories were developed out of motion, as the universal concepts generated from it. Do they suffice, it was asked, for the facts that we know? Then unambiguous phenomena came forward which on all sides proclaimed the concept of end as the ground of their essence, and we therefore seek out what in them reaches further than the categories of the efficient cause exhibited in motion. Since in the concept of end the temporal relation of cause and effect was reversed, the preceding thought appeared as the nearest solution of the marvel. The phenomena are only members of the whole. These individual members—only this lay in the facts—are preceded by the determining thought, and indeed by the thought that, as such, has insight into the efficient cause. Whether in general, and considering the whole, the efficient cause precedes the end or the end the efficient cause: this remains for the present undiscussed. For the matter itself does not yet ask about it.

[[66]] Is it the thought endowed with insight and experience alone? If it builds and thereby attains the end, it is at the same time efficient cause. Without this connection it is dull and flat and never strikes anything new out of the course of the forces. The thought is one with the efficient causes and directs them against one another, so that they serve it.

[[67]] ⟦31⟧ Now is this relation of the thought to the efficient cause cunning or power? It would be cunning if the efficient cause, standing over against it as something alien, were as it were blunted or worn down by itself in order to surrender to the thought. In cunning there reigns a disproportion. The thought feels its impotence in the realm of forces; but in knowing the superior might of the efficient cause, it knows it to be thoughtless and outweighs it through the advantage in which it stands as thought. Then the thought is always only partially recognised in the world, always only like the clever slave in his master's house or the crafty courtier in the vicinity of the prince. The thought then nonetheless remains only a stranger in the world. But if the thought is the first and the last, and no efficient cause is before it: then only does power lie in its hand. If the thought stands only on *one* side of the opposition, and the other side therefore remains over against it like a blind and alien force: then its deed is cunning and its work a virtue born of necessity. But if the thought does not stand between things, but in their midst and above them as that which is the same for all: then its unity with the efficient cause is dominion and power. This question cannot be answered from individual phenomena; but however the relation may be conceived, the unity of end and force results as necessary.

[[68]] The investigation, led by the matter itself, takes a path of its own. The efficient cause, as it appeared in motion, at first excluded the end. The end set itself directly against it, by reversing its law of time and making the later into the earlier, the earlier into the later. The thought that hastens ahead seemed to remove the contradiction; but in order that it may be able to remove it, it demands unity with the efficient cause. This interpenetration of end and force, of thinking and being, is therefore ⟦32⟧ just as much the simple fact as the presupposition of all understanding of it. This interpenetration exhibits itself most intuitively in the seed. The seed is the preformed whole. When, fertilised, it is given over to natural stimuli, it develops. From the germ to the blossom and the fruit, the governing, holding-together end and the appropriating, driving-forth force are in the development one and the same. It may seem that only the efficient cause is active, since the development seems to run its course blindly like a motion; but the development takes place from within and maintains the end. Force stands in the service of the end.

[[69]] Where the end appears, it wills an activity; for rest is what is purely passive and as such falls prey to the efficient cause. Seeing, walking, breathing, generating, etc., are such activities, which realise themselves as ends in organs. And if these ends, working together, form the harmony of the organism, then this life, the determining end of the whole, is in turn activity. Thus motion, which proved to be the first, returns as the last, although with a strong difference. As the last it appears rich and fulfilled in itself, the completing end, as it were; as the first it showed itself simple and almost empty, the grounding beginning. What lies between is the material of experience.

[[70]] The end, whether endowed with insight or with experience, takes possession of the material or of the force dwelling in the material, and by its contrivances and dispositions compels it to the activity which it demands. As one strikes the bright spark from the hard stone, so the end gives existence to the thought in refractory material. When Plato says in the Timaeus that the concept persuades necessity, he indicates in this beautiful image that the thought of the end enters into the most proper nature of the material and accomplishes the work from out of it. Now since the end ⟦33⟧ masters the forces of the material, it has impressed its own traces upon them, like written characters, in the structure and articulation, and the penetrating thought that comes to them will be able to read these signs in turn.

[[71]] The effect of the end on the material Aristotle called that which is necessary from a presupposition. The end is the presupposition. If it is to be fulfilled, this or that must take place in the material or with the forces of the material. To designate this necessity in matter we choose Aristotle's simple example, an instrument such as a saw. The thought of the end is, say, cutting through by friction. The concept of friction points to the nature of the material. No one makes a saw out of wool. A hard metal, e.g. iron, is demanded as the material of the instrument. The thin plate, the structure of the teeth, lie in the same way preformed as what is necessary in the thought of the end (cutting through by friction). What happens here in the instrument of art appears in the organs of nature which the end shapes. What more recent physiology achieves in the interpretation of the organs is only a confirmation of Aristotle's fundamental thought. Thus Aristotle demands that it be shown, from the determinate end of breathing, how this end can necessarily be attained only through something else that is determinate. In this way the nature of the respiratory instruments is to be comprehended. What Aristotle says in this connection about warming and cooling is probably only a presentiment. But the scientific task is sharply set out. More recent physiology has solved it. Since the end of breathing lies in the chemical alteration of the air, and the blood is to receive its oxygen and give off carbonic acid: a contact of the outer air and the ⟦34⟧ blood must be brought about. Hence it follows necessarily that the respiratory organ, in order to increase the contact, enlarges itself in a small space into an extended surface. This enlargement of the surface that decomposes the air now takes place either inwards, in the sac-like or branched manifold cavities of the lungs, or outwards, in the variously projecting formations of the gills, or in the tracheal system of insects, which is spread through all the organs. In this example, besides the necessary determination which the material receives, a second thing lies before our eyes. The determination flows from the nature of the material and is therefore different for the same end with different material. *One* end accomplishes itself here in the most diverse shapes, and indeed according to the higher demand of an overarching whole, e.g. of the element in which the animals are to live. The end demands increase of surface, and this law of the end runs through all forms, and it recurs in a similar way in the manifold leaf-formation of the tree. The many forms of secreting glandular structures all rest on *one* fundamental demand contained in the end. A large secreting surface must be realised in a small space, and this *one* task is solved in the most diverse forms. The fibrous formation of the muscles is necessary if an organ is to become shorter through the contraction of the muscles. Locomotion demands several points of support and the possible alternation of them, and accordingly a movable articulation of the body, which in the animal kinds is laid out and shaped differently according to different considerations.¹ In this way ⟦35⟧ everywhere the material receives a necessary influence from the end and, by entering into this necessary service, becomes the means in different shapes. What comes to light in the physiological sphere like a judgment of creative nature shows itself likewise in the ethical world. The different forms of government are, after all, to realise only *one* idea, unity of disposition, insight and power. According to the given elements of history and of the land, the constitution may show great differences; but the elements must so accommodate themselves that they always strive to satisfy this *one* concept. The end imposes a necessity on the material, and if in the necessity there is at the same time shown a freedom of possibility to attain the same end in different ways: this freedom is in turn restricted and transformed into necessity by higher considerations under which the end stands, or by relations of the efficient cause itself. But this necessity is here an interpenetration of end and force; for the end is empty without the forces of the material, and these are blind without it. Where both, mutually supporting each other, enter into appearance together, there we divine the artistic impulse which projects things from the whole and lays out from within what has been projected.

[[76]] If the end demands for its realisation something necessary, then this necessary thing, if it is not immediately there, becomes in turn an end, in order that it may come into being; and this necessary thing demands another necessary thing. What serves a governing end as a member governs in turn over something new which must submit to it. Thus one activity is fitted into another, and a subordination of ends presents itself. We illustrate this simply by a geometrical problem. The mean proportional between two given lines is to be found. In order that it may come into being, there is needed a right-angled triangle over the two lines, laid together into *one* straight line, as base, and indeed it must be so drawn that the perpendicular from its apex meets the boundary point of the two lines. The perpendicular can be erected. But where are the sides to be drawn so that, among the many possible angles, precisely a right one comes into being? A semicircle over the base resolves the difficulty. Here one problem slides into another. The governing end is the mean proportional. It is the first in thought and the last in actuality. The mean proportional demands a determinate right-angled triangle (that which is necessary according to the presupposition).¹ This right-angled triangle now becomes an end, and this end demands a semicircle over a given line as diameter; thereby the semicircle becomes ⟦37⟧ an end, and this end demands the half of the base, which will form the radius. Since this bisection can be accomplished immediately by two circles: the construction begins here and ends with the mean proportional, the end governing the whole procedure. Thus a system of ends presents itself. Other examples are easily found in the expositions above. We single out the following besides. Aristotle's Ethics begins by drawing attention to the subordination of ends in the circles of ethical life: the art of the saddler stands under the art of the horseman, which demands the former as its instrument, the art of the horseman under the art of the general, the art of the general under the art of the statesman. The ends of the statesman conversely demand the series of those arts as means. When the simple kernel of a sentence is expanded and its original concepts are expressed by clauses: these subordinate clauses are demanded like members by the end of the thought. The thought realises itself in them, and they in turn are borne by it. Thus the subordinate clauses are subordinated to the main clause. The humour in the crystalline lens is subject to the activity and the whole end of the lens; the lens is demanded by the thought of an organ that gathers the rays through refraction; the eye in turn is the necessary instrument of the animal destined for locomotion; and if the organs together serve the self-preservation of life, is the series of ends to break off here? For this individual perhaps, which seems as it were to be born of itself. But the individual serves the genus. Is the series then to close with the genus in such a way that the genus, at least as regards the end, is causa sui? Hardly. But the question becomes transcendent and leaves the concept of end, whose nature and relations are what is at issue.

[[80]] Where force alone governs, the cause dies away in the effect. Motion generates the line; with this generated effect the cause as such comes to an end. When seeing as the end builds the eye, the cause does not die away but first becomes alive in its effect, the organ. [[83]] Let us sum up the attempted analysis in a few words. Where the end appears, we distinguish the ideal of the thought, which Plato called the divine in things, and the real of the means, the force of the efficient cause, which Plato called the necessary. We distinguish both sides, but they are intimately one. The end attains its actuality through the force of the opposing cause, the efficient cause attains its truth through the end. The whole is before the parts, the effect before the cause. This inverted construction of the temporal sequence is the direct construction of the concept.

[[84]] 4. We have brought out the essential determinations of the end from facts which, as it were, came to ⟦40⟧ meet us. We have deliberately sought it out in cases in which it comes plainly to light, and indeed not precisely in ethics, which cannot let go of the end without overthrowing itself, but rather in the midst of physics, in which the efficient cause has its proper seat. Here, so it seems, understanding everywhere reaches its goal without the end. But perhaps it only seems so.

[[85]] If up to this point we have accepted the end without prejudice, but it is now a matter of its grounding: we first cast a glance at the theory of those who restricted or denied the end.

[[86]] Plato had tacitly posited the end in the idea; Aristotle, in keen consideration, had raised it to a principle; the Stoics had founded upon it their doctrine of the unity of providence and fate; patristic and scholastic philosophy had presupposed it, in the divine economy of salvation, as an undoubted concept. This tradition was broken through by Bacon of Verulam, the logician of the natural sciences, with an incisive restriction which he imposed on the concept of end.

[[87]] Bacon¹ rejected the concept of end for the consideration of nature, without condemning it outright. Since, like a universal, it makes the inquiring reason sluggish in the discovery of the efficient cause, it must be banished from the domain of physics into metaphysics. In the efficient cause Bacon saw the power of the world; from out of it he hoped, through science, for an extension of human dominion over nature. For him science counts for nothing in itself without the inventive experiment. He estimates the fruitfulness of the principle less by the measure of the concept that breaks the ⟦41⟧ spell of experience, less by the significance of the scientific consequences, than by utility, and almost by a still medieval, fantastic hope of alchemy, to coax forth from the efficient forces of nature a new creation as the work of man. Bacon feels well enough that the end transfigures nature through God, but the transfiguration is for him only like the useless life of a nun. If the end is banished from living physics into abstract metaphysics, it is thereby forcibly severed from flesh and blood, and it dies of this separation. It is moreover untrue that the knowledge of the end generates nothing. When the gifted physician by his art removes the obstruction that weighs upon an organ and restores to it the freedom for which it was born, or when the circumspect educator leads the capacities, in the whole and in the individual, towards their harmonious destination: they effect this great thing only from the end they have recognised, and the end generates here no less than the physical cause in the experiment.

[[107]] Kant, who in his *Kritik der Urtheilskraft* investigated the nature of the end more deeply, must be heard first on this question.

[[108]] Kant begins with the distinction of the determining and the reflecting power of judgment.¹ Whereas the determining power of judgment subsumes the particular under a given universal, the reflecting power seeks the universal for a given particular; whereas the former proceeds like the judge to whom the law is prescribed, the latter, like the legislator, draws up the rule from the cases.

[[109]] Since the reflecting power of judgment is to ascend from the particular to the universal, it needs a principle which it cannot draw from experience, precisely because this principle has to ground the unity of all empirical principles and the possibility of their systematic subordination. It takes it from its own understanding and considers the empirical laws according to such a unity, as if an understanding likewise, though not ours, had given them for the benefit of our faculties of knowledge, in order to make possible a system of experience according to particular laws of nature. Thus there results the end, through which nature is so represented ⟦47⟧ as if an understanding contained the ground of the unity of its manifoldness. For the end is the concept of an object in so far as the concept at the same time carries within itself the ground of the actuality of the object. The end is therefore only borrowed from us, nothing but an analogy, nothing but a guiding thread for extending natural science according to a new principle. As we cannot at all see a priori the possibility of such a causality of nature according to ends, so we cannot properly observe the ends in nature as intentional either. Accordingly the purposiveness of nature will be only a subjective principle of reason, in that it works regulatively for the human power of judgment, but does not constitutively determine anything about the nature of objects.² As in the practical sphere the point of view of a maxim gives direction and consistency to the motley play of human actions, without a law of the matter itself needing thereby to have been attained: so the concept of end, through which sensible phenomena receive a spiritual order, is such a maxim for the reflecting power of judgment, as it were a human attempt to become master, in its own way, of the infinitely manifold things. 2

[[112]] Kant cannot do otherwise. The closed consistency of his whole view demands it so. Were the end something in things, were the understanding accordingly the architect of the ⟦48⟧ world: then with the end known the thing in itself would be known, and this so carefully veiled image of the gods would be unveiled. If Kant makes space and time into forms of human intuition, the categories into root concepts of the human understanding, the unity of things asserted in judgment into a consequence of the unity of human self-consciousness, the idea of the unconditioned into a mere spur and goad of human knowing, so that it chases after a phantom that recedes ever further into the distance: then Kant must in a similar way degrade the concept of purposiveness into a human maxim. So the knowing human being wanders about, cut off on all sides from things, yet in lawful accord with himself. But is it then to be enough if a clock merely keeps regular time with itself, regardless of whether it goes by the sun, the great clock of the world?

[[115]] We therefore examine the grounds and the inner coherence of the view.

[[116]] Just as, according to Kant, space and time are supposedly unable to be empirical because they condition the possibility of thinking particular spaces and times, and hence condition the whole of experience: so the principle of purposiveness is supposed to be transcendental and not to stem from experience, because it is destined "to ground the unity of all empirical principles under likewise empirical but higher principles, and thus the possibility of their systematic subordination to one another." These proofs run parallel; and hence the same leap is to be recognised here that was pointed out above in the view of space and time. If space and time are subjective forms, or if the representation of space and time is generated by our own act ⟦49⟧ in order to apprehend the objects of experience: it by no means follows from this that space and time have nothing to do with things. Rather the fundamental forms will overlap in such a way that the representation of them is produced by the mind just as they themselves are produced by things. The subjective and the objective express only relations, and therefore the realm of all possibility cannot be so split that what is subjective is not objective, and what is objective is not subjective. We find the same mistake again in the view of the end. Granted that this principle grounds experience and therefore, born in advance from the mind, does not first come into us from experience: this does not prove that the end has no actuality in nature. Its subjective origin testifies not at all against its objective significance.

Certainly, if Kant strictly restricts nature to the "sum total of the objects of the senses" and thereby tears it away from thought: then the efficacy of the end cannot be seen from such a concept, delimited in advance; but neither can it be refuted from it, since the presupposition is only arbitrarily formed. What is supposed to lie of the transcendent in such a mode of explaining nature thus only soars beyond the narrow bounds within which nature, as though it were nothing but the flat fact of the senses, has been unlawfully enclosed.

[[122]] ⟦50⟧ What worth, then, would such a merely regulating, but nothing establishing, concept of the end have? The point of view of the end would have solely the function of bringing order into the confused mass of inflowing representations, a clever order, bound as strictly in unity as a feudal constitution; but it is only our order, not the order of the world, it is only the makeshift of our understanding, not the law of things. We connect representations according to the rule of the end without seeing that the things, whose deputies the representations are, grip one another in the same connection. In this merely subjective significance the thought of the rule falls away from itself. If a rule, as e.g. in geometrical constructions, in arithmetical calculations, in grammatical relations, is destined to form a thing according to it (a figure, a number, a sentence): then the rule plainly touches the origin, and thus the innermost nature, of the thing. What guided the understanding in the universal rule presents itself in the thing as a determinate and individual phenomenon. The end that is "regulative, though not constitutive" falls short of this simple efficacy of a rule. But there are rules, one may say, which, like rules of gender, bring together according to mere appearance even what by the origin of the thing would scarcely belong together, merely in order to come to the aid of memory with a short formula for extensive particulars. Is the rule of the end to mean no more than this? Does one wish to degrade it so low, merely in order to withdraw it from things? And even if one were to bring oneself to that, that external rule of appearance would still stand at a higher level, because it does not let itself be deprived of saying something about things. If the end is to form only a rule in knowing, without at the same time being the rule of the thing: then instead of a necessary concatenation of things it yields only a contingent connection made by the mind.

[[123]] ⟦51⟧ What does it actually mean, then, when Kant asserts that the concept of combinations and forms of nature according to ends is at least *one* principle more for bringing its phenomena under rules where the laws of causality according to its mere mechanism do not suffice? What worth has it when "entirely new prospects" are promised us from it? This one principle more, these new prospects, are truly a wealth of a peculiar kind. Otherwise a principle is supposed to simplify knowledge; in this case it brings only complication, in that it attempts an interpretation of phenomena which directly contradicts the acknowledged principle of the efficient cause. It brings discord instead of unity. And if the contradiction is not meant so seriously, because the principle is after all only a subjective connection: then the whole principle is not much better than that of an alphabetical arrangement or of some other synoptic sequence. It is then only the principle of a registry, so that the mind may find its way about in the voluminous files of the world. The human mind, which directs itself by this rule of the end given in advance, becomes virtually an index of things according to a scheme sketched in advance. It is then no principle of fathoming, which is nonetheless what the cause of the end gives itself out to be, but only a principle of convenient survey.

[[124]] In declaring the end a regulative but not constitutive concept, Kant places it beside the idea of the unconditioned, which according to his doctrine works in the same way.² Has the a priori rule, which modestly renounces the law of the thing, the same sense in both cases? If within the efficient cause the idea of the unconditioned spurs the mind not to rest in the limited and individual, but to advance from the part it has grasped to the conditions: ⟦52⟧ then this motion remains within the same circle of the view. The idea declared regulative works in fact only subjectively, in that it nowhere grants rest to the sluggish understanding and enlivens the activity of investigation. But with the regulative concept of the end it is otherwise. This does not drive onward along the path already taken of the explanation attempted from the nearest efficient cause, but suddenly turns the whole consideration around and compels the understanding, which wants to comprehend things from things, to fall out of its role, as it were. This transformation has a right to be called a rule only if it leads into the truth. But if the end is nothing in things, then it projects things askew into our mind; and the end is not an enlivening rule of inquiry but a falsifying distortion of the view. Hence Kant errs when he writes: "if only we abide by this presupposition as a merely regulative principle, even error cannot harm us." The assumption that the end is regulative but not constitutive is in contradiction with itself, since it can only be an actual rule if at the same time it posits the truth of its mode of consideration.

[[127]] How, further, does the end, declared to be a subjective mode of connection, stand in relation to the other subjective elements of the Kantian philosophy? If the end were a necessary form of our knowledge in the sense in which space and time are the form of intuition and synthetic unity the form of judgment: then the end, like these, would have to appear everywhere and without exception as the necessary stamp of grounding. Without choice the end would always be there wherever we seek the cause of phenomena, as space is always there where we intuit outwardly, and time where we observe inwardly, and unity where we connect two concepts in judgment. Just as, according to Kant, ⟦53⟧ all these forms prove their subjective and a priori nature by the fact that we cannot unchain ourselves from them in our mental activities: so the end too would have to carry this thoroughgoing necessity within itself. In vain do we look about for such a mark. The end is rather first called to aid where the explanation by the efficient cause breaks off. When the object itself compels the inquiring understanding to give up the path it has taken, then the possibility of the end offers itself, as a supplement, so to speak. Where, therefore, the subjective rule of the end is to be applied is decided by the essence of the thing, and the rule therefore cannot close itself off within the narrow circle of a merely subjective mode of consideration, and determines itself from the object. Thus Kant's view leads beyond itself.

[[129]] It happens to Kant once more within the same domain that the concepts spill over the carefully staked-out boundaries. So it happens in the investigation of the beautiful. Since the beautiful arouses satisfaction through the concord of the faculties of knowledge, since it awakens a harmonious play of the powers of representation, since it brings, e.g., the directions of imagination into accord among themselves or formative intuition into accord with the understanding: it is supposed, according to Kant, to carry within itself the form of purposiveness without the representation of an end. In so far as the cognitive powers are harmoniously moved, the object is there, as it were, for them and falls under the form of purposiveness; but the end, which as concept is the producing ground of the object, is nonetheless lacking. Beauty is not the cause of the possibility of the thing.

[[130]] Here too Kant labours in vain to separate the concepts by strict boundary lines of the faculties. The concepts do not lie spatially beside one another, but work into ⟦54⟧ one another. When Kant distinguishes free beauty, in which the imagination plays, as it were, with itself, e.g. the splendour of colour in the blossom, from beauty bound by the concept, in which the imagination enters into agreement with the understanding: then in the latter the teleological power of judgment intervenes determiningly in the aesthetic. Beauty of this kind is, as it were, the concept appearing; and in the concept a mode of appearance lies preformed and intimated for the designing imagination. The beauty of the male body, e.g., is measured against the concept of male strength and dignity, against the idea of man, as it were. Formal purposiveness, as according to Kant it comes forward in the concept of the beautiful, tacitly takes its determining ground from real purposiveness.

[[132]] Thus the end cannot be restricted to a merely subjective and regulative form of judging, and everything depends on the concept being the indwelling, shaping soul of things, and the soul, as Plato puts it, being earlier than the body.

[[133]] The deed corresponds to our representation. We act upon things according to the purposiveness we have apprehended, and things answer in accordance with this action. We thus apply constitutively the concept of end, which was supposed to be only regulative (e.g. in healing, in the training of the body, in education), and the nature of things suffers, demands and confirms this procedure.

[[135]] 6. In Hegel's derivation of the end the following moments come into consideration.

[[136]] The concept has developed itself into the syllogism. The syllogism is mediation. Through its motion this mediation is sublated. Nothing is now in and for itself, each thing only by means of another. The individual is a universal, and indeed because it has particularity in it at the same time; the ⟦55⟧ universal is truly universal only through its realisation in the individual, and particularity is the unity of both. The result is therefore an immediacy that has issued from the sublation of mediation. As the moments interpenetrate, being issues forth as a thing that is in and for itself, that is sufficient to itself: objectivity.¹

[[137]] Objectivity is at first only immediate, as every concept must first free itself from immediacy. The moments still subsist in independent indifference as objects outside one another. Their unity is still only an external one. So it is in the sphere of mechanism.

[[138]] The objects appear in this external pressure and impact as non-independent, but within a greater independence; for objectivity as a whole relates itself negatively to itself and thereby generates the relation of non-independence in the individual objects. Hence arises the concept of centrality, in which the object itself is related to what is external.²

[[139]] The non-independence of the object reveals itself in the striving towards the centre. Since this striving is a striving towards the determinately opposed object, the centre itself thereby falls apart, and its negative unity passes over into objectified opposition. Centrality is therefore the relation of these objectivities, negative and tensed against one another. Thus chemism results, in that the object is posited in its existence as differentiated against its other.³

[[140]] In chemism there is an inner totality of both determinatenesses, and there therefore appears the impulse to sublate the opposed ⟦56⟧ one-sided subsistence of the object and to make itself into the real whole in existence. From the difference of the opposites there arises a neutral product, and the neutral is again kindled into differentiation.

[[144]] These processes are external, and they appear as independent of one another. As they pass over into products, their finitude shows itself, just as, conversely, the process exhibits the presupposed immediacy of the differentiated objects as a null one. Thus the externality and immediacy in which the concept of the object was sunk are negated. Through this negation it becomes free and is posited for itself over against that externality and immediacy. This objective free concept is the end.¹

[[145]] The end is now in itself directed towards the determinateness of externality, and its simple unity is the unity that repels itself from itself and preserves itself therein, a cause which is cause of itself, or whose effect is immediately the cause.² By making itself into the other of its subjectivity and so objectifying itself, it sublates the difference of the subjective and the objective and closes together only with itself.

[[146]] But the end is at first only finite and external, because the content of the end is called forth by the given object and the material for its realisation must be sought in the world as it is found. The end is thereby as contingent as the object is a particular one.

[[147]] The execution is assured in the means, which is subject to the concept. For the concept is this immediate power, because it is self-identical negativity, in which the ⟦57⟧ being of the object is determined throughout only as an ideal one.¹ For since the concept is the truth of substance, and substance repels itself from itself and remains with itself in the things that thereby arise: this power of substance is also the power of the concept, and therefore for the end, the concept that has become free, the means are present without qualification.

[[150]] Thus the objective is made conformable to the end as the free concept. But as soon as the end is attained, the one-sidedness of the finite shows itself. Nothing has been brought about but a form posited externally on the material. The attained end is therefore only an object which is in turn means or material for other ends, and so on to infinity. What was just now end is now again means; and these concepts relieve one another. The end is thus the same as what the means is; and the concept of the end as such has not yet attained any true objectivity. This progress to infinity, this relativity of the executed end, this emerging identity of end and means points to a new stage—the idea; it is the absolute unity of the concept and of objectivity, reason, which is the eternal intuiting of itself in the other, the concept which in its objectivity has carried itself out, the object which is inner purposiveness, essential subjectivity.

[[151]] At these stages objectivity ascends from the pressure and impact of mechanism to the full victory of the end that pervades the world.
