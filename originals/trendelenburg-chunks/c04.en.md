[[230]] All life is supposed to stem from physical and chemical conditions; this presupposes the possibility of a spontaneous generation, an origination of the first organic thing out of the inorganic. Such a thing belongs only to the belief of natural science in its beginnings, when it as yet knew no rigour of knowledge through experiments and abstractions, through reliably exclusive trials. The hypothesis must consistently presuppose a primordial generation, a spontaneous generation, a generation without parents, which is what the expressions generatio aequivoca, generation from the heterogeneous, designate. But as yet no example has been demonstrated; chemistry, which does indeed produce some organic products, has as yet let no organism come into being; up to now this has been denied to it. The lowest, first living beings, the monera or protists, are there, but their origin from the forces of matter has not been demonstrated.

[[231]] The hypothesis must lead to the conclusion that related kinds are capable of producing hybrids and that the hybrids as such are fertile. Just as the individuals of the varieties, which are the basis of the analogy, mate fruitfully with one another, so too the individuals of the species would have to do so. Only thereby would the intermediate forms and transitions be explained, or only thereby would the production of new properties be made possible. Up to now the trials have not had success to the extent that the hypothesis leads one to presuppose. The old theory has ⟦85⟧ in the capacity for reproduction a testimony to the constancy of species.

[[234]] The hypothesis requires new hypotheses in many respects in order to carry through the unity and the continuously progressing descent. Experience lacks the intermediate forms that must be presupposed for the theory, as, for example, the intermediate forms between the apes now living and the human beings descended from them and arisen in the struggle for existence. One may assume that they have perished and that perhaps their fossil remains will one day be found, perhaps brought to light from the bottom of the sea. Up to now experience does not keep pace with the theory, and there are gaps enough.

[[235]] Thus up to now the theory lacks the necessary proofs and evidence precisely at the real nodal points of development.

[[236]] 5. It is a question for natural science whether, the remaining premises being presupposed as true, the principle of adaptation in the struggle for life and the favouring of contingent circumstances suffice to explain the immeasurable manifoldness of species in their form and articulation; for the powers to which, for example, the animal must adapt itself are relatively uniform: the atmospheric air, water, the products of the soil, light and warmth. How are the circumstances to be thought that drew forth this property and no other? and how did they vary in such manifoldness as to drive forth such manifold formations? It is not enough to operate with the universal of adaptation alone; it will be necessary to demonstrate it in the particular and under the particular circumstances; only then will one be able to judge whether it is in fact possible that, for example, the genesis of the compound and yet precise eye becomes comprehensible from the instinct of adaptation and from heredity. This will have to hold still more for the organ of the brain, still unriddled today, the bearer of consciousness and of the higher functions.

[[237]] 6. For the construction of the natural system the fact of heredity suffices in the first instance; but it remains a question for natural science whether inheritance, which is indeed a fact renewing itself everywhere, really becomes intelligible from matter, which is, after all, said to be the same. Is it enough, in order to comprehend inheritance from matter, that we see reproduction take place in the lower animals through division of the same matter? The division of the same matter into other individuals does not explain the propagation of the form that governs the matter and prepares and adapts to itself the future conditions of life.

[[238]] Even if the human egg, like every mammalian egg and ⟦86⟧ every animal egg, is in the first instance a simple cell; if this cell divides into two halves, these divide again and, growing, bring forth by continued division a heap of cells out of which the germ or embryo is formed; if then, out of the simple germinal form, the differences of the organs emerge through a series of developments, and these differences correspond in the order of development to the systematic articulation of the classes; and if in man only towards the end of germinal life, and only shortly before birth, those differences become recognisable which distinguish the mature human germ from the mature germ of the most nearly related tailless ape: then the theory sees in this developmental history of the human individual a brief, compressed repetition of the developmental history of the blood-related vertebrate stock. This may well be too much. Were there in this process a real repetition, and not merely a passage through the external forms, one would have to expect that, for example, the seven-month human child, if it were born and cared for, would become an ape and not a human being. The external shape cannot do it if the essence driving within is a different one. Hence this principle of knowledge drawn from the developmental history of the egg, this dazzling piece of evidence for the hypothesis, requires some restriction in the strength of the proof.

[[239]] 7. It is possible that research in natural science will fill up, in these directions, the gaps that still exist. But we must further follow the consideration of principle which compelled us to enter into this new design of German philosophy of nature.

[[240]] In the first place, matter, and indeed matter alone, is presupposed. If matter is the principle (matter in the first instance in general, a universale taken wholesale, which requires closer determination), then it is consistent to hold the principle, as necessary energy, to be eternal. But if matter is the principle of plurality, then over against it a principle of unity is just as necessary, and it is therefore just as consistent to declare the principle of unity to be just as eternal; and if we ask more closely what this principle of unity is, and we find that it is concept or idea or end, then it is just as necessary to posit this ideal element as the eternal. The question of the relation of the two to each other would then first yield the right view of the principle; for they are not at variance, but plurality is subordinated to unity.

[[241]] Such universal inferences, however, natural science gladly leaves to the inferring metaphysics. The theory leaves the question of the eternal, in which the span of time vanishes, ⟦87⟧ undecided, and begins with a determinate point in time, with the cooling body of the earth, back to which it infers, and over against it with the light and warmth of the sun.

[[242]] 8. The theory begins with what it infers backwards from established facts. But it does not show how out of the inorganic an organism, out of the non-sentient a sentient self, comes to be. The struggle for life always presupposes a unity for which the whole exerts itself. We do not yet ascribe sensation to the plant, but the plant that wrestles for existence works for itself as a whole. Where the root of a tree strives into the depths, but on rocky ground covered with a thin layer of earth cannot go deep and perpendicular, it adapts itself to the given conditions and goes horizontally in the soil all the further and the more branched. The tree thereby preserves itself. Where the animal fights for existence, it has the centre of a whole in the feeling of life, in pleasure and pain, which are for it the immediate indications of a furtherance or an impairment of its existence. From this centre the striving proceeds; in it the lowest animal possesses a self. How out of carbon and oxygen and nitrogen, which do not live and do not feel, there comes to be in combination something living, like the plant, or something sentient, like the animal, or something conscious and self-conscious, like man: this neither the old atomists, such as Lucretius, nor the materialists of the last century, such as La Mettrie, nor the German continuers of Darwin, equipped with more means of explanation, have shown up to now even by approximate surmise. So long as this chasm between the non-sentient and the sentient exists, the ideal, whose kernel is the inner end, has its station in this gap. And should an experiment ever succeed that showed the transition, then at this point the end would grow forth, the end which would have been matter and would from then on become the regulator of the world. But we are not yet so far.

[[243]] The struggle for life is a struggle for ends, for without such no self can be thought. The self exercises its defensive activity according to the measure of its ends, the ends without which its life would not subsist, and it draws to itself the materials and the conditions of its life according to the same measure, felt by it. The animal enjoys its essence in the functions of the ends that underlie its existence. In the strivings of the animal which spring from affects, such as fear or anger, or take place for the sake of affects, such as the satisfaction of pleasure, the inner ends of its own essence make themselves known.

[[244]] 9. The struggle for life, which calls forth new forms of ⟦88⟧ activity and new organs and fixes them through inheritance, is in this direction called adaptation. The struggle for existence produces, for example, in animals the teeth or the lungs or the gills which the being needs, and teaches in instinct the cunning by which it conquers, in that it accommodates itself to the conditions of life and wins furtherance from them out of their own nature.

[[245]] The concept of adaptation leads to the end; there lies in it only another name for the formative end. Through adaptation the theory gains a means which tacitly presupposes the concept of end. A tool, such as the drill, is adapted in its construction to the human hand, a lens to the structure of the eye. The hand is to guide the tool and heighten its capacity in it; the eye is to see a sharper or larger image. The end lies open to view in this adaptation. If adaptation in plants and animals makes itself known in the interaction with the climate, with the surrounding nature, as when a gilled newt, transferred from the water to the land, transforms itself into a being breathing with lungs, or when the wading birds, such as storks, cranes, snipes, have long legs and long bills in keeping with the ground on which they seek their food: then adaptation behaves no differently therein. Ends are plainly there. But since adaptation gives the species the constant property only through long inheritance, since adaptation goes on in unmeasured time, in which the small modification gradually adds up to a large one, adaptation, where it positively creates real tools and invents means, has merely broken up the execution of the *one* end running throughout into the smallest steps. If it could ever be demonstrated that through adaptation and inheritance in the struggle for life the artful, intelligent eye of man had been formed out of the light-spot of the lowest animals, then the *one* end would have worked through aeons and reached its goal little by little in the smallest beginnings and stages. Adaptation, in the interaction of the individual with the conditions of life of its surroundings, has the self-activity and the habituation of the living being as *one* of its factors, and the being exercises these according to the ends that condition its life. Adaptation is no knocking into shape from outside. The Greeks called the beautiful, measured against the necessity of self-preservation, the superfluous, and designated the beautiful by the superfluous (περισσόν). If one applies the truth of this expression to the human eye or the human ear, and if one thinks, in the one case, of the rods and cones of the retina, which according to the physiological interpretation serve for the harmony of colours, and, in the other, of the Corti corpuscles, which act for the striking of the ⟦89⟧ nerves tuned to a tone; if then feelings of harmony are conditioned by both, feelings which seem to be there only for their own sake: then we find it hard to accept that these profound dispositions have worked themselves out merely through the struggle for life favoured by circumstances. In any case it will have to be shown what trait in the struggle for life, what disharmony in the conflict of the being with its conditions, gave existence to these pieces of evidence of ideal harmony, to the beautiful, which belongs to perfection and not to the necessity of existence.

[[246]] 10. The struggle for life, which through exercise of force, through adaptation to the real conditions, through division of labour in unmeasured time, gradually leads to greater perfection and wins thousandfold life from every corner of the world, presupposes a germ that should preserve itself and defend itself and multiply itself. In this germ, then, the future world of life, the life of the aeons on the earth, is enclosed, just as in the acorn the thousand-year oak that developed out of it lay enfolded. The theory posits such beings, the monera, the protists. It is all one whether the seed of the annual plant proves its force and develops its essence for *one* year, the acorn for a thousand years, or the generative germ of all life, the moneron, through periods of time that may count by millions of years; time does not alter the concept, any more than space alters the concept of the ellipse, whether it be described by a planet or, according to the same law, by a pencil. Every moneron or every protist is pregnant with the possibility of a world of life, on condition that it is compelled by its material surroundings into the struggle for existence. Out of the acorn, in interaction with light and air and moisture of the soil, comes this oak and no other being; and out of the moneron, in a similar, only manifoldly modified interaction, comes this infinitely branched world of life which descends from it. The point of the beginning is only pushed back. Leibniz, who did not yet lead man back through the race of apes to the moneron, who stopped at the first-created man as the eternal germ of the human race, once made a remark to the effect that God, in Adam, thought and willed the history of the world. According to Leibniz's thought, the plan of providence lies in the first man. Leibniz, for whom the concept of end and power in the series of efficient causes were harmoniously attuned, would today, if he held the ⟦90⟧ concatenation that has been discovered to be correct, have extended the thought he conceived of Adam, and, stretching the history of men into the history of the living in all the forms of its multiform existence, would have stopped before the moneron and read and admired in it a greater plan. A chance, a favouring by chance, he would not concede, not even chance in the restricted sense in which there is no chance within the necessity of efficient causes, but only chance as the unforeseen, measured against some arbitrary end.

[[248]] 11. Since cells have been found to be the first formations of life, the question has been raised whether the cell, as first unity, already falls under the concept of the individual. One has, however, returned to the old measure of the end. The individual has accordingly been defined as a unitary community in which all the parts work together towards a homogeneous end or are active according to a determinate plan. The subordination of the parts to the unity of the whole is determined by this concept, and the more manifoldly this articulation serves the whole, the unitary community, the more perfect the individual appears.¹

[[249]] Is the inner end, by which the individual is here measured, to be only a principle of knowledge for man? only a mark by which we recognise the individual? as we recognise, for example, the right angle by an arc of 90º, the quadrant of the circle, while the circle has nothing to do with the concept of the right angle as one of two equal adjacent angles. This is not the meaning.

[[250]] 12. The concept of end goes deeper. Even if we were to leave it undecided for a few moments that it is the generating principle, the principle that conditions the plan, it is nevertheless indisputably the preserving, healing, perfecting principle. If, for example, we use an organ in accordance with its end and the end of its parts, and maintain and protect the unity and interaction of the parts, we preserve the organ. The physician grasps the inner end of the parts in relation to the whole when he seeks successfully to remove an obstruction or to initiate a new formation. The disease of the organism fights against the hostile influences in the sense of preservation, and the vis medicatrix naturae works in the sense of the whole, hence of the concept of end. Where the physician prevents, he averts the disturbance of an end; where, in the course of the disease, he assists the furthering forces, he does ⟦91⟧ so according to the measure of the end; where, finally, he has the task of replacing a deficient force or a missing limb, he does so in the sense of the end that has suffered impairment, as, for example, in the sense of the end that refraction has in the eye, when he corrects the range of vision with a lens. Thus the end works in healing. When the teacher of gymnastics strengthens the motions of the body and trains them to manifoldness and beauty, or when the educator leads the pupil towards his inner destination and harmoniously stimulates the faculties of his mind, then the end works perfectingly.

[[252]] Were the end ever driven out of the generation of the living and replaced, as in Empedocles, by a concurrence of chance, it would still remain causal in preservation and perfection.

[[253]] But it is more correct to conclude that preservation and generation, which go together, also have the same ground in their origin.

[[254]] 13. When, having arrived from the moneron at man, we look back and survey the course which the struggle for life took in unmeasured time, it is the course from being to better being (ab esse ad melius esse); and the struggle for life, the arouser of force for the end of the self, is the means to the strengthening and elevation of the self, and, thought universally, the means of development towards better being. Consistently, one will continue this intuition into human life. One will ascribe the arousal of the inventive force, as is done (cf. above II. p. 11 f.), to the need of man, born defenceless, in the struggle for food, in the struggle with nature. One will, with Hobbes, place the war of all against all at the beginning of things, in order thereby to bring men to feel the necessity that many subordinate themselves to the unconditional power of *one*; one will perhaps, with Spinoza, take self-preservation as the basis of ethics too, and, so that a strengthened power of the community may be possible, demand justice as a means of concord; one will let the needs of man refine themselves, and thereby perfect themselves, in the competition of egoistic forces, the morality of a one-sided political economy.

[[255]] But one will not prevent thinking man, as soon as he gains room, from thinking himself, from grasping in the ends of his essence a value above all values, from conceiving the concept of the person, and from seeking the spiritual conditions of the world-order in which he may find himself and, in the struggle of his affects and representations, preserve and hold himself.

[[256]] If, now, one sees the theory which explains the creation of life ⟦92⟧ through adaptation and inheritance in the struggle for life dissolve, through adaptation, into ends, into nothing but small steps of ends, all of which, however, were governed through the aeons by *one* end running throughout, the end of self-preservation, which in the eternal wrestling and straining of beings divides itself as the unity into many ends, pervading the tools; and if thinking man now grasps this great unity, from the moneron up to himself, as a world-order, all the more as an order since it goes from being to better being: then he will ask himself whether this development, governing billions of years, merely appears in his head, the head of erring man, as the grandest mirror-image there is, while in itself it is a product deaf and blind, like carbon and oxygen and nitrogen or the law of gravity or of the refraction of light, or whether, on the contrary, a plan of eternal origin makes itself known here. The word "plan" we take from the natural scientists, and we consistently extend it from the natural-historical developmental history of man or of an animal or of a plant to the developmental history and the genealogical tree of life in general.

[[257]] If development in the struggle for existence keeps to *one* direction, and thus strives towards *one* goal, this consistency, which runs through unmeasured times, vouches for the reality of the driving end. The spectacle of development, which a thought underlies, has become greater, but the thought rules in the end as before.

[[258]] Natural science is unhindered in its intuition in going its great and successful way out of its own need; whoever wished to hinder it would not merely toil in vain, but would also lack trust in the reason in the world-order which science explores. What it yields can stand in contradiction with this only temporarily or only apparently.

[[259]] Thus, for example, one seeks to prove from the squandering of the germs of life in nature that no inner end governs the formation of life, but that only the favour of chance makes it possible. On a blossoming tree many blossoms blow away, and before *one* sets and becomes fruit and bears seed, many perish. From numerous eggs animals of the species could arise, but many are destroyed or consumed, only a few are fertile, and still fewer individuals preserve themselves for development in accordance with nature. By the standard of human understanding, which does nothing uselessly, husbands its means, and seeks to create much with little, but not little with much, there appears here the opposite of a purposive formation. Hence only the efficient cause, and chance in the struggle of efficient causes for existence, is supposed to be what ultimately determines; a fool, it is thought, is he who, in the face of this simple ⟦93⟧ consideration of the germs of life lavishly scattered and carelessly destroyed, believes in an inner end in the process of life. However, the consideration is only a consideration from the standpoint of the part and not of the whole. For in the whole, the perishing of germs of life too will have its significance. If the sure plan demanded this path and no other, if only on it was development towards the higher possible, then one would be demanding the impossible in wanting it otherwise; it is the only purposive path if it yields the best that was possible. The necessary is then the good.

[[260]] The struggle for existence has often before been noticed as the stern background of life. Heraclitus called war the father of all things, and Jacob Böhme spoke of the grim quality of God over against gentle love. But this did not prevent them from recognising therein the sway of a divine power. The same Heraclitus who declared war the father of all things declared the invisible harmony mightier than the visible, and Jacob Böhme proclaimed in the necessary disunion the dawn in its rising.

[[261]] Struggle is the arouser of force, the impulse to invention; but what gives shape in adaptation, what invents and tests, is neither co-posited in the struggle nor to be found in matter as such, for it cannot be thought without end and means.

[[262]] The question of the inner end in nature is no idle question. Where it finds an answer that becomes knowledge, it becomes causal in art, in ethics, in religion.

[[263]] That the search for a plan does not make knowledge divided against itself, and that the question of the end produces no dualism, is evident from the unity for which the end strives. Knowledge of it curtails no knowledge of forces, but grounds in them an intuition of higher unity. Thus the monism of matter, the sole dominion of efficient forces, which seeks to build itself up on the overthrow of the ideal, is a premature song of victory.

[[264]] From the considerations here attempted it may emerge that German Darwinism, which wants to let the end perish in the efficient cause, does not do away with it, but itself presupposes it. What it demonstrates of the struggle for existence as the arouser of forces fits itself, as means, into the end.

[[265]] 12. The two directions of comprehending knowing have now been pursued, one of which is turned towards the efficient cause, the other towards the end. In both a wonder shows itself at first glance. For in the ⟦94⟧ fathoming of the efficient cause, thought goes backwards, out of the present into the vanished past, out of the surface of existence into the depths of becoming; and in the projection of the end, by means of that first process, out of the present into the future, which as yet is not. Thus thought, in its vigorous act, triumphs over the power of time. How this can happen, however, has been discussed above.
