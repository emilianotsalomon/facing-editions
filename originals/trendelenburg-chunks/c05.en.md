[[266]] # ⟦95⟧ X. END AND WILL.

[[267]] 1. The last investigations turned on the objective end, and indeed on the inner end, i.e. one which so orders the parts and forces of an organism into agreement that it constitutes its essence and preserves it and the genus.

[[268]] In the impulses which experience offered to set the end above the efficient cause (the causa finalis above the causa efficiens) as the proper principle, there lay still more; there lay in them a concept which comes about only on the foundation of the end.

[[269]] For it might be thought that the end, imprinted on the world and carried out through the world, made it into one great machine, in which, much as in a planetarium turned by the hand of the astronomer, all motions ran their course according to the alien thought, as if at the finger of God. But in those considerations of organic nature life confronted us, and the concept of the ensouled goes hand in hand with the concept of the living.

[[270]] The inner end is the properly individuating principle of the world. From the standpoint of the efficient cause we measure substance as peculiar according to the law of formation ⟦96⟧ that underlies it, as, e.g., the individual crystal according to the geometrical laws of chemism that give shape in space. But when formation takes place through the end, out of the whole, and when the realisation and preservation of the whole has become the task set by the unity thought beforehand, then the individuating principle, i.e. the principle that strives toward a relative whole, presents itself in this more sharply.

[[271]] Every end is directed to individual activities in space and time; it wills what is individual. Though itself a thought, it does not remain in a universal that hovers there like something merely possible. If space and time alone are taken as the individuating principle, then its essence is found only in this, that a separated multiplicity is produced for our consideration, and no concern is taken whether and through what the separated gathers itself together as a whole. From the inner end there follows separateness in space and time, but from separateness there does not yet follow a true individual. Already in the machine the end sets off the whole cleanly and roundly; yet the moving force, or the will that directs it, remains external to it; and to that extent one will still call it selfless and not an individual in the same sense as the product of nature, which is a natural end.

[[272]] Only with the concept of the end in the living does the proper sense of a self come forth. We merely lend a self to the lifeless from ourselves. When, for example, we say that the water cuts its way for itself, this is indeed meant to express that the force which hollows out the deepening of the way belongs to the same water that flows in the deepened bed. But that it is the same water which cuts the way and uses the way is only an appearance, since we take the water flowing through as a whole; strictly speaking, it is some waves that cut the way and others that afterwards flow through it; the first wave that forces its way through makes the way for others that follow; for it, it is not yet a way. Only in the living, ⟦97⟧ where moving force and inner end coincide, where what the active being does turns to its benefit or to its harm, does the self come fully into its right, as, e.g., when we say that the tree itself puts forth its blossoms. In the concept of the self lie one's own acquisition and possession, or one's own loss. The coincidence of force and end in the same subject conditions the concept of the self, and only with the self is the individual present in the higher sense.

[[273]] In plants the end appears as individuating by making itself known in assimilation, in the transformation of inorganic matter into organic, in the plan of the type, in the propagation of the genus. In the animal it shows itself by producing more and more centralised formation, and its significance rises inwardly in sensation, in desire, outwardly in the many-membered instruments, until in the thinking and willing human being it itself presents an ethical destination.

[[274]] In this whole sphere of life we have the general phenomenon that motions are directed toward a goal and that what directs dwells in what is directed and moves along with it. In the machine what moves and directs remains outside. Now what, regarded from the side of the thing, is the end, forming, building, steering, that is in the individual (subjectively) the soul, realising the end, sensing, desiring, thinking. To that extent the soul can be defined as a thought of an end realising itself. In the machine such a thought is realised; in the living it realises itself.

[[275]] We may compare the definition of the soul in Aristotle, who calls the soul the entelechy of the body,¹ i.e. the realisation, according to the inner end, of what is laid down in the body, or more exactly, the first entelechy of the body, whereby it is indicated that the soul is the realising ⟦98⟧ force which contains the capacity for activity and first brings forth the realisation as act. This definition does indeed attach to the body, in which the ends appear, and the body is that from which the view starts; but this is not meant to take the body as the first and the soul as its accident; rather, just as in general the energy (the actus) is what determines, and not the capacity as such (the potency), which is rather determined according to the energy, so in this intuition the soul is the prius, and in it lie the ends for which the body is the instrument. Thus Aristotle says in a comparison,¹ which at the same time contains a partial representation dependent on the soul: "If the eye were by itself a living being, seeing would be its soul. For this activity is its essence according to the concept, and the outer eye is the body of seeing; and when seeing leaves the eye, it is no longer an eye, but an eye only in name, like the painted eye or the eye of stone." The essence according to the concept clearly expresses the determining end as what is original.

[[278]] If we now call the soul a thought of an end realising itself, the expression is at first only formal. The content of the end, e.g. assimilation, sensation, thinking, is not thereby stated, and we take it from what is given. But the form nevertheless carries something essential in itself. From this determination there flows, e.g., the fundamental drive of all life, self-preservation; and when the determination opens up what lies bound within it, there still springs in man, out of its unity, will and knowledge: will, inasmuch as the end contains an ought, and knowledge, inasmuch as the end, itself a thought, drives to thinking; and will and knowledge demand each other into unity out of the same fundamental determination. The form further contains a subordination, and hence the possibility of a ⟦99⟧ system of ends, such as comes forth in the higher life of the soul. By presupposing the thought that something higher is to come about than the means itself, it purifies the conception of the merely empirical fact and gives it a higher direction. Every determination of value for the individual depends on the legitimate end.

[[279]] In systems in which the end does not occur at all, or, as in Herbart, only incidentally, one must, to be sure, determine the concept of the soul otherwise, if one admits it at all. One then mostly starts from the unity of self-consciousness, thus already from the highest stage of the life of the soul; and by inferring backwards one determines the soul as a simple being, not merely without parts but also without any multiplicity in its quality, which, though it is not anywhere and not at any time, nevertheless has spatial and temporal relations, corresponding to its inner state in the being-together of beings. In the connection of this doctrine 1 it is not explained how the soul, e.g. the human soul, as something simple in being-together with others, in reaction against other things that are, can have so many-sided, internally diverse, infinitely manifold a counter-action; and something spaceless and timeless that has spatial and temporal relations is still less clear, still less free from contradiction, than the concepts of experience, which are supposed to suffer from contradictions. The objective semblance, the assumption of which has been refuted above, does not suffice here.

[[280]] If the soul is a thought of an end realising itself, ⟦100⟧ then unity, and to that extent simplicity, is thereby the first; but relations to space and time are at the same time demanded by the realisation.

[[284]] 2. The end, the centre of the activities, is accordingly not alien in living beings, as it is in the machine; it becomes their own; in the various gradations of beings it is desired, sensed, thought, willed; and when we say: the soul desires, senses, or at a higher stage the soul (the mind) thinks, wills, then the soul is therein the thought of an end realising itself.

[[285]] We call these activities reflexive activities, since they proceed from the living being and take place for the living being, and posit the end of the efficient cause in the being itself. In them the being is an end to itself. The desiring animal desires for itself and wills to still its need; the sentient being, which becomes aware of increased existence in pleasure and of diminished existence in displeasure, therein senses itself; the thinking human being thinks himself, and without thinking himself, without his self-consciousness, he thinks nothing else either; the willing human being wills what he wills as his deed. That the end, which in the machine too is the ultimate unity, becomes in the living the centre which in its realisation affirms itself, senses itself, thinks itself: this is the higher and the new ⟦101⟧ that makes itself known here and that we understand in other beings only from ourselves.

[[286]] The end itself, which is here presupposed as the general foundation, already raises this phenomenon above the possibility of its being explained from blind forces. What is peculiar to reflexive activity, in which at once something other appears to the being and the being appears to itself, points no less beyond efficient causes.

[[287]] The external motions of matter, the so-called organic stimuli, which come from outside, are incommensurable with the peculiar nature of the activities of the soul (sensation, desire, thinking).

[[288]] The sensation of pleasure and displeasure is a familiar phenomenon to us; at every moment we are caught up in it, and yet, so long as we understand only forces of motion, it is incomprehensible to us. Every physical activity takes place spatially, acting out of itself upon another through change of place. But the feeling of pleasure is a reaction of force upon itself, and not, say, in such a way that force as such heightens itself therein and becomes more intensive in opposition to the extensive. The swiftest motion we can think is in and for itself still dull and blunt. The sensation of pleasure indicates in the individual life an end furthered for it by force, and yet it is no mere sign, but something of its own in itself. When we speak of a reaction of force upon itself or upon the active being, of reflexive activity, the expression is spatial, but we understand it only if we substitute something non-spatial. In pleasure or displeasure the being is no longer a stranger to its doing.

[[289]] The sensation of pleasure takes place in the perception, e.g., of something external, and on closer consideration the incommensurability of the inner process with the outer becomes clear here too. The tense string, when struck, sounds; here the tension of the string is external, the excursions of the string are ⟦102⟧ external; the sound waves propagate themselves externally. When they penetrate into the ear, the animal hears. But neither in the chemical composition of the nerve nor in the tension and position and mobility of its parts can the ground be found why a sound wave in the nerve could produce anything other than a vibration similar to itself; one does not see how through the nerve it could transform itself into a conscious sensation, how it could cease spatially as motion and rise again as sensation, as sensation of something external and as sensation of the life moved thereby in pleasure or displeasure. The leap from the last state of the material element to the first dawning of sensation is a leap across the greatest chasm.¹ No adherent of material efficient causes has explained it.

[[290]] With the feeling of pleasure, expansion is the mimetic, physiognomic effect; with displeasure and grief, contraction. Poetic minds such as Campanella regarded expansion as pleasure and constriction as displeasure in general, and in the lifeless too. But the accompanying phenomenon does not express the essence of sensation. The heated iron bar expands and the cooling one contracts. But no one suspects pleasure or displeasure in it.

[[291]] It remains for psychological investigations to show how, in human development, self-consciousness attaches itself to self-feeling in pleasure and displeasure. If some of our representations are marked with pleasure or displeasure, while others, and by far the majority, float along free and more indifferent to our self-feeling, then we stand over against these as more foreign and count the former within the circle of our ego, especially insofar as we were causal in them. They form the empirical material of our empirical ego.

[[292]] In self-consciousness we appear to ourselves, and this ⟦103⟧ appearing to oneself, which begins with self-feeling, is comparable with nothing in the material; higher than self-feeling, self-consciousness is still further removed from the material. Already as a reflexive activity it remains unexplained. It has been proposed to assume that the motion communicated to the nerve in sensation makes a circuit in the brain, and that this return of consciousness to itself can thereby be comprehended. But the circuit, even if it could be demonstrated, explains nothing. The point moving in a circle does not become anything other by describing this motion and no other; it remains as external as it was. Its path returns into itself; but it does not thereby come to itself, so as to know itself. In this explanation the metaphor of language, which speaks of self-consciousness as a motion returning into itself, has been made into what is proper and original; and such an explanation detaches itself, along with the image, which is only a sign, from the essence, and melts away.

[[294]] In self-consciousness unity is the first, much as in the end, in which unity produces multiplicity. In the midst of the manifold of our activities and states we feel ourselves as one, equal to ourselves. In the infinite representations which the open senses unlock for us at every moment, self-consciousness does not lose itself; rather, certain of itself, it hovers freely and purely above them. This unity, equal to itself, which makes itself known to us at every moment, continues through time and in the changing relations to space, and extends itself into that abiding identity with itself which, proper to self-consciousness, stems from no external experience.¹

[[295]] This nature of the self-conscious mind, superior to matter, also makes itself known in the logical fact that ⟦104⟧ there are, as our investigations have shown and will show further, concepts a priori which cannot be explained sensualistically from experience.

[[297]] So far as knowledge of matter reaches at present, it does not reach to self-feeling and self-consciousness, which are phenomena without their like. Were one, on the analogy of the advancing electrical physiology of the nerves, to make our sensing and thinking into electric sparks, or, as Alexander von Humboldt once put it with telling irony, into an electric thunderstorm discharging itself in the brain: still the electric sparks know nothing of themselves. The lightning shines for us, but not for itself.

[[298]] If the end forms the foundation of these phenomena, if the soul is a thought of an end realising itself in individual existence, then the soul is not result but principle. Its appearance, conditioned by the body, is result, but its essence is principle, in a way similar to that in which the thought of a geometrical problem does indeed appear in the system of lines which realises it, yet is the principle of that very appearance. Were it only result, then it would have to be explained how the unity of life, sharp and precise in the manifoldness of forces, how self-consciousness, ruling in calm freedom over the agitated impressions, could arise out of a multiplicity coming together by chance. That this is not thinkable has been shown earlier. The system of lines in a geometrical problem, simple compared with the intricate and yet harmoniously unified phenomena of the life of the soul, never stems from multiplicity. The shipwrecked man who on the lonely shore perceived geometrical figures in the sand recognised in them the nearness of the human mind, in which the unity of thought is what determines. How could we read anything else out of the revelations of the soul?

[[299]] If our investigations have not erred, then the soul is ⟦105⟧ not, as in Kant, veiled from itself by merely subjective forms, by space and time and the categories, so that, always only appearing to itself, it would remain constantly hidden from itself in its essence. The inference back from appearance to essence has its place here too; and the identity which belongs to no thing and only to self-consciousness has, in the connection of the results so far, a deeper significance. If in the rational psychology against which Kant took the field there was squeezed out of it what does not lie in it, then this basis was misused and the bow was drawn for a goal further than it can carry. But we must nevertheless regard the identity of self-consciousness as something real, and indeed as something in which an ideal announces itself, inexplicable from our knowledge of the material.

[[300]] Thus the appearance of the soul is result, but its essence is principle.

[[301]] 3. We must leave it to psychology to pursue this general concept within the range of its wide extension, in the ascending series of the living kinds, and to carry it through into the essence of man. It is to be admired in what abundance of forms such a thought of an end realising itself appears ever otherwise, ever new, and in the animal kingdom is directed toward winning from all elements and all situations of the external conditions the self-enjoyment of existence. The inexhaustible theme of the manifold feeling of pleasure varies itself in ever new ways as the task of a thought of an end realising itself, and nature becomes alive in order to rejoice in existence in countless forms. Sensation, fused with desire, becomes ever richer and more significant in the ascending kinds of animals. Let the above exposition borrowed from Cuvier¹ serve here as an example, the exposition of the strivings and sensations in herbivorous and carnivorous animals, distinguished according to ⟦106⟧ two opposite sides and manifold in themselves. The fundamental desire in the self-preservation of life clearly expresses the inner end which reveals itself in the structure of these animals and asserts itself in all their activities.

[[303]] But in animals the driving thought is still hidden from itself. The underlying end is blindly desired and, as it is attained or missed, blindly sensed in pleasure or displeasure. Further than this they do not come, since, working and fighting for self-preservation or playing with the abundantly offered conditions of life, they pass their existence blindly.

[[304]] Otherwise with man, whose essence it is that he think, and that thinking pervade desire and sensation and draw them up to itself. Through thinking he is capable of the universal, and this conscious universal raises man above the animal, by entering determiningly into the blind stirrings of the life of the self and, conversely, taking up what is one's own into itself.

[[305]] In opposition to the blindly organic in nature, we designate what flows from this peculiarly human source as ethical.

[[306]] The question is how far the principle for the ethical lies in what has preceded, or how far the organic is the same and how far not.

[[307]] 4. The inner end becomes easily recognisable in the ethical.

[[308]] The ethical rises out of the organic as a higher stage. Just as without the thought at the ground of things, e.g. in the life of an animal kind or in the function of a member, e.g. the eye, the hand, there is nothing organic and no organ, so without a directing end, without an inner destination, without a thought for the sake of which life is there, there is no ethics. Without it ethics would lack its peculiar essence. ⟦107⟧ It would become a mechanics of human forces meeting one another, a physics of the self-preservation of the one colliding with the self-preservation of the other. Without the inner end branching out, the idea of action would be lacking.

[[309]] If we recognised it as a character of the organic that the whole is before the parts and that the whole determines the parts, then the same character appears in the ethical, whether we consider the individual human being in agreement with himself or the community, e.g. of the state, of which the individual becomes a member.

[[310]] In this connection one sees how important it is that already in nature the thought that subordinates the forces to itself be recognised and acknowledged. Physics and ethics will breathe their spirit each into the other. A shallower or deeper physics will make ethics shallower or deeper, and the reverse also shows itself, although physics, which in the system is the necessary presupposition of ethics, will determine ethics more powerfully than ethics in turn determines physics.

[[311]] In the history of philosophy the systems of mechanical (atomistic) physics (Epicurus, the système de la nature) show an affinity with hedonism or the morality of well-understood egoistic interest. In more recent times Herbart, without drawing in the inner end, founded practical philosophy on five ideas, which express what is concordant among the elements necessary to action and are supposed to have their evidence in the aesthetic approval they arouse. It has been shown elsewhere¹ and shall not be repeated here, that in this conception, by a hysteron proteron, the harmony of the appearance, ⟦108⟧ which is the effect of the inner ends, has been made the ground, and that moreover the individual ideas suffer from particular difficulties. Should anyone, determined by Herbart, come to believe that even the nobler ethics can dispense with the inner end and the ideal destination that proceeds from it, we refer to earlier discussions.¹

[[313]] On our conception, the transition from nature to mind lies in the organic; for mind is properly already right in the midst of the organic; and through the end nature is bound up with the ethical world.

[[314]] 5. Out of the organic as what is common, the ethical proceeds through the specific difference of the human.

[[315]] In organic nature desire is blind, an expression of the inner end that is a stranger to itself, and is at most sensed in pleasure or displeasure. In man the ends come to consciousness; man thinks what he desires.

[[316]] Further, in organic nature the unity of the ends is preserved of itself; but in man a discord enters, and in the midst of this discord the ethical task is born.

[[317]] Man, himself a life of his own and in himself a whole, whose drive is the preservation and increase of his own being, is to become a member of a higher whole and to seek and increase it; in this destination there springs up a conflict of the life of the self against the ends of the whole or the ends of others who belong to it.

[[318]] Further, a discord arises within man by himself. In the life of the self the individual ends, e.g. the stimuli of ⟦109⟧ sensuous nature, can break loose, and the ends as parts can assert themselves against the higher and against the whole. Since irrational life, in order to become the foundation of rational life, develops before the rational, the lively pleasure of the sensuous drives desire to remain in the ground of nature and resists the labour which lies in every development toward the higher. The ends of individual tendencies, the ends as parts, fall into conflict with the ends of the whole.

[[320]] On this twofold path there arises in man a hostile, factious power which does not let him become man, the selfishness of the part, an "unbridled will to power." In this discord there results the ethical task of raising the resisting natural man rather into the spiritual one, and not merely to think the ends in their subordination to the ultimate end, but to will it.

[[321]] The will is desire which thought has pervaded. Its consistency stems from thinking, and its firmness against fear and hope, against pleasure and displeasure, in general against the selfishness of the part, would not be possible without the cohesive thought of the conscious end.

[[322]] It is the inner freedom of man, the right power over himself, when he brings it about that his desire agrees with his knowledge.

[[323]] The inner end, whether in the part or in the whole, which becomes the thought-out task of action or of life, is called the ethical idea, as in this sense the idea of the judge, the idea of the scholar, express the centre from which their activities go out like radii.

[[324]] The thought which underlies the things of the world is known and willed; in order to realise itself it produces new thoughts which, subordinated to the first, become anew a centre of willing and acting. The end ⟦110⟧ which in the formations of nature appears only objectively becomes subjective in man, indeed in the will, as it were, personal; it moves inventive knowledge and drives in new deeds toward ever more perfect realisation; it extends its organs and fashions things to itself as instruments; it drives toward deepening consciousness and enriching knowledge. The organic falls prey to the obstructions of nature; but in the realm of the ethical, common knowledge succeeds in freeing the inner end more and more from hindrances. Thus the power and dominion of reason over the earth grows, and the ethical world, in opposition to the sameness of nature and of the organic, has development and history. Where it forms, it forms organically and forms organisms themselves. But the moral organisms, even where, like the family, they still stand close to nature, have the drive to become conscious of themselves. Their ultimate elements are not, as in the organisms of nature, selfless parts, but individuals grounded in the centre of their own ends. Hence their essence is articulation in a still higher sense than is already the essence of the organic in nature. In the ethical community there is nothing that could not and should not be at once part and whole.

[[325]] In all these directions the ethical shows itself, insofar as the organic in nature is still blind and bound, as the organic heightened and made free through knowledge and will. It would be a misunderstanding to recognise in the ethical only the external form of the organic, and in the attempted determination only something formally organic. The content is the human essence grasped in the idea in the midst of real psychology; it necessarily lives itself out organically, if indeed it is the essence of thinking that, directed to the whole and universal, it subordinates the parts and the particular to the whole and universal. The ethical is an organic of a higher order.

[[327]] 6. Meanwhile the concept of the will, in which the centre of gravity of the ethical falls, still demands closer consideration.

[[328]] The representation on whose impulse desire acts is called motive, and our animal desire follows sensuous representations as motives. If there is to be a will in the sense we have described, it must be capable of acting on the impulse of thought. For the ultimate end of man, which subordinates all others to itself, contending with the power of the sensuous ones and lacking the intuition of sensuous stimuli, and the ends which proceed from the ultimate one as demands, are only an object of thought. The will is will in the full sense only when it is capable of acting on the motive of this thought. When it does so, when therefore the idea of the human essence drives it, it is the good will.

[[329]] This capacity, in contradiction with the desires and independently of sensuous motives, to have as its motive the good grasped only in thought, we call the freedom of the will.

[[330]] If such a freedom of the will could not be assumed, what is peculiar to everything ethical would come to nothing. For the inner end, which constitutes the destination of man, gathers itself as the ultimate end into a command of an unconditional kind. For man there is no remission of the command to be man at every moment and to fulfil the peculiar human essence. If such a ⟦112⟧ command is not to be vain, like an impossible goal, then man must be able to become master over the inner hindrances to fulfilling it. Every demand of the command is a demand of freedom. As the freedom of the will is known from the command, so it conditions the command as an actual one. The command presupposes freedom, or, expressed otherwise, in the consciousness of its truth the inner end, in order to prevail, must demand freedom.

[[336]] 7. This faith of man in the demanded free will is confronted by the view which strictly and tautly continues the causal law out of nature into mind. According to it, the chain of causes and effects so ensnares and binds man that in the thoroughgoing necessity of efficient causes he is only something done and not a doer. For what he does has its sufficient cause in something else, and he cannot do otherwise. Since man plays out an alien causality, or is only the channel through which it passes, concepts such as guilt become empty semblance. The consciousness that we could also do otherwise if we willed is then only an illusion of deliberating thought that sketches proposals; in reality we can will only the one thing that we happen to will; we suppose otherwise only because the cause that tips the wavering balance remains unknown to us.

[[337]] To rescue the will from this constraint of determinism, and thereby to make morality possible, Kant,¹ who acknowledged the causal law for the whole world of appearance, but only for this, devised intelligible freedom, freedom beyond and as it were behind appearance.

[[338]] Kant's deepest motives lie in ethics. As surely as the essence of reason in general is universality and necessity, so surely must the rational practical law ⟦114⟧ be universal and necessary. But such a law would be vain if the human will, dependent on the natural law of appearance, succumbed to the force of the desires. Without freedom, therefore, no unconditional law is possible, and freedom is to that extent a real ground of the law. Conversely, the unconditional law is the guarantee of freedom, a ground of knowledge of its actuality. Freedom and the unconditional law of practical reason point to each other.

[[340]] Thus reason, determining but not determinable, and therefore lifted out of the connection of appearances, comes forth as causal in the ought, which occurs nowhere in the whole of nature and, if one has merely the course of nature before one's eyes, is altogether without meaning; it is present and the same in all the actions of man in all circumstances of time, but is not itself in time and does not fall into a new state in which it was not before. The thing in itself is indeed veiled by time and space and the categories, purely subjective forms, and is for us an unknown region; but we nevertheless think it as independent of time and space and causality, and the essence of man as thing in itself is therefore suited to be that reason which comes forth plainly in the ought. Accordingly Kant distinguishes between man as phainomenon, who has an empirical character, and man as noumenon, whose character is intelligible and therefore free from the conditions of time. The moral ought is his own necessary willing as a member of an intelligible world, and is thought by him as an ought only insofar as he at the same time regards himself as a member of the world of sense. The empirical character is accordingly the sensuous schema of the intelligible, and every empirical action, regardless of the temporal relation in which it stands with other appearances, is "the immediate effect of the intelligible character of pure reason, which consequently acts freely, without being dynamically determined in the chain of natural causes by external or internal grounds preceding it in time, ⟦115⟧ and this its freedom one can not only regard negatively as independence from empirical conditions (for thereby the faculty of reason would cease to be a cause of appearances), but also designate positively as a faculty of beginning a series of events of itself, in such a way that in it itself nothing begins, but that it, as the unconditioned condition of every voluntary action, admits above itself no conditions preceding in time, while yet its effect begins in the series of appearances, but can never therein make an absolutely first beginning."

[[341]] In the purest ethical interest, and using the advantage which the consistency of his transcendental idealism offered him in the strict separation of the world as appearance from the thing in itself, Kant devised intelligible freedom in order to ground the possibility that every individual action, notwithstanding all empirical conditions, is to be regarded as free and according to reason as a cause which, regardless of all empirical conditions, could have determined the conduct of man otherwise.¹

[[342]] Two difficulties stand in the way of this view, indeed make it impossible.

[[343]] First, it belongs to the general contradiction in which Kant's idealism entangles itself that, according to the result of the doctrine of categories, causality belongs solely to appearance and, applicable only to appearance, has no meaning beyond appearance; and yet in this doctrine, much as in the impulse which the thing in itself gives to sensibility for the apprehension of things in space and time, the thing in itself, though itself not determinable, becomes causal as determining reason.

[[344]] This contradiction of the system with itself reveals itself still more glaringly in the second difficulty. On the one hand ⟦116⟧ human action and character are to be explained according to causality in appearance, and then again the same action is ascribed to intelligible freedom, which, though itself not determinable, has nevertheless determined the individual action. In the first respect Kant says:¹ "Every man has an empirical character of his power of choice, which is nothing other than a certain causality of his reason, insofar as this shows in its effects in appearance a rule according to which one may gather the grounds of reason and its actions according to their kind and their degrees, and judge the subjective principles of his power of choice. Because this empirical character must itself be drawn from appearances as effect, and from the rule of these which experience furnishes, all the actions of man in appearance are determined from his empirical character and the other co-operating causes according to the order of nature; and if we could investigate all the appearances of his power of choice to the ground, there would be no single human action which we could not predict with certainty and know as necessary from its preceding conditions. In respect of this empirical character, therefore, there is no freedom, and yet it is only according to this that we can consider man, if we merely wish to observe and, as happens in anthropology, to investigate physiologically the moving causes of his actions." In this passage and in the whole connection the question is not, as it might at first seem, how we know the intelligible character, say the maxim of freedom, from its sensuous expression, the empirical character; for then necessity would fall only in our knowing; rather, as the conclusion clearly shows, it is a matter of moving real causes which are investigated physiologically, and it is ⟦117⟧ said expressly: "in respect of this empirical character there is no freedom." By contrast, in the second respect Kant says of the intelligible character:¹ "The blame of a lie is grounded on a law of reason, whereby one regards reason as a cause which, regardless of all empirical conditions, could and should have determined the conduct of the man otherwise. And indeed one regards the causality of reason not merely as concurrence, but as complete in itself, even if the sensuous incentives were not at all for it but even quite against it; the action is ascribed to his intelligible character; he is now, at the moment when he lies, entirely to blame; consequently reason, notwithstanding all empirical conditions of the deed, was wholly free, and the deed is to be ascribed entirely to its omission." "One easily sees in this imputing judgment that one has it in mind that reason is not at all affected by all that sensibility, that it does not change (even though its appearances, namely the way in which it shows itself in its effects, change), that in it no state precedes that would determine the following one, and consequently that it does not belong at all to the series of sensuous conditions which make appearance necessary according to laws of nature." Apart from the question whether Kant, consistently with his view, was entitled to posit the thing in itself as causal, and thus as reaching over into time, which after all is only a subjective form, it is hard to comprehend how the free and the unfree fit into each other and work together in the same action, which is to be regarded now empirically as necessary, now intelligibly as free; how freedom "begins its effect in the series of appearances," and thus cannot be taken into account therein, and yet the series of appearances is necessary. No clear imputation results from this consideration wavering between the empirical and the intelligible. ⟦118⟧ Either the intelligible can break through the empirical causal connection, and then the causal law in appearance is not satisfied; or the empirical character is necessary, and then the ought of reason succumbs.

Schopenhauer's whole doctrine, whose principle is the will to existence, comes into play here, and we must weigh its fundamental points; he himself sums them up in these words:¹ the "kernel and chief point of my doctrine, its proper metaphysics," is the "paradoxical fundamental truth that what Kant opposed as the thing in itself to mere appearance, called more decisively by me representation, and held to be absolutely unknowable, that, I say, this thing in itself, this substrate of all appearances, and hence of the whole of nature, is nothing other than that which is immediately known and very familiar to us, which we find in the interior of our own self as will; that accordingly this will, far from being, as all previous philosophers assumed, inseparable from knowledge and even a mere result of it, is fundamentally different from and wholly independent of knowledge, which is entirely secondary and of later origin, and consequently can also exist and express itself without it, which is actually the case in the whole of nature from the animal downwards; indeed, that this will, as the sole thing in itself, the alone truly real, alone original and metaphysical, in a world where everything else is only appearance, i.e. mere representation, lends to every thing, whatever it may be, the force by virtue of which it can exist and act; that accordingly not only the voluntary actions ⟦121⟧ of animal beings, but also the organic workings of their animated body, even its shape and constitution, further also the vegetation of plants, and finally even in the inorganic realm crystallisation and in general every original force that manifests itself in physical and chemical phenomena, indeed gravity itself, are in themselves and outside appearance, which merely means outside our head and its representation, directly identical with what we find in ourselves as will, of which will we have the most immediate and intimate acquaintance that is possible at all; that further the individual expressions of this will are set in motion in knowing, i.e. animal, beings by motives, but no less in the organic life of the animal and the plant by stimuli, and finally in the inorganic by mere causes in the narrowest sense of the word; which difference concerns merely the appearance; that, on the other hand, knowledge and its substrate, the intellect, is a phenomenon wholly different from the will, merely secondary, accompanying only the higher stages of the objectivation of the will, inessential to the will itself, dependent on its appearance in the animal organism, therefore physical, not metaphysical like the will itself; that consequently one can never infer from the absence of knowledge to the absence of will; rather, the will can be demonstrated in all the phenomena of nature devoid of knowledge, the vegetable as well as the inorganic; thus that will is not, as was hitherto assumed without exception, conditioned by knowledge, though knowledge is conditioned by will." If in this way the world is the objectivation or the image of the will, this view turns as follows for the ethical. Man is, like everything else in the world, a being decided once for all by his very constitution, which, like every other thing in nature, has its determinate, persistent properties, from which its reactions to an arising external occasion necessarily ⟦122⟧ proceed, which accordingly bear their character, unalterable from this side, and consequently, in whatever in them may be modifiable, are wholly given over to determination by the occasions from outside. We must therefore seek freedom not, as the common view does, in the individual actions, but in the whole being and essence (existentia et essentia) of man himself, which must be thought as his free deed, which presents itself in a multiplicity and diversity of actions only for the faculty of knowledge bound up in time and space and causality, but which actions, precisely because of the original unity of what presents itself in them, must all bear exactly the same character and therefore appear strictly necessitated by the motives of the moment by which they are called forth and determined in particular. Accordingly, for the world of appearance the old scholastic proposition that acting follows being (operari sequitur esse) holds without exception. Every thing acts in accordance with its constitution, and its action, following upon causes, makes this constitution known. Every man acts according to what he is, and the action each time necessary accordingly is determined in the individual case solely by the motives. Freedom, which therefore cannot be met with in the operari, must lie in the esse. In it all the expressions of the man are already contained potentia, and they enter actu when external causes call them forth. The constitution that reveals itself therein is the empirical character, whereas its inner ultimate ground, inaccessible to experience, is the intelligible character, i.e. the essence in itself of this man. As a man is, so he must act. Natures are as they are; they are in their actions like the seal in a thousand impressions. In the given individual, in every given particular case, absolutely only *one* action is possible. Freedom belongs not to the empirical but solely to the intelligible character. The operari of a ⟦123⟧ given man is necessarily determined from outside by the motives, from within by his character; hence everything he does occurs necessarily. But in his esse, there lies freedom. He could have been another; and in what he is lie guilt and merit. For everything he does follows from it as a mere corollary. One can correct the representations which present themselves to man as motives; but man always only applies his essence to them. The head is set right, but the heart is not bettered. One can thereby reshape action, but not the proper willing, to which alone moral worth belongs. One cannot change the goal toward which the will strives, but only the way it takes to it. Instruction can change the choice of means, but not that of the ultimate universal ends; these every will sets itself in accordance with its original nature. One can show the egoist that by giving up small advantages he will obtain greater ones; the malicious man, that causing the sufferings of others will bring greater ones upon himself. But egoism itself, malice itself, one will talk no one out of.¹
