[[359]] In the closest connection with the above stands his explanation of conscience. "The moral responsibility of man concerns first and ostensibly what he does, but at bottom what he is, since, this being presupposed, his doing, upon the entry of the motives, could never have turned out otherwise ⟦124⟧ than it has turned out. But however strict the necessity may be with which, given the character, the deeds are called forth by the motives, it will nevertheless never occur to anyone, not even to him who is convinced of this, to wish thereby to exculpate himself and to roll the guilt onto the motives; for he clearly recognises that here, as regards the matter and the occasions, and so objectively, an entirely different, even an opposite action was quite possible, indeed would have taken place, if only He had been another. But that he, as is evident from the action, is such a one and no other, — that is what he feels himself responsible for; here in the Esse lies the spot which the sting of conscience strikes. For conscience is precisely nothing but the acquaintance with one's own self that arises from one's own manner of acting and grows ever more intimate. Hence it is, to be sure, on the occasion of the Operari, but properly the Esse, that is accused by conscience. Since we are conscious of freedom only by means of responsibility, freedom too must lie where responsibility lies; and so in the Esse. The Operari falls to necessity. But, as we come to know others, so too we come to know ourselves only empirically, and we have no knowledge a priori of our character."

[[362]] If the intelligible character is responsible for the being from which acting follows, then responsibility will have its measure in the motives.

[[363]] There are, says Schopenhauer, only three fundamental incentives of human actions altogether, and it is solely through the arousal of these that all motives whatever that are possible operate. They are, first, egoism, which wills one's own weal; it is boundless; second, malice, which wills another's woe; it goes as far as the utmost cruelty; third, compassion, which wills another's weal; it goes as far as nobility and ⟦125⟧ magnanimity. Every human action must be traceable to one of these incentives, although two of them can also operate in combination. Egoism, the urge towards existence and well-being, is, in the animal as in man, most closely bound up with his innermost kernel and essence, indeed properly identical with it; hence as a rule all his actions spring from egoism. Each makes himself the centre of the world and regards all others with indifference, as phantoms. This rests ultimately on the fact that each is given to himself immediately, but the others are given to him only mediately, through the representation of them in his head, and immediacy asserts its right. Egoism is counteracted by compassion, in which the other's weal and woe is my motive. Only through knowledge, through representation, can I so identify myself with the other that my deed proclaims the difference between me and him to be cancelled. Compassion is the wholly immediate participation, independent of all other considerations, first in the suffering of another and thereby in the prevention or removal of this suffering, as that in which all satisfaction and all well-being and happiness ultimately consist. This compassion alone is the real basis of all free justice and of all genuine love of mankind. As regards justice, we are all originally inclined to injustice and violence, because our need, our desire, our anger and hatred enter consciousness immediately, whereas the sufferings of others, which our injustice and violence cause, come into consciousness only by the secondary path of representation and only through experience, and so mediately. Hence compassion places itself as a bulwark before the other and preserves him from the injury to which otherwise my egoism or my malice would drive me. Thus from the first degree of compassion springs the maxim: neminem laede, the principle of justice. The second degree in the operation of compassion ⟦126⟧ has a positive character, in that compassion not merely restrains me from injuring the other but even impels me to help him. In this immediate participation, supported by no argumentation, lies the sole pure origin of the love of mankind, whose maxim is: omnes, quantum potes, iuva.

[[365]] If we now ask about the metaphysics of this morality,¹ it is based on the knowledge that distinctness and plurality are illusion and the One is the truth. In egoism man insists on himself as distinct, but compassion aims at unity, in that it finds itself again in the other and cancels the difference. All plurality and all diversity rests on space and time; through these alone is it possible, since the many can be thought and represented only either as beside one another or as after one another. Now because the homogeneous many are the individuals, space and time, in the respect that they make plurality possible, are the principium individuationis; but space and time belong only to representation, and so plurality and separateness too belong merely to appearance, to that world which, playing only in our head, is phantasm and the web of Maya. Hence hatred and malice, through egoism, rest on the entanglement of knowledge in the principio individuationis, in diversity through space and time; but compassion, through which the one individual immediately finds itself again in the other, the origin and essence of justice, love and nobility, rests on seeing through that principii individuationis, which alone, in cancelling the difference between one's own individual and the individuals of others, makes possible and explains perfect goodness of disposition. Before the eyes of the good man the veil of Maya has already been lifted. ⟦127⟧ To be healed of the delusion and deception of Maya and to practise works of love are one. The latter is an unfailing symptom of that knowledge.¹ The emotion and delight which we feel on hearing of, still more on beholding, and most of all on ourselves accomplishing a noble action, rest at the deepest ground on this, that it gives us the certainty that beyond all the plurality and diversity of individuals which the principium individuationis holds before us there lies a unity of them which is truly present, indeed accessible to us, since it has in fact just come forth. As, accordingly, the principle of the world, the will to life, is the affirmation of semblance, so the negation of the will is the redeeming return into the One.

[[370]] Schopenhauer's intelligible freedom had to turn out differently from Kant's for the very reason that, strictly speaking, it is no intelligible freedom at all; for the will to existence, so he teaches, is before the intellect and without the intellect, which, mediated by the senses and the brain, comes afterwards in order to make the objectivation of the will into nothing but a representation.

[[371]] Schopenhauer wants the concept of force to be subsumed under the concept of will for the reason that this concept is the only one among all possible concepts which has its origin not in appearance, not in mere intuitive representation, but comes from within, proceeds from the immediate consciousness of each person, in which he knows his own individual according to its essence immediately, without any form, even without that of subject and object, and at the same time is it, since here the knower and the known coincide. If force is to be subsumed under will, then the latter is the more universal concept, the former the particular one; and it must therefore be shown what specific difference is added to the concept of will in order to produce the concept of force from the more universal one. This demonstration has neither been attempted nor is it possible so long as one keeps the concept of force within the limits of previous linguistic usage. Every reduction leads to something more universal; but Schopenhauer has nowhere said how the concept of will is the more universal one. We know our will, from which, as what is best known, we are supposed to start, only as one which representations not merely accompany but determine; but in that conception of the thing in itself the will is to be thought as free of every representation, before the intellect ⟦129⟧ and without the intellect. Our will acts upon motives; but the will to life, the thing in itself, acts groundlessly, without motives. Our will acts in time; but the will to existence, the thing in itself, is outside time. The supposed reduction is only an analogy, but the analogy must deceive, because it lets drop what constitutes the essence of our will; it does not take the will specifically, and therefore no longer as will, but in its application to the world of forces it tacitly substitutes an analogue of our will, of the will in the specific meaning, of the will determinable from ground and end, as for instance in the explanation of teleology in nature. When we read Schopenhauer we handle the will, as a matter of course, as we know it; but we ought to take it only as we do not know it. In this amphiboly lies the πρῶτον ψεῦδος. Will without representation, without a ground in the impulse, without an end in view, whether these be clearly thought or obscurely felt, is no will; in life such a thing is called caprice; stat pro ratione voluntas. The will to existence, the will to life, is, as Schopenhauer often repeats, ¹ groundless will. But blind will is will into the blue, and yet this groundless will appears in laws, in ends! This wonder is concealed from us only because, instead of that will before the intellect and without the intellect, from which no closely knit order can flow, we involuntarily think an analogue of our will, from which, through the intellect, necessity derives.

[[374]] What, then, does this blind will, which is the thing in itself, properly will? It is surely not so blind after all; for it wills the Platonic Idea. Or, to speak with Schopenhauer,² the immediate and therefore adequate objectity of the thing in itself, which is itself the will in so far as it is not yet ⟦130⟧ objectified, has not yet become representation, is the Idea. "We would no longer know individual things at all, nor events, nor change, nor plurality, but would apprehend only Ideas, only the graded scale of the objectivation of that one will, of the true thing in itself, in pure, untroubled knowledge, if we were not, as subject of knowing, at the same time individuals, i.e. if our intuition were not mediated by a body, from whose affections it proceeds, and which is itself only concrete willing, objectity of the will, and so an object among objects, and as such, as soon as it comes into knowing consciousness, can do so only in the forms of the principle of sufficient reason, and consequently already presupposes time and all the other forms which that principle expresses, and thereby introduces them. Time is merely the divided and fragmented view which an individual being has of the Ideas, which are outside time and hence eternal." Schopenhauer¹ has set out the grades of the objectivation of the will, which by his own declaration are nothing other than Plato's Ideas: as the lowest grade the universal laws of nature, as a second the species in the organic, as a third the character of each individual man, which is individual and not wholly comprised in that of the species. If the will, striving groundlessly and into the infinite, has grades of objectivation as its immediate and adequate expression, if grades designate nothing but presentations of greater and rising perfection,² but perfection has as its measure the Idea of the perfect or of the good, as this stands at the summit in Plato too: then only a twofold choice remains to us for the blind will to life. Either it is the Empedoclean chance, playing with dice of a thousand sides and a thousand eyes and always hitting the mark, and then the name of will does not befit it; or it takes up, like our will, ground and end ⟦131⟧ into itself and is not before the intellect but is itself reason; and the blind will becomes seeing. The former has been shown above to be in itself unthinkable. The latter, on the other hand, is the necessary consequence, so long as we read a sense into the Platonic Ideas, the immediate and adequate mirror-image of the will, as Schopenhauer after all does. "The word Idea," he says,² "is with me always to be understood in its genuine and original meaning bestowed on it by Plato. — — By Idea, then, I understand every determinate and fixed grade of the objectivation of the will, in so far as it is thing in itself and therefore foreign to plurality, which grades are related to individual things, to be sure, as their eternal forms or their prototypes." So long as Schopenhauer denies the good as absolute Idea and calls it a trivial concept, so long are his Ideas not Plato's Ideas; for the head of them is the Idea of the good, which derives not from the blind will but from the kingly understanding and, as one can easily convince oneself from Plato's Phaedo, tacitly carries within itself the concept of the inner end.

[[378]] We briefly sum up the result of the discussion so far as follows. The force which stands before reason is not yet will, and the mirror-image of the groundless will cannot be the Idea that is in accord with itself. Schopenhauer's principle, the will to life, is a metaphor.

[[379]] Schopenhauer designates the world of representation as the objectity of the will, and accordingly says: my body is the objectity of my will. What, as intuitive representation, I call my body, I call my will in so far as I am conscious of it in an entirely different way, comparable to no other.⁵ Schopenhauer chose this expression, which harbours something significant, deliberately; ⟦132⟧ he finds fault¹ with Kant for inferring, according to the concept of causality, which holds only for appearance, to the thing in itself, and for letting intelligible freedom, the thing in itself, become causal. Hence the will to life too is not to be conceived causally; rather, it only becomes object of the subject that represents according to time and space and causality. Objectity is the expression for representation and nothing further. The will itself is timeless; as such, and considered apart from its appearance, it lies² outside time and space, and accordingly knows no plurality, and is consequently one; yet not as an individual nor as a concept is one, but as something to which the condition of the possibility of plurality, the principium individuationis, is foreign. The plurality of things in space and time, which are all of them its objectity, therefore does not touch it, and notwithstanding that plurality it remains indivisible. Its coming forth into visibility, its objectivation, has gradations as infinite as those between the faintest twilight and the brightest sunlight, the loudest tone and the softest echo. But still less than the gradations of its objectivation touch the will itself immediately does the plurality of appearances on these different grades touch it, i.e. the multitude of individuals of each form or of the individual manifestations of each force, since this plurality is immediately conditioned by time and space, into which the will itself never enters. In all the forces of inorganic nature and all the forms of organic nature it is one and the same will that reveals itself, i.e. enters into the form of representation, into objectity. Its unity must therefore also make itself known through an inner kinship between all its appearances. Now this reveals itself on the higher grades of its objectity, where the whole appearance is more distinct, and so in the vegetable and animal kingdoms, through the ⟦133⟧ universally pervading analogy of all forms, the fundamental type that is found again in all appearances, as comparative anatomy, for instance, demonstrates it in the unity of plan. All the parts of nature accommodate one another, because it is *one* will that appears in all of them, while temporal succession is wholly foreign to its original and solely adequate objectity, the Ideas. The soil adapted itself to the nourishment of plants, these to the nourishment of animals, these to the nourishment of other animals, just as, conversely, all these in turn adapted themselves to the former. As instinct is an acting like that according to a concept of an end, and yet wholly without one, so all the forming of nature is like that according to a concept of an end, and yet wholly without one. For in the outer as in the inner teleology of nature, what we must think as means and end is everywhere only the appearance, separated out in space and time for our mode of knowledge, of the one will which is to that extent in agreement with itself. The will always knows, where knowledge illuminates it, what it wills now, what it wills here; but never what it wills in general. Every individual act has an end, willing as a whole has none; just as every individual natural phenomenon is determined by a sufficient cause to its occurrence at this place at this time, but the force manifesting itself in it has no cause at all, since such a thing is a grade of appearance of the thing in itself, of the groundless will.

[[387]] It is necessary to look through the veil of the word to the ground of this doctrine of the appearing will, of the objectity of the will.

[[388]] We conceive our will as striving and therein as causal; but the will to life as the thing in itself, and as such exempt from time and space and causality, may not be thought as causal. That is why in Schopenhauer a category of its own appears, under which the will to life enters into representation: objectity, as for instance the body is called the objectity of the will. The word is new and ⟦134⟧ deliberately coined. For even objectivation, a word which Schopenhauer predicates of the will only occasionally, would already designate a causal activity through which the will makes itself the object of representation. Has the old concept of causality now really been excluded, along with the new word, from the will to existence, which is the thing in itself?

[[389]] The will to life becomes visible, knowable; that is what objectity says. What makes it knowable? Either the will does it, or representation, or both together. Perhaps the last assumption would lie nearest to ordinary understanding. But since the will to life, as the thing in itself, may not be causal at all, the only thing that remains is that the visibility, the knowability of the will to life is effected solely by representation. Its forms, and only its forms, are in Kant's sense space and time and causality, and Schopenhauer in fact calls these forms, which for him are merely subjective, the principium individuationis. Only through them does plurality arise, and therefore it is also only through them that there are individuals.¹ Does the will, then, contribute nothing to its passing over into space and time, and does it have no share in the individuating principle? Representation does not proceed arbitrarily when it apprehends what is given and, for instance, counts the individuals; it holds itself bound by the matter to count so many, and not more and not fewer. Whence does it derive this compelling direction of the given, which points it out beyond itself and ultimately to the thing in itself? Were plurality, number, wholly foreign to the thing in itself, our representation would have to resemble a glass globe with countless facets, which reflects the same *one* object many times over. If nothing of this is known to us, and if we also distinguish recognisably between mirror-images that repeat themselves and real individuals, then a share ⟦135⟧ of the ground must after all lie in the will to existence that it becomes visible thus and not otherwise and in this number. Before it can become object, objectity, it must so far accommodate itself that it lets itself be grasped in space and time and, again, lets itself be presented thus and not otherwise in space and time.

[[391]] If the Platonic Idea is the immediate and adequate expression of the will to life, then it is at least always thought as causal, since it communicates itself to things. Further, the Platonic Ideas are distinguished, like the genera or species of things. If, now, they are called the adequate objectity of the will, it follows that the will wills them as distinguished; and though they themselves may be thought only as being, only as a standing now, the will is the ground of the distinguished eternal images; it is causal in the difference. That objectity is interposed between the will to life as the thing in itself and the mobile, perishable plurality of individuals does not exempt the will from the relations to space and time which arise when the eternal prototypes give form.

[[392]] It is most important to consider the objectity of the will in those formations in which Schopenhauer recognises inner purposiveness and seeks it out with love and contemplates it as a true example of the will to life. It has been shown above that the end is possible only through the thought that anticipates the future effect or the future whole, and that the thought is causal therein. Now since Schopenhauer can neither use nor tolerate thought in the will, for the will is before the intellect, nor causality in the will, which is the thing in itself, he must explain the end otherwise. We therefore consider the solution of the problem which has been given above. All the parts of nature, it says, very simply if not too simply, accommodate one another, as for instance the soil adapts itself to the nourishment of plants, because *one* will appears in all of them, ⟦136⟧ while temporal succession is wholly foreign to its original and solely adequate objectity, the Ideas. Two things will not escape the reader. First, it is presupposed in this connection that the *one* will which appears in the parts also drives them to accommodate one another, and so is causal therein. Then the second reason skips with light foot over the unsolved question how it can happen that in the end the future, something which is not yet, becomes causal. The Idea, whose copy shapes itself purposively and develops as a temporal being, would have to contain an explanatory ground for the inversion of the causal nexus discussed above. This could be found only in the fact that temporal succession is wholly foreign to the Ideas. But from this indifference towards space and time it is impossible to infer a real dominion, a victory over the conditions of time; from this metaphysical determination, which is at most an elevation above space and time, one cannot see how the Idea contrives that in the domain of the end — let time in any case count only as a subjective form — the effect appears as the cause, the posterius as the prius. This defect is not remedied by the assertion that inner purposiveness is nothing at all but the appearance, separated out in space and time for our mode of knowledge, of the *one* will in agreement with itself. This agreement of the will with itself rather betrays the consistent thought from which it springs, but which the will before the intellect is, according to the principle, supposed to lack. Purposiveness never proceeds from the One unless the One carries itself through in the many; but for that the many must be original together with the One and must hang together with it, which does not obtain where the One is the thing in itself but the many derives only from the subjective principium individuationis. It is of no help to cite the instinct of animals as an example of the blind urge of the will which nevertheless appears purposive; for instinct rests ⟦137⟧ on the presupposed objective inner end of life. Accordingly it is shown in his own thoughts, despite Schopenhauer's assertion, that the will which objectifies itself is causality and reason at once, and the objectity of the will, which is supposed to exclude every relation of the will to space and time and causality and assign it to representation, is a metaphor that explains nothing, taken from the object of sight given in apparent rest.¹

[[394]] One is tempted to call Schopenhauer's concept of conscience a metaphor as well; for such a conscience, which relates only to the will before the intellect, to freedom before reason, to the esse in which we find ourselves, and not to the operari which we can accompany with our consciousness and think through before the deed; such a conscience, which knows no responsibility for individual actions, but only the responsibility for the fundamental willing that is itself unconscious to it; such a conscience, which is only the acquaintance, arising from one's own manner of acting, growing ever more intimate and surprising to ourselves, ⟦138⟧ with our self as posited in the blind urge of the pre-temporal will; such a conscience, which is for that reason not of peculiarly human origin, since, for instance, every animal species, sprung from the blind will to its existence, would have to have a similar object of conscience if only the cerebral intellect reached far enough in it — such a conscience is at least not the conscience that is otherwise so called and counts as an ethical fact, those thoughts which accuse and excuse one another and are to bear witness to the law written in our heart.

[[399]] This is in brief the result of an idealism which seeks the individuating principle in the mere representation of space and time, but disdains the true principium individuationis, the inner end, as an original principle; which grounds the will without the end and would draw intelligible freedom from the will before the intellect; of an idealism which, without the intelligible Idea, takes the blind will to life as the basis of things.

[[400]] ⟦140⟧ How is it, then, that notwithstanding this Schopenhauer, who is a master as much in robust polemic as in the delicate apprehension of the poetic, not seldom casts a surprising light on psychological phenomena from his principle? The will to life as pre-temporal, before reason, the principle of the system, refutes itself, as we have seen; and indeed, if our investigations have not deceived us, ¹ together with its twin brother, that transcendental idealism which turns appearances into semblance. But the will to life, taken temporally, active with representations and in representations, is equivalent to the drive of self-preservation, which the Stoics and Spinoza regarded as the fundamental drive of the soul, and which is the centre of an end that draws the rest after it; and as such it has great significance. In seeking to substantiate the principle of his system in experience, Schopenhauer illuminated and interpreted psychological phenomena of self-preservation, which, however, as we have shown, form a false analogue for the naked and blind pre-temporal will to life.
