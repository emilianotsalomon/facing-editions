[[487]] # ⟦167⟧ XII. NEGATION.

[[488]] The operative fundamental concepts have been brought out in the foregoing. One concept worked along with them tacitly, and it must be considered in this collaboration. It is negation.

[[489]] 1. When motion generated determinate formations, first of all figures and numbers, a negative moment appeared in this act. No shape arises without an inhibition of the generating motion. The units of number are set off from one another. Each rests on an activity that at once comprehends and excludes. When determinate products separate themselves out of universal motion, when the categories issue from this act and from its products, then determination appears as limitation, and limitation as negation. Every self-determination carries within itself the negation of what is foreign. Thus negation operates as an element of the thing, but not as an original element, rather as a consequence; not as an end, but as a means; it operates in something positive, but not as something independent on its own account. With individuality there grows the activity by which it repels what is other and closes itself off within itself. Thus Spinoza's proposition, omnis determinatio negatio, is borne out in the act of determination just as in the product. The end that wills something determinate ⟦168⟧ does not will something else, and in carrying itself out it seeks to prevent everything disturbing and to annihilate it while it is still only a possibility. It is in this sense that negation appears in organisms, as they provide in advance against threatening chance. We recall the examples cited above.¹ The child does not learn negation at first by a theoretical route, as for instance through intuition; rather, it is out of its own individual will that it utters its first No, then comprehends from out of itself the individuality of things as well, and now negates in their name too. Accordingly the objective significance of negation lies in determinateness.

[[490]] A second origin of negation is combining reflection. Mobile thought, free comparison, places remote things side by side and asks what they have in common and wherein they differ. The one is what the other is not. Things that do not belong together in their origin enter into a mental community, in order, as it were, to attract or repel one another. Thought hovers above things, and as it relates and transposes them in representation, the excluding self-determination of concepts shows itself anew, and negation shows itself as a consequence of comparison. From this side negation does not result immediately from the consideration of *one* object, but only indirectly, in so far as the object is not something which something else is. A simple example will illustrate this. If we say: the leaf is green, not red, then "not red" is of course judged from the determinateness of the object; but the judgment presupposes that red is known as a colour and has been compared. It is given by no original intuition, but derived from the juxtaposition.

[[491]] Every negation must accordingly present itself, in its ground, as the excluding, repelling force of an affirmation. Otherwise it is nothing but caprice or an empty play ⟦169⟧ of the understanding. Negation is borne by a position. Pure negation is found nowhere except in thought. As soon as it gains a foothold in things, it grows together with the individual. In nature nothing is to be comprehended through mere negation; and only a superficial consideration can rest content with such a determination. The negation that limited motion into shape and fixed it there presented itself positively as inhibiting motion. If one calls rest the negation of motion, one as yet knows nothing of the equilibrium of motion and counter-motion which appears as rest. If one calls darkness the negation of light, one remains stuck in comparing thought, as if the thing were rooted in the comparison as in its ground. Rather, the dense earth casts the great cone of shadow which we call darkness. The solid body shuts off the brightness and exercises that negation of light. Fichte's Not-I designates the world of objects for the subject. But how it comes about that the I sets an object over against itself — of this the negative expression, the Not-I, gives us no understanding. In order to have done with evil, an inconvenient phenomenon, people are apt to let it dissolve into a mere negation of the good. But that is only a word, unless one comprehends the negating spirit in its positive power: the will of the individual stiffening itself within itself against the universal, the force and the lust of false independence.

[[493]] This relation runs through the whole world, and pure negation belongs to thought alone. If one opposes A and not-A (contradictorily), then not-A is everything that is not A, and therefore runs off without limit, just as it is, into the indeterminate. While A is grounded in itself through its positive essence, not-A is only a concept determined by its reference to A; without hold of its own, it seeks subsistence in something else and even then still blurs into the distance; for the negative as negative, as Aristotle already remarks, has no ⟦170⟧ species; and it is inexact to speak of species of the contradictory opposite. It is therefore an abuse to raise pure negation into an independent real factor, as though not-being acted in the same way as being. It is an illusion that springs up in abstraction, but it evaporates when thought follows the generation of things in a living way. Negation is nowhere what is first; rather, it is always only the outflow of something else. And when a work, whatever it may be, begins negatively, an inquiry with criticism, an art with the purification of its material, then negation is indeed the beginning, but not the origin. Rather, the positively shaping end lies in the background as what is prior.

[[494]] 2. In place of logical negation there appears in the real the concept of the other or the different, which stretches as far as the concept of opposition. But negation and opposition are not one and the same. Pure negation, the sharpness of the mind, has as it were become embodied in opposition, but through the particular substrate it has forfeited something of its universality. Affirmation and negation of the same concept exclude ⟦171⟧ one another without any prospect of a settlement. Opposites, however, in so far as they subsist, also essentially have something in common, in which they can come together.

[[495]] > (1) Already in Campanella the error of the view grows, as tends to happen, into a doctrine. Ens particulare finito esse constat et infinito non-esse. The composition of what is and what is not, he says, produces a third, which is neither pure being nor not-being. Thus man is something because he is not everything. Ergo nonesse facit ut sit aliquod non minus quam esse. Cf. Campanella metaphys. P. II. L. VI. c. 1 ff. "Negativity," synonymous with development, is in the most recent philosophy the illusion that the advance to the opposite belongs as peculiarly to pure thought as negation does. But intuition is secretly called to its aid. The nothing is no logical concept but a fantastic hypostasis in which content and form stand in the most glaring contradiction; for the substance of a something is lent to that which is not and is not supposed to be. In this contradiction of language, in this imaginary magnitude, there still reveals itself the fundamental relation that negation, merely in order to be thought, requires a substrate. For even absolute negation takes on the form of absolute position. When one went so far as to hear this nothing called the "concrete nothing," that is as much as meaningful nonsense, or an emptiness overflowing with fullness.

[[496]] The concept of opposition is clear in the individual case. Opposites illuminate and determine one another, for when they are compared they repel one another reciprocally, and their boundaries are sharply drawn against each other; the impressions are heightened into a full picture.

[[497]] It is, however, a difficult question how this concept is to be fixed in general. If one wishes to distinguish opposition (the contrarium) from negation by saying that opposition does not merely negate but at the same time replaces the negation by a new positive: then one has only half the concept. One could then give grey as the opposite of white, a sound as the opposite of red colour, the neutral as the opposite of salt, envy as the opposite of joy. What is different would already be what is opposed; a faint contrast would be counted equal to stark antagonism.¹

[[498]] In the first place, all opposition points to a higher universal, for example to the comprehensive unity of an end, which forms the measure of the relation. Concepts that share nothing with one another cannot step apart into an opposition either.

[[499]] Concepts, as universals, draw the differing individuals together into themselves. But compared with one another they themselves fall apart. Concepts arrange themselves at intervals; for according to their agreement and difference they attract and repel one another. Thus, when one considers the content, series of concepts are formed. Those which ⟦172⟧ stand farthest from one another within the same genus are called opposites.

To a, b is a disjunct concept, one species beside others; but A and Z, that is a and z, are (disjunct) contrary concepts, like beginning and end.

[[501]] This relation results when concepts are considered according to their content and, as it were, at rest side by side. The second thing is the direction of motion, when they are apprehended in their operation. The spatial direction of attracting and repelling, of together and apart, of resisting and yielding, of joining and separating, and so on, throughout forms here the measure of the underlying intuition. All manifestations of matter are subject to this mark, since they go back to motion. Even in the impressions of the senses we recognise this similarity. And since motion is the first act of thought, both as it copies and as it prefigures, this view carries over into mental concepts as well.

[[502]] Distance of concepts and direction in operation would accordingly be the mark of opposition. The clarity lies in the intuition; but in the mere intuition, it seems, there lies at the same time what is inadequate. The mark is only an image.

It is the ultimate unity of origination and the pervading measure of knowing.

[[504]] Concepts form a series according to increasing difference, or intervals according to the degree of dependence, according to the number of intermediate members. The logical relation naturally places itself under spatial intuition, since motion passes through all concepts. But if one regards this quasi-spatial order of concepts as what is original ⟦173⟧ and holds the external space of things to be only a deceptive reflection of this intelligible space, then one misjudges the necessary connection and the original motion that reaches over from thought into being and from being into thought.

[[506]] 3. By these marks real opposition is distinguished from logical contradiction. Only thoughts negate and contradict one another, and phenomena only when a thought underlies the one which the other, with its own, annihilates or weakens. For example, a golden chain on black velvet is set off by the opposition; but golden jewellery worn with soiled linen stands, as Kant remarks in the Anthropologie, not in contrast but in contradiction. Only when phenomena are related to an underlying thought or end is logical contradiction found in the real. In oppositions, which merely present the end-points of a whole, the whole is affirmed, willed; in contradiction it is negated or impaired. In our apprehension of a character we distinguish oppositions and contradictions. The oppositions, whether they stand sharply apart or blend gently together, strengthen its effect and mark the breadth and compass of its force; the contradictions cancel that force or do it harm. Life carries oppositions within itself in its motions and has its power in them; but from the contradictions of forces come anxiety, sickness, war. Of cooperating opposites one has a beautiful example in the harmony of colours that demand one another.¹

[[507]] Contradiction is the expression of what is utterly incompatible, which in itself mocks every mediation; for it is always underlain by the measure of affirmation and negation bearing on the same concept. Therefore in the real one may apply contradiction only sparingly, and must not straightway call every ⟦174⟧ obstacle and everything conflicting a contradiction.

[[509]] 4. Into negation too, which stands over against all the categories as cancelling them, the end reaches, and stamps its thought and will upon it, both when a negation proceeds from it and when it suffers a negation. To the former concept belongs, in Latin, the distinction between non and ne; the latter we noted among the categories of the end as privation, and in the ethical sphere it can rise to the point of evil. Languages mix up in their signs the distinction between pure negation, privation and opposition, as Latin does for instance in impar (pure negation), immemor (privation of what should have been or could have been), impius (privation and opposition). It is not useless for logic to sharpen the understanding of the indeterminate grammatical sign.

[[510]] 5. On the nature of negation rests the principle of agreement and of contradiction, the principium identitatis et contradictionis. A is A, and A is not not-A. The first form is a tautology. The second wards off what is contradictory. The principle is clear in itself. We bring it to bear in dialectical dispute when concepts are exchanged in order to deceive, and in it we insist on the identity of the object, without which there is no mutual understanding, no proof and no refutation. Its proper significance and the limits of its application for objective knowledge follow from the essence of negation. As negation is nowhere what is first, but flows as the second from individual determinateness, so nothing else is expressed in the principle than the right of determinateness asserting itself. Hence a knowledge of A must come first, which is usually placed in a sum of marks. The principle can only preserve this posited determinateness; ⟦175⟧ it prescribes nothing about becoming or arising, but preserves what has come to be and the secure possession of knowledge. The right to this lies in a known necessity; and therefore, if one disregards that subjective application in dialectic, necessity stands behind identity, at its back.¹

[[512]] If one wishes to raise the principle into a metaphysical one, into a norm of origination as it were, then it lacks a footing and one falls into contradictions. It is a principle of the fixating understanding, not of generating intuition; of fixed rest, not of fluid motion. If one wishes, as the Eleatics attempted, to abolish motion by means of contradiction against this principle, one errs; for since motion is what is original, there is still lacking that individual A, that determination, without which there is no negation and without which, therefore, the principle of contradiction has no basis either. Motion is motion and not rest, the law says. But it goes no further. Whether motion can be or not lies outside its range, because it first finds a place where a fixed concept already exists. Just as little as the Pythagorean theorem can be applied to the doctrine of lines and angles that precedes it, just as little as the law of the trajectory can be applied to the law of falling bodies on which it rests: so little can the principle of contradiction be applied to motion, which first conditions and produces the objects of its application. The principle of identity and contradiction accordingly has, as will be shown further below, its proper value in indirect proof.²

[[518]] # ⟦177⟧ XIII. THE MODAL CATEGORIES.

[[519]] 1. The path taken so far leads us further of itself. How the knowing mind could appropriate and penetrate things was the original question and the impulse of the whole investigation. First of all motion offered itself as what is common, destined to mediate the opposition between thought and being. Through motion generating space and time, insight opened into the a priori world of the mathematical and into the possibility of receptive experience. As the mental act of motion, to which the first activity of being corresponds, was observed, the fundamental concepts (categories) emerged, which in accordance with their origin have equally a subjective and an objective, a purely mental and an empirical significance, and govern the whole compass of thought and being. Thus the mind now had dealings with things and was therefore able as much to impress upon them its own stamp, the thoughtful end, as to grasp the creative end already in their origin. The end fused with the intuition of motion and therefore gave the fundamental concepts derived from motion a new mental design.

[[520]] ⟦178⟧ How the mind can know is accordingly indicated in the foregoing. The forms which it describes on this path are to be investigated next. But since we are here concerned first with the categories as the buttresses in the edifice of concepts, another question arises beforehand.

[[521]] The fundamental concepts developed so far bore solely on the thing in its inner nature. The cause in generating motion and the determining end produced, as their peculiar work, the essence of the thing. Consideration, however, does not stop there. When thought works at knowledge, new fundamental concepts must form which designate this act in its moments. These categories, which proceed from the task of the theoretical mind as such and therefore have their measure only in thinking cognition, are commonly comprised under the name of modality.¹ Which, then, are these?

[[522]] 2. Thought is to apprehend and comprehend things. Accordingly things confront it as appearance in a double sense: first as appearance for the knower, then as appearance of the active ground; the former in relation to the mind, the latter in relation to the thing. Thus the germinating plant is on the one side an appearance that stimulates the apprehending mind, and on the other an appearance of the living seed. Appearance mediates the motion from thought to the ground. Appearance in the first sense is a purely modal concept, in which being clothes itself in so far as it is to be apprehended and comprehended. Being, presented in this sense by the asserting (assertoric) judgment, is also called the actual, although, as will become evident, a more pronounced meaning remains reserved for actuality.

[[523]] > (1) On the origin of the expression cf. elementa log. Arist. on § 7.

[[525]] ⟦179⟧ 3. As appearance designates the relation of being to apprehending intuition, so ground designates its relation to comprehending thought. If appearance is a modal concept of sense, ground is a modal concept of the understanding.

[[526]] This determination is not to be understood as though the world were to dissolve into a subjective representation. The preceding investigations have demonstrated an objective knowledge. But what subsists in and for itself, and, active in and for itself, produces a connection, is called appearance and ground in so far as it becomes an element of the knowing mind. In the concepts of appearance and ground the living relation of being to thought is mirrored; without this illumination they become blurred and mixed up with the categories already discussed.

[[527]] It is understandable that linguistic usage wavers to and fro; but one must try to determine it according to the differences that stand out in the matter itself.

[[528]] The efficient cause and the end, which determine the relation of things, can be known according to the foregoing; when they are known, they are called ground in relation to what is comprehended from them. Those concepts remain in their substance but receive a higher value. The cause becomes ground when it is apprehended universally; and the universal is the mark that the concept has passed through thought. The cause is, like the thing, something individual, and as what precedes it relates to an individual fact. If, for example, we say that this seed germinates because it has been laid in the earth, the cause is designated as, being something individual, it precedes in time. But the same cause appears as ground when it is raised into the universal and accordingly placed under the law of organic life. Hence in the ground the temporal relation, which predominates in the cause, also recedes. In the ground the ⟦180⟧ blind concatenation of causes and effects driving onward is transformed into a necessity that is thought. The end, which springs up in the mind, combines still more readily with the concept of ground; and it is called ground when it is thought of as determining something else.

[[529]] When knowledge proceeds from the efficient cause and the end, it proceeds from the grounds of the thing. Since Aristotle,¹ to be sure, the causality of what is has been divided into causa materialis, causa formalis, causa efficiens and causa finalis, and according to this distinction, which has recommended itself by the clarity of its points of view, the grounds, which present causality in knowing thought, will also be distinguishable in this manner. Yet it has been shown in the foregoing that form and matter depend on the efficient cause of motion, and that all three, when the end carries itself out, are governed by the end. Hence it corresponds to the inner connection to grade the ground of the thing first as ground of the efficient cause and of the end. The end then, when it determines the will through representation, becomes motive (moving ground).

[[530]] In a special sense the ground of knowledge stands over against the grounds of the thing, the ratio cognoscendi over against the so-called causa essendi. To be sure, the ground of knowledge, the ground from which we know, can at the same time be the ground of the thing, the ground from which the thing has arisen. But commonly the ground of knowledge, as the starting-point of a logical process, and the ground of the thing, as the starting-point of a real one, will lie at different ends. The appearance that strikes the sense is the effect of the still hidden cause. In the effect, however, the traces of the cause are drawn. It is a creative act of the knowing mind to ⟦181⟧ divine the ground from these indications and to sketch out the appearance from the ground once found. Because the effect that comes to light forms the fixed point for the knowledge of the cause, it is called ground of knowledge. From it one infers backwards what produced the appearances forwards. Yet actual knowledge always comes about from the mind's power over the real ground, and without this it would remain stuck in flat fact. When the mind, however, seizes upon a possible ground of the thing, exploits it, and compares it in its consequences with the appearance, it gains insight. Where it comprehends, it does so by recreating the ground of the thing. The ground of knowledge is the impulse and the goal of this process. We illustrate what has been said by a simple example. If we say that the terrestrial globe is flattened because the measurements of degrees lead to a difference of the radii, we are stating the ground of knowledge. But the nerve of the proof lies in the reproduction of the thing. When from the arcs the radii are obtained, and from the differing radii that result the spheroidal shape of the earth is sketched out, the figure is comprehended from the mathematical construction, that is, from the grounds of the thing. Knowledge remains on the path from outside inwards until it proceeds from the ground of the thing and thereby from inside outwards. If, therefore, the ground of knowledge is taken in the wider sense, it must, in order to complete itself, coincide with the ground of the thing. Thus in the example cited the mind will press on from the geometrical construction into the producing cause, and Newton's theory, for example, grounds the flattening of the earth from the efficient principle. Such a form had to arise, namely, because the mass, not absolutely rigid, swung about its own axis. The geometer who proves the proposition that in a parallelogram the diagonal forms two equal and similar triangles from the nature of parallels, and so knows it from the origin of the figure (the ⟦182⟧ thing), takes the ground of the thing immediately as the ground of knowledge.

[[532]] To be sure, the external grounds of knowledge, the appearances of the effect, are of the greatest importance in all science, since they plant, as it were, fixed signals for the mind constructing from within. We do not, however, pursue this significance further, and hold here to the point that the external ground of knowledge is only destined to lead to the inner one, that is, to the ground of the thing. It is in this sense in particular that the word fact has taken its stamp: it readily signifies an individual effect compelling to the senses, in so far as it is a ground of knowledge.

[[533]] If we accordingly survey the kinds of grounds, they are either grounds of the thing or grounds of knowledge. The grounds of the thing are either ground of the efficient cause or of the end, the end either appearing blindly in nature or as the conscious motive of the will. The grounds of knowledge, on the other hand, are either taken from the effect or drawn from the cause itself.

[[534]] Schopenhauer, who assumes a fourfold root of the ground, attaches in particular a new and restricted meaning to the causa essendi. The kinds of grounds are mirrored for him in the faculties of the human mind, as the consistent view of his subjective idealism demands. Where the mind intuits a priori, and number and figure are known in time and space, the a priori forms, there prevails the sufficient ground of being (principium rationis sufficientis essendi), the law according to which the parts of space and of time determine one another with respect to their mutual relations. Where the mind experiences through the senses and gains objects in changing sensations, the sufficient ground of becoming (the principium ⟦183⟧ rationis sufficientis fiendi) reveals itself. Where the mind wills and is moved in doing so by representations or knowledge, the law of motivation (principium rationis sufficientis agendi) rules. Finally, where it infers (in the wider sense of the word), where it takes representations from representations — which is characteristic in so far as the remaining grounds bear on immediate representations — there the ground of knowledge (principium rationis sufficientis cognoscendi) is at work. This division corresponds to that view, spun into itself, for which the object is nothing but representation. But even in the sense of the doctrine that makes space and time merely subjective forms, the ground in arithmetic and geometry may not be designated as ground of being in opposition to the ground of becoming. For number is generated, figure described; they are what they are through construction. Hence in them too there prevails the ground of becoming, not of a being in quiescent extension. If one is compelled by these considerations to unite the ground of being with the ground of becoming, then three kinds of grounds remain: the ground of becoming, of physical nature; the ground of the will (the motive), of ethical nature; the ground of knowledge, of logical nature. This division then corresponds, to be sure, to an old division of science. But the end, which has the greatest significance for the ground as ground, in that it determines the ground of becoming and pervades the motive, is then misjudged as the most essential point of view for the division.

[[536]] We must not overlook an essential side of the concept of ground. The ground of a thing is commonly expressed in the singular. Whatever cooperates in producing a thing is comprised in the *one* ground. Certainly, however many moments may combine, the generating activity, this ⟦184⟧ living bond, this together, is always only one. But the question is: can this activity be comprehended from *one* ground, is the ground of the thing an indivisible unity? We may answer: in the finite, nowhere. The end always demands, as has been shown,¹ a plurality of elements, and can arise only with the breaking of unity. The efficient cause would be a uniform flow and would deposit nothing new, if as a unity it were to be related only to itself.

[[538]] With this may be compared the contradiction which has been pointed out in the concept of ground and consequence.² The consequence is supposed to lie in the ground; but it is also supposed to result from it, that is, to separate itself from it. Now if it really lies in the ground, it belongs to it. But if the consequence teaches something new, then this new thing is not the old and does not lie in the ground. The consequence must therefore be identical and also not identical with the ground. Were it not identical, it would not lie in the ground and would be no consequence, but something heterogeneous. Were it identical, it would not differ from the ground but would coincide with it; or rather, it would not come out at all, but would remain in the ground. This contradiction is unavoidable if one takes some individual thing, in its unity that merely repeats itself and remains equal to itself, as ground. Whence is the new to be generated as effect out of the unity of the cause, if something else that comes in addition does not drive it forth from it?

[[539]] Such a unity of the cause is an error of comprehensive language; when an individual thing is designated as the cause of a thing, it is only the most active of the conditions. We call the seed, say, the cause of the tree; but the seed, held by itself, locks up ⟦185⟧ its force, and develops it only when it is placed in the natural conditions of its germinating and growing. Or when we one-sidedly designate the impact as the mechanical cause of a local motion, we merely overlook the silently cooperating conditions of size, of mass and of figure. If the impact does not correspond to these, no change of place arises, and so on. According to the foregoing, motion in general appeared as the bearer of the efficient cause, and what is found in the former therefore continues in the latter through the whole domain. When motion shaped itself into geometrical products, it did so through opposed moments, motion and counter-motion. Cause and effect could not be distinguished without such a plurality of determinations. In the physical domain matter at once cooperates. Thus the cause breaks down into conditions, and accordingly the ground too, the cause posited universally, breaks down into moments. If among the conditions the significance of a single one so preponderates that the rest recede before it, then it may be called the cause as the predominant one; but it is not so by itself alone, and the name must not confuse the relations of the thing.¹ Accordingly the ground is a sum of conditions that belong together.²

But it is a misunderstanding, which dialectic to be sure often takes into its service, to transform ideal independence into a real one. If, in order to maintain the unity, one helps oneself by gathering the ground together into the free substance of the world-whole, or by referring to God, the One, as cause of himself, then one leaves the circle of finite knowing which is at issue here and takes refuge in transcendent regions. We are content if by such an evasion the result is conceded as far as motion reaches, and we hope that this relation will clarify the following concepts.

[[545]] 4. For what are possibility and necessity? These concepts can be determined only in accordance with the essence of the ground just set forth. When all conditions are known and the thing is accordingly understood from the whole ground, so that thought completely penetrates being, this gives the concept of necessity. When, on the other hand, only one or some conditions are known, but what is lacking to the ground is supplemented in thought, this gives the concept of possibility. Possibility, which always already includes parts of the ground, accordingly prepares necessity, knowledge from the full ground. We have given provisionally only the first outlines of possibility and necessity, in showing how they arise from the relation to the concept of ground, and we now look for the more precise features.

[[546]] 5. Possibility and necessity, in order to be understood, point to one another reciprocally, like part and ⟦187⟧ whole. As one comes to the whole only through the parts, and again comprehends the parts only through the whole, so the concepts of possibility and necessity also intertwine as into a ring, and the understanding of the one concept grows into the other. We begin, however, with possibility.

[[547]] To be sure, the possible is predicated of things, like a property of them. For example, the ellipse is a possible figure; this or that machine is possible. But the concept, in spite of all its real elements and relations, is what it is in its essence at the same time only through the thought in which the thing is copied or which prefigures the thing.

[[548]] This shows itself with particular clarity in the impossible. The impossible is only thought. While thought is in the course of becoming, in order either to present being or to determine it, the thought that has become and is fixed, which knows itself as the counter-image of the actual, confronts it and contradicts it. The thought striving towards recognition is negated by the recognised one, that is, by the one which is held to have reached the actual. In the impossible, thought is vanquished by the actual. An end is not called impossible as an end thought of, but only in so far as the means are impossible, and thus the existing actual lodges an objection against the thought that hastens ahead. In the impossible, therefore, thought and the actual clash in hostility with one another.

[[549]] But the actual with its mere fact does not after all prevail over thought; for thought boldly passes beyond what is there or was there. Only when the actual, bound by thought, becomes necessity does it in turn bind thought. Thought, in seeking to become actual, will let itself be instructed only by the thought in the actual. Impossibility therefore rests on a negating necessity by which the presumptuous ⟦188⟧ thought is limited. For example, it is impossible that in a plane triangle two angles should be equal to two right angles; it is impossible that the falling stone should rise. The law of the triangle, of falling, that is, the known necessity, raises its objection. The impossible expresses the necessity that something should not be.

[[550]] In the possible only individual conditions of the thing are apprehended, as it were only half a ground of arising. In the impossible a full ground of exclusion makes itself known. In the possible the missing conditions are skipped over, and their consent is, as it were, presupposed. In the impossible it is precisely the missing conditions that are brought forward and directed in hostility against those present. Hence the possible and the impossible are not related to one another as pure negations; rather, the elements of their essence are directly reversed. In the possible the generating ground is only partly there; in the impossible the preventing ground is wholly there. The impossible is accordingly an outflow of the necessary, but the possible is still only a play of thought.

[[551]] If the thought that is to copy or prefigure the actual is related only to itself, so that, detached from the nexus of actuality, it is considered alone by itself, then one disregards entirely the missing conditions and everything that could offer resistance, and possibility is at its widest. In this arbitrary separation of the thought, where nothing objects because one has extinguished everything else in thought, everything appears possible; but the possible then rests only on a notion; and thought stands at the greatest distance from the goal of necessity.

[[552]] Thought, however, goes further and makes earnest of possibility. It seeks to reach the actual, in which it has its measure. But conditions are lacking. When thought supplements them, the possible presents itself. The seed or the egg gives us, in an example from nature, the intuition of ⟦189⟧ possibility. From the seed a tree can come, from the egg an animal. This is no empty play of thought. The possibility lies, as it were, sensibly before our eyes. But by itself the seed remains seed, and the egg an egg. Thought reaches ahead and gathers these existing conditions together with those not yet existing into an active unity, and now pronounces the possibility. Thus the possible is a peculiar double formation. The conditions that are there are supplemented by those that are thought. But since this can happen only in thought, the possible is in the first instance also only something thought.

[[553]] The real elements in this double formation give determinateness; those that are thought and only ideally supplemented give indeterminateness. We see this, for example, when the possible values in an indeterminate equation, or the possible explanations of a difficult passage, are sought out. The more conditions are known and the fewer are still lacking, the more possibility signifies and the more its indeterminate breadth narrows. It is a peculiar greatness of acumen so to measure the existing conditions against the missing ones, and so to determine the character of the missing ones, that even the indeterminateness is enclosed within limits. How this is done only the peculiar nature of the thing can teach. In mathematical procedure such examples lie before us in abundance, and we recall only the Diophantine equations, or indirect proofs in which n possible cases are distinguished so that n − 1 may be refuted by trial. Where facts are to be explained, the possibilities are likewise first assembled. Thus determinateness presses forward into indeterminateness, and necessity shows itself here first in the limitation of the possible.

[[554]] If one looks more closely at the missing conditions which thought supplements, the same consideration repeats itself in them, and there arises a possible ⟦190⟧ within the larger possible. The question is whether conditions for the missing conditions are already there. And even if there again everything inclines towards the assumption of the possible, thought must always hasten ahead, supplementing. And on what does it rely in doing so? Merely on negative concessions. What is known does not contradict. No excluding necessity shows itself. Thought converts what is not excluded into something admitted, and decides the pending indeterminateness by the positive character of its own direction.

[[555]] Where nature has prefigured a possibility, as in the seed, in the egg, it is always only a union of some existing conditions. The rest thought skips over, or posits because the opposite is not enjoined. Thus possibility is confirmed as a modal concept.

[[556]] The possible always remains something future, even where it is a matter of knowledge of the past or of the hidden ground of a present activity; for in this case the possible is indeed not decided by the course of things so that it becomes actual, but it awaits the decision from thought, in order that it may become a known truth.

[[557]] The possible that stems from the efficient cause differs from the possible that the end determines. In the efficient cause different possibilities arise out of the same thing; it becomes something different according as this or that comes in addition. It awaits, as resting and passive, its determinations from outside, as foreign ones. The end often finds several possible paths to its goal. But as it determines itself more precisely and takes up into itself new considerations as ends of the end (for example, compactness in a machine, elegance in a geometrical construction), the means are measured accordingly and the less fitting possibilities are ⟦191⟧ excluded. Thus in the end the possibilities are projected from within, and their indeterminateness is decided from within.

[[558]] In the foregoing the possible was considered in the sense in which it is predicated of a thing (for example, an event, a state is possible), and the real elements in this concept of the possible were sought out and distinguished from the logically modal determination. Thus possibility sprang forth, as it were, as an individual thing. What was at issue was the actuality of the possible.

[[559]] From this is distinguished the reverse relation, the possibility of the actual. We designate it as inner possibility, in which the question is not what is possible, but how something is possible. What is apprehended is not the result, as in the foregoing, but the process; not the thing as it has issued from its conditions, but the thing thrown back precisely into its conditions. Thus one speaks of the inner possibility of a circle when one sees how it arises; of an invention, when one understands how it can come about; of a phenomenon, when one comprehends it; of a machine, when one knows its end and the activity of its parts for this end; of a character, when one sees it come to be out of its original disposition and the surrounding influences, out of action and reaction. When, on the other hand, the seed was considered above as the possibility of the tree, this possibility does of course rest on the inner one; but this was disregarded, and what was apprehended was not the development but the result.

[[560]] This inner possibility is not a double formation like the possible in that first sense, but a pure and full process of comprehending thought. It does not tear itself loose from the actual, but rather seeks to understand it in its becoming. In the domain of the efficient cause this is accomplished by insight into the activities and their ⟦192⟧ interaction; in the domain of the end, by insight into the goal and mastery over the means leading to it through knowledge of the effects. Both lie simply before our eyes in geometrical examples. The inner possibility, for instance, of a figure, of a circle, of an ellipse, lies in the law of construction, which the mind sees through by its own act of motion. The inner possibility of a geometrical problem rests on the end set as a task and on the power to survey the means of construction for it and to direct them towards it. When Euclid begins with definitions of the figures, these are for him only nominal definitions, which have no significance for him until he has demonstrated through construction the inner possibility of what has been defined.¹

[[561]] This inner possibility is related to necessity in a twofold way, in that it presupposes necessity as ground on the one hand, and on the other further grounds its progress. The inner possibility of a figure, which is demonstrated in ⟦193⟧ construction, presupposes insight into the necessary relations of the constructing motion. With inner possibility the essence is known, the basis of all derived properties, the ultimate ground of all proofs. Thus in the Pythagorean theorem everything rests in the first place on the inner possibility (the construction) of the right-angled triangle and of the square. Once this is known from the necessity of the geometrical elements, all further necessity flows from it.

[[562]] > (1) When Spinoza imitates the Euclidean system in the methodical form of his Ethics, there shows itself at once, for all the external sameness, a difference that decides everything. Spinoza begins with definitions (of substantia, of causa sui, etc.), as Euclid does with the definitions of the simplest plane figures. But Spinoza treats his determinations without more ado as real definitions, as though inner possibility did not first have to be demonstrated in order to secure the representation against fiction. Euclid, on the other hand, proves that what he has defined is something actual. Compare, for example, how the square, though defined already in Book I, Def. 30, is not yet there at all for the system until it is constructed at the end of the book (Prop. 46) (cf. Kästner's essay: was heisst in Euklides' Geometrie möglich? in Kästner and Klügel philosophisch-mathematische Abhandlungen. Halle 1807). Spinoza does not proceed in this way with his metaphysical concepts, and cannot do so. What he has defined he takes, in all the books of the Ethics, as an actuality thereby settled. Spinoza's rigid demonstration therefore lacks that transparent genetic view which accompanies the most intricate geometrical proof. Thus, in spite of all the scaffolding of the geometrical edifice, the title of the Ethics refutes itself from the very start: ethica ordine geometrico demonstrata.

[[563]] Leibniz insisted on this inner possibility as the essence in the knowledge of things. But is it sufficient when for him the ultimate standard is that the concept does not contradict itself? Contradiction is that objection of knowledge, that negating necessity, from which, as was shown, the concept of the impossible arises. In the first place it is an infinite and therefore insoluble task if it is to be proved that no objection arises from any side of knowledge. It therefore at once turns into the restricted consideration, which turns from the thing to ourselves, that we find nothing in the rest of our science that resists. Inner possibility, which seeks to comprehend positive becoming and essence, cannot content itself with such a negative determination. It is not enough that the elements of a concept do not cancel one another or are not cancelled from elsewhere. Rather, they should support and enliven one another and likewise be borne by the remaining concepts. How this happens is the difficult, thoroughly positive insight that is demanded in inner possibility. In order to see into the inner possibility of the circle, it is not enough that the concept of a line which in all its parts is at equal distance from *one* point shows no contradiction. It must be comprehended how such a line arises.

[[564]] Leibniz demanded, as a supplement to the ontological ⟦194⟧ proof, that the possibility of the concept of God first be made evident. God, he said, has the prerogative of being necessary if he is possible. Nothing, however, he added, prevents his possibility, because he has no limit. Since, in opposition to limited creatures, it is his concept to be without limit, it includes no negation and hence no contradiction either, and is absolutely possible. In the unrestricted perfection of God — such is the thought — only affirmations, no negations, are contained. But since contradiction arises only where something is affirmed and at the same time negated, no contradiction can arise in the concept of God. Does this logical consideration suffice to know God's existence a priori? Is Leibniz's presupposition, the concept of unrestricted perfection itself, this cancellation of all negations, possible in itself? Or do not the realities which, though unrestricted, are attributed to God as predicates according to the old metaphysical view, restrict one another? Only when it has been shown that they do not hinder but support one another, that they do not negate but demand one another, would a beginning have been made, from this standpoint, towards knowledge of the inner possibility. So inadequate is a merely logical consideration.

[[565]] Inner possibility seeks to understand the process of the thing from the conditions of its becoming. But it is still wholly enclosed in thought and is in this a purely modal concept, since it still awaits its realisation. That process, however, by which the existing conditions, in themselves at rest and incomplete, are supplemented in thought and thereby quickened into a full ground, so that something may be pronounced possible, rests on insight into inner possibility. Thus the threads of thought here join into a knot.

[[566]] 6. We survey what we have before us. Conditions are now there which the inner possibility of a thing demands. ⟦195⟧ They press more and more towards *one* point. The possibility is ripe. The last condition that was still lacking appears, the force that gathers, directs and moves the remaining conditions, and actuality breaks forth.

[[567]] One cannot say that actuality completes possibility.¹ Rather, possibility completes the actual conditions into the thought whole of a full ground. If, to be sure, one looks only at the part of the existing conditions that lies present in the concept of the possible, and if it is asserted that these are fulfilled in the actual, then that is correct. But possibility as such is not yet contained in this; rather, it boldly goes precisely beyond the existing conditions. Nor can actuality be called the completion of the possible in the sense that the thought in which possibility has its essence is a deficiency compared with the actual, and that only the event fills this gap. In such a respect thought and being cannot be compared; for they are, in the parts of their essence, if this expression is permitted, so heterogeneous among themselves that they cannot be added to one another. The actual therefore does not integrate possibility either.

[[568]] While being at first spreads itself out, as it were, only in a surface, undifferentiated and only equal to itself, it receives in the concept of the actual, which has risen out of possibility, the depth that points back to the conditions of the ground.

[[569]] 7. In what has just been discussed, the concepts of potency and actus are already contained, which, introduced by Aristotle and favoured by the Scholastics, have found a new reception in Schelling's most recent philosophy.

[[570]] On the one side they carry real elements within them. ⟦196⟧ For where real conditions for an existence are given, yet not all of them and only a part, potency is posited, and where they are fulfilled, actus; and indeed their nature differs according as the conditions are determined merely physically within the efficient cause or are organically preformed — as an example of the former relation the Aristotelian one may serve, that bronze is the potency of a statue, and of the latter the seed of a tree. Out of bronze many kinds of things can come through the human hand; out of the seed, if it does not rot, only the tree; the former potency is indeterminate, the latter determined in its end.

[[571]] > (1) Thus actuality was called complementum possibilitatis by Chr. Wolff.

[[572]] On the other side the modal nature of these concepts is plain as soon as the concept of potency, to which the actus refers back, is taken as the standard. Into potency there shines thought, looking ahead and anticipating a future existence, as the light in which the real conditions become potency.
