[[571]] It is incorrect when, misled by the predominant usage in Aristotle, one regards only matter as potency. Aristotle himself already takes it more generally.¹ In the ethical sphere the concept of potency presents itself where powers and means are prepared for what is to come. Thus, for example, the books in a library are potency; when read, they are transformed into actual thoughts of men. The money in the chest is potency; in its application it becomes actual power; avarice sates itself on the intuition of the mere potency that belongs to it. Possession is potency (capacity); use is actualization (actus). In making things into potency for reason, man begins to ensoul them. Where a lower stage makes itself the substrate of a higher, it can be regarded as potency in so far as something further must still be added in order to produce a ⟦197⟧ fuller actuality. Thus natural development, for instance, makes itself potency in so far as it becomes the substrate of the development of mind, as it were matter for the latter; "it potentializes itself," one says of the lower stage, in an unclear expression with a philosophical sound and resonance.

[[573]] It follows from the derivation that potency is by its essence finite and limited; for so long as it is potency, a piece of the conditions is always lacking, and to that extent it is needy. Aristotle consistently, when with this concept he arrived at the divine, let fall the dynamis which otherwise precedes energeia, and intuited in the divine energeia alone (pure energeia, as the Scholastics expressed it). When recent philosophers speak of infinite potency or of the infinite possibility of existence, contradictory things are forcibly bent together, and with all dialectical art no profound clarity can issue from unclear profundity. In Plotinus a similar usage begins, when, for example, he calls what lies above all essence the potency of all things; but the potency of all things is after all something other than a potency which is itself called infinite. Plotinus calls the First energeia; for otherwise, he rightly says, the most perfect would be imperfect.³ It is necessary that abstract concepts be grasped as sharply as the concrete ones themselves direct us to; and then every potency is finite and dependent, none infinite.

[[574]] 8. On the side of being, possibility seeks to foresee the actual and strives after it by its innermost impulse. On the side of thinking, it prepares necessity and, although the two seem to stand opposed to each other like play and earnest, puts the means into its hand.

[[575]] Necessity is commonly defined as the impossibility ⟦198⟧ of the opposite, and Aristotle already seeks to reduce the concept of the necessary to that which cannot be otherwise. In formal logic it was believed that through this middle term a transition had been discovered from the principle of contradiction to the proof of the necessary. May one rest content with this determination? It expresses the view of the indirect proof, which tries out the assumption of the opposite in its consequences until these refute it. If the experiment as to whether something could be otherwise turns out negatively, necessity is pronounced. The possibility that something be otherwise already includes a negation. If A is asserted, then not-A (its opposite) is tried. But since this negation is in turn negated in the consequences that would result, the affirmation is restored and the assertion is necessary.

[[579]] So taken, the necessary is nothing but the unavoidable. Evidently there rules in it only an external constraint, which permits no straying and encloses the matter on all sides. We call this necessity the necessity of limitation. Here the necessary is not yet grounded in itself, resting firmly on its own centre of gravity; it appears only as so pressed and held from outside that it cannot give way. In the unavoidable the inner determination is not yet known. The usual definition, however, takes on a particular logical semblance from the fact that it comes about through the negation of the negation; for negation, it is thought, belongs exclusively to thinking as its own. But, more closely considered, that concept of the necessary destroys itself if it means to be the final determination. For what, then, does the negation negate? What convicts the opposite, so that it is cancelled as impossible? The counter-thrust ⟦199⟧ that is made against the consequences, the refutation which negates the opposite in the consequences, proceeds from a fixed point. We have got no further, and have only been thrown, in an external manner, onto another necessary. The fundamental concept of the necessary cannot become clear in this way, since it itself in turn presupposes the necessary. No grounding of its own has been gained, but only a concatenation; and even apart from the first secure point on which the chain is hung, the chain itself has come about only under the tacit condition of a necessary consequence.

[[581]] The definition by way of negation accordingly itself demands another, positive one; the derived demands an original one. It happens not seldom that a concept is first seized in its external and therefore secondary relations; but these can only be the passage to the primary determinations.

[[582]] If, on that first path, the experiment was made as to whether the concept in question could also be otherwise, then the mind at first withholds recognition until it is wrung from it. It is the mind's interest to be conscious and certain of itself in recognition, and this happens in the trial of the opposite. Hence the necessary has been defined by recent thinkers as that which cannot not be thought. Recognition is indicated in this as something essential, and linguistic usage confirms it. We do, it is true, speak of necessary fate, in which, as it seems, only blind force rules, and thought, without which there is no recognition, has vanished. But we call it necessary only when thinking, which seeks a loophole of freedom, surrenders captive. Here, indeed, thinking is no element in the course of events itself; but only when these are measured by thinking that is added to them does the concept of their necessity arise. Until then they were occurrences; now they become ⟦200⟧ necessity. The recognition which the mind performs in this process is not weakness, as though it wanted something else but had been overcome; it is its essence and its strength, in that it stamps upon blind existence the higher impress, necessity.

[[583]] Certainty and truth, subjective and objective, strike together in necessity. The final point on which all necessity rests is therefore a community of thinking and being. What is an element of thinking must immediately be an element of being, and conversely. We could call this final point, if the expression had not been worn out in many senses, the identity of thinking and being. We refer back to the investigations above. Motion, the free property of the mind, appeared at the same time as the first act of the external world. In it a source of necessary knowledge opened up, first the mathematical domain, then the possibility of penetrating into the ground of physical phenomena. Even the material itself resolved into motion and thereby permitted an insight into its necessary formation. Anew we saw the same community of thinking and being in the end. Because our mind is able to determine the parts from the preconceived idea of the whole, it comprehends the same determination when it meets it in nature as a realized fact.

[[584]] An identity of thinking and being is posited unconsciously by the uneducated man in every immediate impulse in which he asserts necessity, because in his limitation he cannot think otherwise and therefore believes himself to be in possession of the object. The educated man finds it in the universal of reflection; science recognizes it only in the principles and in their consistent development; for science, as it were, mediatizes the representations that until then held sway as immediately entitled.

[[585]] If in the domain of the senses the received ⟦201⟧ facts of impressions are interwoven with the original developments of motion, as in the demonstrations of physics, then the first strictness suffers, since the necessity of those received determinations does not stand on the same level as the necessity of these freely commanded constructions. If, however, necessity is spoken of in sense-experiences too, then there is tacitly presupposed in this — as actually happens in unprejudiced consciousness, in which sceptical questions have not yet arisen — an agreement of the impressions with the thing, and hence, since even in the externally conditioned state of the senses the activity of thinking still appears, an identity of thinking and being.

[[586]] In such an accord alone lies the possibility of the recognition which pervades the concept of necessity. What is in this way common to thinking and being is called the universal, and the universal in this sense is the positive ground of necessity.

[[587]] 9. The universal has a manifold meaning; and we must distinguish its meanings if we wish to fix its relation to the necessary.

[[588]] In the most external sense the universal means the common. That in which the groups of phenomena agree is, when set apart in thought, called their universal. Whether it be abstracted or demonstrated, it appears, expressed in this way, as a factual universal, as a universal of fact. If we take the geometrical proposition that in all right-angled triangles the square on the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares on the other two sides, or the physical law that in falling the distances are as the squares of the times, then both, expressed thus in isolation, are nothing but a universal expression of the actual and to that extent a universal of fact. What takes place in all cases is singled out as the common. The universal is apprehended in the ⟦202⟧ state of rest as that in which the individual cases are absorbed, and the kinds of things are determined accordingly.

[[589]] The universal is, secondly, grasped in the motion of developing things. The undifferentiated, out of which what is differentiated within itself can come to be, is called the universal. If out of the sum of conditions which together form the ground a single one is singled out, then it is the universal in this sense. According as the missing conditions are added in different ways, something different can come of it. The Aristotelian dynamis is the universal in relation to these various shapes which it as it were encloses. The bronze out of which, in Aristotle's example, a statue comes to be, and which in the statue takes different shapes to present the limbs, is in this meaning the universal of the parts differentiated within it. When the rudiment of a thought shoots up in the mind, this first beginning, this as it were fertilized representation, is the universal with respect to the articulated series of thoughts which unfolds from this first embryonic state. The child compresses a whole sentence into its stressed principal concept and utters only this *one* word with the significant gesture of judging or desiring. This word is the germ of the period that differentiates itself within itself, and its universal. When from the germinating seed of the tree the root and the plumule (the stem) separate out in two different directions, the indifferent seed is the universal of these different directions. In all these cases the universal lies before us in the appearing conditions of a thing and presents itself sensibly in the history of becoming itself. We may call it the universal of the real condition. It shows itself, as the examples already demonstrate, as much in the material which the efficient cause shapes as in the end which articulates itself as if from an inner impulse.

[[590]] ⟦203⟧ The universal completes itself in the universal of the ground. The universal of fact, as the common, rests only in the comprehending thought, whether it has been found from actuality or set up before actuality, as it were, as the archetype of the kind. The universal of the real condition falls to the history of the appearance; it does reveal the sequence of becoming, but still only in an external connection; it presents only *one* condition in its significance, and the others which co-operate in the development are deliberately disregarded. The universal of the ground gathers all the conditions together and, being stirred by other co-operating universals, draws the resting universal into motion. The universal of fact lies there isolated, an image of a group of phenomena. The universal of the ground has its life in co-operation. In the universal of the real condition the efficient ground appears, and this is why the history of a process, e.g. the developmental history of the animal, is so stimulating; it supplies the fixed data for insight into the ground. The universal of the ground opens up understanding by producing in thought the same thing that brought forth the thing itself. The final moment in it always remains that community of thinking and being. Without such a community thinking scarcely advances within itself, and never in things, and the final task, that we understand a thing as it came to be, remains for ever unsolved without it. At most we would only arrange things to suit ourselves, but never become masters of them as they are in themselves. We recall here the rich possibility of penetrating, through the motion of the mind, into the shape of things that has sprung from motion. We recall here what emerged above concerning the unity of the subjective and the objective in the wide domain of the mathematical and in the world-governing end. If in this way first universal points have been gained in which thinking must recognize the essence of things as its ⟦204⟧ own essence, then these points develop by acting upon one another. They become conditions which together form the ground. This togetherness is mediated by motion or by the unity of the whole, whose origin has been shown. What came forth as a universal fact in the imposing repose of law now produces something new by combining with other universals. Once it has been understood in this development, it takes its place as a new law, as a ruling fact, in the proud series of the others. Thus one succeeds ever further, through the combination of secure points, in reproducing in the mind the bodily world of the individual as a universal world.

[[591]] We illustrate this universal of the ground by some examples. Above we showed how, according to the genesis of the thing, according to the first elements common to thinking and to things, the geometrical square corresponds to the arithmetical second power. As a result, it becomes a universal fact, having arisen from the development of the ground. This simple universal fact is one of the most effective conditions in geometrical analysis for carrying the geometrical over into the far-reaching calculations of arithmetic. This *one* universal, together with others, produces what is more individual. — The Pythagorean theorem, taken above as an example of a factual universal (not as though it were not proved, but because in its formulation it expresses only what is common to the phenomena), comes into play in the consideration of plane trigonometry and, in combination with the ratios arising from the similarity of triangles, produces the most important formulae, which express a universal law. — The law of free fall, that the distances are as the squares of the times, grows together with horizontal ⟦205⟧ or oblique projection. Both factors are followed in their co-operation, and the parabola results as the path of projection for these cases.¹ This universal is determined anew when it combines with the law of the resistance of the air, and so on. The end, in Goethe's expression the synthetic universal, stirs many connections at *one* stroke and, with its unity, calls the manifold into its service in realization. The process begins with the universal, in that thought seeks to become one with actuality, and indeed with an already particularized universal, and seeks the more universal factors that can produce this particularization.

[[593]] In all these cases the universal as ground is the sum of co-operating universal conditions. The universal, traced to its first roots, rests here either on an identity of thinking and being which is posited outright, as in the first principles, or on a relative identity, as in the received elements of sense-perception. This unity of thinking and being returns still more clearly in the ethical sphere, where the object proceeds from man himself. Hence in this domain it is equally possible to determine universally the combination of the universal, the interaction of laws. It is the task of science to lead the relative identity, which is merely assumed and immediate, back more and more to the original one through mediation. Since this community of thinking and being does not consist in fixed, resting points, as the geometrical axioms, for instance, may well be regarded, but in a generative activity, it is possible to accompany the effect with thought and to remain in the midst of what comes to be. On this rests ⟦206⟧ the universality of consequence. If, starting out from our own states, we think ourselves into human ends and motives, into human means and passions, and thereby understand warp and weft in the fabric of history, or if the plastic art of the historian or the poet lets a character come to be before our eyes so that we recognize its necessity, then we comprehend what is foreign from the universal within us; and what we do not understand or misunderstand runs aground or is clouded on the limitation of the particular within us. Hence all culture is directed toward bringing to freedom this universal out of which we understand the world.

[[595]] One further application of the universal must be mentioned here in passing. Hegel calls the I the universal because the particular objects fall into its consciousness, and calls man universal because he thinks things. This usage is forced and confuses the meaning. The universal, it is true, springing immediately from thinking, forms the peculiar character of man, and the universal is his stamp; but he finds the universal in the phenomena received from outside, or he creates it in determining things through the end; in short, he thinks the universal, but is not himself the universal. As little as the mirror can be called a universal in so far as the surrounding objects fall into it, so little can the I. The expression blurs the great fact that it is precisely in the most individual thing there is, which is man, that the universal is brought forth.

[[596]] 10. Let us return to necessity. It rises out of the universal of the ground, in which being and thinking enter into community, and it alone gives the full right to pronounce the universal of fact. The universal of the ground is a qualitative determination, the universal of fact only the quantitative totality which comprehends the ⟦207⟧ homogeneous phenomena under it. Only that is universal in appearance which is necessary; but only that is necessary which stems from the universal of the ground. Since these concepts thus condition one another, the universal of appearance, where it presents itself in experience, permits an inference backwards.

[[597]] Necessity accordingly rests in the final sense on the community of thinking and being, and it springs forth only when being is penetrated by thinking. Here again efficient cause and end part ways. Both fall under necessity, but in directly opposite fashion. In the efficient cause being is the first and is copied by thinking. When it has been attained, necessity results. In the end, conversely, thinking is the first and demands the shaping of being. When the end reveals itself in the appearance, and the appearance thereby gathers itself into a necessary whole, then cognizing thought grasps thought at its origin and in the phenomena returns into itself. Hence the necessity of the end reconciles the free mind. As the end gave all the categories a new and ideal character,¹ so it animates necessity with the life of mind.

[[598]] How does the first, formal concept, the impossibility of the opposite, stand to this concept of necessity, that it is being penetrated by thinking? The negating opposite is assumed until it proves itself impossible in its consequences. But the inferences from the assumption already presuppose a necessity of the deriving activity, and this works with elements borrowed from the community of thinking and being. Other universals must be combined with the assumed opposite so that something new may arise which reveals the nature ⟦208⟧ of the assumption; this new thing is then refuted by the necessary. Hence the concept that the necessary cannot be otherwise has only an apparent independence and cannot do without the necessary itself. Under this presupposition, however, the determination has value. For it sharpens knowledge by delimiting it through the relation of concepts. If it is to be tried whether the matter could not be otherwise, there is need of a comprehensive view in order to determine the possible cases, and of an agile and rigorous mind in order to draw the inferences from what is merely posited and assumed. What exists readily reveals itself in its effects; but what does not exist and yet is assumed enters into connections only under constraint. Hence the indirect proof demands an agile power of mind and stimulates acuteness in a peculiar way.

[[600]] The two concepts of necessity support each other. Where the end acts creatively, the preventive activity goes alongside the productive, so that the thing cannot turn out otherwise. In all necessity intended by the human mind its positive and its negative concept appear. The end demands determinate means; but one foresees which foreign influences could, in spite of the means, frustrate or disturb the end, and one takes precautions. Nature proceeds likewise. We recall the provision in the structure of the eye, the protective diaphragm of the iris, the black pigment of the walls of the eye, the prevention of colour dispersion in the media, and so on.¹ The physical necessity which is to serve the end is so isolated or so directed that it can swerve neither left nor right and cannot refuse its obedience. Care is taken that it behave "not otherwise." The opposite is made impossible. But this concept, like all negation, ⟦209⟧ appears only as something second and, as it were, only as an aid to the positive path.

[[602]] At the Diet of Worms Luther spoke his firm word, "I cannot do otherwise," and thereby comprehended freedom within necessity. Only in a few self-possessed individuals does history celebrate so great a self-conscious, one might say theoretical, moment in the very midst of the turning-point of events. Creative freedom does not act groundlessly out of itself; rather, in the service and as the instrument of a divine end, freedom is as it were absorbed into necessity and can even borrow the expression of external constraint: "I cannot do otherwise." One's own development is self-limitation, and firm limitation is necessity. Whoever cannot believe in the reconciliation of freedom and necessity must look into the depth of such moments of history. But the reconciliation always takes place only on the presupposition of the end to which victory is due, and of the freedom which seizes this end. If, however, this "not being able to do otherwise" is understood only of the pressure and thrust of circumstances, then history becomes a selfless event and an unavoidable chance; for such a necessity is only like a famine of history, in which one greedily seizes and devours the first thing that comes to hand. In the conscious moments of life the twofold character of necessity comes to light, the positive and the negative. Freedom lies in being able to do otherwise according to the circumstances and the formal side, and again the higher freedom lies in not being able to do otherwise according to the content and the willed end. If in the same way one sees in nature only this external necessity, that something cannot be otherwise, then, consistently carried through, God's creation becomes an unavoidable error. The rigid formal character of necessity then cuts off any reconciliation with the freedom of the content.

[[603]] ⟦210⟧ 11. In the necessary, which by its concept is the unchangeable and is therefore already called ἀΐδιον by Aristotle and aeternum by Spinoza, the identical presents itself. Everything necessary is identical with itself and maintains itself as such.¹ While the identical is given by no perception, by no experience of the changing phenomena, the mind knows it in them by thinking the ground. Where it exercises the causality which it recognizes again as law in its own product, as in mathematics, it has built its concept of the identical into it. The question arises² whether, conversely, everything identical, even if it shows itself in appearance only relatively, is necessary. At first the presumption is in its favour, and inquiry here turns with confidence toward the ground that would prove it necessary. Plato already, in his concept of the Same (ταὐτὸ), stamps out the character of self-equal reason.

[[604]] But even apart from this pointing toward the necessary of the ground, the identical is everywhere the help of the mind, and without the identical the mind would be at a loss. The identical of the object is for it the condition of knowing at all.³ No apprehension of the surrounding spatial image is possible without a relatively fixed point, which man at first makes of himself. By this identical we find our way in space, as the astronomer does by his identical circles; by the identical we direct our motions, as the mariner does by the pole star or the magnetic needle. Through the identical, e.g. the constant sign of the word, men understand one another across the centuries. On the law, which is the same for all, rest the community and security of intercourse. The most far-reaching inventions seek to represent or to preserve the identical, such as the inventions of writing, of ⟦211⟧ measure, of money, of the measurement of time. In this they turn the a priori concept outwards and connect with it one thing after another, so that a domain of knowing and acting thereby arises in which the mind becomes sure of itself.

[[610]] 12. Necessity is commonly divided, corresponding to the parts of philosophy, into logical, physical and ethical. It is more fitting that the division follow the ascending stages of the principles. Otherwise, under the name of physical necessity, one blurs the essential differences which present themselves between the mathematical, that of physics, and the organic. Right through physical necessity runs the line which separates necessity from the efficient cause (the mathematical and that of physics) and necessity from the end (the organic and the ethical). Logical necessity is opposed to physical and ethical necessity in so far as it apprehends consequence. It will make itself known further below, but is already clear here in so far ⟦212⟧ as only that universal which has something necessary for its content yields true inferences and carries proper consequence within itself.

[[611]] For logical necessity the word "necessity of thought" (Denknothwendigkeit) has recently been introduced. The invention is not a happy one. For every necessity, including the necessity of the thing, the physical and the ethical, bears thinking in its origin. Moreover the word suffers from a refractory accentuation.

[[612]] 13. Now that the necessary has been set out in its relation to the universal, light falls on the concept of law, in which the isolated necessary takes on the form of the universal ruling over the facts. The universal of the ground, which rests on that community of thinking and being, produces the universal of fact. The latter is the external result of the former. Thus law presents itself as the universal which determines the appearance before the appearance. It is imprinted in the phenomena and can therefore be known from the phenomena; as from the individual decisions and verdicts of a judge the universal norm, the law according to which he has pronounced judgment, can be found, so the law of the thing can be found through comparative observation of the facts. Conversely, the end, which determines and orders the individual to its service, resembles the law as it is projected in the mind of the legislator. Here it is at the source, there poured out into things. It lies in the nature of the matter that the concept of law is graded first according to efficient cause and end, and more closely according to the gradation of the principles, as mathematical and physical, organic and ethical.

[[613]] 14. Finally, the contingent is measured against the necessary. The actual which is not necessary, or more correctly is not regarded as necessary, is called contingent. This emerges in the following connection.

[[614]] ⟦213⟧ The universal of the ground, since it passes through thought, is the simple, raised above the differences of the individual. Now since these differences, not comprehended by the necessary, nevertheless come into contact with the necessary, they are called (in opposition to the necessary) the contingent. If, further, it has been shown how the universal, particularly in the domain of sense-experience, owing to the merely relative identity of thinking and being, still carries within it a wide remainder which is for the present incommensurable with thinking, then there arises in this a counterplay against the necessity that has been known, and a field of chance opens up. If, finally, the end generated in thought seeks to realize itself, there remains, in the individual activity that carries out the end and in the individual material into which it is formed, something impenetrable to thinking and therefore foreign to necessity.

[[615]] What thus comes externally in addition to necessity language designates as chance. As in necessity the greatness of the universal reveals itself, so in chance does the defect that clings to it. The more widely the universal is conceived, the more freedom is given to the particular, the more scope to the contingent. Into the arithmetical formula, expressed universally by letters, the most various values can be substituted in the individual case. The law of the geometrical figure leaves free the magnitude of the space in which the figure is presented. If, in Aristotle's example, someone ploughs and in doing so finds a treasure, something foreign, which was not comprehended under it, is added to the activity necessarily determined by the end.

[[616]] In all these cases the contingent is something that actually occurs. Even the sudden notion that we call contingent as a thought is an actual thought. It is now a matter of designating more closely the relation of this actual to the necessary.

[[617]] ⟦214⟧ Necessity appeared essentially in a twofold shape, as the necessity of the efficient cause and the necessity of the end. In the former, being was the first and original, which is only recognized by thinking; in the latter, thought, which seizes and determines being. Accordingly the concept of chance too, which is itself nothing, but is only separated off and thrown back by necessity as something foreign, will have a twofold meaning.

[[618]] The law of the efficient cause is produced from a sum of conditions and on their presupposition. If they take place, the law also takes place. Whether they take place the individual law does not determine. For in order to grasp the conditions in their necessary power, they had to be lifted out of the sphere of the individual and handed over to universal thinking. If, therefore, the thing exists, it is subject to the law; but whether it exists depends on something else. On account of this foreign influence the individual which is subject to the law appears contingent when it is related only to this law; and language therefore designates these individuals as cases of the law, in which the play of the inflowing individual against the universal hovering above it seems to be indicated. The law itself, though determinate in itself, yet grown out of universal conditions, repeats a similar relation within itself through this universality of its elements. Through this universality there arises an indifferent, neutral element which can take shape thus and otherwise, which can rise and fall, without escaping the law. It is a free motion which even the physical law permits within the range of its realm. Since the law expresses the qualitative, the quantitative mostly appears as this undetermined and contingent element, unless the qualitative itself, as was shown above,¹ has proceeded from quantitative ⟦215⟧ relations. In so far as these elements, which are left free within the law and open to foreign determination, vary, they are called contingent. Thus, e.g., it is contingent for the law of the Pythagorean theorem how large the sides of the right-angled triangle are, and for the law of fall how great the height of the fall is. There thus results, in the domain of physical necessity, a contingent in a twofold sense. But the contingent does not withdraw itself. However often it occurs and however it may occur, it always stands under the necessity of the law.

[[620]] The necessity of the end gives chance an independent position and a greater importance. Thought determines actuality and demands determinate shapes of it. The particular is not left free but is ruled and formed by the law itself. But thought, which takes physical necessity into its service for the execution of the end, seizes and possesses nature only in its universal aspects. There remained, as we saw, something foreign and impenetrable in the concrete. If this element, not mastered by thought, furthers or hinders the end in a manner not foreseen by the end, or produces alongside the end something wholly other, then this lies outside necessity and is called chance. In this sense Aristotle¹ defines as chance, in the human domain, the event which was not the end of the action but which, had it been foreseen, could have been or ought to have been the end of the action, whether in order to bring it about or in order to avoid it. Matter in particular, grasped only according to its universal properties, stands over against thought as a foreign and even an uncanny realm. But thought must enter into it and overpower it; yet in this struggle chance ⟦216⟧ inevitably plays its part, now favouring, now disturbing. The artist chisels his statue from the most delicate marble, and suddenly a vein appears which destroys the perfection of his work. The general sees through the great game of chess that is war; what he can do, what the enemy must do, lies clearly before his eyes. His difficult plan has been drawn up, in that he had to take the correct tactics of his opponent into his calculation as an element. But this element is variable. His enemy plays wrongly, and this chance, which he exploits, brings him victory. In this way, in the sphere of the end, chance stands outside necessity and must therefore be declared an outlaw by science. Now it humbles proud thought, like an irony; now it raises sinking courage, like a divine sign.

[[622]] When the end realizes and maintains itself in nature, it appears as natural law, and therefore within the end too the contingent in the first meaning can recur. The animal, destined for motion, must see; but here there is a broad possibility as to how it sees.

[[623]] Thus the contingent presents itself as a relative concept. What, measured against an isolated necessity, be it a law or be it an end, does not proceed from that necessity but is handed over to a foreign determination, is called contingent. Chance is governed by something other. But this foreign element can be necessary in itself. The contingent therefore appears only from the standpoint of a necessity restricted to a part, and it vanishes in the same measure as knowing advances and raises the necessity of the individual to the necessity of the whole. The contingent is therefore in science always only a transition and the impulse to further inquiry.¹

[[626]] Freedom and chance both carry with them the common mark of that which can also be otherwise, and are therefore indeed both comprehended under the common expression contingens. To that extent they seem akin. And yet in their proper sense they are opposed. Freedom, when it follows its vocation, seeks to unite itself with reason and to that extent with necessity; but chance, so long as it is called chance, remains outside necessity. Only when freedom is without necessity does it become, like caprice, equal to chance. It can indeed happen that chance apes freedom. Epicurus, for example, introduces chance into his principles, the chance deviation of the falling atoms from the straight line, in order thereby to make freedom possible, which Lucretius expressly designates as *one* motive.¹ In political institutions, as in popular elections and in majority decisions, we desire freedom and not seldom embrace chance. But the concepts of the two remain distinct.

[[630]] 16. Finally we compare the contingent and the possible. The two concepts are akin, but a different view prevails in each. The possible prepares the necessary, as knowledge of the part prepares the whole of the conditions. The contingent arises only when measured by full necessity. In the possible the mind looks ahead, supplying the missing conditions for representation; in the ⟦219⟧ contingent it is blind and surrenders itself to a foreign power. In the possible the lack of the existing conditions is cancelled in the mind, and thus the real is surpassed by the ideal. In the contingent, conversely, the impotence of necessity appears, and the ideal is overtaken by the real. There is a reason of the possible, as there is a reason of poetry and of the ideal, but no reason of chance, as there is no reason of a lottery ticket or of stumbling. Since in the possible conditions are lacking to the whole from which necessity would spring, and since these missing conditions are in the possible abandoned to a foreign determination, one can determine the relation of possibility to necessity by means of chance, in that in the possible the necessary is still alloyed with the contingent. The possible is contingent not in what is known in it but rather in what is not known in it. The contingent already ceases to be contingent and already inclines toward future necessity when it appears as possible in the sense that it is expected from known conditions.

[[632]] 17. The modal concepts of the possible, the actual and the necessary have been conceived in opposite ways by Kant and Hegel.

[[633]] Kant¹ compares modality with the other categories, of quantity, quality and relation, and finds its distinguishing character in this, that the modal determinations do not in the least augment the concept to which they are attached as predicates, considered as a determination of the object, but only express its relation to the faculty of knowledge. Besides magnitude, quality and relation there is nothing further that makes up the content of a judgment, and modality concerns only the value of the copula in relation to thinking in general. If the concept of a thing is already quite complete, it ⟦220⟧ can still be asked of this object whether it is merely possible or also actual, or, if it were the latter, whether it is even necessary as well. No further determinations in the object are thereby thought; rather the objects are only incorporated into the understanding by degrees, so that these three stages of modality are so many moments of thinking in general. According to this view, the concepts of the possible, the actual and the necessary express only a slacker or stricter bond of the judgment, only a lower or higher stage of thinking, and the content of knowledge has remained unchanged, neither diminished nor augmented. Thus Kant lets these concepts be wholly absorbed into a subjective relation.

[[635]] Hegel¹ proceeded conversely. Hegel places these concepts before the subjective concept and lets them issue in the dialectic from purely objective elements. For as pure thought generates the determinations of being out of itself, actuality has resulted as the immediacy in which inner and outer are in and for themselves identical. What lies in it, it must develop. As identity in general it is first possibility, reflection-into-self, which, over against the concrete unity of the actual, is posited as the abstract and unessential essentiality. Possibility is what is essential to actuality, and it is at the same time only possibility. Accordingly, first,² the inner is one-sidedly driven forth and held fast out of the unity of inner and outer that has come to be; it is taken as the possible, and since it is thus without external being, it is what is essential to actuality; but because essence itself is only a moment and has no truth without being, possibility is only possibility.

[[638]] ⟦221⟧ But the actual, in its difference from possibility as reflection-into-self, is itself only the external concrete, the unessential immediate. If in possibility the inner was detached from the identity of inner and outer, here the outer is separated off. The actual thereby forfeits essence and becomes a mere outer. In this value of a mere possibility or unessential actuality it is something contingent, and possibility is mere chance itself. The truly actual is something absolute in itself. Since it takes inner and outer together into a solid unity, the modes of the absolutely actual are possibility and contingency. The externality of actuality consists more precisely in this, that it is as mediation, possibility of an other, condition. "This externality, thus developed, is, as this circle of determinations, first of all real possibility in general. As such a circle it is further the totality as content, thus the matter determined in and for itself, and likewise, according to the difference of the determinations in this unity, the concrete totality of the form for itself, the immediate self-translation of the inner into the outer and of the outer into the inner. This self-moving of the form is activity, activation of the matter as the real ground which sublates itself into actuality, and activation of contingent actuality, of the conditions, their reflection-into-self and their self-sublation into another actuality, the actuality of the matter. When all the conditions are present, the matter must become actual, and the matter is itself one of the conditions, for as inner it is at first itself only something presupposed. This developed actuality, as the alternation of inner and outer coinciding in one, the alternation of their opposed motions which are united into *one* motion, is necessity."

[[639]] Hegel thus conceives necessity as ⟦222⟧ developed actuality, so that the moments of inner and outer, which at first are only in themselves, set forth as possibility and contingency, now move into one another. Into these determinations subjective thought nowhere shines. Possibility and necessity lie in the thing and in its motions. Of those degrees of knowledge from which, according to Kant, these concepts arise, no trace has remained, and they rest no less in themselves and in the thing than, say, the preceding concepts of quantity, intensity, measure, content and form, etc. The subjective concept is treated only later. Hegel does say, to be sure, in a passage in which he examines Kant's determination, that possibility is in fact at first the empty abstraction of reflection-into-self, so that it belongs only to subjective thinking. Actuality and necessity, on the other hand, are anything but a mere mode for an other, rather precisely the opposite. But this concession regarding possibility cannot be meant in earnest in a logic in which with every moment "thinking determines itself to being," and in which therefore everything, and especially everything on this side of the subjective concept, must be held to be objective. There is also an express declaration on this point. It is even said in the subjective logic, in the determination of judgment:² judgment is commonly taken in a subjective sense, as an operation and form occurring merely in self-conscious thinking; but this distinction does not yet exist at all in the logical. According to the whole standpoint, subjective thinking, as Hegel puts it, merely looks on at how the matter makes itself; it observes but does not intervene, and it is not subjective but real relations that are ⟦223⟧ produced. The possible is explained by the inner thrown back into itself. But is not the inner, which has issued from the relation of force to its expression, thoroughly something that must belong to the thing?

[[642]] Thus Kant's and Hegel's views of the modal concepts stand directly opposed. What the one throws entirely into subjective thinking, the other throws entirely into the thing.

[[643]] Kant evidently took into consideration only the result as it appears in the form of the modal judgment. The expression of the possible, the actual and the necessary then falls to the bond of the judgment, not to the concepts connected. The content of subject and predicate remains the same. If we take, e.g., the judgment "the earth is spheroidal," neither the concept earth nor the concept spheroidal seems to be altered, whether one connects them by "is" or "can be" or "must be." But the matter looks different when it is apprehended not merely at the external point at which the process ends, but in the genesis and connection of the whole. Then possibility points back to a part of the conditions, necessity to the whole of them, and knowledge has been augmented by this living relation to the ground of the thing; and precisely herein all the wealth and all the depth of knowledge is contained. As the ground lies in the midst of the thing, so possibility and necessity cannot be ascribed merely to thinking.

[[644]] Hegel brought out this objective meaning of the modal concepts, but at the same time let it alone be valid. Has he succeeded in this way in freeing them from their relation to subjective thinking as from an imposed bond? The derivation rests on a concept common to the whole sphere of essence, reflection-into-self. When the inner reflects itself into itself, possibility arises; when ⟦224⟧ the outer reflects itself into itself, contingency. But how can the inner or the outer thus throw itself back upon itself? The unity of force and expression, of inner and outer, has been developed by Hegel in what precedes. This identity constitutes actuality. It cannot be said whence what has grown together should suddenly separate and the individual elements confine themselves to themselves, unless it be in the process of human thinking. Reflection-into-self, conceived after the image of a physical activity of light, conceals in its objective expression the isolating power of thinking. The inner or the outer, the possible or the externally actual, does not reflect itself into itself but is only reflected by thinking. The objective name is a semblance. Inner and outer are distinguished in the efficient cause only by a measure of subjective thinking. As observation sharpens and the field of intuition widens, the inner decreases and the outer increases. Only in the end does an inner really stand over against the outer: the thing projected in the mind, which strives out of it, over against the thing externally realized. Only in the end can it be said that the thing is at first presupposed only as inner, yet as presupposed helps to ground its realization. Only in the end, in which thought hastens ahead, does this take place. In the blind conditions of the efficient cause, which drives forward, nothing future is presupposed. The consideration of the end has been tacitly anticipated by Hegel at this point, and this anticipation betrays the subjective relation of the modal concept. If, finally, developed actuality is to become necessity, in that the alternation of inner and outer coincides in one and the alternations of their opposed motions are united into *one* motion, then what is described here is only the process that takes place in every occurrence, a mere fact, not yet necessity. Only when it is penetrated by thinking is it ⟦225⟧ recognized in this value.¹ Thus in the determinations themselves the clear traces of subjective thinking shining into them reveal themselves.
