[[644]] Since Kant and Hegel determine the modal concepts in two opposite directions, both are one-sided. Possibility and necessity can be understood neither merely from the stage of thinking nor merely from the relations of the matter. They are a peculiar double formation, in which both elements mingle, partly complementing, partly interpenetrating one another.

[[645]] 18. The double formation is easily recognised. The objective element lies in the conditions of the matter, out of which possibility and necessity arise as out of their material. But when a part of the conditions, or all of them, are taken together, then the foresight in possibility and the closing of the whole in necessity is a deed of subjective thinking. The formal character of necessity confirms this. If that is necessary which cannot be otherwise, then in every case only subjective thinking can attempt and test whether something could be otherwise.

[[646]] The two elements, grown together in this double formation, ⟦226⟧ can alternately predominate in the way it is conceived. At one time modality is taken really, and then again logically. In the first case everything is placed in the matter. The matter either contains only a part of the conditions for another, or encloses all of them. In the second case the act of subjective thinking, the judgment, is the object of possibility or necessity. The thought either contains a part of the conditions for a judgment, or encloses all of them. Kant evidently has this latter phenomenon before his eyes, since, although it too goes back to connections in the matter, it represents in the first instance only the judgment ripening or ripened within itself. Language, with its fine sense, expresses this logical modality preferably through the grammatical mood, and that real modality through peculiar concept-words of possibility or necessity. It can even happen that the two conceptions interweave; for real possibility or necessity can be more or less grounded subjectively and can therefore claim a different stage of the logical. Real possibility (e.g. a tree can come from the seed) is usually expressed as an assertoric judgment. The double formation is already present in this. For subjective thinking reaches out of the present, which still conceals the matter and reveals only a part of the conditions, into the future. But when this judgment is either only in the process of becoming or is already conceived in its completion, then the subjective elements multiply, and in the grammatical expression they are indicated by added particles (such as perhaps, necessarily, etc.) or by relations of mood. The "perhaps" springs from a reservation, because the ground of knowledge is not yet full.

[[647]] > (1) Hegel's derivation occasions yet another comparison. In §. 148, condition, matter, and activity are expressly designated as the three moments of necessity, so that activity translates the more passive conditions into the predetermined matter. The distinction of these three moments, however, cannot be held fast. The matter is only the result of necessity, and is thought in advance and presupposed only in the necessity of the end. But what the activity is, and what the passive conditions are that are used by the activity as a material, cannot be determined in the particular case, since no condition is purely passive and no activity purely active. The activity, named only after what predominates, is rather only one of the conditions. We may therefore say in general: when the conditions are fulfilled and are recognised in the whole that they form, necessity stands there.

[[648]] 19. If we look back to the point from which we set out in considering the modal categories, they are all determined by it. To the perceived appearance there corresponds the actual as fact, and from the ground and in the ground the rest arise. ⟦227⟧ If, in the ground as the sum of the conditions, conditions are lacking, then the possible results for the judging mind. If, on the other hand, the conditions which the ground contains are gathered together into a whole, then the necessary results; in its negative shape, the impossible; together with it, partly as the origin of the necessary, partly as its expression, the universal; as its presentation in appearance, the identical; and, as the opposite of all these concepts, the contingent.

[[649]] # ⟦228⟧ XIV. CONCEPT AND JUDGMENT.

[[650]] 1. Without an activity which thinking and being share with each other, it was impossible to understand either how thinking, by copying, comprehends given objects, or how, by prefiguring, it projects things. Neither the a priori of mathematics nor the a posteriori of experience, neither the architectonic power of the end nor the will in the ethical sphere, could be understood without such a common activity. Once it had been found, its objective side was pursued first. The fundamental concepts were set forth which, valid in the same way for thinking and being, flowed from this living source. In the investigation there finally emerged concepts which had arisen not from the development of the activity by itself, whether in being or in thinking, but from the relation of comprehending thought to its objects. Thus negation presented itself, in its unmixed shape, as a purely logical concept, yet always fixed by the opposition of a real point. The universal showed its proper roots in the community of thinking and being, and appeared in a strict meaning only where the actual is comprehended or the concept realised. The possible and the necessary, finally, revealed a double ⟦229⟧ formation, in which the objective element of the ground grew together in a peculiar way with the limited or complete knowledge of it. Hence the last concepts appeared properly only in the judgment, and indeed in such a way that they do not touch the content immediately, but only loosen or tighten the bond of the relation, the copula. In general, the necessary which we have considered, if it is indeed the ripe fruit of knowing, presupposes the ripening process through which it came to be, in all its stages, and it is now necessary to set this forth.

[[651]] In this way the matter leads us further. So far it has been shown how knowing is possible, i.e. how thinking can penetrate into things, and in the process the mediating fundamental concepts have been sketched. The question now is in what peculiar forms thinking solves the real task whose possibility has so far been demonstrated. This will make clear how the fundamental concepts, derived only one by one, receive relation and life in their application. The fundamental relation must be found here again. For since the possibility of knowing proceeds from an activity which belongs in common to thinking and being, the forms of thinking and its connections must also correspond to the forms of being and its connections. In this parallelism of form will be mirrored that agreement of the subjective and the objective at which thinking aims in its content.

[[652]] 2. Since motion, the principle of our consideration, underlies thinking and being in the same way, intuited being can thereby be thought and, conversely, what is thought can be intuited. Motion as the living ground of thinking has the character of universality, while the motion of being is bound and thereby individualised. Hence all forms of thinking bear universality within them as their pervading fundamental trait. The individual, when it is thought, becomes a universal, and we grasp the concept of the ⟦230⟧ individual itself through the universal, by producing and delimiting it with that universal activity. If in language the universal concept is transposed back into individuality, the relation through which this happens is again a universal one. The here and now, the this and that, is the universal form of individualisation, and what they designate as individual, by attaching it to the individual point of the thinker, is thought only through the universal. In the judgment "this silver is white," everything is universal in so far as it is thought, and only an individual (this silver) in so far as it is referred to the presence of the speaker; yet the form of this relation is again universal. The individual is in itself what is incommensurable with thinking; but the perception of the senses or the creation of imagination, through which we represent it, is possible only through the first deed common to thinking and being. In this way all forms of thinking must be universal. If, therefore, the forms of thinking and of being will stand over against each other as universal and individual, this opposition does not cancel their agreement.

[[653]] 3. We look back on the development we have traversed. The activity of generating motion was the first, and from it sprang the image of a closed whole, of a substance; but the substances, to which motion adheres without being extinguished in them, became causal in their properties. Everything finished, everything assumed as finished, appeared as an error of the dull understanding, which, occupied with fixing, will recognise only fixed substance as the first. Rest can be comprehended through motion, in that the directions hold each other in equilibrium, but not motion through rest; where this is attempted, contradiction is there. The determining end, which forms the resting centre of the higher shapes and makes a whole into a whole from within, is again directing, delimiting motion.

[[654]] ⟦231⟧ Activity and substance are the forms of being. Which are the forms that correspond in thinking to the fundamental relation thus designated?

[[655]] If in general what lies nearest to us is not the original but the fullness poured out of it, then in our conception the thing with its activities will outweigh that first activity which also generates the thing. And so it actually happens. We judge when we think, and in every complete judgment we distinguish subject and predicate, the former representing the substance, the latter its activity, or the property which bears within it the fundamental concept of activity.

[[656]] From this differentiated form we are driven back towards a unity. We find it where the activity alone forms the judgment. In language this presents itself in the so-called impersonal verbs, e.g. it roars, it lightens, it freezes, etc. This activity is conceived for the moment, and relatively, as an original one; for the judgment does not state whence it comes.¹ In these judgments we must seek the germ of further formation. As the activities become fixed in substances, these in turn come alive in new activities. From the judgments which present only an activity, or which grasp being and activity in one, there arise concepts which ground new judgments.

[[657]] But may the subjectless judgment (e.g. it roars, it hisses) already be regarded as a judgment? If one takes only the more complete form of judgment (e.g. the water roars) as the standard, one will resist this. Yet even in a judgment of this kind the predicate, which presents the activity, is the principal concept, just as the predominant stress makes the predicate ⟦232⟧ the living soul of the sentence. We think in predicates. In the origin this principal concept appears alone, until reflection begins the derivation and brings things and activities into connection. A full act of knowing is here the first, not a half act, not a dead element, as is the case when the finished concepts are regarded as the first and their composition in the judgment as the second.

[[659]] This, however, is what usually happens. Either the judgment is called the concept particularising itself, and a judgment without the concept of the subject is held to be no judgment. Or the judgment is derived in such a way that a pair of concepts meet in thinking and the question is whether they will enter into a connection or not. The complete judgment later conceives the concept, in the finite too, as the source of an activity, and may then, despite the generalisation in the predicate, be called the concept particularising itself.

But whence does this come? Perhaps the concepts are only the images transmitted by the senses. By no means; for concepts are born of the universal. The predicate as predicate still bears the mark of the dependent; it becomes a free concept only when it assumes the form of substance and in this form can become subject. This transformation is accomplished by creative imagination, which goes along even with isolating abstraction. Activities are represented as things, abstracta as substances. Language shows this transformation especially in the infinitive.

[[664]] Substance, says Spinoza, is that which is in itself and is conceived through itself. The concept is here the measure of substance; substance, however, is God. In Hegel the concept has taken the place of substance, and the concept is God, the concept the truth of substance. As here, in the heightening of usage, substance and concept, as the corresponding individual and universal, relieve one another, so in a subordinate sense they run parallel. Only by standing over against each other do they confirm each other. Every substance receives the measure and warrant of its independence and of its significance in the ground of the concept; every concept receives the realm of its power in substance. Every substance seeks its spirit in the concept, every concept its body in substance.

[[665]] In a similar way the logical judgment always refers to a real activity or to the activity of a substance, and it cannot be comprehended without this counterpart in the actual. Attempts have often been made to define the judgment purely logically, keeping within the world of concepts; but such a definition does not suffice. The judgment is called, for instance, a connection of concepts. This determination, however, embraces too much. According to their grammatical expression, concepts can be connected predicatively (the tree blooms), attributively (the blooming tree), and objectively (blooms ⟦234⟧ splendidly). The judgment as judgment shows itself only in the first way. Hence a further distinction has been drawn between the result of the connection (the blooming tree) and the act itself (the tree blooms), and the judgment has been called this act of connecting. But this expedient too is insufficient. For the act in which thinking connects concepts is momentary; the act of the matter expressed in the judgment can be lasting. It is this act of the matter, which the mind grasps, that matters first; the subjective connection of concepts follows from it. What a thing does is judged of its concept. In this brief expression we sum up the fundamental thought of the judgment. For every property that can be predicated goes back to the concept of activity, and relations which can enter into the predicate depend on activities.

[[666]] The concept arises from the first judgment of mere activity in a way similar to that in which substance arises from shaping activity; and as, further, substance expresses itself in activity, so the subject comes alive in the predicate, the concept in the judgment.

[[667]] A simple example may illustrate this. Language conceives the sentence "it lightens," according to its form, as a judgment of an original activity. This activity becomes substance in the concept lightning, and the substance expresses itself in properties. The concept reveals itself in the predicate, e.g. the lightning shines, zigzags, etc. So it always is originally; only we seldom derive from first activities, but mostly from the activity of subjects.

[[668]] Gruppe¹ has shown that a judgment underlies every concept, and that the judgment is therefore wrongly treated according to the concept and from the concept. His evidence is taken chiefly from language. If at the later stage the names of ⟦235⟧ substances seem to designate something immediate, so that the substantives, that is, the resting concepts, would be the first, they often show, when returned to their origin, a subtle judgment that preceded them. If, for example, according to etymological researches in Indian or German, the cloud properly means the lightening one, the earth the bearing one, the hand the making or catching one, etc., then the judgment "it lightens, it bears, it catches," etc., runs ahead of the finished concept. In compounds the earlier judgment is still recognisable at present, just as everywhere the predicative relation of syntax precedes the attributive as its ground; and very great is the mass of those words which at first sight appear simple, but are analysed by the penetrating research of grammarians and thereby traced back to compounds.

Themselves formless and, as it were, free, they appear only in bound form. The energy of the verb brings what is thought into relation to actuality. The activity can be conceived by itself, as we still see in subjectless sentences, but the thing only through the activity. Hence the beginnings of language will lie in verbs, but in such a way that they form a judgment by themselves. Even the development of language in the child cannot be adduced as an analogy. Are the child's first words only names? To be sure, they appear isolated. But they are already a sentence. Children, with a fine sense, utter as the representative of the whole sentence that word on which, even in the articulated period, the predominant stress would fall as on the principal concept of the whole. Thus they bring out the predicate or the object or the attribute, according as the one or the other would form the goal of the sentence. They utter only this *one* word, but the judgment is nevertheless ⟦237⟧ complete. What is lacking to the judgment in linguistic expression is supplied by soulful intonation or lively gesture. The tone of astonishment designates the judgment of actuality, the hurrying urgency in the tone, desire. The unity of the thought, the judgment, is always there. As it is first the activity of the external world that strikes the mind of man, or his own activity that reaches over into it, so the counterpart of activity must necessarily also be the first in language.

[[677]] # ⟦239⟧ XV. THE CONCEPT.

[[678]] It is not our intention to carry out completely the doctrine of the concept and of the judgment, of the syllogism and of proof, since then much would have to be repeated that is adequately treated in the expositions of logic; only those points are to be brought out which are either doubtful or may be fruitful for what follows.

[[679]] 1. Is it then correct that the concept corresponds in a logical way to real substance? Would there, then, be no concept of activities? This question has already been answered in general above. The concept remains the ⟦240⟧ substantial form of a mental content. But it is the essence of a substance that it be relatively independent, closed within itself as a whole, and the source of accidents. Activity, to be sure, is in another or from another, and to that extent stands over against substance. But since the finite thing too is not absolutely independent, the opposition is not fixed. The finite substance persists as a whole in space, while activity detaches itself from it, as it were, and either passes fleetingly through space or even appears only in time. But as substances are separated in space, so activities are separated in time, and the mind closes them off into a whole in this element. Finally, no activity is so poor that other activities should not stand to it as accidents to substance. The more generating power it has, the more it differentiates itself or excites something else, the more is it, like substance, a source of something else. In these three points lies the possibility that activity can assume the substantial form of the concept. Activity has become a thing when there is talk of its concept. If the concept of the logarithm, which in itself is only an active relation, is asked for, it has become an object within the system. In a similar sense the concept of form can be sought when it, as the shaping element, enters into one series with the substantial ground. When there is talk of the concept of decided activities, e.g. of remembering or counting or breathing, these functions are considered by themselves and, as it were, lifted out of their soil like wholes of their own.

In the latter work an attempt is made to show by examples how the Aristotelian laws still govern the sciences today. Aristotle in his Organon is the Euclid of logic.

[[681]] 2. If we take the concept as the universal conception of substance, just as we tried to show above that the character of thinking in general is universality, a significant objection arises. "Concepts cannot be characterised as universal representations. ⟦241⟧ Universality, to be sure, always belongs only to concepts, but not all concepts are universal."

[[682]] What is at issue here is the determination of the universal. Certainly the number of instances does not in the first place concern the concept, and the concept remains a concept whether it is realised in *one* case or in countless cases. If, then, one wishes to take the universal only as what is common to a number, one can rightly say that not all concepts are universal. Otherwise one would have to assert that history or the work of art is excluded from the concept. The stamp of the whole of history is individual, and phenomena such as, for example, Hellenism stand out so creatively and originally that they can no more arise twice than can the individual world-historical characters. Are such phenomena, then, the deepest we perceive at all, to pass by without concept or uncomprehended? Impossible; for what is without concept is without rights for science, like chance. But if we ask how the work of art that stands unique, how the character in history that towers above the common multitude, is comprehended, it happens through the universal. From the universal we grasp the idea of the work of art, from the universal the treatment of the material; down to the smallest trait we individualise only from the universal; and when the whole strikes our mind and magically excites it to re-create, or binds it in a peculiar mood, this communication is evidence of the universal in the work of art. Without the universal this multiplication in the souls of the beholders would not be possible. What is great in history is always the organ of a development; but the end appearing in the organ, the divine will, is a "synthetic universal." How it is seized, how it is ⟦242⟧ impressed upon the hard world, how it suffers a reaction from that side, this is the individual; but whence do we understand it? Only from the universal elements. Only from a common point, from the universality of human conditions, from the power common in its germ, from imagination, which mobile creates the individual out of the universal. In this sense we must assert that even the concept of the most individual is universal, and may not exclude even the concept of God from this law. What is comprehensible, and so far as it is comprehensible, is comprehensible from the universal, i.e. ultimately from the principles which thinking and being possess in common. What we understand of matter we understand not in so far as properties repeat themselves in matter and in this sense are called universal, but in so far as these, accessible first to the senses, are grasped by thinking through constructive motion and thereby, homogeneous with thinking, present an inner universality. If, then, we say that the concept is always universal, this universal refers not to a multitude of things comprised under it, but only to its origin, as it were to the stuff of which it is woven. A contract for *one* legal transaction, say for a joint enterprise, is an example taken from life of a concept of an individual thing. This *one* legal relation is usually stated in the first paragraph of the contract in its abiding fundamental determinations, within which the particular can vary according to circumstances. In disputes this declaration has in law a force similar to that which the definition has elsewhere, e.g. in criminal law. But the nature of these determinations, made for an individual object, is drawn from the universal, in which alone wills can agree upon a contract at all. Thus the concept is universal in its inner nature, even if it does not refer to an abundance of homogeneous objects.

[[684]] ⟦243⟧ 3. If, further, the concept is universally conceived substance, what becomes of the pure concept? The pure concept is the doctrine of the most recent philosophy. As everything sensuous is extinguished, the concept is supposed to grasp itself and develop itself. We refuted it above from its own premises. Here it may be asked once more, in the context of the positive investigations, how such a doctrine stands in relation to them.

[[685]] Thought can neither stir nor move without that motion which is the counterpart of spatial motion. In motion thinking translates itself into intuition, and the abstract concept has in it immediately a sensuous form. The end fuses with this form, since it already presupposes elements which it is to order and determine; and it permeates in a peculiar way the categories projected from motion. In these concepts, projected from the very deed of thinking, intuition is the condition of life.

[[686]] If concepts spring from the received perceptions of the senses, it is no different. The image lives on in the representation; for this is the common image of individual groups; and the common image still gives freshness to the concept when the representation, determined by a law or having become fixed in constant marks, rises to the concept.¹ The representation as a common image seems to suffer from an inner contradiction; for an image is essentially individual; ⟦244⟧ how can the individual stand for the genus? And yet this happens scientifically in the figures of geometry with the most fruitful success, and unconsciously in every stirring of the mind. Or is it perhaps only the indeterminate drawing, marked in a few fundamental features, so that on the whole the outlines stand there, but in the particulars a free play-space is left for the supplementing imagination? This comparison can certainly be made. But the inner common image is no resting drawing pinned down. Within the fundamental strokes that form its limits it is, as it were, elastic, and the motion that dwells in it solves the riddle. The concept, abstract as against the fullness of the sensuous, must retain within itself the principle of the sensuous; for in order to be applied, it must be able instantaneously to expand out of the contraction of the smallest space, in which it holds its power compressed, into the most manifold configuration, into the infinite breadth of species and individuals. Otherwise it would lack application, and with that its dominion would be at an end. However much the concept may fold itself together, this point of attachment must always remain in it, this principle of transition; and this is none other than the accompanying common image, the image "displaceable" according to the particular motives, or the motion that projects according to the law of the concept. In the algebraic formula which gives the concept of a line (e.g. in the formula of the parabola y² = px), the elements of the expression, letter and number, are imageless and abstract; but in fact they are intuitions. The equation states how *one* constant magnitude (p) binds the development of the variable ones. The magnitudes are lines, multiplication yields areas, etc. Thus the abstract expression contains intuition within itself and comes alive only through the intuition of constructive motion. The concept lies only apparently beyond intuition. After all this, the pure, imageless concept thrives nowhere. Rather, ⟦245⟧ the true concept resembles the reduced but brightened image of the converging lens.

[[688]] The greatness of the concept lies in the consciousness of the universal and in the freedom of its productions. In every point of the motion, in every projection of spatial shape, in every temporal activity, in every determination of matter that proceeds from the concept, there lives the consciousness referred to unity. In the same principle which, blind by itself, forms the appearances of being, there is a mental presence which holds fast the past and draws the future to it into *one* whole. This deed of the concept, which pervades time and, as it were, conquers transience, has given it the semblance of a timeless eternity. But with such a representation the gulf between concept and intuition would be fixed anew, only in another way.

[[689]] 4. Let the concept, then, be universally grasped substance. From the conception of substance springs the content, from the determination of the universal the extension.

[[690]] Substance, separated out from generating motion, is independent in itself and active from itself. The conception of this delimitation gives the content of the concept, in that the so-called marks, as they are commonly named, represent in the concept the essence and the properties of the thing. In its content the concept reveals the object thought, separated out by itself. What in intuition is the individualised thing, manifold in itself, is in the concept the universal, comprehensive content. Universality in that original meaning in which thinking and being become identical distinguishes the content of the concept as such from the object of intuition.

[[691]] Universality goes further. In being thought, the thing ceases to be an individual, since it is raised into that creative element which itself produces space and time and is therefore bound to no individual point of space ⟦246⟧ and time. Hence there lies in every concept the possibility that it may refer to several appearances comprised under it. Number does not fetter it. This relation to the appearances it governs is called the extension of the concept. It has indeed been shown above that the individual and singular too can be comprehended, and that there can therefore be a concept even of the most singular; but in this case too one will distinguish extension from content. The extension is then nothing but the unity of the appearance.

[[692]] The content of the concept, taken in the highest sense, is the law which, as a universal, governs the appearance; for thought first completes itself in necessity. As substance as such rests upon a peculiar mode of origination, so the concept must present this as its law. The concept accordingly seeks to express the procedure of generation, the mode of action of determination. In mathematics the mind has itself exercised it and can therefore find it again there. It stands similarly in the ethical sphere. In nature it frees it, in order to know it, from the manifold entanglement in which it loses itself. All conceptions of a concept which do not yet contain this law must nevertheless take the path toward this goal. Extension, on the other hand, whether it comprises the individuals immediately or the species first, is directed toward the side of appearances. Content and extension are therefore related, taken in their completion, as law and appearances. As the law is a law only in so far as it exercises its power in the realm of appearance, so content and extension of the concept are most intimately linked; and from this view one understands why content has been called the intensive, extension the extensive aspect of the concept.

[[693]] In formal logic the usual distinction is that the marks of a concept form its content, while those concepts of which it is itself ⟦247⟧ a mark form its extension. This determination is correct, but it appears as an arbitrary assumption and could in any case be explained only in the doctrine of the judgment; for according to it the predicates of the categorical judgment would form the content of the subject, and the subjects the extension of the predicate.¹

[[694]] The distinction of content and extension hardly needs an example, since what mattered was only the derivation. The concept of the parallelogram has as its content the determination that it is a plane four-sided figure enclosed by parallels; as its extension, on the other hand, the species: square, rectangle, rhombus, rhomboid.

[[695]] 5. Definition (the explanation of the concept) determines the content of the concept, division orders its extension; the former is the compressed expression of the essence, the latter the methodical survey of the appearances. Both belong together and complement each other, the former presenting what is homogeneous in what is distinguished, the latter what is distinguished in what is homogeneous. Plato already demanded of science, with equal force, both: the bringing together of the many into the one, and the lawful division of the one into the many; and whoever wishes to guard his understanding against one-sidedness and to school his head scientifically must practise both. Definition and division, both of far-reaching significance, are to be regarded as inventions of the scientific mind, the former in order to keep the essence present in the motley appearances, the latter in order to survey, according to points of view, the mass confused by its very fullness, or to see in it the law advancing from the universal into the particular. Neither definition with its measured expression nor division with its striving ⟦248⟧ for an easily grasped survey disowns the subjective interest of our human limitation; but both nevertheless have their objective goal, definition in the law of the matter, division in the dominion of that law in particularisation. If one wishes to behold in their scientific greatness and power definition, which Socrates first coined, and division, which Plato compared with the spark of Prometheus, as a flash of light, so it seems, that separates for the eye what is undistinguished, then let one turn to the definitions in Euclid, in Spinoza, or in precise jurists, and to the divisions in the systems of the descriptive natural sciences, e.g. to Linnaeus, who, from 8000 plants whose characters he examined, acutely designed a system still suited today to take up precisely into itself the number of plants, which has since grown incredibly.¹

[[698]] On the theory we remark only the following.

[[699]] 6. To the concept there corresponds an independent object which is comprehended. But in the sphere of the finite, of whose knowledge we are treating, everything that presents itself as independent is independent only conditionally and relatively. The individual points beyond itself, and observation itself exhibits the dependence. The plant, for example, closed within itself as an organism, can be regarded as an independent whole; but it drives its roots into the soil, needs a higher or lower position of the sun, breathes the air, etc. To this dependence of things there necessarily corresponds a relativity of concepts. Like substances, concepts too demand a complement – a ⟦249⟧ wide field for the semblance of dialectic. Since the interaction in which the life of things rests must present itself in their concept, if the concept is indeed to be true, there necessarily arises that relativity of concepts which, since the time of the ancient sceptics, has been turned in hostility against their truth. If we now compare the modes and classes of the sceptical arguments, as Sextus Empiricus, for example, has preserved them for us from the doctrine of Pyrrho, and as they have later been renewed again and again only in altered form, they all come back to the interaction of things, to their active relation to the knower or to the other things. This relation is then so conceived as if the concepts, being absorbed in it, had only the semblance and shadow of a substance. This relativity, however, in which only the reciprocal activities of things are reflected, must make up an essential constituent of the individual concept, if the latter is to be true at all. The individual is only a member, and therefore has its essence and life precisely in relation. Accordingly every concept points beyond itself to the whole of concepts, which, unconditioned in itself, conditions and bears it. If this necessary relativity seems to endanger the firm hold of concepts, it also in turn preserves them from stiffening in closed isolation.

[[701]] 7. "In every composite concept one can think away, abstract, each individual mark. The concept which then still remains is called, in relation to that from which it arose by abstraction of a mark, the next higher. Every concept therefore has as many next higher concepts as marks. If one ascends in a similar way, by abstraction from these next higher concepts, to their next higher ones, one obtains, in relation to the concept first given, higher concepts of the second order, and in a similar way of the third, fourth order, and so on. Every concept stands to all its higher concepts in the relation of ⟦250⟧ subordination." This is how formal logic conceives the relation of the marks. The marks are summed or multiplied, and can therefore also be separated like summands or factors in any order whatever. Then the last remainder, or the factor left over last, is called the highest concept, and, isolated and impoverished in itself, is nevertheless supposed to produce the greatest extension; for the smaller the content, the greater the extension.

[[702]] This last relation is utterly incomprehensible if one makes the breadth of the extension depend on the power of the content. Should not the power be greater when the concept grows inwardly in richness and capacity? This is not the case. Formal logic derives the greater extension with smaller content not from the inner significance of the concept but rather from its growing indeterminacy. The concept with fewer marks is less determinate and therefore leaves a greater breadth. This view rests more on the defect than on the merit of the universal.

[[703]] But are the marks of concepts really so indifferent to one another, do they stand on *one* line in such a way that it is all the same which one abstracts first? What, then, does the expression subordination of concepts still mean?

[[704]] Individual examples may instruct us first. If one determines the square as a rectangular, equilateral plane figure enclosed by parallels, one cannot arbitrarily take rectangular or equilateral as the highest concept. For these are nothing without the presupposition of the figure in which they are thought. If one has before one the formula of one of the coordinate curves, e.g. of the conic sections, it is hardly a matter of indifference which marks (i.e. which parts of the formula) one leaves out in order to find the universal expression, i.e. the higher concept. Over all of them only *one* formula is superordinate, the universal expression of the curves ⟦251⟧ of the second degree. It is indeed contained in each of them, but it is found only by knowing how to distinguish, from the nature of the matter (according to the universal form of the equation of the second degree), the original and abiding element from the supervening elements, which can be posited as indifferent. As in this case the marks show themselves grown together and separable only in *one* direction, so it is in all cases. We choose an arbitrary example from another sphere. The Solanaceae are, according to the determination of the botanical system, plants with five stamens, one style, a wheel-shaped corolla, mostly alternate leaves, etc. Can one separate arbitrarily here? The concept flower, plant, exists by itself and can therefore be detached by itself. The marks, however, flowering with five stamens, with *one* style, exhibiting a wheel-shaped corolla, etc., hover in the air by themselves and demand a substance (flower, plant) to which they can adhere. In conceiving them in the neuter (what flowers with five stamens), one already secretly substantiates them.

[[705]] Thus in the simple consideration of concepts we distinguish that part of the marks which presents in itself what is relatively substantial, and that which presents what is dependent, yet determines the substance. The former makes up the genus (genus proximum), the latter, demanding the genus as the basis of its subsistence, the peculiar determination, the species-forming difference (differentia specifica).

[[706]] Since one part of the marks gathers itself together into the genus by maintaining within itself what is conceived relatively as the substantial whole, it thereby already forms the more important element. Since, further, this part of the concept contains the common origin or the common determination of different species, it runs through all of them, and the different specific differences refer to it as to the basis of the same genus. ⟦252⟧ Or, taken genetically, the concept of the genus, which holds fast the essence and the unity, leaves open the possibility of a manifoldness that would develop within the unity. This indeterminate element is subject to the determinate; it must decide itself, but what it becomes in the decision is always bound by that substantial unity. This remarkable relation is grounded in the fact that the indeterminate, although it bears within itself the possibility of difference, is nevertheless homogeneous and in this homogeneity is governed by the higher concept.

[[707]] The parallelogram, for example, is the plane four-sided figure bounded by parallels. In this the relation of magnitude of the angles and of the sides enclosing the angles has remained indeterminate. The sides can be equal or unequal, the angles can incline more or less. From this wide possibility the species of the parallelogram proceed. But the freedom of development lies only within the higher law and remains bound by it. Nothing new suddenly arises that would abolish the subordination; rather, the indeterminate is homogeneous in itself and therefore governed in its development by the higher law. If the indeterminate that remained in the concept of the parallelogram is determined according to equality or inequality of sides and angles, there arise the species square, rectangle, rhombus, and rhomboid.

[[708]] Under the formula y² = px are comprised countless individual parabolas of flatter and steeper curvature, none similar to another, all peculiar in themselves. For one and the same of these curves p is constant; but p can be taken in infinitely many lengths, and each time a different parabola arises. But p always remains a constant straight line and x the abscissa, which together determine the corresponding ordinate according to the law of the formula. The indeterminate is homogeneous in itself and therefore, however it may take shape, remains subject to the higher concept.

[[709]] ⟦253⟧ In a similar way constant and variable elements generally present themselves in the concept of things, in that *one* fundamental relation binds them to each other. The ethical sphere, however opposed it may otherwise be to the mathematical, shows us the same. In the moral deed we distinguish the disposition, the knowledge of the matter and its ends, and finally the executing personality. While the disposition has its unchanging determination, as the divine to which it ought to be directed is eternal, while the matter remains fixed and equal to itself, but already the subjective knowledge of it admits or produces a diversity of convictions, the executing personality is partly manifold, partly limited in its powers. If, now, what is right is to arise in each case, these elements must complete themselves into a determinate fundamental relation of unity. Courage, for example, has the most various modes of appearance; for according to the strength that is at its command in the individual case it has its peculiar measure. Liberality is similarly governed by one's means. These indications may suffice to show how in the ethical sphere too, through the homogeneity of certain elements within the governing concept, a free space is left for particular shaping.

[[710]] If the fundamental concept is determined by the end, freedom lies in the choice of means. In the sense of sight the end is the sensation of the light-image. According to Johannes Müller, since what matters is that the images should not blur on the retina, nature presents this partly through refracting, converging media (in the higher animals), partly through an apparatus isolating the ray of light (in insects). The particularisation, the species-forming difference, lies in the different means, which, however, may never withdraw themselves from the determining law of the fundamental concept. It is similar with other organs, e.g. the organs of respiration or the organs of motion.

[[711]] ⟦254⟧ Thus the real ground of logical subordination becomes clear. In fact, really too, in the origination of the matter, the lower concepts are subject to the higher. As a philosophical example of such a shaping of concepts from the fundamental concept, Spinoza's development of the passive states of the soul in the third part of his Ethics may be adduced.

[[712]] The measure of this subordination of concepts is accordingly nothing other than the essence and origination of the matter. If the definition which sets out the content of a concept is to be found from observation, as e.g. with natural bodies, it has its hold in this respect. In the persisting mark, in the abiding activity, in general in the comprehensive, pervading determination, an inner kinship with the substantial essence is surmised. But since the essence proceeds from the mode of origination, whether this rests solely in the efficient cause or is determined by the end, it is only in the genetic definition that full insight into the essence is opened up. It hardly needs a renewed reminder that the genetic is something other than an external historical narrative, and that, especially in the organic, it includes the end that governs from the beginning as a driving moment.

[[713]] The relation just discussed, we mean the superordination of concepts, which is nothing other than subordination under the laws of the matter, is of the most thoroughgoing importance for knowledge. Without it there would be no deduction from the universal; for the worry would always remain that the specific might raise an objection. But because the indeterminate element, out of which the closer determinations can develop, is incorporated from the outset into the governing fundamental concept, what follows from the universal determination governs the subordinate species without their first needing to be investigated. Geometry ⟦255⟧ does not first prove of the individual species of the parallelogram, of the square, rectangle, rhombus, and rhomboid, that they are divided by the diagonal into two equal and similar triangles, in order to assert this proposition of the parallelogram in general. Rather, in the proof it does not concern itself at all with what is peculiar to the species; what it constructs from the fundamental concept holds of them all. As it is in this simple example, so it is in general. Our knowledge would be endless induction, and even induction would become entangled in its own deed, if the overarching fundamental concept did not interrupt the unsupported level plane of the marks. In the derivation from the concept we shall have to take up this point again.
