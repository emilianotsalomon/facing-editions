[[713]] If the marks are not apprehended in their necessary relation but are isolated, if accordingly the specific difference too, by being posited as substantial, is raised into the superordinate concept, then the lower has arbitrarily been made the higher, the included the comprehending. One then has only an external arrangement and can only range the material in rows externally, not generate it internally.

[[714]] The old logic divides the marks into original and derived (constitutiva and consecutiva), and the derived into common and proper (communia and propria). This distinction has great value. In the consecutiva propria the constitutivum reveals itself in its specific difference. Observation will penetrate through the former to the latter; and in this point of view one has a hint for grasping the essence.

[[715]] In this whole discussion the mark has not been taken in that subjective meaning which the name first expresses, so that it would be only a sign for recognition, but in the objective one which usage has long conceded to it, as that which forms the concept in the thing ⟦256⟧ itself. In the marks the concept is related purely to itself, whereas in the genetic definition it comes to be, and in the symptoms it manifests its effects.¹

[[716]] 8. Little is to be added about the extension. What is essential is prefigured or anticipated in the content.

[[717]] Division articulates the extension. There are indeed as many systems of division as there are views of the whole; for every point of view can be made into a ground of division. Such divisions can facilitate a survey of an extensive material and have value for the determinate end of an investigation. But they are contingent until the law that advances from the universal can be raised into the principle of the system. Where the essence and the becoming of the thing already lie open, as with mathematical concepts, or where the thing is so simple that it hardly offers several points of view, this goal ⟦257⟧ can well be reached. It is more difficult in the domain of experience. Yet precisely in these sciences ingenious observation has done much. The artificial systems are abandoned and natural ones are designed, in which not *one* one-sided mark but the progressive development, the individualisation of the creative law, forms the norm of the logical procedure.

[[718]] > (1) Given the great importance which definition has for the distinctness and determinacy of all knowledge, the following literary references may be permitted. The Aristotelian theory of definition and division is found in its main features in the author's elementa logices Aristoteleae, 6th ed. 1568, §§ 54–64; cf. on this the "Erläuterungen zu den Elementen der aristotelischen Logik," 2nd ed. 1861, p. 105 ff. In connection with the plan of a universal characteristic, Leibniz occupied himself most earnestly with definitions. The table of definitions worked out for this characteristic, which contains curiosities and pearls mixed together, has been edited in the Monatsberichten der k. Akademie der Wissenschaften, Feb. 1861. On "Ueber das Element der Definition in Leibnizens Philosophie," see the author's lecture in the Monatsberichten, July 1860, and in the historischen Beiträgen zur Philosophie, vol. III, 1867, p. 48. Leibniz's definitio iustitiae universalis contains a series of concise definitions of the relevant concepts. It has been published from Leibniz's literary remains in the author's "historischen Beiträgen zur Philosophie" II, p. 265 ff. With definition, the philosophers who disdain it give up sharpness, clarity and the last dominant major premise. But definition, to be sure, cannot be the beginning of an investigation; it is its yield.

[[719]] Where the law of the thing, advancing from the universal through the specific difference into the particular, forms the ground of division, there definitions are in turn gained through division. Thus, for example, in Spinoza's exposition of the affects the mode of origin is made the division, and definitions are gained from it immediately. In such divisions everything is drawn from what is peculiar to the thing; and it is vain to try to replace this peculiar essence by recurring universal categories, be they Kant's root concepts of the understanding or, among the moderns, oppositions and their resolution; one thereby runs the danger of sacrificing the richer, deeper truth to the psychological convenience of symmetrical points of view and to the psychological pleasure taken in them. It is true that mighty nature comprehends the oppositions and binds them into unity. But this does not at all say whether, and how many, intermediate formations it brings forth between the oppositions, which are the end-points of the particular. The oppositions result from the correct division, but the division does not, conversely, result from oppositions taken in advance.

[[720]] The rule of definition, to make the determination through the next higher genus and the species-forming difference, presents the concept to be defined as a species. To this extent it belongs to division, and, analogously to definition, it is applied to description by characteristic marks in the divisions of natural bodies. Hence the same rule becomes the thread that runs through the divisions, the real guiding thread for finding one's way in the passages of the system, ⟦258⟧ which without it would be labyrinthine, and for bringing the unknown to its place according to the given points of view. Through the carrying-out of this rule the systems of natural science, e.g. the Linnaean system of plants, become a real *Reallexicon* in the proper sense, not the kind one usually calls by that name, but one in which one looks things up by the systematic marks and thereby finds the name and gains further knowledge.

[[721]] If the species determine themselves differently according to the specific difference, they must exclude one another, and the principle of identity, grounded on the negation of the determination, has its full place here. The square is not a rhombus, nor can it have the properties peculiar to the rhombus (consecutiva propria). Indirect proof frequently goes back to this determination.

[[722]] Shall we now say with Schleiermacher¹ that the lower concept is related to the higher as the appearance to the force? The Aristotelian dynamis, as we can take force in this connection, does indeed contain within it the concept of a universal, but in the meaning of the indeterminate. Bronze, the possibility of a statue, of a vessel, of a tool, cannot be called their higher concept, unless one wants only an external subordination. The power of the eye to see is not the higher concept of the individual images, the motive power of the arm not the higher concept of its manifold actual turnings. The expression is therefore not sharp.

[[723]] One can conceive the relation thus: the content of the concept (the definition) governs, the extension obeys, but under a determinate law of its own (the specific difference in division).

[[725]] ⟦259⟧ 9. From what has gone before it is self-evident what great significance the concept has.

[[726]] The concept corresponds to substance. Inasmuch as the latter forms a self-standing whole, it is thereby spiritually justified, and the concept is the consciousness of this justification. Hence the concept too is whole in itself and has a centre of its own, like substance or activity raised to substance.

[[727]] The concept is, for substance, what is constant and universal. Through the concept the thing is what it is and does what it does. Realising itself in the most diverse appearances, it remains equal to itself and is the universal present in the corresponding substance. The concept of the circle pervades the appearance of the circle, the concept of the straight line the straight line; and when circle and straight line enter into a mutual relation, as in the theorems on the tangent and the chords, the properties flow from the interaction of the two concepts. The concepts of the circle and of the straight line reveal their energy therein.

[[728]] The constant and changeless, which the concept apprehends in the change of appearances, vouches for the spiritual origin of substance. Or should what is constant and persisting in the appearances be nothing but an equilibrium of blind forces? In nature the end, designed and grasped only by mind, is what is spiritual. If the concept contains the end, it designs the means accordingly and shapes actuality. It is then the creative universal. The structure of the eye is determined through and through by the concept; it is to see, and everything is directed to that. Thus the spiritual power of existence is concentrated in the concept.

[[729]] 10. The greatness of the individual concept, however, is limited, like the individual substance. If they appear whole and self-standing in themselves, they are so only compara⟦260⟧tively. They are what they are only in a comprehensive whole.

[[730]] The concept taken by itself is only a member, like the isolated substance. Its two functions, content and extension, really already move in the judgment. Abstraction and determination, the former forming the content, the latter the extension, are continually judging. The living relations of content and extension are judgments.

[[731]] # ⟦261⟧ XVI. THE FORMS OF JUDGMENT.

[[732]] 1. The concept first becomes living in the judgment, and indeed its content as well as its extension. If it did not always unfold its content or determine its extension in the judgment, it would be nothing better than the caput mortuum of Locke's substance, which is supposed to be only what remains when one strips away its properties, that is, its life. The concept conic section sets out its content in the judgment: the conic sections are regular curves of the second order; and articulates its extension in the judgment: the conic sections are either circles or ellipses or parabolas or hyperbolas. In both cases the concept of the subject grounds the predicate; in the first, the ground of the predicate lies in the developed content of the subject; in the second, in the given possibility and universality of its extension. As substance manifests itself in its activities, or the universal particularises itself in the species, so the predicate proceeds from the subject. The real relation of substance and the logical relation of the concept correspond here completely, in so far as the universal, which is the character of what is thought, does not make a difference. As substance appears causal with respect to its activities or properties, so ⟦262⟧ subject and predicate too are distinguished as antecedent and consequent, or as what is enfolded and what is unfolded. In the judgment "the conic sections are regular curves," the determination of the curve is a consequence of the section through the cone and a development of the subject concept. Even in the tautological proposition, if it is not to be entirely senseless, this relation remains. Whoever says "body is body" assuredly thinks of something other in the subject of the proposition than in the predicate: in the former the unity, in the latter the individual properties contained in the concept of body.¹ Pilate says, "What I have written, I have written," and in the tautological predicate presses the inner sense of the subject, what is completed, closed, done with. What the word deliberately keeps silent, the emphasis indicates with fine sense. Inasmuch as the emphasis places itself like a soul into the predicate and thereby gives it an expressive physiognomy distinct from the identically sounding subject, the judgment ceases to be purely tautological. The external sameness of subject and predicate, together with the difference intimated from within, arouses precisely the tacit representation that differences develop in the concept of the subject itself which must fall into the predicate.

[[733]] The judgment of content accordingly appears as a generalisation, the judgment of extension as a particularisation, of the subject. In the former are expressed the properties or the activities of substance which go out into the common world, or the elements of the concept, which are of a universal nature; in the latter, the limitation which the universal gives itself in the forms of the species. Since, however, the universal that becomes predicate in the judgment of content is mostly only *one* side of the universal, the inexact expression that the judgment in general presents the self-particularising concept may in this respect be excused.

[[734]] > (1) See this remark in Schelling, über die Freiheit, 1809, p. 407 f.

[[735]] ⟦263⟧ 2. If, accordingly, the predicate is always grounded in the concept of the subject, all judgments seem to be analytic. Against this, however, there arises the distinction between analytic and synthetic judgment developed in particular by Kant.¹ What is this distinction, and what are we to think of it?

[[736]] In all judgments, says Kant, the relation of the predicate to the subject is possible in two ways. Either the predicate B belongs to the subject A as something which is contained (covertly) in this concept A; or B lies entirely outside the concept A, although it stands in connection with it. In the first case the judgment is called analytic, in the second synthetic. Analytic judgments (affirmative ones) are therefore those in which the connection of the predicate with the subject is thought through identity; those, however, in which this connection is thought without identity are synthetic judgments. The former may be called explicative judgments, because through the predicate they add nothing to the concept of the subject but only break it up by analysis into its constituent concepts, which were already thought in it, though confusedly. Synthetic judgments, on the other hand, are ampliative judgments, since they add to the concept of the subject a predicate which was not thought in it at all and could not have been extracted from it by any analysis. For example, the judgment "all bodies are extended" is an analytic judgment. For, says Kant, I need not go beyond the concept which I connect with body in order to find extension as connected with it, but need only analyse that concept, i.e. become conscious of the manifold which I always think in it, in order to en⟦264⟧counter this predicate in it. If, on the other hand, I say "all bodies are heavy," then the predicate is something quite different from what I think in the mere concept of a body in general. The addition of such a predicate yields a synthetic judgment. Kant accordingly determines the judgments of the empirical sciences and the principles of arithmetic and geometry as synthetic.

[[737]] > (1) Kritik der reinen Vernunft p. 10 ff., 2nd ed.; Werke II, p. 21 ff. The determination of identical judgments in Leibniz (nouveaux essais p. 327, 328, ed. Raspe) is narrower than that of analytic judgments in Kant.

[[738]] First let us attend to the name, so that usage does not become confused. The analytic judgment can be traced back to the analogy of the analytic procedure, since in the latter, similarly to the judgment of content, the universal is brought out from the particular. But the use of the term synthetic judgment does not agree in the same way with the synthetic procedure. For in the synthetic method, as in Euclid's Elements, the particular phenomena are developed from the universal; in the synthetic judgment, subject and predicate are merely put together in consequence of an external ground.

[[739]] The point of view of composition and decomposition governs the whole distinction. In the analytic judgment the whole is broken up into its constituent concepts; in the synthetic, something new is added to the old and a new whole is in this way composed. We depress the formations of thought below the value of organic ones, however, when we allow such mechanical points of view to arise. In the organic everything is development; only in handicraft is there composition.

[[740]] Nor are the boundaries sharply drawn. One person already thinks a mark in a concept which for another comes in as a new one. For the physicist heaviness is as much an analytic mark of the concept body as extension is for the mathematician. What is a new connection for one science is for another only a resolution. The greater or lesser determinacy of the subjective representation cannot furnish an objective ground of division ⟦265⟧ for the kinds of judgment. In order to uphold the distinction, one lets concept and intuition run into one another. "The parabola is a conic section": this, it is said, is an analytic judgment; for the predicate (conic section) lies enclosed in the concept of the subject (parabola). "This parabola cuts a circle": such a judgment, it is said, is synthetic; for the intuition of the predicate (cuts a circle) lies in no way in the concept of a parabola. This intuition certainly does not lie in the universal concept. But is the subject such a concept? "This parabola cuts a circle" is a judgment of intuition. What lies in this intuition is expressed in the predicate.

[[741]] If one is willing to let the point of view stand, every complete judgment appears from the one side as analytic, from the other as synthetic.

[[742]] Every judgment is analytic. For whence would the truth of the predicate come if it did not lie grounded in the subject? This is confirmed in all the judgments which Kant declares to be simply synthetic. Thus the arithmetical proposition 7 + 5 = 12, or the geometrical one, that the straight line is the shortest way between two points, is supposed to be synthetic. 7 + 5 = 12 is evidently an analytic judgment, in so far as, presupposing the decimal number system, the sum 7 + 5 grounds the number 12. That the straight line is the shortest way between two points lies nowhere but in the essence of the straight line itself. Kant designates the judgment that ascribes an original attraction to all matter as synthetic, since he expressly argues that this property¹ belongs to the concept of matter but is not contained in it. And yet Kant himself has shown how attraction follows from space-filling matter if this ⟦266⟧ is to be possible in itself at all. This inference is an analysis of the possibility of matter. The negative judgment will appear predominantly synthetic; for concepts which originally do not belong together are brought together in order to determine and set themselves off against one another, e.g. two lines enclose no space. What is foreign to the concept of the two lines is put into connection with it (synthetically). But the power to separate the foreign from itself and thereby to form a negative judgment stems precisely from the determinacy of the concept and from its might to maintain itself in this determinacy. After all this, every judgment is analytic from the side of objective grounding.

[[743]] > (1) Metaphysische Anfangsgründe der Naturwissenschaft, 2nd ed. 1787, p. 54; Werke V, p. 360; cf. on the other hand Hegel, Logik I, p. 203.

[[744]] But every judgment is just as much synthetic. For since the ground, as we saw,¹ never shows itself in unity but only in the sum of several conditions, the subject too, so long as it is thought only as simple in itself, does not contain the full ground. Development takes place only through excitation. That other conditions come in to bring the predicate to light, in this lies the synthetic character. In the judgment 7 + 5 = 12 the decimal number system, an arithmetical synthesis of the most important kind, is presupposed; in the judgment "the straight line is the shortest way between two points," comparison with other lines (the superlative, the shortest way, indicates it sufficiently) is incidentally demanded. Even in definitions, which show the analytic judgment in its perfection, one easily recognises the synthesis. For the concept which is brought out of the subject as a universal mark links on to something else and, through its universality, posits a relation to other concepts. If we take the definition of a triangle (a plane figure enclosed by three sides), the concepts three, sides, figure, plane, ⟦267⟧ lie analytically in the subject. If they were known only through the subject, they would coincide undistinguished with the intuition of it. In so far as they have a universal nature, they point to a relation of different spheres of concepts, and this linking on to something else is the tacit synthesis. So it stands even with the judgment "body is extended." Finally, if we consider the subjective end of a judgment: why, then, does one judge? Perhaps in order to state superfluously what is known and what is already thought in the subject? Rather, what lies hidden in the subject is brought to light in the predicate as something new, and it is the interest of the judgment that something should come before the soul which was not yet immediately there in the representation of the subject. Thus even the judgment of clarification, in which something known that had receded is brought forward, is a synthetic judgment. Every clarification is accordingly an extension of knowledge, every resolution a construction.

[[746]] If, then, the opposition of analytic and synthetic is equalised in every judgment, has that fundamental question of the Kantian critique, how synthetic judgments a priori are possible, no further sense? In the context of the Kantian investigations the question concerns what is original and creative in knowing; this, however, we are not to seek in the connection of subject and predicate. In the question how synthetic judgments a priori are possible, they already appear as finished and given; for according to Kant's definition the synthetic judgment adds a new predicate to the known subject. What is given and finished is not what is original. That investigation must therefore rather relate to a first activity and to the way in which the substance of the concept forms itself out of it. In language the judgment at the first stage (it lightens, it rustles) has the form of original activity, and these judgments are therefore purely synthetic (or, ⟦268⟧ more correctly, thetic). They must be taken so for the very reason that the subject which could be analysed is lacking. In the critique of the ontological proof Kant declared the so-called existential propositions (e.g. there is a God) to be synthetic, and they do indeed stand in the same relation as the judgments of the first stage.¹

[[747]] 3. Since the judgment animates the concept, the development of the judgment must first present the two essential sides of the concept. Judgments are therefore either judgments of content or judgments of extension.

[[748]] The so-called categorical judgment (the rose is red, the parabola is a conic section) is in the proper sense designed to state the content of the subject. When the activities or properties enter the predicate, they give the marks of the concept or what follows from them.

[[749]] The disjunctive judgment articulates the extension of a concept (the conic sections are either circles or ellipses or parabolas or hyperbolas). Grammatically, other forms too can serve to determine the extension, partly the conjunctive (circles, ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas are conic sections), partly the partitive (the conic sections are partly circles, partly ellipses, partly parabolas, partly hyperbolas). Whereas the conjunctive form merely collects the species of the extension and ranges them side by side without closing them off, in the disjunctive they are projected with necessity and into a closed whole.² In Aristotle's proposition, we are certain of our knowledge either through syllogism or through induction, we have the example of a disjunctive judgment. The whole extension of the means by which we attain certainty is presented and divided into determinate species.

[[752]] ⟦269⟧ The disjunctive judgment appears in yet another particular use. When a concept is to be determined, one is accustomed to set the universal possibilities side by side in a disjunctive proposition until what is impossible in the particular case is found out and the *one* actuality remains. Does the given concept hold good in this case too? It is indeed the content of a concept that is to be established, but this happens by means of the extension. For example, an antinomy in Kant runs: the world has come to be either through a free cause or through a blind necessity. In such a case the final end may be to form a judgment of content, e.g. the world has come to be through a free cause. But the form before us (either, or) divides the wide possibility of causality and articulates the extension of the possible which the thought spans. The extension here is not the extension of an actual genus but of a world gained through representation. Thus it is throughout the character of the disjunctive judgment that it articulates the extension of the concept.

[[753]] Between the content and the extension of a concept there exists, as we saw, the closest connection. When a concept sets out its content, it acknowledges that it belongs to the extension of that concept which constitutes its essential component. When several concepts in this way place themselves under the same higher one, a judgment of extension is thereby prepared. The judgments "the circle is a conic section," "the ellipse is a conic section," "the parabola is a conic section," etc., are judgments of content. But when they are drawn together in the conjunctive form, they set the species side by side. In this way a natural transition results from the judgment of content to the judgment of extension. But only the disjunctive judgment contains the strict order of the extension and, in the imperious tone of its either, or, intimates the necessity of its ⟦270⟧ origin, inasmuch as the law advancing from the universal into the individual announces itself in the articulation.

[[754]] 4. From the organic determination of the judgment two necessary forms have been gained, which, as the highest difference, must govern all the rest. The judgment of content is found again in the categorical, the judgment of extension in the disjunctive judgment of formal logic.

[[755]] The categorical and the disjunctive form, however, are not conceived in this way. One associates the hypothetical judgment with them and ranks them under the point of view of relation. The predicate is accordingly thought to stand in a different relation to the subject in each: in the categorical judgment by means of inherence (substance and accident), in the hypothetical by means of causal connection (causality and dependence), in the disjunctive according to the relation of the parts engaged in reciprocal action to the logical whole.

[[756]] Formal logic here becomes metaphysical, evidently against its own will. Only Kant may help himself here, by declaring the metaphysical categories to be root concepts, hence original forms of the understanding. Thereby the real which appears here in the form is set aside. But whoever leaves Kant's standpoint is inconsistent if, with a changed view, he still believes in the possibility of a strictly formal logic.

[[757]] That the explanation of the disjunctive judgment given in the category of relation does not suffice has already been shown. How does it stand with the determinations of the categorical and the hypothetical judgment?

[[758]] Boundaries have been drawn so that the two forms do not break into each other's territory. We shall see whether the partitions hold.

[[759]] First, the categorical judgment is supposed to correspond to the relation of thing and property (inherence), the ⟦271⟧ hypothetical to the relation of cause and effect. If, however, we recall the investigations above,¹ these concepts form no opposition. Substance is causal in the property. The property is activity bound to the thing. The strictest form of inherence is the relation of the parts indwelling in the whole. But since the whole bears the parts and makes them what they are, here too inherence turns over into causality. Language confirms this transition. It expresses the categorical judgment just as much through the effective activity of the verb as through the property of the adjective (cf. e.g. the mirror is hollowed, the mirror is parabolic). Further, it does not hold the categorical and the hypothetical judgment strictly apart. The form of the one puts itself in place of the other with a scarcely noticeable difference. The antecedent of the hypothetical judgment passes into the subject of a categorical one, and the consequent of a hypothetical into the predicate of a categorical, and conversely. For example: if a triangle is right-angled, it has the property expressed in the Pythagorean theorem (hypothetical). The right-angled triangle has this property (categorical). Inherence finds an even more fitting expression in this case: in the right-angled triangle the square on the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares on the other two sides.

[[762]] Other distinctions extend still less through all cases. In the categorical judgment the connection of subject and predicate is supposed to take place under the form of sameness, in the hypothetical under the form of mere connection. In the former one is to think under A and B (A is B) the same identical thing, in the latter different but connected objects. In a hypothetical judgment, however, an identical object is frequently treated, as in the example just mentioned. If a triangle ⟦272⟧ is right-angled, it has the Pythagorean property. Or is the countless multitude of such cases supposed to contain only a grammatical and not a logical hypothesis? When a hypothetical judgment brings together different objects, they are always one under a higher concept, since they are posited as one as condition and conditioned, as ground and consequence. By virtue of this unity, which rests in an activity or a property, it can happen that in this case too the hypothetical judgment has an almost equivalent categorical one beside it. Compare, for example, the judgments: If amber is rubbed, electricity is developed. Rubbed amber develops electricity. Further: If a cone is cut by a plane, regular curves arise. The section through a cone produces regular curves.

[[763]] If, finally, the connection in the categorical judgment is supposed to be an inner one and in the hypothetical an outer one, then in any case the outer must be conditioned by an inner one. Only the form of the clauses stands more externally and independently in the hypothetical judgment. But the bond that unites them is neither more external nor more internal than the copula in the categorical judgment.

[[764]] One may further take the view that in the categorical judgment subject and predicate are posited as finished and undoubted, whereas in the hypothetical proposition they stand there problematically as concepts in formation. But if in the hypothetical judgment the problematic character of the individual thoughts linked with one another is to be expressed, the emphasis throws itself with noticeable preponderance onto the conditional particles. Where this does not happen, the thoughts of the antecedent and the consequent are put in question neither more nor less than the subject and predicate of the categorical judgment. For the categorical judgment too, taken in logical strictness, is affected with a hypothesis. For example, the judgment "the right-angled ⟦273⟧ triangle has the Pythagorean property" is categorical.¹ But whether a triangle is right-angled remains undecided. Only where the perceived individual is the subject (e.g. this painting is of the Florentine school) does the hypothesis, which makes the subject problematic, not arise at all against the decided intuition. But this hypothetical nature of the subject must emerge again where the judgment is conceived by itself and independently of the positing intuition. This relation has been urged with full right against various attempts at the ontological proof.

[[765]] Hegel, in the opposition of the categorical and the hypothetical judgment, has lent the former a meaning deviating from the usual one, an essentially narrower one, perhaps in order to escape the prevailing indeterminacy. We shall discuss his conception below in connection with the other forms of judgment.

[[766]] From this criticism it first of all follows that a greater community prevails between the categorical and the hypothetical judgment than has hitherto been recognised. Their difference does not lie in an altered relation of the predicate to the subject. Both share the determination of stating the content of the subject. There is, in the general ⟦274⟧ representation, no difficulty in calling the categorical judgment a judgment of content, since the subject presents its nature in the predicate. But the effect of a cause too (the predicate of a hypothetical proposition) is to be regarded as content of the cause. Even where the hypothetical antecedent contains only a ground of knowledge (e.g. if the thermometer rises, it is getting warmer), the probative force is the content of the subject (the rising thermometer indicates that it is getting warmer). The hypothetical judgment can intertwine with the disjunctive, or arise out of it. But this case is not original and pure, and therefore does not belong here. Accordingly, two groups form out of the judgments placed under relation: on the one side the categorical and the hypothetical, on the other the disjunctive judgment, inasmuch as the former unfold the content, while the latter orders the domain of the extension.

Whereas in the categorical the predicate falls into the subject as activity or property, in the hypothetical both can be thought by themselves; only, according to the consequence (if, then), they again become essentially dependent and ⟦275⟧ must be thought together. The hypothetical judgment will therefore appear especially where two activities are related to one another as subject and predicate. Next, the hypothetical judgment is a judgment of sharper reflection, while the categorical purely states the fact. The condition and the conditioned are isolated, while in the categorical judgment the unity of the causal process is intuited. The investigation of conditions reaches over into the modal concepts of the possible and the necessary; hence the hypothetical judgment is akin to both. Now the consecutive particles, the element of reflection, are made prominent in contrast to actuality, and the hypothetical judgment receives the secondary meaning of the problematic; now the cogent consequence of the relations is attended to, and the hypothetical judgment becomes in particular the expression of laws of nature. In the latter case the hypothetical judgment can be a singular one and yet bear the character of necessity, which springs from an underlying universal.

[[771]] Since the hypothetical judgment presents the most abstract form of causality, we may also look in it for the designation of the end. Commonly the connection "if, then" serves the real ground, and the form-word "in order that" designates the ideal ground of the end. But grammatical expression is in logic only an indication and not a decisive determination. The matter belongs here, and the expression adapts itself to some extent. Compare the judgments: "The eye has refracting media in order that it may see." "If the eye is to see, it must have refracting media." "If the eye was to see, it had to have refracting media." But one feels at the same time that the judgments do not express the same thing. A thought which is co-designated in the first clothes itself in no hypothetical form. For the hypothetical judgment remains, in its configurations, within universal consideration; whether the eye has refracting media ⟦276⟧ or not, the actuality which that proposition (the eye has refracting media in order that it may see) also contains, it does not state. Accordingly it will be necessary here too to distinguish universal causality into its two kinds.

[[772]] Perhaps the following connection lies in the nature of the matter.

[[773]] In the judgment of content the point of view of causality prevails throughout. In the categorical judgment (of inherence) it is expressed as adhering; in the hypothetical it is brought out more strictly. In both kinds it is first conceived assertorically as something actual (e.g. in the right-angled triangle the squares on the two sides are equal to the square on the hypotenuse; and likewise: if a triangle is right-angled, it has this property). Since causality as such is accessible only to thought, it is natural, when it comes to consciousness, to turn out in it the reflection which itself plays with the conditions. Thereby the hypothetical form receives the meaning of the problematic, and from this arise in particular the fine combinations of the thought with expectation or of the thought with renunciation of possibility, which the Greek language, for example, expresses so delicately and so variously. But thought can go deeper into ⟦277⟧ causality and recognise itself again in it as the determining factor. The effect is thought, willed, and therefore also, or therefore only then, the cause. The problematic character of the effect thought as condition shows itself here too and comes out grammatically even in the subjunctive (e.g. the eye has refracting media in order that it may see). It is not said whether the effect occurs or not. Accordingly this judgment of the end will have to be recognised as a form of its own, and indeed as positive and negative, just as it has a natural form in the German damit and even a double form in the Latin ut and ne, even though in ut the consideration of the efficient cause and of the end are not sharply separated; such a judgment is hypothetical in the wider sense.¹ Then the division turns out simply. The judgments of content are judgments of activity, determined either purely by the efficient cause or by the end. The judgment of the end is lacking in formal logic and is yet, as the treatment of the end shows, of the greatest significance. The end, pervading and shaping the parts of the concept like a soul, belongs to its content and therefore falls here.

[[775]] A remark may still be in place here, in order to keep the grammatical and the logical within their bounds. What logic calls predicate differs from the narrower concept which grammar is accustomed to assign to it. Logic understands by predicate what is stated of the subject, regardless of whether what is stated is a simple concept or one determined by an object, and includes the grammatical object in the predicate. Grammar has to consider more closely the determinations which come to the concept of activity as grammatical object, be it as complements in the form of a case or only as amplifications, adverbially. For example, in the sentence from Gellert's fable, "now the paper runs through all the streets," logic regards everything that is stated of the paper as predicate; grammar at first only "runs," and ⟦278⟧ takes everything else as objective determinations. These depend on the real nature of the activity and must have become intelligible through the real categories; they do not touch the judgment as judgment. Recently they have been taken up into logic.¹ If this is to happen, they need a treatment of their own, independent of grammar.

[[776]] 5. From the inner end of the judgment the highest species-forming difference has been gained. Judgments are first judgments of content (categorical and hypothetical, the latter partly within the efficient cause, partly through the end) and judgments of extension (divisive, disjunctive). If one wishes to place this twofold formation under relation, in so far as in both the relation of the predicate to the subject is essentially altered, so be it; but the name is wide and indeterminate, since it can comprise inner as much as outer relations of the judgment. In any case, however, the previous points of view of relation (inherence, causality, reciprocal action) must be abolished, and in this category the much-praised triad of kinds goes back into a necessary dyad. The doctrine of the concept and the doctrine of the judgment are thereby as closely bound together as concept and judgment always are in living thought itself.

[[777]] The next task is to determine the judgments of content and of extension more closely from their own nature and to derive from it the formation of their kinds. In this respect we consider the judgment of content first.

[[778]] As substance discloses itself in activity, so the content of the concept expresses itself in the statement of the judgment. At first both happen positively, and the affirmative judgment presents the generating activity of things. With the determinacy of the generating activity a ⟦279⟧ repelling one is at one. To this repelling activity, which proceeds from the positive essence of things and through which the thing maintains itself by thrusting away what is foreign, the negative judgment corresponds. In so far as every positing and generating acts unitingly and hence attractively, every self-maintenance against others separatingly and hence repulsively, one may say that the affirmation and the negation of the judgment have the ground of their truth in the attraction and repulsion of things.¹

[[780]] If the activities or relations which flow immediately from the essence make up the fundamental property (quality) of a thing, then the quality of the judgment, which is to reveal the content of a concept (of the subject), comprises these forms of the affirmative and the negative judgment. Attributing and denying, in which all judging is commonly placed, correspond in the way indicated to the active essence of things.

[[781]] 6. Under the category of quality the infinite judgment, more correctly the indeterminate one, is co-ordinated with the affirmative and the negative judgment.²

[[782]] Negation does not in itself belong to the content of the predicate concept but only gives the universal concept of activity its repelling direction. Negation therefore fuses with the copula, the formal force of the judgment,³ or, where the copula has found no particular expression, with the predicating act of the verb. If, nevertheless, the negation is added to the material content of the ⟦280⟧ predicate concept, the predicate becomes a merely negative concept (contradictorie oppositum in the traditional usage), without intuition and indeterminate, but it has nevertheless been lent, as a concept, an independence which belongs only to what is determinate in itself. In this way the infinite judgment is formed. For example, "mercury is not red" is a negative judgment; but "mercury is a not-red thing" is an infinite judgment. Red and not-red stand contradictorily opposed to each other when they are attributed to the same subject. The not-red is a manufactured concept, since substance has been given to a mere negation, an indeterminate, unbounded possibility; and this artificial product is the predicate of the infinite judgment "mercury is a not-red thing." Form and content here stand in open contradiction; for the content of the judgment is negative, but the form affirmative. The infinite judgment suffers from this discord. Hence it is not found in the natural course of thinking and is merely a trick of logic. Much as the art of gardening forms hybrid forms of plants, logic has made this form of judgment, which hovers between the affirmative and the negative judgment.

[[787]] If the infinite judgment is to have a sense, an intuition must push itself into the place of the negative concept; but then, precisely thereby, the infinite judgment ceases to be infinite. This happens when the infinite judgment is used for dichotomous divisions (e.g. parallelograms are either right-angled or not right-angled). In such a case the ⟦281⟧ bounding sphere (parallelogram) already gives a determinacy, and the predicate does not roam out into all the possibility of the world. The opposition, too, mostly supplies a determinate thought (acute- and obtuse-angled), which is immediately connected with the seemingly infinite predicate (not right-angled). So too language in ordinary speech has the form but not the sense of the infinite judgment. We take the nearest example. "He does not call me" is a negative judgment; "he calls not me" would by its form be an infinite judgment, since the negation is drawn not to the predicating element but to the content of the predicate, to the object. Language in general resists, with a right sense, combining pure negation (not) with elements other than those in which the repelling direction can combine with a concept of the predicating activity. Negation and noun fuse with more difficulty. (Cf. a sentence such as this one: in this direction of the work lies the ground of its non-completion.)

[[788]] Yet *one* phenomenon in language may be noticed which might belong to the infinite judgment. These are the formations, chiefly of adjectives, with a purely negative prefix (the German un-, the Latin in-, the Greek alpha privative), e.g. continens, incontinens.¹ Such an adjective, stated of a subject, apparently yields an infinite judgment, i.e. an affirmative judgment with a negative predicate. If, then, continens and ⟦282⟧ incontinens are predicated of *one* and the same subject, two mutually contradictory judgments arise, and hence such mutually opposed concepts have been called by an old name contradictorie opposita, although it must be admitted that this expression, used of concepts, is inexact; for only thoughts,¹ hence only judgments, assertions, and not detached concepts, stand in the relation of contradiction.

[[790]] At first sight these formations contain only negative attributes, as if they presented a non-a purely. Against this it is surprising that some such concepts, instead of being negative, are positive through and through, and even the support of everything positive. Thus Cartesius remarks (Meditation 3) that the representation of the infinite (infinitum) is grasped not through the negation of the finite but through a true idea, for the infinite has more being than the finite. The infinite, though expressed negatively, is accordingly the affirmative predicate through whose negation the finite is thought. Spinoza (de intellectus emendatione p. 448) continues this remark and warns against the illusion which arises from language expressing the positive negatively, as e.g. in the words: the uncreated, the immortal. Chrysippus had viewed this last concept similarly. Considerable examples can be added which, akin in meaning to the absolute, express the positive in negative form, such as the unconditioned, the ἀνυπόθετον in Plato, the independent as what is grounded in itself, the unavoidable as the necessary, the immediate, which reveals itself solely through its own power (ἄμεσον, preserving this sense in two directions in Aristotle), the impassive intellect (νοῦς ἀπαθής), which receives nothing and draws from itself ⟦283⟧ and thinks, the atom, the indivisible, as what is original in the material. Similarly in other concepts. The Roman still felt in securus the condition relieved of fear, as the Greek did in ἄδεια. In our word sicher the etymology which made the negation known has become effaced, and we intuit in it rather the positive, the arrangements for a firm condition in which we can have reliable expectations for the ends of action. The unchangeable, e.g. the unchangeable direction, expresses the identical, this support of our thoughts, negatively, and, since "other" means as much as "not so" and hence is a negation, really produces the positive through the negation of a negation (not not-so). Similarly the certain and necessary is designated by infallible. The lasting is expressed by unceasing, incessant; the pure by unstained, innocent; what offers bodily resistance by impenetrable; love that overcomes itself by unselfish: always the positive through the negation of the opposite. Language, which starts from what, like the conditioned, the sensible, the transient, lies nearest to us and is evident to us, gains in this way a more perceptible expression for what is by nature original and withdrawn from appearance.

[[793]] Other attributes, formed in language by the same path of negation, signify instead of pure negation a lack (a privation) and are therefore not pure negation, but designate the secondary thought that the opposite was demanded by the determination of nature or was expected by the speaker. Concepts such as Unmensch behave thus, i.e. the man who, measured by the concept of man, is no man; Unzahl, i.e. the number which is no longer countable; Unding, i.e. a thing which, measured by what is possible, is no thing; Ungedanke, i.e. a thought which is not thinkable. These concepts, much as in Greek δῶρα ἄδωρα, γάμος ἄγαμος, designate ⟦284⟧ phenomena which lack the specific essence of their concept. A lack is shown by concepts such as unmindful (immemor), undetermined, unconscious, irresolute, spurious, etc., and through the lack others pass over into the censuring meaning of the opposite, e.g. impius (godless), unfaithful, unfair, shameless, monstrous, absurd, misshapen (ἀσχήμων, informis in the sense of ugly), etc. Since the sensible and intuitable is the first impulse of language to the formation of words, the opposite that stands out (the contrarium) is the concept which presents itself to it instead of pure negation. Hence in concepts which, strictly speaking, express only a pure negation, such as unequal (impar, ἄνισον) — for the slightest deviation from the equal is unequal, and there can be nothing intermediate between equal and unequal — the positive intuition of difference may have prevailed in their origin. Only when science finds distinguished properties precisely in the negation do concepts with purely negative content form, as in geometry incommensurable (measurable by no common measure), in Roman law the indebitum, cf. the condictio indebiti; but such concepts are always referred to a determinate sphere, e.g. of magnitudes, of numbers, and are therefore not purely negative in the sense that they point a subject to which they are attributed, with the exception of the concept they negate, out into the infinite space of everything else possible, as would have to be the case if they were to be used as instances of the infinite judgment.

[[794]] If, finally, one asks how it comes about that one and the same sign of negation (un-, in-, ἀ-), combined with *one* representation, designates pure negation, with another lack, with a third the opposite (cf. e.g. impar, immemor, impius), the ground lies, it seems, in the expectation or opinion which tacitly hovers over a concept. Impius does not designate for the Roman one who is indifferent towards the gods; the concept pius has for him ⟦285⟧ such validity as the foundation of everything good that indifference is hardly thinkable to him and he already sees in forgetfulness of the gods the source of all wickedness. With concepts of reflection, which arise soberly, pure negation may maintain itself, as in unequal, incommensurable, unavoidable, unconditioned, and point through the opposite to the positive nature.
