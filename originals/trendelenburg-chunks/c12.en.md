[[795]] Whether a concept that becomes predicate in the judgment is in itself positive or negative may not be judged by the form of the word alone. The investigation is of a material nature and can be conducted only from the content of the concept. Hence the positive and the negative cannot, in this sense, be attached, as has been done, to Kant's formal logic as a correction of his doctrine of the quality of judgments. Negative predicates which are thought and named after the analogy of opposite magnitudes do not belong here; for they are in themselves positive.

[[796]] Accordingly, language gives the infinite judgment no support. If one wishes finally to say that the infinite judgment has the determination of restriction (limitation), one errs in this too. For to exclude a single point is still far from restricting a concept in itself, or even from obtaining degrees as a transition from reality to negation (Kant's *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*, 2nd ed., p. 184). Only from the positive essence can the restriction be projected.

[[797]] After all this, logic will rid itself of the infinite judgment without loss.

[[798]] 7. With the infinite judgment there falls Kant's ground of knowledge for limitation as a root concept of the understanding; for Kant draws out the unifying concepts of the understanding from the various forms of judgments, which present the various ways in which concepts are united in consciousness. If, therefore, there is no infinite judgment in which limitation is supposed to manifest itself, then ⟦286⟧ limitation itself becomes doubtful. Recently, however, a logician who rejects the infinite judgment but will not do without limiting, and who must therefore also find an expression in the judgment for limitation, has provided help¹ by drawing emphasis, which was otherwise regarded as a rhetorical element, into logic. What the plain words in the proposition do not yield is to be indicated by the tone laid into them. Limitation is now supposed to come forward in the judgment in the following way. The limited form is distinguished from the negative by this, that in the latter the negation belongs to the copula, in the former the negation, with subordinate stress, belongs to the predicate, e.g. this statement is not correct. This is interpreted in the following way. "The judgment: this statement is correct, has positive form; the other: it is incorrect, negative; but it can also run: this statement is not-correct, where the 'not' remains unstressed and the stress falls on correct. In this last proposition the negation decidedly belongs to the attribute 'correct,' not to the copula 'is.'" "If I express myself thus, it is quite possible that I find some points in the statement correct in themselves, but as a whole it does not appear to me to hold good; the decided incorrectness of other points cancels the correctness of those, so that it loses its value. This third form of the judgment in respect of the predicate is the limited or restricted one."

[[799]] It is an error that Kant ascribed to the infinite judgment the stress just described (the statement is not-correct). "Kant," it is said (p. 42), "declares the example: the soul is not-mortal, to be an infinite judgment." On the contrary, in the infinite judgment Kant sharpens the ⟦287⟧ negation; he declares: the soul is placed in the unlimited extension of non-dying beings,¹ with which is to be compared, in Kant's *Logik* (§. 22), the example of the infinite judgment: some men are non-learned. The composition (non-dying, non-learned) removes every doubt about the stress; by every analogy it falls on the determining word contained in the first syllable. The interpretation given therefore lacks a Kantian foundation.

[[801]] It is awkward when the fleeting, hard-to-grasp, easily slipping tone is supposed to be the adequate expression of essential differences. The suggestive tone, which awakens indeterminate secondary thoughts, which says more, or even, as in irony, says something other than the simple word expresses, not seldom mixes in affect, and will be as little entitled to raise its voice in Kant's *Kritik der reinen Vernunft* as in Aristotle's *Analytics*. It has no place there, unless perhaps in the "is" distinguished by the accent as copula and as verbum substantivum (ἐστὶ and ἔστι), which belongs elsewhere. One may well ask how it comes about that the expression: the statement is not-correct, an expression which contains only a negation, arrives at the sense: the statement is half correct, only not wholly, so that the negation turns into a restriction of the affirmation. The explanation is lacking. The connection will be the following.

[[802]] In calm speech we stress the predicate by a slight degree; for the predicate is the principal concept, the concept which we have before our eyes as the goal of the proposition. If now the stress on the predicate is heightened, its concept is thereby sharpened; it is thrown back into its own and proper essence, grasped in its intensification and completion, and thereby more strictly separated from what is opposed to it. In the same sense language resorts to the means of inversion. Position and stress then work together ⟦288⟧ to arouse all the more determinately the representation of the excluded opposite. E.g. all theory is grey; grey, friend, is all theory. The awakened opposite leads on in natural connection: yet green is life's golden tree. If now such a predicate with marked stress, and thereby the concept in its completion, is negated, the concession can thereby be indicated that something of the concept of the predicate is indeed present, but that the predicate in its full truth is not present. E.g. "theory is not grey"; "grey it is not" will mean, through the opposite awakened in the tone: granted that it is not full of life, as green is, still it is not Cimmerian, cheerless, as grey is. The proposition: "the judgment is sharp" means to say: the judgment is hair-sharp; and the negation: the judgment is not sharp, sharp it is not, denies the judgment this ultimate sharpness, but, precisely by already applying the highest standard, concedes the possibility of other merits. Where the judgment is not hair-sharp, it will perhaps approach sharpness. In this and in no other sense is the proposition chosen as the example of limitation to be understood: "this statement is not-correct." More markedly it runs: correct it is not; a certain truth is conceded to it, but correctness in the ultimate and full sense is denied it. It is an erroneous view that in this example and in similar cases the "not" ceased to stand to the copula and became content of the predicate. Since the predicate is stressed and its concept thereby sharpened, it may seem to the ear as if the "not," receding outwardly before the predicate, had been taken up into the predicate. But it only seems so. Through the elevation of the predicate the negation does not migrate from the copula into the predicate. This is recognisable enough in the inversion, which brings out that intensification still more clearly: "correct the statement is not"; for here the "not" has separated itself so completely from the "correct" that it stands at the ⟦289⟧ opposite end of the proposition. Hence the hyphen in the proposition: "this statement is not-correct" must be dropped, and the judgment remains a negative judgment. Thereby the semblance of a similarity dissolves which this form might have with Kant's infinite judgment, the representative of limitation. Judgments of this kind negate the completion of the predicate expressed by the heightened stress. What, however, is perhaps conceded thereby can only be guessed from the context and yields an affirmative judgment. The sharpening of the predicate by the tone therefore properly forms two judgments out of *one*. If in the affirmative judgment the predicate is more forcibly stressed, the sharpening separates off what is foreign, what was perhaps expected, or what might restrict the completion, and, when the hint is developed into consciousness of the content, brings about a negative judgment: e.g. all theory is grey, i.e. through and through, and has nothing of life in it. If, conversely, in the negative judgment the predicate is more vividly stressed, the concession of an affirmative one can thereby be indicated, as has been shown. The one of the two judgments appears expressed and unveiled (explicite), e.g. the judgment is not sharp (not hair-sharp), the other, awakened by the tone, veiled (implicite) and only in germ (the judgment approaches sharpness). Hence it cannot correspond to the analytical spirit of logic to regard this knotting together as something simple and original, and therefore as the adequate expression of a category. Stress, as the language of a feeling which first stirs up thoughts, is not the straight and open designation of the logical, and easily becomes ambiguous.

[[803]] The restriction of a judgment which belongs to the copula signifies a diminished assertion and therefore falls under modality, e.g. the statement is perhaps correct;¹ the ground of ⟦290⟧ knowledge is still lacking; whereas the restriction in the predicate, e.g. half-true, light-blue, in general the difference of degrees, concerns the content of the concept and not the judgment in its universal nature.

[[805]] 8. Hegel has lifted the concept of the infinite judgment out of its original meaning, handed down since Aristotle. According to his exposition, infinite judgments arise when determinations are negatively combined as subject and predicate, "of which the one contains not only not the determinateness of the other, but also not its universal sphere." E.g. spirit is not red, not sour, not alkaline; the understanding is not a table. The genus as well as the species is left undetermined in the same way. The form of such a judgment is negative, and the ground is the same as with other negative ones; for the determinateness of the subject-concept drives back the proposed combination. But the content is absurd because the spheres lie so far from one another and the concepts are so heterogeneous that the possibility of such a combination is a mere whim. Subject and predicate have in the real no relation or connection of any kind with one another. One cannot see why in the sound development of the judgment this "absurd judgment" should have a place, nor understand why such a manner can be called an infinite judgment, and wherein the similarity of this form with the one otherwise so named might consist.²

[[806]] 9. The judgment of content is, accordingly, either affirmative or negative. Both forms can now run through the stages of ⟦291⟧ modality.¹ The knowing mind rises from the immediate certainty of the fact to reflection on the conditions, from reflection on the conditions to the sum of the ground, and presents this in the act of judgment, which ripens on the objective, in the assertoric, problematic and apodictic form. It has been shown above how actuality, possibility and necessity, which appear here in the judgment, correspond in their own way to relations of the thing.

[[809]] Inner necessity and outer universality are related as content and extension. The dominion of the necessary law proclaims itself in the appearance that is subjected to it without exception. [[810]] Thus there results a kinship between the modality and the quantity of judgments. The forms of the one category do not stand beside the forms of the other, but ⟦292⟧ are inwardly one, notably at the highest point, necessity and universality.

[[812]] The name of quantity betrays the external point of view that founded this category. One counts the subjects (one, some, all). One takes, it is said, either the whole extension of the subject-concept or only a part. This part is usually not more closely delimited. [[813]] 10. The judgment of content which we pass offers to consideration at *one* stroke the various points of view that have been run through, all at once. The same judgment, this rose is red, is in the same act a judgment of content, affirmative, singular, assertoric. If one holds fast to this, one will not fall into the opinion that there is one negation in the category of quality and another in the category of modality. In the judgment: this rose is not red, modality deems actual what is negated in quality. Here a development shows itself. The judgment of content appears (qualitatively) affirmative and negative; as it ripens from within (modally), it grows in outer power (quantitatively). In this way the determinations of the individual categories fuse.

[[814]] 11. How does the judgment of extension stand toward the same points of view? We consider here its most pronounced form, the disjunctive.

[[816]] ⟦293⟧ First, quality. Since extension comprehends and articulates the positive appearances of the concept, the disjunctive judgment, taken in the proper act of division and of drawing together, cannot be a negative judgment. If the dichotomous division expresses the one member negatively, the negative designation only veils the positive species. The assertion itself is always an affirmative act; for it is the confirmation of the universal in the species.

[[817]] In contradiction with the derivation just attempted, the form A is neither B nor C has been designated as the negative form of the disjunctive judgment (e.g. the circle is neither an ellipse nor a hyperbola). But here one errs, misled by the grammatical expression, since "neither – nor" seems to correspond to the disjunction "either – or." But these particles do not correspond to each other as negation and position. While "either – or" coordinates the species with one another, "neither – nor" is only the opposing form of a double not. The former particles announce the extension, the latter negate the expected representation of a content. In the judgment "the circle is neither an ellipse nor a hyperbola" it is not the sense of the negation to ward off the possible presupposition that the ellipse, the hyperbola, is a species of the circle, but rather to declare the essence and content of the circle, of the ellipse, of the hyperbola to be different. We look in vain in language for a negation of the disjunctive judgment, because division is a deed that generates out of the essence.

[[818]] Let us consider further modality and quantity. The disjunctive judgment presents the activity of the universal as it particularises itself into its species, and in its form makes the claim to exhaust the species which it coordinates side by side. Hence its either, or, its aut, aut, speaks in a tone of imperious necessity. If the division claims to be complete, this ⟦294⟧ claim cannot rest on contingently gathering experience, but must be derived from the essence and content of the concept. Hence the disjunctive judgment, by its own nature, stands at the highest stage of modality and quantity; it is universal and necessary.

[[819]] The disjunctive judgment, the fruit of the mature concept, is prepared by the judgments of content. The conjunctive form gathers the species under the universal concept of the predicate and has for the knowledge of extension only assertoric value. Language has developed yet a partitive form (partly, partly) and applies it where less a logical necessity is asserted than an empirical apprehension of the species is admitted. E.g. men have partly a white, partly a black, partly an olive, partly a copper-red skin colour.

[[820]] Thus the forms of the judgment develop simply and significantly. Their whole system, if one will grant it this name, presents the genesis of the thing.

[[821]] Out of the essence of the concept, which the complete judgment sets in living relation, the two principal forms separate out, the judgment of content and that of extension. The judgment of content is either affirmative or negative. As both forms rise from immediate apprehension to grounded necessity, they at the same time acquire for the concept of the subject extension and range. To intensive modality corresponds extensive quantity. The judgment of extension then arises in its disjunctive form out of the judgment of content ripened in itself, and is in its direction a creative affirmation, in its value universality and necessity.

[[833]] c. The judgment of allness (the universal judgment) leads immediately to the judgment of necessity. Until then the predicate was determined as the universal being in itself over against its subject; according to its content it could be taken as an essential determination of relation or also as a mark. [[842]] What, then, has been achieved in this dialectical derivation?

[[843]] First, something must be remarked about the names of the forms of judgment, which, however, at once touches the thing itself. The terms have remained the same as in the old logic. One believes one has the same thing, but for the most part has an entirely different one. This has already been mentioned of the infinite judgment; of others it must be specially remarked, in order to remove the ambiguity that follows from such arbitrariness.

[[844]] Hegel leaves the positive judgment to the stage of contingent, immediate quality, and distinguishes from this qualitative judgment the categorical (properly the substantial) as a judgment of necessity, e.g. the ring is yellow (positive and qualitative), the ring is gold (categorical). Affirmation, for the expression of which the positive judgment served, was otherwise regarded only as *one* side of judgments; it combined as well with quantity as with relation and modality. With Hegel the positive judgment is relegated to ⟦300⟧ the lowest stage of sensuous existence (the rose is red).

[[847]] The categorical judgment¹ has been restricted to expressing the immanent nature, the kind, as the substance of the subject. According to previous usage the judgment "the ring is yellow" is just as much a categorical one as the judgment "the ring is gold." The new distinction has no full ground. If the judgment "the ring is gold" is called categorical, then in fairness its consequence too, "the ring is yellow," must fall under the same determination. Does not the immediate belong just as much to the immanent nature of the thing? The separation is arbitrary and therefore cancels itself in application. If one declares it a character of the qualitative judgment that its content of immediate existence has been picked up contingently, then such a subjective relation has no right in a doctrine that starts from the premise that things are the judgment. Such a subjective criterion is properly none; for a criterion must lie in the thing.

[[848]] Assertoric, further, was the name for every judgment that claimed to correspond to an actuality. This view, which touches only *one* side of the judgment, fused with the quality, quantity and relation of the judgment. Hegel, however, makes the assertoric judgment into a peculiar, narrowly bounded kind, in that the mere assertion is supposed to have the value of a judicial verdict. Compare the judgments: the house is high (qualitative), the house is a dwelling (categorical), the house is bad (assertoric). Since here only in the last judgment is the thing measured by the concept, according to Hegel only this one is called assertoric. According to the old, well-grounded determination all three judgments are assertoric, ⟦301⟧ without what they otherwise are being thereby impaired; they all assert something as actual. In accordance with the transformation just discussed, the problematic and the apodictic judgment too have received another sense. They are no longer referred to the possible or necessary combination of subject and predicate, as in this meaning they could otherwise unite, as closer determinations, with all the remaining forms of judgment. The problematic judgment is rather supposed, like the judgment of the concept in general, to pass sentence, only in such a way that it makes the verdict dependent on a third in which subject and predicate (house, good) have their connection: the house is good, according as it is constituted. The apodictic judgment has the same end, only it grounds the pronouncement in the particularity of the concept that forms the subject, so that now, in the last and completed kind of judgment, the three moments of the concept come forward and thereby pave the transition to the syllogism. How, then, does the essence of the old name relate to the new meanings? In the name of the problematic judgment the doubtful outcome of a disputed question is indicated, in the apodictic the necessity of proof. Assertion, question, proof extend over the whole domain of knowledge, and there is no right whatever to claim these names, stamped in the wider sense of modality, solely for the restricted use of a judgment passing sentence according to the concept. The stamp and content of these words have another value. Is language so poor, then, that one can take the designations of new concepts only from the unlawful spoils of well-grounded names? The other sciences watch more carefully that no confusion of language arise. Logic, which cannot, as the natural sciences perhaps can, at once find its bearings in the intuition of a fixed and finished object, philosophy, which must place everything in the inner and self-active generation of concepts and has in ⟦302⟧ names its only external support, ought to hold the property of scientific language all the more sacred. Is it, then, by names that one should and will understand one another, or misunderstand?

[[850]] We ask further about the immanent necessity which the development claims. In linking the forms, the dialectic essentially ties the following knots.

[[851]] It starts from the immediate and frees itself through mediation into the self-determined whole; it therefore begins with the judgment of contingent sensuous quality and completes itself in the apodictic judgment, which, directed to the whole of the concept, presents a necessary bond of subject and predicate. This course from the immediate and contingent to the self-determined and necessary whole, from the external to its own freedom, is the general progress of the dialectic whenever the concept of a thing develops in a determinate domain.

[[852]] The immediate – a thoroughly logical word – conceals intuition, which the logic of pure thought cannot yet know, and lends the logical semblance where in fact perception is meant. The immediate has its logical content only in the negation of logical mediation. But if, in this determination of the dialectic, one presses for the affirmative concept, there at once emerges from the background of a presupposed mass of thought a representation which must otherwise be foreign to the logic of pure thinking. This is not a consequence foisted upon the dialectic, but comes clearly forward in tacitly inserted explanations. Thus it is said expressly: "The immediate judgment is the judgment of existence; the subject posited in a universality as its predicate, which is an immediate (hence sensuous) ⟦303⟧ quality." Whence, then, the sensuous in a logical development which has promised to move, without presuppositions, only in thought freed from the sensuous? In the concept of the immediate, pushed forward here, lies, as in so many places, the illusion of the pure presuppositionless dialectic. Whoever calmly resolves it will find that it is only a negative concept ("not mediate"). But such a concept has support only insofar as it knows how to fuse with a foreign but solid mass.¹

[[854]] Let the starting-point, however, be granted, the unlogical but broad basis of the sensuous. The rose is red. The individual is universal. How does this first state of the matter lead further? Rather, it is said, such an immediate individual is not universal. The subject, immediately being for itself, is the opposite of abstract universality. Consequently the negative follows at once upon the positive as the higher truth. First it must be remarked that in the individual case no negative judgment arises in the way it is here derived in general. Otherwise from the judgment "the rose is red" the judgment "the rose is not red" would have to come forth. The negative judgment has its backing precisely in another, positive one. Insofar as one positive determination raises objection against another positive one that was tried, the negative judgment arises as the defence which the positive judgment performs.³ So the negative judgment is grounded in all the sciences. Compare only its extensive use in indirect proof, where a firm proposition or the concatenation of the whole repels an attempted positive assertion in a negative judgment. Because precisely a ⟦304⟧ determinate generalisation is acknowledged (the rose is red), another must be negated (the rose is not white). If now no individual case of a negative judgment as it arises corresponds to the universal dialectical development, then the universal has thereby ceased to be, in the same sense in which number ceases when it no longer comprehends units under itself. The universal now only hovers, like a relationless process, high above the individual.

[[858]] But we must follow the universal even into this height. The rose is red. The individual is universal. So runs the premise from which the opposite at once springs forth: the concrete is not abstract, the individual is not universal, therefore the rose is not red. But what does the comprehensive expression of the positive judgment mean: "the individual is universal"? The sense can be twofold. First, the individual is universal insofar as it develops activities, properties, which other individuals also develop. In this sense of the common the universal does not contradict the individual. In being active the thing steps out of isolation into the common world and must to that extent be universal. Secondly, the individual can also be called universal because the determination of the predicate goes back to that community of thinking and being which underlies the concept in general. In this sense too, that the individual is rooted in a thought concept, the universal does not contradict the individual. But at this stage of the immediate and sensuous this meaning will have to be set aside. On what, then, does the dialectical advance still rest? "The individual is universal. But the individual is not universal; more precisely, such an individual quality does not correspond to the concrete nature of the subject." The whole motion lies in an arbitrary change of representation. The individual has revealed itself according to the content of the positive judgment. We express it: the individual is ⟦305⟧ universal. But at once the individual is drawn back into itself and the concept of the individual is forcibly shut up within itself, so that its revelation of the universal must now appear as a contradiction driving further. The stress betrays this interplay. First it is said: the individual is universal; then: but the individual is not universal. One should least of all expect such a contrived difficulty in the course of a development which started precisely from the premise that all things are a judgment, i.e. "individuals which are a universality within themselves."¹

[[859]] The negative judgment intensifies itself into the negation of the judgment itself. In the identical judgment the relation of subject and predicate is empty, in the infinite absurd. Thus the judgment of existence falls apart; it has sublated itself, and the determinations of the judgment are reflected into themselves. Hence arise the judgments of reflection, the singular, particular, universal judgment.

[[860]] This transition from the judgment of existence to the judgment of comprehension is peculiar. We know of no counterpart to it in the whole range of the rest of the dialectic. Elsewhere the third moment reconciles thesis and antithesis, and it can at least be understood in general how a new sphere of concepts is supposed to spring forth from this positive mediation. It can be conceived, for instance, how from becoming, which fuses being and not-being, existence is born. Elsewhere a living germ is always left behind in the third moment, which shoots up and ripens in the next concepts. Has this happened in our case? The identical judgment is hollow; the infinite has denied the sense of all judging. The previous cycle therefore does not close in the third moment, but "falls apart" in itself. Where is there here even the rudiment of a germ, even the beginning of a new start, even the possibility of a ⟦306⟧ rebirth? On the ruins of the judgment of existence the judgment of reflection is supposed to rise. When it is said that the judgment of existence has sublated itself, this sublation is admittedly a "falling apart," so that here one can by no means, as is otherwise asserted, see in sublation something that still preserves. There is *one* thing more. In the natural life of concepts linking and loosening themselves, such infinite judgments as, e.g., spirit is not sour, is not a table, etc., occur as good as never. In ordinary thinking there would therefore be no transition from the judgment of existence to the judgment of reflection. The second form would sprout without roots. However, we are content. The original dialectic will not concern itself with the sauntering gait of vulgar thinking.

[[862]] We grant this transition for the time being, although we do not understand it, and consider the further advance. The singular judgment runs: a this is a universal. But a this is not an existence commensurate with a universal. Hence rather not a this is a universal; and there arises the particular judgment: some thises are a universal. In this form too subject and predicate are still incommensurate. Particularity (some) therefore extends itself to universality – and indeed first to allness in the universal judgment. The dialectical impulse of this motion comes from the fact that the subject is not commensurate with the universal predicate of comprehension (useful, heavy, sour, elastic), and it aims at equalising the two. The heightened predicate of more universal relativity, in contrast to immediate quality, is indeed not grounded in what precedes, but we accept it without conceding it.

[[863]] Does the advance, then, follow? In the individual case never. E.g. "this solution is acid" is a correct judgment. But not a this is universal. Therefore some solutions are acid. However, since now some (solutions) are the universal, particularity has extended itself to universality. ⟦307⟧ Consequently all solutions are acid. As little as this argumentation is correct in this case, so little is it correct in any. What extension a judgment has must be carefully observed or proved from the nature of the individual thing, and cannot be settled by a universality hovering above it. Thus the conception refutes itself in application.

[[864]] But the theory too falls apart in itself. The advance takes place in order that the external individualities which the subject presents may become equal to the universality of the predicate. Before this happens, subject and predicate are not commensurate. But it is only a semblance that the judgment of allness reaches this goal. The predicate always remains more universal and still comprehends various species under itself. If, e.g., one had gained as the end-point of the motion the judgment: all metals are heavy, usable, etc., the predicates nevertheless go far beyond the allness of this *one* genus.

[[865]] With the words "Some are the universal; thus particularity is extended to universality"¹ the advance is introduced. What we remarked above, at the transition of the positive into the negative judgment, about the concept of the universal holds here too. "Some are universal" is the formula of the particular judgment; but the concept "universal" does not here have the meaning of number (some are all), nor can one admit the expression "some are the universal," a designation, as it seems, substituted for the sake of an easier transition. "Some this is universal." This extension into the particular judgment is supposed to contradict the this; for some is indeterminate, this determinate; hence the judgment must pass over into determinate allness in order to be commensurate with the this. This proof refutes itself. If "some thises," i.e. some substances, some things, contained a contradiction that wanted to be resolved, ⟦308⟧ then it would also be a contradiction to count things. Moreover, the particular judgment for the most part designates nothing indeterminate, but a determinate part of the sphere, a species.

[[867]] Numerical allness attests the essence of the kind. Hence the universal judgment passes over into a judgment of necessity, first into the categorical, which expresses the genus. But since the concept of the subject differentiates itself within itself without forfeiting its identity, since it therefore repels itself from itself into actuality, the hypothetical judgment arises. If in this self-externalisation of the concept the inner identity is posited, the universal is the genus, which is identical with itself in its excluding individuality. This third form, the disjunctive judgment, therefore gathers the differences of the second into the unity of the first.

[[868]] This whole development has the greatest similarity with the development of substantiality, causality and reciprocity. But then similar misgivings remain as with Kant. If the disjunctive judgment were to present the unity of the hypothetical and the categorical, the concept, in repelling itself from itself in the hypothetical judgment, would have to generate species. For it is said expressly: "in this self-externalisation of the concept (which the hypothetical judgment contains) the inner identity posited," and the disjunctive judgment comes forth. One can at once form a hypothetical judgment from a disjunctive one, grounded on the exclusion of the species; but not, conversely, a disjunctive from a hypothetical. A is either B or C, therefore if A is B, it is not C. But from the proposition: if A is B, it is not C, that coordination of disjunct concepts standing on the same line does not follow; the proposition presents a much more universal relation.

[[869]] The disjunctive judgment completes the striven-for equalisation of subject and predicate, in that once the universal as such, the other time the circle of its mutually ⟦309⟧ exclusive particularisation, is posited. Hence there now appears the judgment of totality. In the assertoric, problematic and apodictic judgment the concept in its totality is the measure by which to pronounce whether the subject agrees with it or not. Since Hegel sought the concepts of the possible and the necessary purely in the thing,¹ he also had to place these forms differently from before.

[[871]] We pass over the fact that such an apodictic judgment is properly already a formal syllogism, whereas it is supposed only to pave the transition to the syllogism.

[[872]] *One* thing must be especially striking. According to the express declaration, the judgment is not to be taken in a subjective sense, as an operation and form occurring merely in self-conscious thinking. "Since this distinction is not yet present at all in the logical, the judgment is quite universal, and all things are a judgment." Accordingly the real judgment, which is the soul of things, ought to be unfolded. Whence, then, on this straight path, such side-leaps as the identical judgment, which has no content, the infinite judgment, which is absurd and therefore cannot be a judgment of the thing, and the problematic judgment, which is nevertheless literally designated as mere assurance, as subjective particularity? One may seek for the infinite judgment an actuality in the sphere of freedom (in crime); this hardly gives it a right to a place in the development of the forms of judgment of immediate, "hence sensuous," quality.

[[873]] The fundamental defect of this whole derivation seems to lie in this, that the forms are not developed from their peculiar end and content, but are supposed to generate one another for themselves, as forms. It is not the content that is to form the form of the judgment, as content and form are otherwise one, ⟦310⟧ but the earlier form is supposed to put the later out of itself, e.g. the positive judgment is supposed immediately to bring forth the negative, the particular the universal, and so on. A life is ascribed to mere forms which they nowhere have. Such a dialectic of the forms of judgment is not much better than if one were to arrange and present the organs of local motion, the instruments of swimming, flying, walking, creeping, in such a way that the one was supposed to spring from the other. One could, in comparing them, devise kinships and transitions. But the forms would nevertheless have another origin. They do not descend from one another in a progressive course, and one will comprehend them only by looking to the element for which they are destined, to the body which they are to move, and so on. Such organs, generated by the end of the content, are the judgments too, and in vain does one implant in dependent forms, with a violent fancy that is called dialectic, an independent development.

[[878]] Let one try, following the direction of the dialectical intermediate links, to raise any example from the lowest stage through the continuum of all the forms to the highest stage of the apodictic judgment. Let one keep strictly to the prescription of the dialectical turns of thought, so that, according to the indications, not only the universal form of the judgment changes, but likewise the content of the predicate rises from sensuous existence to concepts of reflection, from these to the substance of the thing, and finally to the judicial verdict of the concept. Were the general development correct, this attempt would have to succeed in the individual case. But why, then, has it nowhere been carried out? If one begins it, one soon feels the impossibility. Here the apparently tenable thoughts of transition collapse when one sets one's foot on them; there the connection is cut off from the outset. The whole difficult derivation offers the peculiar spectacle of a universal that comprehends no individual under itself.

[[879]] ⟦311⟧ In Hegel's exposition psychological elements cross the objective dialectic. If one wishes to pursue the psychological development, one will indeed see the judgments that rest on experience reach the stage of the concept from the stage of perception only by passing through reflection, but the positive as well as the negative, the singular as well as the particular, the categorical as well as the conjunctive. These three stages are the ones which present themselves predominantly in modality. But then the whole division will turn out otherwise than with Hegel, and one will be able to assign neither the positive and negative judgment to immediacy alone, nor the universal to reflection alone, nor the categorical and hypothetical to necessity alone, and one will be compelled to let fall the kinds which Hegel placed under the concept, in the sense in which he took them. The judgments of immediacy (unsuitably called judgments of quality), of reflection and of the concept thereby receive another meaning and their connection in the subjective mind: Hegel has, in his system of the judgment, welded together the logical and the psychological.
