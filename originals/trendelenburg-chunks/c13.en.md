[[882]] # ⟦312⟧ XVII. GROUNDING.

[[883]] 1. The individual judgment springs from the concept like a single ray. Since it represents a living activity, it excites other judgments in the measure in which one activity excites another. The judgment acts, like activity, generatively, groundingly, and on this side it itself cancels its isolation.

[[884]] The judgment ripens as it passes through the modal stages. The problematic and the apodictic judgment, resting on conditions, already indicate in their form that they presuppose a grounding. The activity of the substance is excited and conditioned by an external cause; the judgment by an external ground.

[[885]] Thus the judgment is given back into the grounding connection, and it seeks the higher whole in which it has its subsistence as a member.

[[886]] The judgment becomes clear by isolating itself, but it becomes firm by linking itself into a chain.

[[887]] 2. Necessary grounding takes place from a universal, as became evident above; for only from the living universal, in which thinking and being touch and interpenetrate, does the necessary proceed.

[[888]] Even the individual judgment which merely expresses the given ⟦313⟧ fact as a result of perception already stands, in the midst of the individual, upon the universal, before it seeks any grounding at all. For the senses, which are the organ of the individual, behave universally, inasmuch as their power spans a whole domain of objects, and they themselves carry within them the element of motion through which they are accessible to universal thought. If the individual judgment of sense perception, which still carries within it the whole changeableness of the subjective, is to be verified, this takes place through means that are designed from the universal. The observational sciences, which establish individual judgments, have developed, out of a scepticism of exactness, a peculiar logic of the senses. Thus, for example, the knowledge of heat is not left to wavering feeling, but is referred to the thermometer or pyrometer. The decisive observation now falls not to the indeterminate vital feeling, but to the sharper sense of sight. The possibility of such a translation from the subjective into the objective stems from the thought of the universal. When the senses are armed in order to determine indeterminate perception, this takes place out of the universal. The microscope and the telescope are grounded on an insight into the universal nature of sight and of the ray of light. Science becomes so objective out of the universal that it makes even illusion its object, for example the double image of the single object in the double refraction of certain crystals. Time falls only within inner sense; but since it is the number of motion, the mind forces it, out of this universal thought, into the realm of outer observation, as happens in the ingenious clock, and so on. When in history or in the transmission of literature the individual wavers, criticism ⟦314⟧ steps in with its universal judgment. In these few examples the fact of science sufficiently presents itself, that the verification of the individual judgment as individual rests on a universal. Subjective perception is objectified through the dexterity of universal thought. Thus the universal nature reveals itself in the individual. The universal in general does not let the individual go, but leads it back to itself.

[[890]] If, now, grounding stems from the universal, whence, then, is the universal grounded?

[[891]] An ultimate principle lies in the universal itself. We appeal to the whole preceding investigation of principles and of the ground. But for human knowledge something else must not be overlooked. Necessity includes within itself the universality of the actual. The necessary is universally actual. The power of the ground presents itself outwardly in the universal appearance, and this cannot be comprehended without that. If, therefore, the universal is an exclusive property of the necessary, one can infer back from the universal fact to the ground. In this case the universal flows from observation.

[[892]] 3. It has already been shown above that the universal ⟦315⟧ as ground and as fact must be distinguished. According as the method has the one or the other for its goal or for its beginning, it will be different.

[[893]] > (1) See above section XIII. modal categories. vol. II. p. 201 ff. There the misunderstanding (Ueberweg's Logik. 1857. §. 101. p. 263 ff.) has already been forestalled, as though the universal of fact were meant to signify that it is, like facts otherwise, always drawn from experience. Kant extended the usage of the word (Kr. d. Urtheilskraft p. 451. 1st ed. 1790). The mathematical proposition that all plane triangles have the sum of their angles equal to two right angles expresses, although proved, a universal of fact (of the actual) just as much as the empirical one: all men are mortal. The mathematical proposition, in this its form, states only what is, and only as such a universal of the genus. In opposition to a universal which, together with others, creates as ground, it has been called, as a resting universal that merely states how all individuals of a genus behave in actuality, a universal of fact. In this connection the expression is meant to designate only that a universal is asserted as actual.

[[894]] Induction sums up from the individual the fact of the universal; the syllogism infers from the fact of the universal the individual. The analytic procedure seeks from the given appearance the universal ground; the synthetic constructs from the universal ground the appearances as consequence.

[[895]] 4. Let the following serve provisionally to elucidate these four modes of grounding.

[[896]] The opposition which in the first place governs the division, so that the path is taken now from the individual to the universal, now from the universal to the individual, is none other than the old opposition in which the highest difference of thinking and being has been more closely determined, and which, with slight alteration of the thought, is called now law and appearance, now content and extension, now concept and intuition. Grounding, which is to penetrate both, starts from the one and proceeds to the other, or conversely.

[[897]] 5. The analytic and the inductive procedure, which both start from the given appearance, do not simply coincide. While the analytic procedure takes the appearance apart or works it through in order to seize in the appearance the producing ground, induction leaves the individual as it is and merely gathers it together in what it has in common, in order to sketch the universality of the appearance. When, for example, empirical grammar collects the rule from individual cases (say, that ut, "in order that," governs the subjunctive), it proceeds inductively; but when it seeks the ground of this universal appearance (say, in the agreement of the concept of the conjunction and the concept of the mood, since both in the first instance designate the possibility of the thought), it proceeds analytically. ⟦316⟧ Induction prepares analysis, since the universal fact is an expression of the necessary ground.

[[898]] 6. The logical value of induction is not to be discussed at length here. Certainly it cannot close itself off into a whole by itself, and it remains open to the chance of a contradicting experience, and its universality is therefore incomplete, properly nothing but the greater or smaller sum of the observed appearances. Certainly induction already presupposes a universal under whose guidance it stands and for whose ends it works; and it is therefore helpless by itself. But notwithstanding this it is of far-reaching significance. Though its result may not be absolutely grounded, it nevertheless grounds other judgments dependent on it, even if on no surer footing than it has itself. Its result is provisional, but it is a transition to a firm possession. External universality takes the place of the universality that is thought and seen through; and it so governs thinking that many remarkable inventions of experience rest upon it, while insight into the process follows only late upon the skilfully used fact. Induction is, as for example in statistics, the collector of experience, which sets research its task. The a priori sciences know no induction proper; where they employ it, a deduction, a synthetic procedure, is interwoven with it. But the ⟦317⟧ empirical sciences owe to it the whole firm and full content of which they are proud.

[[901]] 7. The syllogism is more than a mere inversion of induction, although it otherwise stands opposed to it in its direction. The difference lies in this, that the universal fact is indeed striven for by induction but never reached, whereas the syllogism must assert its major premise as absolutely universal if it does not wish to founder entirely. But is it then right to designate the basis of the syllogism as a mere universal fact? By this expression it is by no means meant that the fact from which the syllogism starts has merely been gathered up into a collective universality (see p. 314 note). A law is expressed which is there. Whence it stems remains undecided. To be sure, it will be the brief result of an inner grounding if it is to have the right to reach out over the individual; but for subsumption only the universal fact comes into consideration. In the syllogism, for example, that in all triangles, and hence also in the right-angled one, the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles, the major premise, in all triangles the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles, is expressed merely as a universal fact. Since, however, the law is derived from the genesis of the triangle itself, it has a quite different significance from the universal fact of induction. The latter rests on limited observation, the former on unlimited insight into the essence and becoming of the triangle. The next section is to treat of the syllogism in particular.

[[902]] 8. Syllogism and synthetic method agree in this, that they both start from the universal, and they are therefore very frequently declared to be the same mode of procedure. The difference, however, soon becomes evident if one distinguishes the universal as fact and as ground.¹ The former ⟦318⟧ procedure has merely the power of an external subsumption, the latter the force of a progressively forming generation. The syllogism moves within the same genus; synthesis can generate new ones for thought. The syllogism contains in the universal law of the major premise the whole ground of the subsumption; synthesis at first brings forward only *one* condition, which it must combine with others in order to produce, as ground, something new. The major premise of the syllogism cannot be grounded on and on by a syllogism; otherwise we fall into an infinite series of prosyllogisms and episyllogisms and find a determinate hold nowhere. The first generating activity, which is certain in itself, precedes the resting, self-enclosed law of the major premise. The transition to the concept of a new genus, the activity which, according to an inner connection, carries the genera over into new shapes, lies beyond the syllogism, whose power is only formal, not real, like that of synthesis.

[[903]] > (1) See above section XIII. modal categories. vol. II. p. 201 ff.

[[904]] When the syllogism is worked out: in all triangles the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles, the right-angled triangle is a triangle, therefore in the right-angled triangle the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles, the universal acts only in its dominion over what is given and finished. When, on the other hand, the same proposition is applied to calculate the magnitude of the angle of a regular polygon, it acts productively (synthetically), in that it fuses with the original construction, conditioned by no syllogism, which divides the polygon into regular triangles and composes it again out of them. In the first case the procedure remains within the sphere of the same concept; in the other it generates something new. Geometry is famous for its strictly syllogistic proofs. It transforms the elements of space-generating motion into universal propositions and then gives every advance the appearance of a purely syllogistic subsumption. One easily notices, however, the ⟦319⟧ synthetic determinations which run alongside the syllogism. Drobisch¹ has, for example, analysed in an instructive way the proof of the theorem that parallelograms on the same base and between the same parallels are equal to one another in area, and has resolved the intricate chain of syllogisms into its simple subordination. Syllogism follows upon syllogism. But the synthetic elements act through all the syllogisms; they are, first, the first principles of construction, then the division of the parallelogram into triangles and its composition out of the triangles, finally the superposition for the cognition of congruence, which rests on a free transference of the figure in indifferent space. The difference between the subordinating syllogism and generating synthesis can be shown in many examples. When the conditions of the similarity of triangles are applied to the smaller triangles which arise in the right-angled triangle through the perpendicular dropped from the vertex, the similarity of these among themselves and with the enclosing triangle proceeds purely through the syllogism. When, on the other hand, the same law of similarity is used in order to derive from it, with the help of proportions and equations and through substitution of equivalent values, trigonometrical formulae, and again by means of these to determine new triangles or to solve higher equations, or when the same law of similarity is used to prove on the cone the fundamental property of the parabola, that the abscissae are to one another as the squares of the corresponding ordinates, then the same universal acts as a member of a development; and in the individual cases it will be easy to find the construction or combination ⟦320⟧ that intervenes creatively in the syllogisms. The further the object removes itself from the simple, a priori element of mathematical consideration, the more the determinations grow together empirically, the more the synthetic procedure outweighs the syllogism. Only try the syllogistic form when one wishes to comprehend an individual fact of history (and we certainly do comprehend it from the universal). In another way the end, which is universal in itself, acts not immediately syllogistically, but synthetically.

[[907]] Since the universal of the ground has its outward expression in the universal of fact, and the latter springs forth from the former as extension from content, the syllogistic procedure goes protectively alongside the synthetic as its outward exposition. From the broad base of the universal the thoughts taper to a point until with the point of the apex they reach the individual point that is to be grounded. The universal mind thereby itself becomes the matter. Thought is itself conscious of its strictness and therein, for itself, initially secure. But if it wishes to present what it has grasped to itself or to others, the binding, subordinating syllogisms serve to present visibly the invisible course of thought and to grasp the final point from the same breadth of the first universality. The individual glance of synthesis is related to the syllogistic unwinding as judgment by eye to the surveyor's chain. That immediate connection is, as it were, the community of thinking and being continued creatively from the principles, the deed of genius; but for its own certainty and for recognition by others it must submit itself to mediation. Only thus does the secure common possession of science arise.

[[908]] 9. The analytic and the synthetic procedure are, in the point from which they start and in the direction which they pursue, as clearly distinguished as oppositions in general. But the separation is more difficult if one observes them in their progress. Are they separated there too, ⟦321⟧ and can they both, each alone by itself, the one without the other, continue their way and reach their goal?

[[909]] The analytic procedure seeks from the given appearances the shaping ground; the synthetic designs the appearances from the ground it has grasped. We shall therefore have to regard as an analytic element what can be understood only from the appearance, as a synthetic element what can be understood only from the ground.

[[910]] Mathematics, with methodical acumen, first distinguished analysis and synthesis and lent to the other sciences, for kindred relations, the names which in the first instance apply to the outer intuition of spatial magnitude. Arithmetic, which derives all the laws of the operations from the genesis of number through combination and is active out of the ground of the matter, is rightly designated as synthetic. Algebra behaves analytically in its direction, since it posits the equation as possible, like something given, and seeks its roots, that is, the grounds which satisfy the equation. Euclid's Elements, starting from the simplest grounds and advancing through construction to the developed figures, proceed synthetically, his Data analytically. The course of the empirical sciences is analytic, that of the speculative ones synthetic. Thus the relation presents itself in general if one has in view the beginning and the direction of these disciplines.

[[911]] 10. We first follow the analytic path, in order to see whether by itself it leads to the goal.

[[912]] What compels the mind to go beyond the appearances? What gives it everywhere the direction toward the ground from which they derive? In fact a synthetic element reveals itself in the direction of the analytic. Only because ⟦322⟧ the nature of the mind is itself creative, only because it produces appearances of a similar essence, does it seek the producing ground. Otherwise, as the animal grazes the meadow, it would calmly accept the present and seek nothing further.

[[913]] > (1) Cf., however, Hegel Logik III. p. 282, who restricts the meaning of the analytic to the identical and calls arithmetic, despite the laws which it generates with the matter itself, an analytic science.

[[914]] Even perception itself, which does not passively receive what is given but re-creates in response to the stimulus received, might from one side be called synthetic; for the appearances are united into a whole. Observation is synthetic in its innermost nature; for it is observation only in so far as, guided by the universal, it is directed to what is essential. Perception would glide along as over the wide undifferentiated surface of the water, and nothing would stand out above it, if observation did not divine and pursue what is more essential in the matter. Analysis dissects the appearances; but in order to hit upon the members, it must guess their significance and in turn distinguish according to the essence. Thus the first step of the analytic procedure is already synthetic; for what is essential is measured only by the determinations of the ground.

[[915]] If perception is to verify itself, it does so through universal considerations that are added to it. Illusion of the senses already induces the ordinary man to practise on a small scale what on a large scale science calls hypothesis. What meets the eye is confirmed or refuted through its harmony or disharmony with the whole of perception and with the other marks.

[[916]] The given material of the senses is worked up in analysis and transformed into concepts. The contingent is stripped off; what remains and persists is grasped. That what is essential appears in the latter is a synthetic presupposition of the mind.

[[917]] The analytic concept is completed only when it takes the ground up into itself. But the ground is grasped only when a possibility proves so fruitful that it is able to generate the ⟦323⟧ appearances which form the task of analysis. The mind must provisionally exploit a point and, as it were, foresee its logical yield before it introduces it even as a problematic ground of the given appearances. This intervention of synthesis in analysis, or properly this supplementing of analysis by synthesis, appears most clearly where the ground runs out into an end, for example in the analysis of the organic. Since here the continuous connection of the efficient cause breaks off, which otherwise gives the appearance of an exclusive, solely active analysis, different directions must be linked into a unity of thought which can be accessible only to the mind that looks ahead.¹ Thus the analytic procedure ends with a synthetic moment.

[[918]] If the ground is to verify itself, it must unfold synthetically on all sides and measure itself against the appearances which it is to satisfy. This final comparison is again analytic; but it is possible only after the completed process of synthesis. In hypothesis, which is peculiar to the analytic procedure, analysis and synthesis touch one another most intimately and strive to regulate and balance each other. Analysis, in proud possession of the facts, asks synthesis whether it is able to generate and exhaust them. Where the task of analysis is not yet reached by synthesis, the latter, with the preponderance of the intellectual ground, asks in turn, backwards, whether observation and dissection, and accordingly the task, have been correctly determined by analysis. Thus the two procedures sharpen each other.

[[919]] It always remains the essence of the analytic procedure that it draws the firm line of the appearances and thereby affords fixed points to inquiry into grounds. What gives it value is not merely the external force of what exists and ⟦324⟧ is actual; for such barriers the mind would break, but would not be willing to recognise. In the facts which the analytic procedure investigates, the re-creating synthesis beholds the signals by which it has to direct itself in its motions. The more securely the points of that line are determined, the more sharply can the line running through them be drawn, the more easily and certainly is the formula found which satisfies it and contains the generating ground.

[[921]] The analytic sciences attest in detail what has just been presented in general. Analysis advances only with the help of synthesis; but synthesis is here always determined by the beginning and the direction of analysis.

[[922]] In the analytic problem of geometry, what is required is provisionally sketched, and it is asked under what conditions such a sketch becomes possible out of what is given. The resolution of the sketch leads to the means of execution. The provisional construction, the discovery of the mutual relations, the linking with the means are synthetic elements in it. If the direction of equations is analytic, in that the roots (the possible grounds) are to be sought, then the operations for this end, and indeed not merely the application of foreign trigonometrical formulae but even the simplest transpositions and eliminations, are synthetic combinations. One detaches the terms one after another, to be sure, in order to isolate the value of the unknown; but the means of this analytic procedure are synthetic, taken from the universal law of the genesis of numbers.

[[923]] Physics has become great through induction and analysis. But only mathematical synthesis completes its theories; and that its dissection ends in the most creative concept is proved by its results. When we hear, say in optics, that 458 billion vibrations of the ether in one second, and waves of which 37640 go to an inch, ⟦325⟧ produce the sensation of a red colour, or indeed that 727 billion vibrations produce the extreme violet, no one will any longer divine in these enormous numbers the dissection of simple red or violet. And yet they have been calculated from the phenomena of interference. So they have been found by analysis? Rather, the presupposition of wave motion, and the appearance that is necessary if such waves should meet, form the synthesis of such an analysis. Astronomy, in the Copernican system, has inverted the appearances of analysis through the boldest synthesis. Physiology stands on analytic anatomy and on the sharp observation of the phenomena of life; but the ingenious construction of the living process out of the individual data, the organic interaction of the parts toward the whole, are its synthesis.

[[924]] The experiment is peculiar to the natural sciences. Grown out of the mystical alchemy of the Middle Ages, it now serves as the most significant organ of lucid physics and is the mightiest vehicle of its progress. Bacon of Verulam demanded above all, for his induction and analysis, the service and the warrant of the experiment. Is the experiment still analytic? In the experiment a question is put to nature, and the outcome gives the answer. The end of the experiment is synthetic. The arrangement of the experiment is its proper soul, observation only its passive side. Hence the execution of the experiment is synthetic too.

[[925]] Within this universal determination we can distinguish a twofold direction of the experiment. The experiment presents outwardly the essential directions of mental activity; it is either abstraction become external, in order to isolate the intertwined activities and to relate them, as in their essence, as it were to themselves (the experiments with the air pump may serve as an example), or it is combination become external, so that the isolated activities, in meeting with others, may reveal their ⟦326⟧ hidden essence, as for example in the experiments of electromagnetism. The experiment is nothing other than the activity of the mind become objective, the mind which has the things carry out the abstraction or combination that it cannot carry through to the result by itself. In this respect one can say that in these kinds of experiments analysis and synthesis again present themselves. But just as abstraction is directed to what is essential, and to that extent is guided by an anticipating synthesis in order to reach its end, so too the analytic experiment is essentially synthetic. The question which the mind puts to nature, the means which it employs in order to compel nature to a pure answer, evidently stem from the divined or already known ground of things; they are synthetic.

[[926]] There are sciences which admit of no experiment and whose object is accessible only to calm consideration. The more individual the object is, and the more, therefore, it realises its own thought, the less does it permit the intervention of an external arrangement. Consideration must investigate it as it is. But in these sciences too synthesis at once meets analysis. Grammar dissects the forms and finds through analytic comparison the transitions of sounds; its path is not purely analytic, but the analogy which it follows in the significance of the forms, the insight into the possibility of articulated sounds, into the linking of sound and concept, the understanding which always takes place from the whole, and so on, are synthetic elements with which it is equipped in the dissection of the appearances. In history, criticism proceeds analytically when it collects and compares the testimonies, but synthetically in deciding their value and determining the doubtful fact accordingly. Historical exposition may be called analytic so long as it follows chronology; but when it marks what is essential with stronger strokes, or even wishes to comprehend the ages out of the course of the whole, out of the natural elements of the ⟦327⟧ geographical and the national, out of the development of what is human, it becomes synthetic. Matters stand in a similar way in the other sciences which, starting from the observation of the actual and working only for it, have in the first instance an analytic character.

[[927]] Common to all the sciences is the indirect proof. As they seek the ground, various possibilities offer themselves. Only from the consequences of the possible grounds can it be recognised which agrees with the appearances and which does not. The struggle of hypotheses presents this side of the indirect proof on a large scale. The possibilities are exploited, and there appears therein even a synthesis of what does not take place, a synthesis of what is false for the question at hand, so that it may make itself known as impossible. This synthesis of the false must serve to limit the possibilities until they contract to the *one* actual ground.

[[928]] Because the possibility of error lies close to the self-activity of synthesis, the analytic sciences would gladly confine all knowledge to bound observation. But despite this ban, the creative mind tacitly does the best in them. Synthesis, turned toward the whole and toward the ground, is the nobility of the sciences. But it is admittedly arbitrariness if it does not submit itself to the strict discipline of the analytic method.

[[929]] 11. The analytic method, accordingly, builds no science without the synthetic. We therefore ask further how the synthetic procedure fares without the analytic.

[[930]] Pure thinking would be purely synthetic; since it would be imageless and without intuition, it would also not have a remnant of appearance which it could dissect. But in accordance with our investigations we have had to deny entirely such a pure thinking, for us human beings a non-entity. ⟦328⟧ Rather, the first activity of thinking at once generates an intuition. Once it is generated, the fixed image acts back on the invisible thought, and in the analysis of the product synthesis has its verification. In this way the generating force flows back multiplied from the counter-image.

[[931]] What results here from universal relations is confirmed in the factual state of the sciences. The geometry of Euclid is synthetic; for it knows by generating. It proceeds with the elements of the matter itself. But its proofs are in part analytic. It demonstrates, for example, the Pythagorean theorem by dividing up the constructed squares, hence by dissecting the appearance.

[[932]] The synthetic procedure of the end is at the same time an analytic one, in that the means are found from the execution as thought. This analytic procedure appears most purely in the treatment of the geometrical problem, which is synthetic in its demand.

[[933]] The most external appearance of synthesis is combination. Taken by itself alone, it becomes a contingent throwing together, and the glance matured in the dissection of the matter stands far higher than the formal completeness of the synthetic calculus of combinations, which some here and there wish to take as the basis of logic as the proper principle of thinking.

[[934]] 12. The yield of all these considerations is simple. The analytic and the synthetic procedure are determined only by the starting point and the direction; in execution the one demands the other. Analysis without synthesis remains on the surface of the appearances, in the infinity of the individual; synthesis without analysis remains in groundless thought. In grounding, analysis draws the firm boundaries; synthesis gives the motion within them.

[[935]] ⟦329⟧ Thus the mind acts wholly in every single direction and in every part of its activity; it invents while it dissects, and dissects when it invents. "Analysis and synthesis, both together like breathing out and breathing in, make the life of science."

[[936]] 13. In agreement with the preceding considerations, we attempt in addition a survey in the following way.

[[937]] If in knowing thinking and what is at first stand opposed to one another, then what is is an activity from itself, and thinking an activity after it. Such an activity as the latter is possible only in that what is, as activity, is connected with activities of whose causality we have consciousness. Our own conscious causality opens up to us the causality of others, conscious and unconscious.

[[938]] This conscious causality we exercise, first through constructive motion, purely and determined only through itself, in the mathematical domain, and pure mathematics is an expansion of this conscious causality and of its great consequence in all knowledge. With similar power a conscious causality recurs in the ethical domain and, from our own conscious end, casts a light backwards on the blind end in nature. In the end that first relation is reversed, and thinking is now the activity from itself which what is must follow. Only in a conditioned sense and in part by detours do we have consciousness of causality in the domain of material forces. We ourselves are, in the great causal connection of the world, effect and cause at once, and exercise certain effects with consciousness, for example when the hand presses. From these few, with the help of observation, of experiment and of the mathematical element, knowledge spreads with ever greater sharpness and in ever greater ⟦330⟧ extent. Thus the ground of all science is set upon consciousness and self-activity.
