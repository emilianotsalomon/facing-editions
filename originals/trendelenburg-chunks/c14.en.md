[[942]] # ⟦331⟧ XVIII. THE SYLLOGISM.

[[943]] 1. Syllogisms are distinguished into mediate and immediate. The latter need no new concept in order to produce a new judgment from a judgment, but ground another judgment from the mere form of one judgment. In this way no properly new content of the judgment is gained, but only a more determinate relation for a given purpose. The only question here is what is expressed together with the judgment that has been passed.

[[944]] Formal logic, which is entirely in its place in this question, since what matters here is the exploitation of the form, brings together several modes of such immediate inferences: subalternation, opposition, equipollence, conversion and contraposition. If one asks about the means, the consideration is confined to two simple points of view: to the relation of the more universal to the particular, and to the nature of negation.

[[945]] On the relation of the more universal to the particular rests subalternation, in that the degree of the quantity of the subject is taken into account, and conversion, in that the extension of subject and predicate is weighed in order to determine their reciprocal relation.

[[946]] ⟦332⟧ On the nature of negation rests opposition, in that it is weighed how far the determinations of contradictory, contrary and subcontrary judgments depend on one another, and equipollence, in that an equivalent negative expression is put in place of the positive, and conversely.

[[947]] Finally, on both points of view together rests contraposition, in that a negation is taken up into the conversion.

[[948]] If the immediate inferences — with the exception of the transformation of the disjunctive judgment into a hypothetical one with a negative antecedent or consequent, which follows from the relation of extension to content — come back to the two concepts of the universal and of negation as the sole determining ones, then this progression confirms the exposition of the judgment, since, as we saw, these same concepts alone condition the development of the judgment.

[[949]] We pass over the sufficiently explored particulars which would have to be considered in the immediate inferences, and refer to Twesten's logic, which discusses them most exactly.¹

[[950]] Conversion is the most important of these relations and finds its application, for example, in the converse propositions of the geometrical system. Yet the consideration of the form of the judgment, on which the doctrine of conversion is grounded, proves to be an insufficient ground except in the universal negative judgment, and formal logic does not suffice for this task either.

[[951]] Universal affirmative judgments, it is shown, can be converted only with limitation of the quantity (per accidens). From the form of the judgment no more can be inferred. But in point of the matter itself the most significant exception is found. For if the predicate belongs to the subject ⟦333⟧ peculiarly and exclusively, then unlimited conversion is certainly admissible, and is precisely a sign of the indissoluble connection of subject and predicate.¹ But only the content decides this, and the form determines nothing about this relation. Hence the geometer proves the so-called converse propositions with strict exactness, and through the conversion separates the specific difference of a concept and its consequences from the mass of what the concept has in common with others. Thus conversion gives some theorems significance before others and interrupts their uniform series by a noticeable elevation. The convertible theorems, expressing the exclusive peculiarity of a concept, are throughout the fruitful ones for the further development of the science. But what would happen if one stopped at the rule of formal logic, to convert the universal affirmative judgment only per accidens? An example may give us the answer. The Pythagorean theorem states that all right-angled triangles have a side whose square is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides. According to the prescription of conversion this judgment would take the shape: some triangles in which the square of one side is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides are right-angled. Formal logic is right when it cautiously teaches that no more follows from the form; but here too little follows.

[[954]] The particular affirmative judgment can, according to the logical rule, be converted simply. Certainly. But there is nevertheless a great difference here whether the predicate expresses a mere accident or the substantial species of the subject. An example of the latter case is the proposition: some ⟦334⟧ parallelograms are squares; an example of the former the proposition: some parallelograms serve as mechanical instruments. Here one will approve the conversion: some mechanical instruments form parallelograms; but hardly the conversion carried out by the same prescription: some squares are parallelograms; for obviously all are. The result of logical conversion says too little, and logic, which means to be a canon against what is false, itself produces the semblance of error. The grammatical expression does not distinguish what logical consideration ought to distinguish. Since formal logic has no other support than the grammatical expression, it takes up into itself the whole indeterminacy of that expression. The essence of the difference in question can be made intuitive by the schema of one circle enclosed by another and of two intersecting circles. Let the whole surrounding circle be called a, the enclosed one b. Or let one of the intersecting circles be called a, the other b. Fig. 1 represents the case in which the predicate designates the essential species of the subject, e.g. some parallelograms are squares; Fig. 2, on the other hand, the case in which the predicate states a specific difference or an external determination of the subject, e.g. some parallelograms are right-angled; some parallelograms serve as mechanical instruments. In this last figure it lies before one's eyes that a part of the one circle always includes one part of the other and excludes the other part. The determination "some" therefore has its full sense forwards and backwards, wherever the predicate may stand. But in the first case, although only a part of the larger circle coincides with the enclosed one (some a are b), the smaller always falls wholly within the larger (all b are a). Hence only an expanding conversion (the opposite of the limiting one per accidens) would satisfy the ⟦335⟧ truth; the conversion which the logical rule demands (some b are a) throws a false light on the matter, as though only some b were a.

[[955]] The universal negative judgment is converted simply; for since the concepts of subject and predicate have nothing in common with each other and fall entirely outside each other, they always repel each other, whichever of the two concepts one chooses as subject. Hence the conversion of a negative proposition does not first need to be proved.

[[956]] The particular negative judgment, it is finally shown, cannot be converted; but notwithstanding this it holds true when converted if the predicate does not compare the narrower and subordinate concept with the wider concept of the subject, but contains only an accident. For example, the proposition "some parallelograms are not squares" cannot be converted, for all squares are parallelograms. The first figure above represents this intuitively. A part of the circle a (the ring) is not the circle b; but the whole circle b falls within a. The judgments "some parallelograms have no right angles" and "some parallelograms do not serve as mechanical instruments", however, can be turned round. Some right-angled figures are not parallelograms, e.g. the right-angled triangle. Some mechanical instruments do not form a parallelogram. In the second figure above it is clearly shown that parts of the two circles always mutually exclude each other, and that therefore, however one may relate these parts to each other, particular negative judgments must be formed.

[[957]] Thus, then, with the exception of the universal negative judgment, the whole doctrine of conversion becomes doubtful. Conversion with limitation of quantity (per accidens) is a makeshift and in essential cases gives too little, and thereby, strictly speaking, something incorrect.

[[958]] The natural origin of the judgment is always turned upside down in conversion; for the concept of the ⟦336⟧ subject does not produce the predicate from within; rather, one experiments with the form.

[[959]] If in the predicate of the original judgment an accident is asserted, which in itself is no substance and therefore also cannot become a concept, as the subject requires, then in conversion the accident is tacitly raised to a substance, and therein lies a surreptitious assumption which must be well heeded. For example: all triangles have the sum of their angles equal to two right angles. According to the rule of conversion this proposition will run: something that has the sum of its angles equal to two right angles is a triangle. Apart from the defect (something, etc.), one tacitly adds in thought: some figures. If one wishes to say that this subject lies necessarily in the concept "having an angle-sum", then one enters upon a development which is foreign to the formal consideration of conversion. The concept of substance is arbitrarily borrowed from the subject that is to be converted.

[[960]] Thus conversion, apart from that case of the universal negative judgment, appears only as a trick of formal logic. And if one does wish to convert a judgment, one has to consider the content and not the form. Otherwise one obtains only a blunted, not a sharp, judgment of the matter.¹

[[961]] Contraposition (A is B; no A is a not-B; the not-B is not A) might seem to be an application of the infinite judgment rejected ⟦337⟧ above; the ὄνομα ἀόριστον of Aristotle would even stand in the subject. But considered more closely, the transformation takes place only through a negative judgment.

We part company only in the view formed from them. They hold the consideration to be significant that no more follows from the universal form of the judgment, even if the content of the concepts should yield more. [[963]] 2. Sir William Hamilton, under the name of the "quantification of the predicate", has devised a new theory for conversion and carried it through into syllogistic.¹ Since he considers the extension of the predicate as well as of the subject, and regards the judgment in its logical significance as an equation of the two, he demands that the extension of the subject be thought strictly in relation to the predicate, and that both be stated as equal in the affirmative judgment and as unequal in the negative. If the quantity of the predicate is stated, there is, he holds, only a conversio simplex. It will suffice to make this doctrine intuitive in the most important case, the universal affirmative judgment. Compare the examples: all men are imperfect, and all men are responsible. In the former we think only a part of the extension of the predicate; for besides man there are other imperfect beings. In the second example we think the whole extension of the predicate; for besides man we know of no responsible beings. The former example represents the equation: "all men are some imperfect beings"; the latter the equation "all men are all responsible beings". As this is thought implicite in those judgments, so, he holds, it must be ⟦338⟧ expressed explicite. Through this quantification of the predicate conversion becomes a simple interchange of subject and predicate, the ground for which lies in the equation.

[[965]] Against this theory essential objections arise. The judgment is psychologically no equation and has no tendency at all to be an equation. The mathematical invention of an equation and the natural formation of a judgment lie far apart. The judgment of content (the categorical judgment) does not aim at comparing the extension of two concepts (subject and predicate). It is, as has been shown, the counter-image of something real that stimulates thought. What the thing does, that the judgment means to assert of the subject. Whether the thing or its kind (all) alone among all others does this, or others do it too — whether, therefore, the subject is exclusively the predicate or not — is a further investigation of knowledge, but is not thought along with it in the judgment at all. It does not lie implicite in it at all, so that explicite it could take, e.g., the form "all men are some imperfect beings, or all men are all responsible beings". The forced, artificial, scarcely intelligible form of these propositions shows clearly that natural thought has not clothed itself in it. What language can scarcely express, the mind has also not thought in the simple form of the judgment. Psychology and grammar alike contradict this conception of the judgment.

[[966]] To the judgment: all men are imperfect, the judgment: all men are some imperfect beings, does not correspond. For this contorted form has welded two judgments into *one* expression, namely the judgment: all men are imperfect, and other beings besides men are imperfect too. To the other example: all men are responsible, the judgment: all men are all responsible beings, corresponds just as little; for ⟦339⟧ this expression forces two judgments into *one* form, the judgment: all men are responsible; and besides men no beings are responsible. In the former case a judgment of experience is added, in the latter a judgment of exclusive knowledge. The right of exclusion is grounded in the specific nature of man, his moral essence. One sees, then, that in that so-called quantification of the predicate one takes out of the judgment what the thought did not put into it.

[[967]] It is the peculiarity of conversion to be an immediate inference, i.e. to ground a new judgment from what follows from the mere form of a judgment (from the signs of quantity, quality, modality). But whether a judgment, to use Hamilton's expression, is a toto-total one (all men are all responsible beings) or a toto-partial one (all men are some imperfect beings, all men are some mortals), cannot be seen at all from the identical form (all men are responsible, all men are imperfect). It may be that it is easily determined how the extension of the subject is related to the extension of the predicate; but in the most important cases a deeper investigation, a concatenated proof, is required. Whoever, for example, learns the proposition that in a plane triangle the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles, or whoever first grasps the Pythagorean theorem, does not yet know at all whether the judgments in which these propositions are presented are toto-total or toto-partial; he knows it only when the converse propositions have been proved. Consequently he has not at all thought implicite what is expressed explicite in that quantification of the predicate. The mathematician proves the converse propositions, and only now can he state that the predicate belongs exclusively to the subject (thus, in Hamilton's language, that the judgment is a toto-total one). Whoever therefore already knows this distinction no longer needs conversion and has long since gone beyond the immediate inference, which ⟦340⟧ concerns solely the form of the judgment. In short, this theory of conversion contradicts formal logic, on whose ground all conversion keeps.

[[968]] And yet it is precisely the author of this "quantification of the predicate" who appeals to the formal.¹ It is not to be denied that it belongs to the formal element of the judgment, and that on this foundation one can build a formal system, as its acute inventor did. But this formal system is of a peculiar kind. Taken by itself the distinction is formal, but one arrives at it only through knowledge of the matter, and in the most important cases only by a path which presupposes the application of the whole of syllogistic. The system is formal as long as it calculates in the abstract notation of the equation. For if a judgment is toto-total, it can be converted simply. But in order to apply the system one must go far beyond the form of the judgment. Whether the judgment is toto-total cannot be recognised from the form. The point of view of the whole doctrine is therefore unsuitable, and a falling away from the formal consideration. If it is applied to syllogistic, an artificially contrived substitution takes the place of natural subsumption.

[[969]] Sir William Hamilton gives his theory the heading: new analytic of logical forms, and to that extent offers it as a correction or completion of the Aristotelian Analytics. Is this name justified? Analytic seeks to resolve the composite operations of thinking, e.g. the syllogism, into the simple constituents that ground them, and to comprehend them from these. But the new analytic does not resolve; it composes; it forces two judgments into one, e.g. man is responsible and besides him no other being, into the form: all men are all ⟦341⟧ responsible beings. It does not ground the composite through the simple, but grounds through what is more composite. To that extent the new analytic does not correspond to the sense of its name.

[[971]] 3. From the immediate inferences are distinguished the mediate ones, which take place through the intermediate link of a concept of their own. They form the syllogism in the narrower sense.

[[972]] Aristotle examined the forms of syllogisms with admirable acuteness. What he had sketched, commentators and scholastics spun out into the fine and the small. Kant censured the false subtlety of the syllogistic figures, and, while asserting the basic features of the principal form (the first figure), he rejected the "useless lumber" of the others in order to make room for things more worth knowing. The trump that Kant played on this did no good. Hegel, on the contrary, declared the syllogism to be the absolute form of everything rational. Everything rational, he asserts, is a syllogism, e.g. the planetary system, the state, God himself, and these present a firm living whole by virtue of the fact that the three figures of the syllogism interpenetrate in them. In this state of affairs it is necessary to enter more closely into some essential points.

[[973]] 4. According to Aristotle, the subordination of concepts underlies the syllogism as its common principle, which comes out most clearly in the first figure. Because the concept C (terminus minor) stands under the concept B (terminus medius), and B under the concept A (terminus maior), C stands under A.

[[974]] All B are A.

[[975]] All C are B.

[[976]] Therefore all C are A.

[[977]] > (1) In the cogent essay on the false subtlety of the four syllogistic figures, written as early as 1762, see Kant's Werke. ed. Rosenkranz I. p. 55 ff.

[[978]] ⟦342⟧ Three circles, of which the outer encloses the middle one and the middle one the innermost, represent this relation pictorially. Since Aristotle reduced the cases of the other figures to the first, they too follow the law of subordination. In general he drew up three figures, according as the terminus medius occupies the middle place in the series of subordinated concepts (first figure), or the highest (second figure), or forms the lowest concept (third figure). On this view of the subordination of the three concepts necessary to a syllogism, three figures result. When later four figures were counted, one followed another principle of division, namely the possibility of the different positions which the middle concept can have in the two premisses. Aristotle looked to the inner relation of the three terms occurring in the syllogism; later one considered externally whether the middle concept holds the place of subject or of predicate in the two premisses.

[[979]] Four figures of the syllogism are drawn up according to the following schema, in which M is understood as the middle concept, S as the subject and P as the predicate of the conclusion.

[[980]] 1. M. P. / S. M. / S. P. — 2. P. M. / S. M. / S. P. — 3. M. P. / M. S. / S. P. — 4. P. M. / M. S. / S. P.

[[981]] If one wishes to keep these designations, the three Aristotelian figures are to be determined as follows:

[[982]] 1. P. M. S. 2. M. P. S. 3. P. S. M.

[[983]] Here, however, the transposition of P and S must be permitted. Otherwise, on the Aristotelian principle, six figures of the syllogism would arise. The designation of the premisses by the subject and predicate of the conclusion also contains, properly speaking, a hysteron proteron. For the conclusion first proceeds from the premisses, and not conversely; and one orders what is earlier (the premisses) according to what is later (the conclusion), of which one properly as yet knows nothing, and which in ⟦343⟧ natural thinking only follows. One must already deliberately abandon the simple progress of thought and dissolve the interlacing judgments into bare concepts, in order to put the question for the syllogism, say, thus: what formal conditions must be fulfilled in order to attribute to a concept (S) as subject another concept (P) as predicate, or to deny it of it, through the mediation of some third concept (M) which already stands in a determinate relation to both? Then one does not follow the free pull of the indication given in the premisses for the production of a new judgment, but already knows, as it were, what is to come about, or asks, testing, after the warrant of what has come about. The disadvantage of such an arbitrary fixing will show itself further below.

[[984]] That Aristotle held fast to this inner principle of the subordination of concepts in the division is very clearly evident from the definition of the individual figures, and especially from the reduction of the second and third to the first, in which subordination presents itself most clearly. Only in a later passage, where he takes the three figures together and compares them, is the other view found, since he there derives these same figures from the different ways in which the three concepts can be predicated of one another.

[[985]] But even in this passage Aristotle has left no considerable gap. "If the middle concept is the concept which is both itself affirmatively predicated and has something affirmatively predicated of it, or which is both itself affirmatively predicated and has something denied of it, then the first figure is present; but if it is both affirmatively predicated of something else and denied of it, the second; but if different things are affirmatively predicated of it, or partly denied of it, partly affirmatively predicated, the third."

Only two cases of the fourth figure escape the definition of the first figure given in the words cited, namely fesapo and fresison, since in their premisses the mediating concept is not affirmatively predicated (forms no affirmative predicate). But precisely these cases suffer from particular defects.

[[989]] If we bring the forms of the fourth figure under the first, it must not be overlooked that Aristotle leaves the order of the premisses free. In the more recent view this order is bound, in that the concept which becomes subject in the conclusion is always assigned to the minor premiss. This arrangement, however, is, as was remarked, an arbitrary contrivance and an inversion of the natural relations, since the conclusion that follows from the premisses cannot react in any determination upon its grounds (the premisses).¹

[[990]] If we follow this hint and let the premisses freely interchange with one another, the syllogism becomes more significant than it otherwise is according to the formulas of the fourth figure.

[[991]] One need only consider the familiar rules of this whole group. 1. Calemes, after transposition of the premisses, concludes in celarent. If one wishes to insist that the conclusion ⟦345⟧ receive as its subject the concept which the minor premiss had in the arrangement of the fourth figure, then the unlimited conversion of the universal negative conclusion easily helps out. Free thought breaks through such contrived obstacles of its own accord. An example of premisses in calemes runs, say: All squares are parallelograms. No parallelogram has converging opposite sides. Obviously the natural conclusion will be: No square has converging opposite sides. But the technical obstinacy of formal logic forms the clumsy conclusion: Nothing that has converging opposite sides is a square. 2. Bamalip, after transposition of the premisses, concludes in barbara. Then the yield for knowledge is more significant than in the particular affirmative conclusion which the formula calculates out merely in order to bring the subject of the minor premiss back as subject into the conclusion. For example: all triangles in which the square of one side is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides are right-angled triangles. All right-angled triangles are so constituted that a semicircle can be drawn about them. The natural conclusion would run: All triangles in which the square of one side is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides are so constituted that a semicircle can be drawn through their vertices. But formal logic brings to light only the indeterminate judgment: Something about which a semicircle can be drawn has that Pythagorean property. If with the representation "something" one stays within the triangle, as the predicate requires, which presupposes a three-sided plane figure, then too little is asserted. 3. Dimatis, after transposition of the premisses, concludes in darii. For example: some parallelograms are squares; all squares have four equal sides and four equal angles. The syllogism, put into the first figure, will yield: some parallelograms have four equal sides and four equal angles. According to dimatis there results what proceeds from the conversion of the conclusion just ⟦346⟧ obtained: Something that has four equal sides and four equal angles is a parallelogram. Here again the old ambiguity arising through conversion prevails; for not some, but all plane figures that have four equal sides and four equal angles are parallelograms.

[[996]] The derivation of the syllogism from subordination, attempted and carried through by Aristotle, flattened out into the so-called dictum de omni et nullo, in which there is no longer ⟦347⟧ thinking but only counting;¹ and the view was put forward that the syllogism is properly nothing but an extended subalternation. Subordination moves solely within the extension of concepts. This view, however, when examined more closely, does not suffice.

[[997]] > (1) Quidquid de omnibus valet, valet etiam de quibusdam et singulis; quidquid de nullo valet, nec de quibusdam et singulis valet.

[[998]] If in the principal mood of the first figure the major premiss expresses the exclusively peculiar mark or the exhaustive law of the middle concept, and the minor premiss subjects the species contained under the middle concept to the property or the law — a case which presents the syllogism in its full power — then there is properly no complete series of subordination; for in this case the predicate of the major premiss is neither wider nor narrower than the subject, but covers it.¹ Then it is not the middle concept that is subordinated to the higher, but only, one-sidedly, the lower to the middle. If, further, one of the premisses is negative, one of the terms is posited simply outside the others, and the relation of subordination ceases. Hence already in the second figure, which concludes only negatively, there can no longer strictly be talk of a complete series of subordination; and that in the second figure the middle concept is the highest is more an assumption by analogy, since as a rule the predicate is more universal than the subject, than strictly true, since the negation, which must lie in one of the premisses of the second figure and mostly even lies there in simply universal form, tears apart the bond of subordination. Obviously, therefore, the syllogism cannot be comprehended from the relations of extension ⟦348⟧ alone. A similar objection arises in those moods of the first and third figures which have a negative premiss.

[[999]] > (1) Let the major premiss be, for example: in every right-angled triangle the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides; further, the minor premiss: every triangle in a semicircle is right-angled; then this last is indeed subordinated to the universality; but the subordination goes no further; the extension of the terminus maior is rather equal and similar to the extension of the medius, since it expresses a relation that obtains only in the right-angled triangle and always in it.

[[1000]] Kant, in the process of the syllogism, grasped precisely the opposite view, the view of content. For him the first and universal rule of all affirmative syllogisms is: a mark of a mark is a mark of the thing itself; of all negative ones: what contradicts the mark of a thing contradicts the thing itself.¹ Since content determines extension and extension develops out of content, this view of Kant's comes nearer to what is original. Those difficulties which arise when one asserts extension alone do not come up here at all. Nevertheless it may be striking in this formula that the genus sinks to a mere mark of the thing and subsumption is translated into a relation of content.²

[[1001]] Perhaps the nature of the syllogism can be presented more simply and brought into closer agreement with the development of the concept and the judgment. The syllogism, namely, proceeds from the mutual relation of the content and the extension of concepts. If the content (the positive or negative law) of a concept is applied to its extension, the categorical syllogism arises. The content (terminus maior) of a concept (medius) governs its extension (the species, terminus minor). If, conversely, the same law of all the species is expressed, and from this content of the extension the content of the comprehending universal is drawn ⟦349⟧ together, the disjunctive syllogism arises. The species form the middle concept, whose content becomes the content of the genus.

[[1004]] Examples readily elucidate what has been said. The essence of the categorical syllogism becomes clearest in universal affirmative judgments. For example: all parallelograms are divided by the diagonal into two equal and similar triangles. The square is a parallelogram. Therefore the square is divided by the diagonal into two equal and similar triangles. The major premiss expresses the property (the content) of the middle concept (parallelogram). The minor premiss subjects to this content the species (square) which belongs to the extension of the middle concept.

[[1005]] There are, to be sure, cases of the syllogism in which, on closer investigation, the matter can also be taken in such a way that content is related to content. This will occur when the concepts in the premisses contain only what is mutually specific. For example: all right-angled triangles are so constituted that their vertices determine a semicircle. All triangles in which the square of one side is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides are right-angled. Therefore all triangles in which the designated relation obtains are so constituted that their vertices determine a semicircle. Here one can decline subsumption (the relation of a law to the extension) insofar as the subject of the minor premiss coincides completely with the middle concept and the latter has no wider sphere. This consideration, however, lies beyond the form of the syllogism and becomes evident only from investigations of another kind. It was remarked above that in the judgment of content the predicate at the same time contains the relation to the — mostly higher — extension, and nowhere can the predicate have a narrower extension than the subject, even if it can equal it, as in the definition. Hence the categorical syllogism can ⟦350⟧ be regarded as a relation of content to extension.¹

[[1006]] The procedure of the disjunctive syllogism is the reverse. For example, the proposition: in every circle the angle at the centre is twice as great as the angle at the circumference when both stand on the same arc, is proved by a disjunctive syllogism.² The centre of the circle falls either within the arms of the angle at the circumference, or on *one* of them, or outside them. In all these cases the angle at the centre is twice as great (according to the proof); therefore in general. Here, as everywhere in the syllogism of complete induction, the law of the individual cases or of the species forms the middle concept, in order, as it were, to draw together the content of the universal out of the extension. In a corresponding manner the negative disjunctive syllogism drives the negation through all the species of a concept, in order to compress it into a universal negation of the genus.³

[[1007]] In this way the categorical syllogism is related to the disjunctive as the judgment of content to the judgment of extension, and one could also call the former the syllogism of content, the latter the syllogism of extension.

[[1008]] Content and extension, in the relation of law and appearance, constitute the essential sides of the concept, and their reciprocal relation its life. The understanding is educated to govern freely this interaction of content and extension, and its formation is completed when in the direction of thought neither content nor extension one-sidedly predominates, but the ⟦351⟧ manifoldness of appearances is always compressed into the determining, pervading simplicity, and the simplicity proves itself in the outflowing manifoldness. The syllogism is nothing but this easy motion of thought from content to extension and from extension to content; and therefore the indication of the moments suffices, and the working out becomes tedious. This original process is demonstrated especially by the cases designated by logic as irreducible. In these, thought, measuring content and extension against each other, draws the conclusion without effort; but logic provides only an indirect proof of its correctness.

[[1014]] ⟦352⟧ From the view that the syllogism mediates the relation of content to extension and conversely, the familiar syllogistic rules follow of themselves. Ex mere particularibus nihil sequitur; for the right to relate the content to the extension lies solely in the at least relative universality of the content. Ex mere negativis nihil sequitur. The law can be negative, but then the subsumption must be positive. Mere negations separate, but give the law no domain of rule. Conclusio sequitur partem debiliorem; for the conclusion will have to acknowledge the limitation of the law or of its application that is given in the premisses.

[[1015]] The first and second figures of the categorical syllogism present this application of content to extension most clearly. Since in the first figure the minor premiss introduces the species which submit to the (positive or negative) law of the genus, it is, in this act of subsumption, always positive.¹ Were it negative, and thus to reject the possible assumption of a species, then nothing would follow for this concept excluded from the extension; for possibly it might still have the same mark as the concept of which it is not a species, since the law expressed in the mark can also hold for other genera. But if, conversely, a concept excludes itself from the law of another, it also excludes itself from the extension of that other; for the content determines the extension. This the second figure presents. It has an adversative character, and major and minor premiss therefore necessarily alternate in affirmation and negation. It is just as original as the first figure and forms its opposition.²

[[1016]] To these two original ways of relating content to ⟦353⟧ extension correspond two ways of the disjunctive syllogism, in which the content of a concept is determined from its extension. For the positive form, which is to be compared with the first figure of the categorical syllogism, the proposition of Euclid cited above¹ can serve as an example. For the negative, which corresponds to the second figure of the categorical syllogism, the following example, say, can be formed. Parallels, when cut by a third line, are recognised either from the equality of the interior and exterior angle, or from the equality of the alternate angles, or from the sum of the interior angles being equal to two right angles. The sides of a pentagon have none of these properties. Therefore they are not parallel.²

[[1020]] The third figure of the categorical and hypothetical syllogism has not the same value nor the same degree of clarity as the first and second. Two concepts meet in the same subject. What follows from this? The concepts of the predicate are either the substantial genus or else a species-forming or accidental difference. If one of the two premisses is universal affirmative and has a ⟦354⟧ predicate of the first kind, the syllogism can most simply be regarded as the genus being more closely determined in one of its species. For example: all squares are parallelograms, all squares have right angles; some parallelograms have right angles.¹ But if in both premisses the concept predicated of the same subject is a dependent mark, then the syllogism gives rise to an artificial and, in several cases, a thoroughly ambiguous formation. The artificiality consists in this, that the dependent mark must first be made independent in order to become subject of the conclusion. Conversion suffers from this defect, as was shown above, and transplants it to the syllogism of the third as of the fourth figure.

[[1034]] But what, then, guarantees the completeness of the forms of the syllogism? Content is related to extension, and content is determined from extension, and indeed in both ways positively and negatively. This simple survey affords the insight that the relations are exhausted.

[[1035]] If, however, one wishes to insist on a complete knowledge of the forms of judgment in the premisses and in the conclusion, ⟦357⟧ then the path of combination, taken more than once, leads to the goal. The elements are a, e, i, o.¹ There result 64 possibilities of different premisses; from these the impossible and unfruitful ones are to be eliminated. But according to what rule? First, two relations present themselves which prevent any conclusion from the premisses. If the premisses are only negative or only particular, ⟦358⟧ nothing can follow from them. This flowed, as we saw, from the essence of the syllogism itself. In 16 cases among those 64 the premisses will present themselves as purely negative, and these thereby drop out. In another 12 cases the premisses will present themselves as purely particular, and these too drop out of themselves. In several cases, however, the premisses will outwardly contain a universal determination, but in the mutual relation of content and extension have only a particular value. Then too no syllogism will be possible. For if in both premisses the middle concept occurs only as a species,¹ the law of its content operates only partially, and there is no right to a conclusion. Of such cases there will be 8 besides the above. If, further, a species of a concept is denied of another, it remains undetermined whether the concept itself is to be denied or attributed to it. For where the particular is excluded, the universal may obtain and may also not obtain. Of such cases there are 9 besides the previous ones. In this way the familiar 19 cases of the categorical syllogism remain, which, however, are to be comprehended from 3, and not as the remainder of the possible after deduction of the impossible. In order to remove the unfruitful possibilities, only the relations following from the nature of the syllogism were applied here as the standard. However, ⟦359⟧ several of the 19 cases again remain undetermined on closer investigation, as has just been shown.

[[1038]] > (1) On the use of a, e, i, o the following may be remarked in passing. Since in quantity we count three kinds of judgments, the universal, the particular and the singular, and possess signs in those letters only for the universal and the particular, whether affirmative or negative, the subsumption of the singular affirmative and negative judgment under them wavers. Should the singular judgment (e.g. Caius is a man, a man invented that) fall under the sign of the universal (a) or of the particular (i)? Opinion differs. Aristotle leads to the view (analyt. pr. I. 1) that one regards the individual, without distinction, as part of a sphere, and therefore opposes the singular judgment to the universal and places it under the particular. On the other hand, already before Kant (see Kritik der reinen Vernunft §. 9. ed. Rosenkranz p. 72) logicians remarked that in syllogisms of reason it is to be treated like the universal (thus a, e), because it has no extension at all, and Kant concurs in this view. Herbart, however (Lehrbuch zur Einleitung in die Philosophie §. 62), demands a more exact distinction. That equating, he holds, is valid with a determinate subject, e.g. Vesuvius spews fire; but it is not valid when, with the help of the indefinite article, the meaning of a universal expression is restricted to some individual or other, e.g. a man invented that. Cf. Ueberweg Logik §. 70. p. 159. and §. 107 p. 296. Accordingly there is dispute whether, in that old example of a syllogism which holds before us the necessity of dying, Caius dies in barbara or in darii. It will be all the same to him. But the mode of consideration is different. As long as one represents Caius as one of many and indeterminately as a part of all, darii applies; but if in Caius one thinks only the *one*, and not individuals in general, then barbara is certainly more correct. It is inconvenient that the application of those letters first requires a preliminary investigation into the meaning of the singular judgment; yet for an exact determination it is in accordance with the matter. Thus it is more correct to take the syllogism of the Stoic who ensouls the world — Nothing unconscious has conscious parts; the world has conscious parts (man); therefore the world is not unconscious — not according to festino, for in the sense of the Stoics the world is only *one*, but according to cesare.
