[[1054]] In the foregoing, the syllogism in all its manifoldness has been set out in consistent agreement with the concept. Syllogisms reveal the community and intercourse of concepts among themselves. In linking themselves together they support one another and, through their interaction, exclude what is absolutely contradictory. Concepts by themselves are merely determinations at rest. In weaving themselves together, they present the world of thoughts, in which the bodily world is born again out of the spiritual depth.

[[1055]] The universal law of a concept, to which its extension submits, is the fundamental view of the syllogism. The ⟦360⟧ universal law, however, is the quantitative expression of that qualitative universality which rests on the community of thinking and being. From this side an insight into the real significance of the syllogism readily opens up. Before this is developed, however, we must cast a glance at Hegel's comprehensive treatment.

The syllogism presents the circuit of these self-mediating moments of the concept (the universal, the particular and the individual).

Through its own dialectic it makes itself into the syllogism of reflection, just as the judgments of reflection formed the second stage of judgments. [[1064]] The qualitative syllogism, or the syllogism of existence, is also called the formal syllogism. In its first figure particularity mediates individuality with universality (I-P-U). A subject, as individual, is joined together through a quality with a more universal determinateness. The middle is some property or other of the subject; since the subject has many properties, termini medii can also be found in it which allow the opposite to be inferred. The isolated middle concept is one-sided in this contingent relation. The premises demand proofs, and so a series of prosyllogisms opens up into infinity. From these defects arises the necessity of the next figures.

[[1065]] In the second figure the mediation takes place through individuality. The particular joins itself together with the universal through the individual. Since in this figure the terminus medius is a contingency and the syllogism yields only a particular judgment, this second figure is the truth of the first; for, while the first was contingent in itself, in the second the contingency is posited and brought to light. It mediates the major premise of the first figure (P-U). Since the second figure admits only a particular conclusion, it sublates the determinateness of the particular, and hence the terminus medius will become merely abstract universality.

[[1066]] The third figure therefore mediates the individual with the particular through the universal and grounds the minor premise of the first figure. It is the truth of the formal syllogism in general, since it expresses that the mediation of the latter is the abstractly universal one. Its conclusion is necessarily negative.

[[1067]] Since each moment has thus passed through the place of the middle and of the extremes, their determinate difference ⟦362⟧ from one another has been sublated, and the syllogism now, since its moments have become undifferentiated, has equality for its relation (the external identity of the understanding). Thus arises the fourth figure, or the mathematical syllogism. If two things are equal to a third, they are equal to each other.

[[1068]] Since in this course each moment has received the determination and position of the middle, and so of the whole in general, it is thereby freed from one-sidedness and immediacy. The first figure, it is true, pointed out into infinity for the grounding of its premises. But the mediation is completed, since the figures presuppose one another and the conditions close themselves into a circle. In the first figure I - P - U the premises P - U and I - P are still unmediated; but the former is mediated in the second figure, the latter in the third. Each of these two figures likewise presupposes its two others for the mediation of its premises.¹

[[1069]] In the qualitative syllogism the middle is abstract particularity, by itself a simple determinateness, and the middle only externally and relatively to the self-subsistent extremes. Now it is posited as the totality of the determinations; thus it is the posited unity of the extremes; at first, however, the unity of reflection which comprehends them within itself² (the syllogism of comprehension). Individuality is at the same time determined as universality.

[[1070]] In the syllogism of reflection the middle is not merely an abstract particular determinateness of the subject, but concrete, since it comprehends all the individuals to which, among others, that determinateness also belongs. Thus the syllogism of allness is formed under the form of the first figure. But since the major premise comprises all the individuals, it presupposes the conclusion, which it ought rather to ground.

[[1075]] In the syllogism of analogy universality is still encumbered with individuality as the immediate. When the mediation frees itself from this, in the syllogism of necessity the universal that is in and for itself becomes the middle.

[[1076]] The first syllogism of necessity is the categorical syllogism, in which a subject is joined together with its predicate through its substance. Substance raised into the concept is the universal in and for itself, whose essential difference is the specific difference. In the syllogism that has such a foundation the subsumption is no longer contingent, and the conclusion is no longer presupposed in order that the major premise may be true.

[[1077]] When the solid positive identity, which in the ⟦364⟧ categorical syllogism forms the universality of the middle, opens itself out into the negativity of the extremes, there arises the hypothetical syllogism, in which the individual appears in the significance of immediate being, such that it is as much mediating as mediated. In it externality and its unity that has gone into itself are posited.

[[1079]] The mediation of the syllogism is accordingly the identity that differentiates and draws itself together into itself out of the difference. The syllogism in this determination is the disjunctive syllogism. The middle is the universality filled with form. The mediating universal is posited as the totality of its particularisations and as an individual particular, so that one and the same universal is, in these determinations, only in forms of difference.

[[1080]] In this completion of the syllogism the difference between the mediating and the mediated has fallen away. The result is therefore an immediacy that has emerged through the sublating of mediation, a being that is just as much identical with mediation, and the concept that has restored itself out of and in its otherness. This being is therefore a thing that is in and for itself, objectivity.

[[1081]] In these determinations Hegel develops the forms of the syllogism and, through their own process, purifies them of the admixture of the contingent and immediate into a content solid in itself.

[[1084]] The judgments of existence, which grasp an "immediate, hence sensuous quality", are not yet universal.¹ Only at the later stage of the judgment of reflection does allness come forward, only in the judgment of necessity the concrete universal. But if the universal is an essential element of every syllogism, then a syllogism is not possible from premises which apprehend only what is immediately sensuous and therefore do not even so much as divine the universal. All syllogisms from such premises are mere semblance; but deceptive semblance is surely not to be acknowledged as the first stage and the foundation of truth. The sophism is no syllogism. How, in general, is the major premise in a syllogism of existence to run, so as to distinguish itself from the syllogism of allness? Unfortunately examples and applications are lacking which would have led us out of indeterminate assertions of the universal into the determinate confirmation of the individual. We do, however, find some information under the syllogism of allness.⁴ A syllogism of existence would run, for example: the green is pleasant; the painting is green; therefore the painting is pleasant. The major premise of the syllogism of allness, on the other hand, would not content itself with the abstraction of green, but would comprehend all the actual concrete objects that are green, and would therefore run: everything green is pleasant. On closer consideration, however, the boundary drawn here dissolves like a furrow in water. For in the syllogism of existence cited, the expression "the green" means everything green ⟦366⟧ and has the significance of allness. Perhaps the allness has been closed too hastily; perhaps the proposition was meant to say only: some green (what has been perceived) is pleasant. But in this shape it would remain on its own and would never become a major premise. If it is to introduce a syllogism, if it is to have the power of taking the minor premise up into itself, then that expression is the expression of allness and tacitly ascribes this power to itself. It must therefore be maintained that the qualitative syllogism, as a syllogism, is a syllogism of allness; the syllogism, stemming from universality, has naked immediacy behind it in its procedure, and the syllogism of immediacy is an idle, strictly speaking an impossible, formation.

[[1107]] We now ask about a subsidiary determination. The syllogism of allness is the syllogism of the first figure. But can it be said that induction concludes in the second figure, analogy in the third? We recall here that, although Hegel's second figure is the third Aristotelian one and Aristotle's second figure is Hegel's third, Hegel otherwise, like Aristotle, articulates the three figures according to the particular, the individual and the universal, which form the middle concept one after another.

[[1108]] Induction agrees at one point with the third Aristotelian figure. Induction infers from the individual, the third figure from the lowest concept of a series; but the essence of induction remains number, and only if the middle concept splits into its individuals or species and thereby becomes manifold can induction be brought under the schema of the third figure. This collection of the same subjects in both premises, this multiplication of the middle concept, already contains the essence of induction. Further, induction aims at universality, and indeed by means of the individuals and species; the third figure, however, always yields only a particular judgment. Let it not be said that induction too remains incomplete and therefore has precisely in the particular judgment the expression of this defect. The particular judgment is indeterminate; the comparative universality which induction must at any rate claim is determinate. This essential relation drops out in the third figure. Hence Aristotle, where he treats the third figure, makes no mention of induction; and while he binds the third figure to the law of a merely partial validity, ⟦371⟧ he demands universality of induction. Later, to be sure, Aristotle does compare induction to the procedure of the third figure.¹ But besides that peculiar dissection of the minor concept into its individual species, a particular condition is added which at once goes beyond the third figure and transforms its particular result into a universal one. The minor premise, namely, is to be so constituted that it can be converted simply. If this is ⟦372⟧ the case, the particular which enters the syllogism through the limited conversion of the minor premise is thereby avoided. Subject and predicate are now identical; but this can only be the case if the species have been completely enumerated. Evidently this condition of Aristotle's conceals the disjunctive major premise which lawful induction demands. Through the third figure alone, therefore, induction does not come about, and we may not assert that Aristotle regarded it as a species of the third figure.

[[1120]] With analogy the syllogism of reflection has run its course. The universal nature of the matter, the genus, has now become what mediates. Thus, according to Hegel, arise the syllogisms of necessity, and first the categorical syllogism, whose middle is objective universality. The syllogism of existence and the syllogism of allness still suffered from this defect. But by what is it overcome? The intermediate members are solely induction and analogy, so that the great advance must have taken place in these. But induction remains incomplete in the face of the infinite fullness of phenomena. Analogy is indeterminate, since it is properly experimenting with the universal. They still leave a great gap for grounding by themselves a judgment of necessity. Have the necessary judgments of geometry, which form the basis of chains of syllogisms, become what they are out of induction or analogy? Rather, these two forms do not come into play at all. Or have the categorical propositions of ethics arisen in this way? The genesis of the categorical syllogism in the unconditioned significance in which it is taken here is not comprehended in this dialectical development. A leap suddenly transports us into this form so rich in content. The immanent connection is broken off. What came earlier does not suffice for understanding this shape. What, then, is the use of this dialectic?

[[1121]] According to the dialectical view, the categorical syllogism develops further into the hypothetical, in that the inner substantial identity becomes negative and, without giving itself up, displays an external diversity of existence. The hypothetical syllogism presents the necessary relation as connection through the form or negative unity. This shape already contains what constitutes the essence of the disjunctive syllogism, the unity of the mediating and the mediated. In the disjunctive syllogism what mediates is the universal sphere of its particularisation and something determined as individual. What is mediated is itself an essential moment of what mediates it, and each moment is as the totality of the mediated. Thus the syllogism is supposed to complete itself into objectivity.

[[1122]] The hypothetical syllogism runs: if A is, then B is; now A is; therefore B is. In this form it is expressed most abstractly that the individual is subject to the universal. No new content is connected with the middle concept, as otherwise happens in the categorical syllogism, but only the pure existence of the middle concept (A) is pronounced, without any connection. ⟦377⟧ Hence also only the existence of the predicate (B), bare and detached, is inferred. If one counts three terms in the syllogism, only two are present here at first, and existence, this most abstract result of intuition, the mere foundation of the individual, appears colourlessly as the third. Whereas in the categorical syllogism (according to its universal significance) existence is presupposed, because the individual that rests on its basis appears in the minor premise, whereas therefore the categorical syllogism offers, on the presupposition of existence, a richer relation of content: the hypothetical syllogism presents only this isolation, the relationless existence of the predicate (B), and is in this respect poorer than the categorical syllogism. It has also been pointed out above that the categorical syllogism admits of the same form. We therefore cannot regard the hypothetical syllogism, which has for its content nothing but the fact of subsumption, without anything else, as a fuller unfolding of the categorical syllogism, but only as a shape that has stripped off the blossom and left behind only the supporting stem.

[[1123]] The disjunctive judgment is, as was shown above, the ripe fruit of an important development and a fully formed form. The disjunctive syllogism, however, does not stand higher in the significance of its form than the categorical, with which it runs parallel. In the sciences it is essentially applied in a twofold way, first for the grounding of a complete induction and then for the methodical laying-out of indirect proof. Neither procedure can be described as the completion of the syllogism.

[[1124]] If the hypothetical and the disjunctive syllogism are placed as high as they are with Hegel, and regarded as the apex of the pyramid which builds itself up from broad immediacy to clear height, then such a view may fairly be met with ⟦378⟧ the demand to demonstrate this great significance in actual application. The sciences, with their quiet acuteness, are the sole guarantee of logical theories. Where, anywhere in their wide compass, does the hypothetical and disjunctive syllogism appear with such an all-completing power?

[[1125]] > (1) See above vol. II. p. 356.

[[1126]] If the dialectic, from the contingent syllogism of existence up to the disjunctive syllogism, in which the mediating and the mediated are supposed to be one, presented not merely an artificial chain but the natural development, then it would have to be possible to show in a continuum of examples how knowledge ripens from one form to the other. But for such a confirmation of the dialectic nothing has yet been done, and we doubt its possibility.

[[1127]] If we sum up the misgivings that have pressed themselves upon us, then the qualitative syllogism set up, with its variations, falls away, since the syllogism as such begins from the universal and is therefore already removed from immediacy; the qualitative syllogism flows over into that of allness, and the latter into the categorical syllogism, since allness is only the outer expression of inner universality. Induction and analogy cannot be conceived as figures of the syllogism of allness, and in the hypothetical and disjunctive syllogisms as such there lies no greater completion. Moreover, the transitions are empty.

[[1128]] Syllogisms move within delimited concepts and relate to one another. But how do concepts come to be? This question points to the formation of the universal, which takes place beyond the formal syllogism, whether in outer experience or in synthetic construction. The forms do not arise out of one another, but jointly out of the mutually related sides of the concept.

[[1129]] But the syllogism is supposed to accomplish still more. Not merely is one form to produce the other, but the last is even to produce the world of objectivity.

[[1130]] ⟦379⟧ "The syllogism," it is said, "is mediation, the complete concept in its positedness. Its motion is the sublating of this mediation, in which nothing is in and for itself, but each is only by means of another. The result is therefore an immediacy that has emerged through the sublating of mediation, a being that is just as much identical with mediation, and the concept that has restored itself out of and in its otherness. This being is therefore a thing that is in and for itself, objectivity." Through the development of the syllogism, accordingly, since each moment became the mediation of the whole, a self-subsistent, self-sufficient being has formed itself. This is objectivity.

[[1131]] Hegel puts this transition from the subjective concept and its unfolding to objectivity alongside the ontological proof, in which the existence of God is inferred from the concept of God. The comparison can hold only in a remote respect.

[[1132]] In the ontological proof existence is supposed to follow from our concept of God. But this difficulty, if the course of Hegel's logic is correct, is not present at all at the present point. For thinking determines itself to being throughout, and with every moment of thinking, according to the fundamental view, a determination of being has been gained. The concept has never come out of the objective at all and therefore has no difficult transition to the objective to make. The concept, as the truth of substance, has always remained in the objective. The judgment, according to Hegel, presents the immanent nature of things, and the syllogism is the unity of the concept and the judgment. This point of view is carried through by Hegel, and only singly and inadvertently do contradictory determinations escape him, e.g. in the infinite and the problematic judgment, in the subjective ⟦380⟧ syllogism of analogy. Here, therefore, there can be no talk at all of a transition into the object.

[[1134]] The matter could be taken differently. How does God (the subjective concept) externalise himself into the world? All those systems which begin with the absolute as subjective have to withstand this difficult question. Can the transition under discussion be an answer to this question? If one examines the nature of the concept as it has produced itself, one must deny it. Since substance is identical with itself in interaction, it remains with itself and is free concept. But the identity is nothing but a logical relation, a repetition of the same form of substance and effect.¹ It does not touch the content of the matter, and still less does it produce such a centre of subjectivity as is surely posited where the question how God externalises himself into the world is raised at all.

[[1135]] The system therefore needs at the present place no transition to objectivity at all, neither from our subjective concept nor from God's subjective determination. It is mere semblance that one is entering a new world. One remains on the ground of the old.

[[1136]] But if a transition were nevertheless to be made, as is asserted, by what would it take place? Mediation has sublated itself; for the moments of the concept interpenetrate to form a whole. This sublation of mediation is immediacy, and immediacy is objectivity. But the immediacy which is otherwise assigned to the sensuous world must not here bribe us and sweep us along into the same representation. This immediacy here, the self-supporting whole, would always remain within inner subjectivity, like a spaceless and timeless metaphysical system. But that external world, as objectivity is nevertheless understood forthwith in ⟦381⟧ mechanism, chemism and end, is in this transition indicated by nothing, represented by nothing.

[[1137]] > (1) See above vol. I. p. 75 ff.

[[1138]] Nevertheless, let us take the leap with Hegel from this self-mediating and therefore immediate, self-subsistent thing of thought into the world of the object, as though the latter had really been derived. It now follows necessarily that objectivity is a system of syllogisms, and Hegel seeks to comprehend mechanism, chemism and teleology as such a system. The nature of the thing itself has the form of the syllogism and is thereby rational.

[[1139]] We first single out individual examples. Mechanism, in which only pressure and impact relate the objects, self-subsistent for themselves, to one another, runs its process as an objective syllogism. The product of the formal mechanical process is the heap. Its determination is given thus: "It is the conclusion in which the communicated universal is joined together through the particularity of the object with individuality." The heap, the very counterpart of logical order and interpenetration, may be hard to trace back to the syllogism. Here, however, it is done, and the following might be the sense of the obscure words. The communicated universal is the relation into which the things, in themselves self-subsistent and individual, are placed with respect to one another. Their particularity is the reaction which they exert and by which the form is determined. Thus the individual has externally become universal, just as the conclusion of the first figure pronounces the individual as universal.

[[1140]] The chemical process, it is said further, has for its product the neutral of its tensed extremes. The concept, the universal, joins itself together through the difference of the objects, the particularisation, with individuality, the product, and therein only with itself. Equally, the other syllogisms are also contained in this ⟦382⟧ process; individuality as activity is likewise what mediates, as is the universal, the essence of the tensed extremes, which comes to existence in the product.

[[1143]] In the neutral product the tension of the opposition and the negative unity as activity of the process are extinguished.¹ Something alien, which contains the negative unity outside the object, kindles it anew. The neutral is thereby sundered. "This determination belongs to the immediate relation of the differentiating principle to the middle, in which this principle gives itself its immediate reality; it is the determinateness which, in the disjunctive syllogism, the middle has at the same time, besides being the universal nature of the object, whereby the latter is just as much objective universality as determinate particularity. The other extreme of the syllogism stands over against the external self-subsistent extreme of individuality; it is therefore the equally self-subsistent extreme of universality; the sundering which the real neutrality of the middle therefore undergoes in it is that it is broken up into moments that are not different but indifferent towards one another. These moments are accordingly, on the one hand, the abstract indifferent basis, and on the other, its animating principle, which through its separation from the basis likewise attains the form of indifferent objectivity. This disjunctive syllogism is the totality of chemism, in which the same objective whole is presented, as well as the self-subsistent negative unity, then in the middle as real unity, but finally the chemical reality dissolved into its abstract moments."

[[1144]] The teleological relation, finally,² is the syllogism in which the subjective end joins itself together with the objectivity external to it through a middle. This middle (the means) is the unity of the subjective end and ⟦383⟧ objectivity, objectivity posited under the end. The means is the formal middle of a formal syllogism; it is something external relative to the extreme of the subjective end, and hence also relative to the extreme of the objective end.

[[1147]] In this way the syllogism has become real and actuality logical.

[[1148]] Attention has already been drawn above to the consistent character of the view just indicated. The only question is whether the consistency of the view is the truth of the matter, or whether, conversely, the consistency of the matter rather makes the truth of the view doubtful.

[[1149]] In the first place it is questionable that the system of the three syllogistic figures repeats itself in mechanism and chemism entirely, and in end essentially, in the same way. It cannot be said that they differ, say, as the stages of the qualitative, the reflecting and the necessary syllogism do. For in chemism the mode of the disjunctive syllogism, that is, of the syllogism completed at the last stage of necessity, has been expressly brought out. The heap (in mechanism) now stands logically under the same form as the product of the end. Both are a conclusion of the first figure. It is more than questionable that what is externally thrown together and what is spiritually shaped should bear the same logical signature. Even if the stage is higher, the logical relation nevertheless recurs.

[[1150]] The difficulty grows when one examines the terms of the supposed syllogisms. In the subjective syllogism they were related, in Hegel's treatment too, as the universal, the particular and the individual, and indeed in the sense of subordination. The particular appeared as the species of the universal, as a part of its conceptual sphere, the individual as comprised by the species. Are, then, in the objective syllogism too the major term and the minor term related as genus and individual, and the major term and the middle term as genus and species? Is the minor term logically subsumed under the ⟦384⟧ middle term? Let us compare mechanism first. If one sought to regard the given reciprocal relation in the heap of stones as the universal genus or the universal property of the stones themselves, one would be wrong; and one will hardly even attempt to set the reacting particularity of the stones, which is supposed to form the middle term, in such a relation to the major term as the concepts man and mortal have to one another in the usual example of the syllogism; and yet this would have to be the case if more than a vague analogy is to remain. In chemism, further, neither can the neutral be regarded as a conclusion from the difference of the tensed substances, nor the sundering of the neutral as a disjunctive syllogism. Or would one declare the formation of gypsum, to keep the example given above from Goethe's Wahlverwandtschaften, to be a conclusion from sulphuric acid and lime? According to the view Hegel has indicated, sulphuric acid and lime would be the terminus medius through which the concept (gypsum?) joined itself together with individuality (gypsum). Is the terminus medius here to be a duality? and if it is, can one say that gypsum is a species of sulphuric acid and lime? What, properly, is the universal in this process? The concept, which is designated as the universal, hides itself here and seems to mean only the chemical process in general. But even then the proper subsumption is lacking. Conversely, when the neutral is sundered, new compounds arise; but we surely do not have before us a disjunctive syllogism that exhausts the universal in its species. In end, finally, the means is supposed to form the terminus medius through which the subjective representation joins itself together with objectivity. The three terms of the syllogism would be, in a simple example: wanting to see distinctly, the one extreme; the optical glass, the middle term; the actual distinct image, the other extreme. One may here say by way of comparison that the ⟦385⟧ subject joins itself together with the objective world, from which it wins or into which it imagines its end: this alliance is not yet a logical syllogism. How, among the three terms named, will one subordinate seeing distinctly to the optical glass as extension to intension? or even subsume the actual distinct image under wanting to see distinctly in the way that the minor term otherwise falls within the logical extension of the major term? One surely cannot regard the actual execution as a species of the one represented. If the real syllogism, as Hegel has introduced it into objectivity, really corresponded to the logical one, then it would have to be possible to put it into the complete form of a syllogism. But one will attempt this in vain. In the teleological relation the means is the producing ground; when its law is applied to the extension, the real process initiated by the end can be presented in a syllogism; but the end itself, which appropriates this process to the subject, which makes the effect into the cause and the anticipated conclusion into the impulse of the syllogism, precisely the reconciliation of the subjective and the objective, is not contained in the syllogism and belongs to synthesis, which produces and does not infer. Within the sciences, end appears most simply and most intuitively in the geometrical problem, as was remarked above. The solution and the proof take place through syllogisms, but the problem itself arises through the apprehended requirement of other propositions or through a creative foresight. Its origin lies beyond the syllogism.

[[1151]] If the syllogism is sought in actuality in the way Hegel has done in the application set out, then the three terms are distributed arbitrarily among different realities from the point of view of the universal, the particular and the individual, without holding fast the mutual relation of logical subordination. In the teleological relation the subjective thought of the end is universal in and for itself; but it is not the universal ⟦386⟧ genus of its means and its execution; the means are for themselves the particular and the different, but surely not the species of that thought; they are really subject to it and are governed by it, but surely not logically subordinated to it as its species; the realisation of the end is an individual, but neither the individual of the heterogeneous means nor of the thought that projects the end. If one wishes to say that the means is subordinate to the design, and the execution to both, then one must carefully distinguish this real dependence from the logical one, which arises from the relation of the intension and extension of concepts and alone conditions the syllogism.

[[1152]] If, finally, logical inferring by means of the terminus medius is transformed in reality in such a way that two extremes join together in a third, this already alters the matter, since the determinate syllogistic relation becomes more indeterminate. Every union in a third can now be regarded as a joining together. But how the product can be the conclusion, what in it, corresponding to the extremes, becomes subject and predicate, the individual and the more universal, remains uncertain.

[[1153]] In a similar but still more significant way the power of the syllogism is supposed to present itself in every whole. Through the nature of joining together, through the triad of syllogisms of the same terms, a whole is supposed to be truly understood in its organisation for the first time. In this sense it is said: everything rational is a syllogism, the living body is a syllogism, God (the triune) is a syllogism, and so on. Thus everything real becomes logical.

[[1154]] We insert the clearest explanation of this doctrine.¹ "Like the solar system, so, for example, in the practical sphere the state is a system of three syllogisms. First, the individual (the person) joins himself together through his particularity (the physical ⟦387⟧ and spiritual needs, which, further developed on their own account, give civil society) with the universal (society, right, law, government). Secondly, the will, the activity of individuals, is what mediates, which gives satisfaction to the needs in society, in right and so on, as well as fulfilment and realisation to society, right and so on. Thirdly, however, the universal (state, government, right) is the substantial middle in which the individuals and their satisfaction have and maintain their fulfilled reality, mediation and subsistence. Each of the determinations, in that the mediation joins it together with the other extreme, precisely therein joins itself together with itself, produces itself, and this production is self-preservation."

[[1155]] > (1) Encyklopaedie §. 195.

[[1156]] According to this view the whole grows vigorously together through the particular, the individual and the universal becoming alternately and mutually ground and consequence. That the activities of the whole and of the parts intimately interpenetrate does indeed constitute the self-preservation of the organic whole. If one wishes to call the cooperating members the universal, the particular and the individual, this too has a ground in linguistic usage. But one confuses the matter when one forms the analogue of a syllogism; for the needs are not subsumed as a species under the universality of the state, nor the individual citizens as individuals or species of a genus under the needs. With which syllogistic figures, in general, is one to compare this process? The syllogism of allness, in which the particular forms the middle, induction, in which the individuals do, and analogy, in which the universal does, lie nearest. And yet it is evident, especially at first glance, that analogy, which with its admitted indeterminacy is only a human inference, cannot present itself in the real at all. If the doctrine that every living whole bears the typical form of the three syllogistic figures is to be not merely a logical semblance but ⟦388⟧ a real truth, then the agreement, which rests only on the indeterminate and ambiguous use of the universal, the particular and the individual, must be demonstrated more rigorously.

[[1157]] When one says, or repeats after others, that God is in himself a syllogism, one calls this the speculative concept of the Trinity. A syllogism is certainly comprehensible; but not that the persons of the Trinity are related to one another as universal, particular and individual, i.e. as genus, species and individual. Without this God is no syllogism.¹
