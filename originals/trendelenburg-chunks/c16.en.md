[[1156]] 6. Is the syllogism then, as might appear after this refutation, to remain nothing but a subjective function, without any real counterpart? The whole derivation preserves us from this. The content, presenting the law of the extension, contains the possibility of the syllogism, and in this its objective value is at the same time indicated. To the genetically universal, which is grounded on an original community of thinking and being, the quantitatively universal corresponds. The necessary ground therefore clothes itself in the expression of a universal fact and in this shape becomes the middle term of an objective syllogism. What in the real is the ground, that in the logical is the middle term of the syllogism.

[[1157]] Aristotle already demonstrated this parallelism with acuteness. Formal logic, which wished to have nothing to do with the real, left this profound indication lying unheeded. The producing ground, in unfolding its content, will always form the universal middle term in the major premise; for the necessary converts itself into external ⟦389⟧ universality. The syllogism, whenever it is positive, must fall into the first figure, in which the dominion of the law over the extension expresses itself most purely. All synthetic sciences, which project the phenomena from the ground, can furnish the attentive observer with examples in abundance, and the more apt ones, the more faithfully they reproduce the course of the creative ground.

[[1160]] Aristotle has already adduced examples enough. Arithmetic and geometry, which demonstrate most strictly, furnish the proof on every page. In order not to have to stretch the chain of syllogisms into several links, we choose a couple of fundamental propositions. For example, in a geometrical proportion the product of the outer terms is equal to the product of the middle terms. The proof is usually drawn up algebraically. a: aeb: be; axbxe = axexb. The syllogism would run: equal factors give equal products; the outer and the middle terms contain mutually equal factors. Therefore, etc. The equal factors are the producing ground of the phenomenon and form the middle term of the syllogism. The proposition that the diagonal in a parallelogram forms two equal and similar triangles is proved genetically from the doctrine of parallel sides, in that the diagonal forms equal alternate angles and becomes the common base of two triangles. The syllogism will proceed in the first figure. All triangles in which one side and the two adjacent angles are equal are equal to one another. The diagonal forms two triangles in which one side and the two adjacent angles are equal, therefore two equal triangles. The conditions of the triangles lying in the parallelogram and in the diagonal are the producing ground of the phenomenon and become the middle term of the syllogism. Universal grammar will derive the necessity of the cases, or of the prepositions that take their place, from the concept of the verb. If one wished to form the complete syllogism from this, it would run roughly: most activities include a direction; ⟦390⟧ verbs express such an activity; therefore verbs include an expression of direction, etc. Aristotle has presented a suitable example in the physical process of the lunar eclipse. When nature spreads out the phenomena before the perceiving sense, it gives the conclusion as a problem for which the terminus medius is to be found. Such a conclusion would be the observed fact, e.g. the moon is eclipsed, language has cases, the transversal of a square forms two equal triangles. Nature has inferred in creating. The result lies before us. The contemplating mind seeks the middle term of this creative syllogism. That is its task in all analytic sciences, a task which it solves only synthetically.

[[1161]] The producing ground stamps itself in a universal fact. Thereby arises the law of the middle term. Where, therefore, the ground is known, a terminus medius is tacitly generated. But conversely, is every middle term of a syllogism the logical expression of a real ground?

[[1162]] We distinguished above the ground of being and the ground of knowing.¹ Where both coincide, as in genetic knowledge, science completes itself. So long as consideration must proceed analytically, the two fall apart. The grounds of knowing, the effects of things, initiate a course opposite to the creative procedure of nature. The empirical sciences have their greatness in establishing such grounds of knowledge through observation. If these now form the middle term, this external relation does not reach the inner producing ground. In this multitude of cases the terminus medius does not present the real ground.

[[1163]] But if the middle term corresponds to the producing ground, the syllogism completes itself. In this ⟦391⟧ significance it is an inference from the essence to the appearance, as induction is an inference from the appearances to the essence. As the essence pours itself out into the appearances and confirms itself in them, so induction is from this side too a counterpart of the syllogism.

[[1165]] If in the theory as carried out we placed the second figure of the syllogism on a level with the first, which Aristotle alone held to be the principal one, the question arises whether we can now carry further the Aristotelian thought of the real force of the middle term and recognise a real significance in the second figure as well.

[[1166]] It is the law of the second figure that it admits only negative results. Where it is overhasty and concludes positively, fallacies do not fail to appear.¹ We really have a natural propensity to positive syllogisms of the second figure, in that the association of ideas puts itself in the place of thinking. For this association playfully carries our representations onward according to kinship, that is, according to a common predicate, and, governing the subjective course of our thoughts, follows a connection which corresponds to positive premises of the second figure. The connections of mythology in its symbols, the transition of meanings in the sign of words, show us such combinations, which would be fallacies if they claimed to count as syllogisms. The mighty impression, for example, which raised the eagle to be the bird of Zeus, symbolised, as this explanation has only recently been given, according to what struck it as evident. The lightning of Zeus darts through the air; the eagle darts through the air; therefore the eagle is the lightning of Zeus. Already an interpretation of a myth which Aristotle has preserved for us in the Metaphysics (I. 3. p. 983b 32) proceeds in an affirmative syllogism of the second figure. The aroused representation stops at connection, but thinking begins with subordination. The metaphysician who concludes affirmatively in the second ⟦392⟧ figure steps out of science into the association of ideas. The second figure, with its direction toward negation, serves in particular for the grounding of distinction and acts critically in science.

[[1168]] Since, then, the second figure may conclude only negatively, it can have a real significance, if at all, only in presenting negation in its real ground, which according to the discussion above was none other than the positive determination of the essence. And indeed, in two moods of the second figure (cesare, festino), if we consider the minor premise, this determination appears to us immediately in the terminus medius. We illustrate this by an example from Aristotle's Ethics: ¹ the affects do not rest on deliberate choice; the virtues rest on deliberate choice; therefore the virtues are not affects. It lies in the essence of virtue that it rests on deliberate choice, which is what the terminus medius of the minor premise states; hence it rejects the predicate imputed to it of the affect that arises blindly and surprises us. In the two other moods (camestres, baroco) the minor premise itself already contains a negation, which by means of the positive major premise leads to a new negation. Let another example from Aristotle's Ethics serve as illustration:² all original faculties are gifts of nature; virtues are not gifts of nature (they are acquired), therefore virtues are not original faculties. The minor premise generates a new negation in that it excludes the subject from the predicate which it first denies. Only mediately does one arrive at the same result, virtues are not original faculties, if one starts from the major premise, which, affirming the middle term, excludes from the original faculties everything that the middle term is not, and so also the concept virtue. Thus it is inferred: original faculties are not virtue, which only by conversion becomes: no virtue is an original faculty. The thought has the concept virtue as its ⟦393⟧ goal and makes straight for it. Hence the roundabout way indicated is hardly the natural course. We may therefore assert of those first moods (cesare, festino) that they present the real ground of negation; of the other two (camestres, baroco), the same only in a derived manner. In any case it is evident from this that the second figure too has a real significance, so far as, being negative, it can have one at all. The third and fourth figures we may not investigate in this direction; for they are artificial or ambiguous.

[[1170]] Against Hegel's crabbedly intertwined theory of the three times three syllogisms, which are supposed to generate and articulate the system of things really, the indicated view of Aristotle on the real significance of the syllogism stands simple and plain. Whereas the former forces an artificial logical formalism upon things, the latter, conversely, gives the formal syllogism support and content in the development of things. The former volatilises the actual into a play of forms; the latter fills the form with the actual.

[[1171]] # ⟦394⟧ XIX. DERIVATION FROM THE CONCEPT AND GROUNDING THROUGH CONTINGENT VIEW.

[[1172]] 1. The syllogism is not the ultimate form of knowing. The universal major premise already comprises the conclusion which it only means to generate, and, in order to be true, itself presupposes it. Prosyllogisms multiply the difficulty but do not remove it. The syllogism would describe a circle if it did not have an origin that is no syllogism.

[[1173]] A fact proves that the syllogism is not that form of truth into which nothing false can be fitted. For from untrue premises something true can follow. Aristotle already carried this possibility carefully through the three figures. In hypotheses the same phenomenon merely repeats itself on a larger scale. From the false premises of a hypothesis conclusions are drawn which agree with the actual, and this derivation of true propositions carries and supports for a time the untenable presupposition. For example, the phenomenon of the ⟦395⟧ lunar eclipse is derived just as consistently from the hypothesis of the Ptolemaic system of the world as from the Copernican.

[[1174]] > (1) Analyt. pr. II. 2-5.

[[1175]] Not seldom a weakness of the syllogism lies where it operates with the indeterminate "some" and runs out into an indeterminate or even ambiguous "some." Only where it is entitled to posit the premises universally and to conclude universally does it have its full strength. And were it really so, as some logicians may imagine, that God thinks in syllogisms, then at least he would not think in the moods burdened with "some."

[[1176]] The quantitative universality which the syllogism demands is the expression of something necessary that rests on the community of thinking and being. This synthetically universal is the higher-lying source. In motion and in the end it appeared like a simple abstraction, yet so original that it presses forward into the concrete and regenerates it. In the original element the mind frees itself from the rigid syllogism. In creating (constructing) the image, it intuits the universal in the individual and is able to translate the necessary, which it grasps creatively, into external universality.

[[1177]] There are domains, such as history, in which the individual so prevails that they withdraw from the detour of the syllogism; and yet one infers in history and is able through inferences to comprehend its development. This is not accomplished by the universalities of experience. The greatest figures of history stand alone in their greatness, grounded in themselves, without their equal; and, arisen as it were from themselves, they give laws to experience without receiving them from it. Whoever comprehends such figures comprehends them from that part of living human development which is in himself, and through the re-creating thought aroused by these elements. Thus the syllogism, a cautious support, gives way to the freer, bolder mind. One goes toward the goal ⟦396⟧ without measuring and counting the pendulum-beats of one's steps.

[[1178]] Here consideration returns to the first grounds discussed above. In motion, whose laws underlie experience, and in the end, which governs it spiritually, thought translates itself into intuition, and intuition remains in faithful union with thought. Through this fundamental relation alone is that glance possible which, like the idea of the artist, is at once individual and universal. Thought generates a pure image of the coming-to-be and intuits the universal law in it. What was said above about knowledge that prefigures in the a priori elements and knowledge that re-figures in experience finds its application here.

[[1179]] In the manner indicated, universal concepts arise and are now the norm of the phenomena that fall within their extension. Since the concept apprehends and preserves what is peculiar and necessary in the coming-to-be of the thing, one can in turn also know from it what is given potentially with the act of coming-to-be and then emerges when the thing enters into further relations. Such a derivation from the concept of the thing is a knowledge necessary in its origin and its progress. With such knowledge chance has vanished, and the mind rejoices in its pure property.

[[1180]] 2. But the limits are narrowly set; care is taken that the trees do not grow into the sky. Experience is shot through with chance, and it is the common labour of the sciences, divided among the generations of mankind, as they draw their net ever tighter, to exclude chance and to win fixed points capable of generating the particular in synthetic development. Every age attempts in its own way to comprehend the contingently given as necessary and to gather the individual together into a synthetically universal. In attempting this, the ⟦397⟧ mind, which would otherwise be buried in the contingent, seeks to rise again in victory over the external world. Every science works at this according to its part. From this calling springs, consciously or unconsciously, the enthusiasm of the inquirer. Even in the consideration of the most individual detail this universal striving makes itself known. But it is, as it were, the last day of the sciences when the whole of experience, variously clouded and strictly bound, is freed and transfigured in *one* great glance.

[[1181]] In mathematics, it seems, this goal of letting the individual come to be synthetically from the universal and of comprehending it in its coming-to-be ought to be most attainable, since mathematics proceeds from the element which, as the most original, underlies thinking and being. It does indeed stand at an admirable height, and from Plato to our own day the more ideal direction of knowledge has again and again raised itself up on the grand fact of mathematical science. But nevertheless chance seems still to play into the auxiliary lines of construction and into the methods of calculation to such a degree that Herbart in particular has grounded the doctrine of contingent view on their example.¹

[[1182]] The ground, Herbart teaches, is composite, and the composition produces the consequence. Hence in a derivation the ground at hand must be augmented by a contingent view in order to yield anything. Herbart illustrates this especially by the Pythagorean theorem, the pillar of all analysis. The usual proofs of it rest on a contingent view. It is a lucky stroke that one drops a perpendicular from the vertex of the right angle onto the base. Thereby one gains either, by the doctrine of similar triangles, proportions ⟦398⟧ which by calculation yield the proposition, or a construction, as in Euclid,¹ which, by means of a new contingent view, a decomposition of the squares and parallelograms into triangles half as large, shows that the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares of the two legs. Everything here rests on the perpendicular drawn in, which augmented the figure. This intervention, says Herbart, is one of the artifices which we so often meet in mathematics, and whose effect consists in leading the object at hand into a known and finished series of representations, which then runs off of itself. These artifices enlarge the ground from which the consequence is to proceed. Thus one sees the initial ground first enlarge itself and then contract again. When, to take another example, the mixed quadratic equation becomes soluble by completing the square into a complete binomial, one takes a contingent view of the quantity x² + ax. In this way science advances through a contingent view, as Herbart seeks to illustrate by several examples.

[[1191]] We wish to mark out a way that is regulated entirely by the necessity of the concept, and to illustrate it by a couple of salient examples.

[[1192]] The concept of a thing apprehends its peculiarity. This peculiarity must contain, in the whole extent of possibility, the action and reaction of the thing. The point is to set out what lies therein. The concept has as its elements the next higher universal and the species-forming difference. What follows from the universal is determined in the particular by the specific difference. Hence the task is to apprehend the thing, as it were, at the point of contact of the universal and the particular. Where the two interpenetrate in living fashion, there the properties of the thing have their origin. Geometry will therefore have to design the construction so that the universal and the specific difference are presented in interaction. From such a construction the properties spring forth.

[[1193]] We shall make what has been said intuitive by the same example by which the dominion of contingent view in the midst of necessary knowing was just demonstrated, and for this purpose we consider the right-angled triangle.

[[1194]] The concept of the right-angled triangle readily resolves itself into its universal and the species-forming difference. From the universal there follow for the right-angled triangle the necessary properties of every triangle. The proposition that in a triangle the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles contains the fundamental relation of the triangle in general. To this ⟦401⟧ property of the angles the specific difference, right-angled, points. If both determinations are brought into connection, it follows that in the right-angled triangle, and only in it, one angle is equal to the two others. If now this exclusive property is presented in the general image of the right-angled triangle, as indeed the construction proceeding from the concept is what is sought, a twofold case necessarily results, in that the right angle divides itself into the two others; for the two angles at the base can have a twofold position within the right angle. Either the angle at the base on the right will also take the place on the right within the right angle, and the angle on the left the place on the left. Or the angles will exchange places, and the angle at the base on the right will be thrown over to the left side, and the angle at the base on the left to the right side, of the dividing line. Only these two constructions are possible; and precisely they at once yield the two principal theorems of the right-angled triangle.

[[1195]] > (1) The old rule, already drawn up by Aristotle, of defining per genus proximum et differentiam specificam is here taken up and developed in its consequences.

[[1196]] In the first case there arise, in accordance with the construction, two isosceles triangles within the right-angled one. One of the equal sides is common to both triangles. The three equal sides thus radiate like radii from one point. Or, what is the same thing, a semicircle lays itself around every right-angled triangle in such a way that the hypotenuse forms the diameter.

[[1197]] In the second case there arise within the enclosing right-angled triangle triangles which are similar to one another and to the enclosing one, since two angles in these three triangles at once present themselves as equal. From this follows, by means of proportions, the Pythagorean theorem. One might suppose that this proof is one and the same as the so-called arithmetical one. The difference lies, however, in the construction. In the arithmetical one a perpendicular is dropped according to contingent view; in the one just ⟦402⟧ attempted, what is demanded and indicated in the concept is constructed. That the line which divides the right angle into the two others is precisely a perpendicular follows only as an after-born property from the original construction and does not concern the consideration at all. Before there can be any talk at all of a square of the hypotenuse or of the legs, the theme of the multiplication of lines, obscure up to that point, must have gone before. The proof therefore presupposes nothing that must not, in a genetic development, be established before the theorem.

[[1198]] The construction was determined by nothing external, but solely by the elements of the concept. What lay tacitly in the nature of the thing has been realised. The universal and the difference (the generic and the specific) entered into interaction; and this projection of the concept, in which the synthetically universal emerged, at once revealed the necessary properties. The yield is surprising in the present case. No proposition expresses so essentially the nature of the right-angled triangle as the proposition that a semicircle can be described about every right-angled triangle, and the Pythagorean one, that the sum of the squares of the two legs is equal to the square of the hypotenuse. From these the remaining properties follow further. Both propositions belong to the most fruitful of the whole of geometry. They spring forth here, as at *one* stroke, from the simple construction of what is given in the concept. If now in the proposed procedure everything is determined by the necessity of the concept, contingent view has thereby become superfluous. What had to be demanded in general, but seemed unattainable, has been achieved in the particular instance.

[[1199]] What follows from the universal and the exclusively peculiar (from the generic and the specific) can belong only ⟦403⟧ to the thing whose concept is taken as the basis, and to no other; for the specific difference, the source of the proof, cuts it off from all others, since it apprehends precisely what other things do not have. Where, therefore, a presentation of the exclusively peculiar, such as has just been attempted in the example, yields determinate propositions, these propositions belong as its own only to the object of the concept taken as the basis, and to no other. A perpendicular can be dropped from the vertex of every triangle. What follows from such an auxiliary line, as in the Euclidean proof of the Pythagorean theorem, therefore possibly follows for triangles other than the right-angled one as well. Hence it is necessary to prove the converse of the theorem. But since among plane triangles only the right-angled one is so constituted that one angle can be divided into the two others, the proposition follows as a specific one from such a specific construction. Or, if we translate this into the logical language of the system: in the case indicated it is superfluous still to seek a proof for the converse of the proposition. The proof of the principal proposition contains at the same time the proof of the converse. If it has been proved from the peculiar concept of the right-angled triangle that the square of one side is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides, it follows that this relation, which flows from the exclusive nature of the right-angled triangle, always and everywhere indicates the right-angled triangle. That is the content of the converse proposition: if in a triangle the square of one side is equal to the sum of the squares of the two other sides, the triangle is right-angled. Whereas in the system the converse propositions for the most part lead to indirect proofs, on the proposed way one is spared these. So long as the proof of a proposition rests on a contingent view, and consequently on a contingent connection with other propositions, the possibility remains open that the property expressed in the proposition ⟦404⟧ belongs equally to other figures too and is thus to be found more generally. The proof of the converse first removes this possibility, which remained over like a residue from the externally conducted proof, and is therefore unavoidable in this connection in order to establish the exclusive peculiarity.

[[1201]] The converse of a proposition can, according to the reciprocity of a function, have a still more real significance. The subject of a proposition presents itself as the ground of the predicate. In the formulation of the principal proposition, therefore, the concept of the subject appears as the original ground and the predicate as the derived property. If the proposition is converted, the predicate receives the place of the subject and so the significance of the original ground, and what was just now subject and ground receives the significance of the consequence. If actuality corresponds to this reciprocal relation, the mutual dependence of the members is thereby expressed. Neither has a prior right over the other. Each can be regarded as cause and in turn as effect of the other. Where this is the sense of a converse, a direct proof will be found for it just as for the first formulation, provided that the direct proof imitates the course of coming-to-be. Only then a contrary point of attachment is needed, a derivation from the contrary possibility of origination; and the proof of the converse proposition must renounce leaning merely on the principal proposition already established. Where the converse has the significance just indicated, it ought not to appear even in the system as the logical trick of an inference backwards, but as the expression of the contrary mode of origination.

[[1202]] In Euclid the most important propositions are proved only from the external connection and by means of contingent views, but not under the guidance of the elements necessarily given in the concept of the thing. Yet in some ⟦405⟧ propositions what was just demanded has already been accomplished. Thus in particular the propositions on the parallelogram are established immediately from the specific difference of a figure enclosed by parallels. Compare, for example, the proposition¹ that the parallelogram is divided by the diagonal into two equal triangles. The exclusive property of the parallels and the cutting diagonal are therein solely the factors of the proof, and nothing is taken up from outside. In such models there already lies the impulse toward a higher logical completion of the system.²

[[1203]] When the theorem is sent ahead ready-made and the proof is dispatched after it, the whole looks like a series of rigid assertions which gain a foothold and then entrench themselves. So appear Euclid's Elements, so Spinoza's Ethics, and whatever other writings take the well-fortified way of Euclid. Everywhere there is an artful concatenation, but nowhere a coming-to-be and growing. The proposed way leads further. For it guides us to find what lies in the nature of the thing, not to secure, by a discovered connection, what has been found from elsewhere. The theorem is newly won and not merely externally vouched for.

[[1204]] 3. No science has so fortunate a position as mathematics for developing its content from the concept of the thing. Hence analytic geometry too, which derives the properties and relations from the formulae of the figures, as from algebraic definitions of the thing, has reached an admirable height.

[[1205]] In no other science can the coming-to-be and essence of the object be so purely observed and therefore also so purely fixed in the concept. In no other ⟦406⟧ science do the relations which can be given to the object, in order to call its dormant properties into actual life, lie in the same way in the hand of him who wishes to know the object. Nowhere does the element lie before us so purely, and nowhere is it in the same way accessible to the eye of the mind, since it is projected by the mind's own creative hand.

[[1208]] Nevertheless the demand goes beyond mathematics. In the example treated we merely see in its simplest shape that task which must be set everywhere where the mind has taken possession of the origin of the thing and advances from it into the phenomena.

[[1209]] Where the peculiar grows into the universal, where the specific fuses with the generic, there, in every thing as in the geometrical theorem, is the seat of life. From there, as from the source, the phenomena and properties radiate forth. There the brighter image of the thing gathers, as in the focus of the lens.

[[1210]] If science wishes to advance synthetically and to guide its course with the necessity that lies in the nature of the thing, it has no other norm than to penetrate to the point where the universal is determined by the peculiar, the generic of the thing by its specific, and is as it were enlivened and fertilised. The concept of the thing fixes these elements. And a derivation that proceeds in this way is a development from the concept and completes knowledge in the particular. If now the concept, as it ought and as it does at the last stage, grasps those elements genetically, then the derivation from the concept indicated is only a continuation of the genetic procedure, which has first deposited its yield in the concept of the thing.

[[1211]] What in life is called a "glance" is something similar. Whoever, for example, apprehends the physiognomy he encounters in such a way as ⟦407⟧ the particular bent in it, and in general the individual, is as it were interwoven with the universal nature of man, like warp and weft; whoever from this living point divines the bearing and the activity of the mind sketched out in the physiognomy; or whoever knows how to intuit, in a man, the abiding character and the momentary influences in their living relation and to survey them in their consequences; or whoever understands, in a work of art, the idea as it had to become embodied in this or that way in the particular material, or in general grasps the universal motive and the peculiar situation in one another; whoever in this way penetrates the universal with the peculiar and the peculiar with the universal and so judges from the living essence: to him one ascribes a glance. The procedure of science indicated is such a glance, only enlarged and elevated. Yet the difference is easily noticed. If the glance strikes that vital point of the thing with genius, but only in the individual case and without consciousness of the grounds, science does indeed follow this stroke of the mind, but it raises the individual to the universal, the unconscious to conscious necessity. In what we call a glance there is already one element more present, and a twofold motion is intertwined. The glance is fixed on the individual case, reads in it the universal and the peculiar, and from here projects its judgment. But this individual case is not yet present in the derivation from the concept; it is only won there.

[[1212]] The hardest thing always remains to win the basis of the procedure, the concept. Where science possesses it, it is no doubt able to exploit it in a similar way. If, for example, calculation is applied to a physical phenomenon, the concept formed of the latter seems as it were to form the specific difference to the more universal power of the mathematical element. The application is the living relation of the universal and the peculiar. This holds ⟦408⟧ in law as in mathematics. Universal grammar seeks to understand the essential phenomena of language from logical concepts which are peculiarly determined by the condition of their presentation in sound. History comprehends the shapes of the world from the universal task of the age and from the tribal character of a people in conflict with the physical conditions of the land and the other stirring events. In the organism the determinate end appears as such a specific difference, which in interaction with universal life governs the activities and stamps the character. The examples presented above can also bear this out.¹ Aristotle in the Nicomachean Ethics (I. 6) seeks to determine ethical eudaemonia from the peculiar essence of man. These indications may suffice to show that in all sciences the same elements for a development from the concept are found as in the case of geometry carried through above. The more sharply that point is apprehended where the universal and the peculiar touch, limit and enliven each other, the more fruitful the derivation becomes, and the more the connections of contingent views are excluded.

[[1213]] The seed-grain, in order to germinate and grow, must be given back to the natural conditions out of which it itself came to be. Isolated, it remains shut up in itself. The concept behaves in a similar way. By itself it is indeed capable of formation, but it must be thought in the interaction of the context in order to develop.

[[1214]] A concept by itself alone never sets forth out of itself what lies in it, although it is often so presented, and the matter then appears more wonderful. Development never takes place ⟦409⟧ from the *one* concept alone, progressive motion never from its own elastic force confined to itself. Development presupposes stimulating elements in the external world, motion a resistance against which the force pushes itself onward. It is similar in the mind. Concepts must work together in order to produce something new. It is the creative act of the mind to apprehend this working together and to project the new from it. For example, the concept of the circle, which is simple in itself, does not develop from itself alone into the propositions on the relations of chords and tangents and on the relation of the angles within the circle. The concept of the straight line must be added, and what they generate together is the development of the concept, or properly of both concepts.

[[1216]] With derivation from the concept there results an organic form of knowledge. The form does not stand externally over against the material, so that it would merely be added as the ordering factor. Form and content generate themselves, as it were, together. Every organic development proceeds from a whole (the seed and germ), and since the power of the whole remains the dominant factor, the parts become members which serve the whole and in which the whole is mirrored. In the procedure indicated, the concept is this whole, which carries through the power of its law in manifold knowledge. Hence the derivation from the concept here attempted, and only this, is called development.

[[1217]] 4. The procedure indicated is synthetic and presupposes the full knowledge of the concept of a thing as the basis of derivation. But how many concepts then lie so open in their grounds? How many things can then be observed in their coming-to-be, so as to let themselves be gathered synthetically into a concept? What was possible in numerical and spatial magnitudes repeats itself with the same strictness perhaps only in simple ethical relations.

[[1218]] Most concepts must be lifted out of the phenomena by the analytic way, ⟦410⟧ and the question arises whether on this course from the phenomenon to the grounds a necessity is possible similar to that in the synthetic development of the concept.

[[1219]] The analytic procedure first considers the phenomenon and seeks in it the traces which the essence of the generating concept has left behind. What does it hold to in the phenomenon? First, perhaps, to an articulation of the parts. But in order even to find the joints of the parts, one needs a guiding representation of the whole, from which one divines the significance of the parts. Even analytic decomposition is guided by a provisional construction of the causal connection. In order to dissect (analyse) the phenomenon, one needs points of view that stem from the whole or are taken from known cases of coming-to-be. Only through the open or tacit help of this synthetic element is this first step of the analytic procedure taken. Without it we should always remain within the phenomenon. Such a linking of synthetic points of view with the analytic procedure is a contingent view.

[[1220]] Where the analytic procedure advances with certainty, it combines out of a fund of specific causal connections whose possession is already secure.¹ Where it newly invents, it mostly follows the thread of analogy or gives a creative construction its fixed data. In all these cases the analytic procedure goes by the hand of a synthesis.

[[1221]] How known causal connections (synthetic elements) form the supports in the analytic procedure ⟦411⟧ shows itself most simply and purely in the analytic problems of mathematics. To solve the analytic problem of a division, one needs insight into the genesis of the number system, and we make use of the synthetic results of the multiplication table. For the extraction of a root one needs knowledge of the binomial law. Other problems can be solved only by means of logarithms already calculated. Every equation demands operations guided by a supreme end (e.g. to isolate x); the knowledge of what can be done with the equation without cancelling the expression of an equation, the knowledge of the means for this end, is just as synthetic as the guiding point of view itself. In the solution of an equation one easily distinguishes synthetic elements which are subordinated as members to the universal analytic method, e.g. in the usual derivation of Cardano's formula for the reduced cubic equation, the assumption x = y + z, 3yz + p = 0, y = (y³)2 6 etc. The acumen of the method lies in skilfully combining the possible decompositions and compositions for the determinate end. But to know what is possible is only the consequence of an insight opened into the coming-to-be, and therefore a synthetic element. In the analytic problems of geometry the same shows itself, as even the simplest cases prove. If a circle is to be described through three points which do not lie in a straight line, one thinks of the circle as drawn. Then the points form the ends of chords, and the perpendiculars at the midpoints of the two chords pass through the centre. Where the perpendiculars intersect, there the centre must lie. The synthetic element here is in particular the proposition that the perpendiculars erected at the midpoint of a chord pass through the centre. Or if a triangle is to be inscribed in a circle which is equiangular (similar) to a given triangle,¹ then the ⟦412⟧ tangent, which is drawn first in order to lay off at the point of contact a chord making one of the angles of the triangle, is a contingent view, but one that has arisen from knowledge of the causal connection that every angle at the circumference standing on this chord as the base of the required triangle is equal to the angle constructed at the tangent. This construction of the tangent is only an attack upon the matter, or as it were only like a substructure which is broken away again when the vault is finished. If a circle touching the three sides is to be inscribed in a given triangle,¹ the bisection of the angle is the contingent view, the synthetic element of the analysis. What is evident here from examples of that science in which method appears most purely holds equally in others. If a difficult passage in an author is to be explained, an analytic problem lies before us; for the expression, the appearance as it were of the thought, is to be led back to its producing ground. The mind created the word and the sentence as its external form (synthetically); and understanding is this reanimation of the husk with one's own mind. Analysis at once appears here as a synthesis turned backwards. The passage is understood only when the indications of the appearance are sharply grasped and, in accordance with them, the knowledge of words and forms (synthetic elements) is combined. In the domain of law, circumstantial proof is an analytic problem; but it comes about only because one already knows causal connections of intentions and actions, of moods of the soul and their expressions, of instruments and their effects, etc. The fact is dissected, but the dissection is compared with the possibilities arising from these synthetic elements. The analytic way, the way from the broadly poured-out phenomenon to the simple ⟦413⟧ ground, has a wider scope than the synthetic, since with *one* act the phenomenon is produced. With *one* radius the circumference is generated from the *one* centre; but on the circumference there are infinitely many points from which one strives back to the centre in order to find the radius. In the same way the synthetic procedure is more bound, the analytic more manifold and more subject to chance. What Herbart demonstrates concerning the contingent view that cooperates in grounding has its proper place in the analytic procedure and must there be recognised as essential.

[[1225]] 5. Where synthetic points of view leading directly to the ground do not immediately offer themselves to the analytic procedure, there it commonly follows analogy, the most widespread kind of contingent view.

[[1226]] It may at first sight seem as if analogy were initially without objective logical significance and nothing but the psychological association of ideas. When the course of representations shoots onward by itself, without being guided and regulated by the methodical consideration of the matter, this flow is driven and renewed by the similarity of the things represented. One similar representation awakens another, as if according to a physical law of attraction, and the images of things spring up in the mind according to this relation. It may seem as if the analogy of logic rested only on this involuntary connection. The logical course would then be wholly abandoned to chance. For that arousal of representations through an elective affinity depends besides on many influences, in particular on temporal succession, on intervening series, and on the vividness of the first apprehension. It is not to be denied that the improvising association of representations ⟦414⟧ can contribute to a quick and happy apprehension of analogy; but its logical essence does not lie in this.

[[1227]] > (1) On the formal side of the syllogism of analogy we have spoken above, see section XVIII. vol. II. p. 372 ff.

[[1228]] Analogy follows an indeterminate view. From the selfsame conditions the same phenomenon proceeds. For this no proof is needed. But must the same phenomena necessarily have *one* ground? Must similar phenomena have a kindred ground? The first is not necessary; the second still less. But since the similar approaches the equal, one sets the difference aside provisionally in order to arrive from an unknown domain at a known one. Every analogy, on account of this indeterminate foundation, remains a hypothesis until it proves itself. But as an attempt to form a concept it has great significance.

[[1229]] Analogy presents itself in the sciences partly as analogy of identity, partly as analogy of co-ordination. In the first case the circle of the same concept widens, and phenomena that were separate flow into a unity. In the other case the phenomena do indeed meet in a higher universal, but they shape themselves out of it anew and peculiarly. The pull of the similar has, in the one kind as in the other, initiated the greatest discoveries, but not completed them.

[[1230]] The phenomena lie there separated. The less known is compared with the known. The similarity is taken as the point of attachment, and the unity running through both is found. In such cases analogy runs out into identity. When Newton comprehended the motions of the heavenly bodies from terrestrial gravity, so that in his view *one* force of attraction reveals itself in both; when the properties of attraction and repulsion first observed in rubbed amber extend into the most various phenomena, as far as the electrical phenomena of meteors; when the perceived force of the ⟦415⟧ lodestone, like an inconspicuous beginning, widens into terrestrial magnetism; or when the currents of the winds, dependent on the sun, are understood from the draught of heated rooms: then in these cognitions, on which the centuries labour, it is analogy that is first at work. It is the nearest point of view for connection and illuminates what is remote. Identity is its result.¹

[[1231]] It may happen, however, that the phenomena do not have an identical ground and do not run out into *one* substantial determination, but nevertheless agree in individual cooperating conditions of the ground or in the species-forming differences. Then analogy leads to a co-ordinate concept. What is common, uniting the phenomena, shapes itself in each in accordance with the other, differing conditions. An example of this kind lies before us in the theory of light, of sound and of heat, in which more and more analogies are being worked out, e.g. in propagation, reflection, etc.² The agreeing phenomena lead to an agreeing element of grounding. The analogies of history which instruct us accomplish the same; for events do not recur as the same; but equal conditions which emerge in the sum of the events of an age remind us in advance to expect equal phenomena, even if these are determined differently by the other influences, and equal phenomena remind us to seek similar conditions.

[[1232]] In the first case analogy hits upon the substance of the concept; in the second, with differing essence, an equal secondary determination. In both cases it attempts to form a ⟦416⟧ concept or its essential constituents. But it does not complete the concept; it generates it only by half and skips the closer determination, since it is directed only to what is equal, whether this be the essence or the manner in which something otherwise different appears. This other half of concept-formation, which first gives analogy support and firmness, is a peculiar synthesis in the midst of the analysis of experience.

[[1235]] Examples readily illustrate this. If at first the analogy of free motion guided Newton's thought when he traced back the fall of bodies on earth and the motion of the heavenly bodies to one and the same attraction of masses, it was his penetration that, in the solar system, balanced this attraction toward the centre by the centrifugal force and diverted it into elliptical motion. This fusion was the synthesis in the analogy. The analogy led to the decomposition of elliptical motion into centripetal and tangential force. Without a construction comprehending both, the thought was not possible. When further Newton, following the analogy of a rotating soft mass, asserted the flattening of the earth at the poles before any investigation by measurements of degrees, the analogy gave only the universal. How great this flattening is, the specific difference of the concept, remained to be determined. When finally Newton, in whose scientific discoveries analogy plays so great a part, surmised from optical grounds, namely from the ratio of the refracting power of a ray of light, which he investigated in a series of transparent substances, to the density of their masses, a combustible constituent of water, which until then had been believed undecomposed and simple (for water had in that series a middle position between combustible and incombustible substances¹), the acute remark was only an indication; but later ⟦417⟧ chemistry fulfilled it, when it separated water into hydrogen and oxygen, a combustible constituent and one that serves combustion. In the first examples analogy divined the essence of the thing, in the last only a single side or relation; there the task remains to establish the universal essence in the peculiar difference of the thing; here, on the contrary, to carry the difference in its determinateness over to the essence of the thing. When analogy, as we mentioned, led to tracing back the phenomena of colours and of tones alike to wave-vibrations, this gives only *one* common side; the domains throw light on one another in this *one* respect; but the determination of the concept is only half. In what different element do these vibrations take place in these phenomena? Does the wave-motion have the same direction in both, or is it longitudinal in the one, transverse in the other? Such and similar questions, in answering which science first strives to give its object full determinateness, complete the acquisition of analogy.
