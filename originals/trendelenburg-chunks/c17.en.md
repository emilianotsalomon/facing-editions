[[1238]] In recent times language has been conceived as an individual function of the whole organic life, and the investigated laws of the organism illuminate the phenomena of language in a great analogy. But if what is most significant was not to be lost in this resemblance, then the peculiar essence of language, as it proceeds from the spiritual life of man, had always to form the ground-plan, for which those analogies supply the points of view of the execution. This apprehension of the particular essence, and this working of the analogy into this essence, is a synthetic and as it were artistic deed. What is here indicated in actual events of the sciences could also be made evident in the example borrowed above from Aristotle. "The war of the Athenians with the Thebans is ruinous; for the war of the Thebans with the Phocians, their neighbours on the border, brought calamity." So runs the analogy, which, in the convincing power of the individual case, warns against ⟦418⟧ war with neighbours in general and therefore also against the war of the Athenians with the Thebans. The analogy, however, gives the matter only by half; and the orator who chooses this example has to complete the universal thought into a picture, by presenting, in an insightful connection of the prevailing circumstances, the calamity that is to be expected in the case at hand.

[[1239]] In individual investigations it can still be shown historically how analogy formed the thread by whose guidance they advanced. Thus, for example, the conditions of the sense of sight have been investigated most finely and most completely of all the senses. One knows the after-images, the subjective phenomena of vision, and so on, and their laws, to a great extent and to a high degree of depth and precision. But since the senses, in their common vocation of appropriating the external world to the mind, must also present common phenomena, the known conditions of the sense of sight have been used in order to search for similar phenomena of the other senses, for example for subjective phenomena of hearing, of taste, and so on. The transference of the points of view from one domain to another, the forming of the analogy into the particular sphere, at once oversteps the analogy itself. In the sciences of language the richer languages already investigated form the standard that is applied to the phenomena of the new ones. The comparison leads to knowledge of what is peculiar.

[[1240]] If we trace in general the first beginnings of scientific discoveries and theories, they lie for the most part in the connection of analogies. Such a contingent view opens a deeper glance. Since, however, it always presupposes relations already known, from which the points of view are borrowed, it already follows that analogy cannot be the original method of investigation, and that other domains must open themselves to the mind independently of ⟦419⟧ analogy, through the consideration of the matter itself. As, therefore, according to the above, analogy, where it is the guide, leaves the most essential part of the task to be solved by another procedure, so it itself moves on a foreign basis.

[[1241]] Usually analogy is relegated, as an individual and subordinate procedure, to natural science, or is treated contemptuously as an element of Epicurus's unscientific logic. But no procedure governs all the sciences more universally than analogy, and one forgets what insights one owes to it. Whence has Plato's system its grand unity and its surprising symmetry? The same analogy of the whole that is mirrored in the part and is therefore to be known from the part, the same analogy of archetype and copy, of the Same and the Other, the same harmony of measure, recurs everywhere and illuminates the most diverse domains with the same creative unity, whether Plato in the Timaeus compares the world-soul and the human soul, or in the Politie sketches the state from the individual or the characters from the constitution, or finds in the conditions of the sense of sight the higher relations of knowledge as a whole.¹ The survey of affinity which Plato demands of dialectic, the pleasing proportion which distinguishes the architectonic of the whole edifice, rests on the logical ground of analogy. But from Plato one also learns to distinguish a deep analogy, which penetrates into the essence of the matter, from a shallow comparison.

[[1242]] 6. Analogy by itself alone is nothing but a hypothesis or the provisional basis of consideration. For it is not necessary that a similar phenomenon have an ⟦420⟧ equal ground. The hovering thought seeks a hold and strikes root only through foreign help.

[[1244]] The confirmation of every hypothesis lies in a peculiar combination of analysis and synthesis. The provisional concept is constructed synthetically in its consequences. With these consequences, which must result if the concept is true, the phenomena which the concept is to satisfy are compared analytically. Two processes are to coincide with each other: the logical, which would reproduce the world in the mind, and the real, which lies before knowledge as its task. The end-points of the real process come to light in the phenomena. The starting-point of the logical process is seized by thought. From this germ the mind develops what it contains, and thereby also gains end-points of the logical process; and now the judgment becomes possible whether the end-points of the two processes correspond. If this is the case, then with every compared consequence of the provisional concept the hope grows that the starting-points too, and thus the whole motions, coincide; but only the hope grows; for possibly false premisses have yielded what is true. From the phenomenon the ground is divined, and the divined ground is driven out into the consequences which it must have, as into its proper phenomena. Whether these coincide entirely and in every respect with those is the test of the assumption. A single incongruity already makes it doubtful, unless the phenomena of the real ground have not been correctly observed or the phenomena of the logical ground not correctly derived.

[[1245]] A hypothesis maintains itself by combining with those concepts that already stand secure. Where these contradict the assumption, it suffers an attack and runs into danger; where, on the other hand, they confirm the view in a concordant spirit, it fortifies itself through this support.

[[1247]] ⟦421⟧ Our whole world of concepts presents the spectacle of *one* great hypothesis. Our representations are always measured against the phenomena. The concepts that have been investigated stand firm; those that are forming still hover. The hovering ones seek to gain ground by wishing to support themselves on the firm ones or to hold on to them. Thereupon an attracting and repelling arises, according as they are compatible or incompatible. It may happen that in this struggle the concept believed to be firm is defeated by the hostile new one, in that the latter knows how to set the firm concept at variance with the other concepts and with the phenomena, and itself in harmony with them. Before the firm ones took root, they had to withstand the same struggle. In this interaction the realm of the knowing mind arises and grows and maintains itself. Whoever regards truth as a finished and secure possession of the mind will no doubt, when he perceives this pervasive struggle, fall into sceptical misgivings. But the mind knows no idle inheritance; it calls its own only what it has acquired and maintains. This labour is its pride and the common good of the race.

[[1248]] The form of hypothesis is the manner of every concept in the making. When the representations of the child are educated by the things themselves, it proceeds unconsciously according to the same law according to which mature science attempts to establish a provisional concept. The child has a representation of the shining object that is held out to it. It reaches for it. It misses at first, and now tries to sharpen the representation and reaches anew, until it attains the object and, as it were through the consequence of its representation, becomes certain of the truth of the latter. So man grows up, regulating his representations by success and by the phenomena. What is certain to him stands firm for him through this agreement. Science proceeds no differently when, instead of the mere representation turned toward the phenomenon, it seeks the concept of the ground. All that happens is that the ⟦422⟧ intermediate links multiply, and the synthetic deed of the mind becomes more chained together and more intertwined.

[[1249]] In knowledge produced in this way there arises between concept and consequences an organic interaction. The provisional concept produces the consequences, and the consequences confirm the concept. Thus *one* life pervades the whole process.

[[1250]] If, according to the whole investigation we have just conducted, the analytic method advances only through the synthetic, the dissecting only through the inventive, then the creative power rises in all the sciences, and the humility of the empirical sciences is mere show when they claim to arise and grow only through observation, only through what they faithfully take in from without. Through perception alone they would forever remain only on the surface of things, just as, conversely, without observation they would never reach the depth from which the wide, shining surface rises.

[[1251]] 7. In the above the genetic method has been presented as the last and consummating one. It rests on the matter's being known from its producing grounds. If these are only the efficient cause, the method follows this alone; if, on the other hand, the end determines the efficient cause, then the end becomes the guiding thought of the method in the same measure as it conditions the origination. In the former case, what precedes in the ground is also earlier in actuality; in the latter, the grounding end is indeed earlier as a co-operating thought, but later as attained actuality, and is precisely only the result. In the genetic procedure, therefore, the grounds of the matter are also the grounds of knowing, and because the former are earlier, be it really, as in the efficient cause, or ideally, as in the end, so too are the latter. What grounds cannot in this method be the result, as it is, say, in the regressively inferring, dissecting procedure of analysis.

[[1252]] ⟦423⟧ Against this view an important declaration of Hegel's is raised. In the speculative, everywhere "what is at first posited as following is rather to be the absolute prius, the truth of that through which it appears as mediated." The appearance in the scientific sequence would accordingly not be the sequence in the development of the matter; rather, the order would be precisely reversed. The case for which this assertion is inculcated may serve as an example. From the dialectic of subjective spirit there develops in Hegel's system objective spirit: first formal abstract right, then the right of the subjective will, morality, and finally the substantial will, ethical life, and indeed as family, civil society and state. In the dialectic of world history, further, objective spirit sublates itself. In the objective knowing of living ethical life the externalities of the world-spirit and the oppositions of finitude are stripped away, and this knowing thereby raises itself to the knowing of absolute spirit and gives birth to itself first in art, then in religion, until at last it frees and completes itself in philosophy. That is the course of the highest method, dialectic; but the course of the matter is precisely the reverse. Art is begotten and nourished by religion above all; and the whole history of art bears witness to this. In the "appearance of the scientific sequence," however, the dialectical spirit of art conversely brings forth religion. The state rests in its actuality on the ethical disposition, and this on the religious, and "develops out of religion." In the dialectical method, which alone claims to be method in the true sense, the state is conversely developed without religion, without consciousness of the divine, and so strictly by itself that the church is wholly foreign to it. But since the state goes first and ⟦424⟧ religion follows after, it is precisely "in the nature of this transition from the state to religion" that it is supposed to lie expressed that religion is "the basis of the state, being in and for itself, the source and power which has founded and brought forth it and its constitution," since, namely, religion, in which individuals have their deepest consciousness, pervades and shapes the state. Now can one understand the state scientifically without having understood this producing ground? The dialectical method demands it, and since it comprehends the state without religion (its basis), it sees precisely in this a guarantee that religion really is its basis. This paradoxical doctrine is extended to the appearance of the scientific sequence in general. For, as we have already stated, in the speculative "what is at first posited as following is rather the absolute prius." "The backward-going grounding of the beginning and the forward-going further determining coincide and are the same." In other words: the consequence of the concept is precisely the ground of the thing. The development of the highest scientific method stands in inverse relation to the development of the matter. Thus the genetic procedure, in which ground of knowing and ground of being go together, is turned outright on its head.

[[1256]] The scientific order and the order of actual origination do, to be sure, stand in inverse relation when one infers back from the consequences to the ground, when from the indications of the phenomena, as mere grounds of knowing, the ground of being is gathered. This hardly needs explanation. When, for example, the shape of the terrestrial spheroid is worked out from the segments of the arc measurements, or the former whole shape of a building from its ruins, or the essence of the primal ground from the fragments of our view of the world, then what is inferred, which in ⟦425⟧ the scientific appearance comes after, is indeed, according to the order of the matter, what is absolutely earlier. What grounds the matter and goes first in the matter is found in knowledge as what is grounded, and is therefore what is later in consideration. We have shown above how such a procedure must still supplement itself. Does dialectic perhaps wish to be nothing other than such a regressive inference? Then its laborious course, its artful unfolding, would be only a history of human consciousness, in which the grounds lying behind spring forth little by little. Whoever took it for this alone would earn no thanks from it. Often enough it has declared that it merely looks on at how the matter makes itself. Where, therefore, this inversion of earlier and later takes place, there dialectic does not wish to be; and where it wishes to be, there this common inversion does not take place.

[[1258]] Dialectic seeks to comprehend the shape which it lets arise in the mind in its origination. If the origination does not take place from the full grounds (the source and basis of the shape comes only afterwards), then the shape remains incomplete and distorted; and since the concept has its measure only in that origination which it follows, it becomes just as incomplete and distorted.

[[1259]] This shows itself precisely in the example of art and religion, whose position in the system is chiefly supposed to be protected by that doctrine of the reversed course.

[[1260]] The state does not produce religion as free fall produces accelerated motion, or a throw a curve, or motion in general a figure. Hence the state does not precede religion as the concept of the efficient cause from which the product is comprehended. Just as little is the state the means of religion, religion the end of the state, such that religion, as the end attained through the state, would have to follow the state. And were this the case, the very concept of the state would be governed by the thought of this end, and even then the state ⟦426⟧ could not be comprehended in its directions without religion. Rather, it is acknowledged in a fine way in the same passage that religion, as the animating disposition, determines from within the directions of the state and pervades its institutions. The state "rests on religion" and "develops out of religion." Hence the state is evidently as little comprehended without religion as the body without the moving, directing soul. One would not even believe one had understood a machine if one knew only the gearing of the wheels and not also the moving force, or knew the latter but not the directing end. How then would one comprehend the state with such gaps? For in the state the religious disposition that animates it is at work, now moving quietly, like a driving wind, now directing in the midst of the passions, like the pole star upon the restless sea. One must therefore regard the clause that the scientific order is the reverse of actual origination only as a clause, since it is no more than that. If one brings to life the real content of what is conceded in that passage as the basis of the matter, and lets it react upon the conception of the state, then the dialectical development of the state reveals itself as incomplete and insufficient. Either the state rests on religion, and then the state is not comprehended. Or the state is comprehended, and then it does not rest on religion, and religion comes afterwards and appears at most like the ornament attached to the finished house. As little as the development of the Germanic state can be understood without Christianity, so little is religion a dialectical shape which first raises itself through the negation of the state. If comprehending constitutes the concept, then the sequence of the concept, which develops the state by itself and religion out of the state, is a hysteron proteron of dialectic. The inconsistency betrays itself. For in the philosophy of world history, which concludes the philosophy of right and ⟦427⟧ precedes the philosophy of religion, the substance of the individual states is everywhere derived, profoundly and in accordance with the matter, from their religion. Thus in the midst of the system concrete religion appears with its power before one knows through the system what religion is, before the method has grasped religion. The same inconsistency repeats itself in the philosophy of art. In the dialectical system it precedes religion. For revealed religion is supposed, through the negative moment, to set itself over against art,¹ until both (through the negation of the negative) sublate themselves into their truth, philosophy. But in the matter it is otherwise. Art in its greatness and depth was conceived and born of the devotion of religion, and grew and ripened in the life of the cult. Art builds temple and church for the religious idea, presents its image, and lets its soul become tone and its spirit word. The different epochs, the different styles, are comprehended only from the different directions of religion. Aesthetics must therefore, as it in fact does in Hegel, reach ahead into the following shape of the system, into revealed religion. Thus the edifice is broken through in order to borrow light through an opening. If, with respect to the whole system, which strictly seeks to comprehend the one shape from the other, such an anticipation appears as inconsistency, it is on the other hand the consistency of the individual matter and its right. The eye for the matter has here grasped more correctly than the artificial dialectic.²

[[1270]] # ⟦431⟧ XX. THE INDIRECT PROOF.

[[1271]] 1. The genetic proof does not depart from the course of what is, and finds its measure in it. The indirect proof, which has precisely what is not as its basis, forms the opposition to it. Whereas the former lets necessity come to be, the latter establishes it by delimitation. It is shown that the assumption of the (contradictory) opposite is impossible.

[[1272]] Everything that falls outside a posited concept is repelled and annihilated from other fixed points, so that the boundaries are thereby closed and only what lies within them remains over as the sole possible remainder. But what all falls outside a concept? If the concept itself is affirmative, an infinite circumference of the opposite is designated by the negation. In order to find out the affirmative cases that are contained in it, there is need of a universal insight, of a larger, encompassing concept, which lies beyond mere negation. The acuteness of the indirect proof shows itself further in this, that something which is merely thought (the contradictory opposite) is thrown into the connection of the actual as if it were actual, in order that it may maintain itself in this connection ⟦432⟧ or give itself up. Since the basis is what is not actual, the help of intuition is lacking here. The whole procedure remains within thinking. While the direct proof keeps calmly and simply to the indicative, the indirect is as it were the struggle of the decided indicative against the supple conditional. The victory is the confirmation of the necessary.

[[1273]] In the indirect proof it is not the generating power of the origin of a concept that expresses itself, but the repulsion of the neighbouring propositions, or in general of what is already known.

[[1274]] If a negative judgment is to be proved, this happens genetically in the indirect proof; for negation is nothing other than the repelling force of the positive. The indirect proof leads to this source. For example, an equilateral triangle is not right-angled; for otherwise the sum of all the angles of the triangle would be equal to three right angles. The fixed proposition that in a triangle the sum of the angles is equal to two right angles refutes the consequence and thereby denies its ground. The first negative theorem in Euclid (elem. I. 7) is proved indirectly. Cf. elem. III. 4. III. 5. III. 6. In German the conjunction *sonst* ("otherwise") introduces the indirect proof.

[[1275]] Since the negative judgment is grounded indirectly, the indirect proof is the proof of refutation, which with the power of the actual defeats the false presupposition, and in particular the proof of negative criticism. Refutation rests everywhere on indirect proofs. For example, before Olaus Römer's discovery it was assumed in natural science that light appears instantaneously at the illuminated body. But were this the case, the eclipses of Jupiter's satellites would not be perceived a quarter of an hour later when the sun stands between Jupiter and the earth than at the time when both planets are on the same side of the sun. In this fact the velocity of light is observed. Therefore illumination does not take place ⟦433⟧ instantaneously. — Newton assumed that light passing close by bodies is bent, as though attracted by them. Fresnel refutes him. His proof is indirect.¹ "If forces of attraction or repulsion proceed from the edges of the diffracting body, acting with less energy upon the more distant particles of light than upon those grazing past more closely, then one does indeed understand how condensations or rarefactions now arise in their previously uniformly dense mass. But the particles of light emerging from the not far-extending sphere of action of those edges would then, moving in a medium acting equally upon them on all sides, have to shoot onward in straight lines. Those alternately bright and dark bands arising at the edge of the shadows, however, prove, when they are caught at different distances from the shadow-casting body, to be hyperbolically curved; such a bright band cannot therefore be the visible path of the same particles of light." What is here to be refuted, and in the end turns out not to be actual, is at the beginning assumed as true. From this assumption there follows, according to the co-operating conditions, as soon as a certain distance is reached, rectilinear motion. The fact, however, shows hyperbolic curvature. This actuality defeats the mere assumption. There results therefore (modo tollente) the negative judgment that those so-called phenomena of diffraction do not arise through attracting or repelling forces. — What appears here in examples from natural science shows itself likewise throughout the domains of the science of language and of history. For example, the peace of Cimon was not concluded; for had it been, Thucydides could not have kept silent about it, Cimon could not ⟦434⟧ immediately afterwards have undertaken his incursions into the Thracian Chersonese, and so on. Such facts overturn the assumption of the peace, and the negating assertion draws its force from these positive grounds.

[[1276]] > (1) Dove die neuere Farbenlehre mit anderen chromatischen Theorien verglichen 1838. p. 3. Cf. another example in the same work p. 10, Brewster's proof that the colours of the prismatic image of the sun are not homogeneous. What is added as positive to this indirect proof is, logically considered, only hypothesis.

[[1277]] In all these cases the positive opposite of the purely negative judgment is the point which stirs up a whole series of thoughts, in order to rule or fall with it. If the assertion is correct, it can take into itself what is correct lying alongside it, or at least be compatible with it. If it is incorrect, it will either annihilate itself in its consequences or be annihilated from the side of other knowledge. The consistency of concepts is always the power of the indirect proof. One sees this in an instructive way in the refutation of theories. The reproducing motion animates the supposed principle on all sides. Thereby there arises a world of thought consistent in itself, which now offers points of comparison by which to be measured against facts. Without such a development error hides itself and is impregnable, like a single point that conceals itself within itself. But in that the false draws nourishment from the true (from the causal connections of consistency), it grows and now reveals itself as semblance. Isolated, error maintains itself; but it annihilates itself as soon as it enters into relation on all sides. For the true at first checks it, until it clasps it so tightly that it suffocates. One sees this in the history of hypotheses. Whoever first puts forward an explanatory thought gives it hold and root within itself, and usually perceives only the factual that supports it. Whoever refutes it abolishes this happy, self-assured narrowness. The art of criticism consists partly in driving the necessary consequences of the assumed thought out to the point of impossibility, partly in directing the points fixed in knowledge, and their consequences, against the presupposition. We admire in it the greatness of the cool, penetrating ⟦435⟧ acuteness, in that the germ of the foreign thought is fertilised on all sides so that it may betray its misshapen essence. The basic type in this is always the indirect proof, which starts precisely from the opposite of what is recognised as true.

[[1278]] In the philosophical investigations of the masters many instructive examples are found. We recall, say, Plato in the Theaetetus. There he combats Protagoras, who asserts that man is the measure of all things. On the one hand he develops the grounds of the thought with full consistency to the point that, accordingly, not only man but equally every animal (the pig or the ape) would properly be the measure of things. On the other hand he directs the fixed facts of knowing that go beyond mere perception, such as the concepts of the universal and of the useful, the activities of remembering, understanding, and so on, against the assertion and lets it shatter powerlessly upon them. We recall further the manner in which Plato in the Philebus refutes the view that pleasure is the highest good. First he assumes it. But in sharpening the concept of the highest good, which is sufficient to itself, and of needy pleasure, they repel each other, and pleasure is not the highest good. We recall Aristotle's polemic against Plato's Ideas, and the criticism which Aristotle exercises on the earlier views about the essence of the soul.⁴ By letting them have their way in their consistency, but also granting them no consistency, he lets them ruin themselves. It does not belong here what Hegel has otherwise significantly brought out in Aristotle's critical art, that Aristotle knows how to prepare precisely in the negative the future positive, precisely in the contradictions against the untenable the future reconciliation of what is correct. Among the moderns we mention by way of example the procedure by which Leibniz ⟦436⟧ refutes Locke's empirical view,¹ or how Kant combats the ethical principle of one's own happiness.² In the individual sciences the path of refutation is the same, the universal course of the indirect proof.

[[1283]] 2. Accordingly the indirect proof is the proper proof of negation; yet in combination with a disjunctive judgment which sets the possible cases side by side it can ground an affirmation. The disjunctive members exclude one another; if the one is, the others are not; and if the others, all but one, do not belong to the subject of the universal sphere, then the *one* belongs to it as predicate. In this procedure the strict and complete division of the possible cases is necessary, but often extremely difficult. The indirect proof does not yield in and for itself the knowledge of the affirmation, but acts only as a member in a larger methodical whole.

[[1284]] Aristotle often made use of this procedure, and shows in it both the comprehensive view in the outline of the possible cases and the penetrating acuteness in the indirect proof, through which what is possible in the universal becomes impossible for the particular. He found, for example, the formal laws of the syllogism, down to the individual moods of the figures, by way of this method. First he outlines, according to the inner relation of the three terms, the three figures of the syllogism.⁴ In the individual figures he combines the possible cases of how, in the premisses of the syllogism, the universal affirmative, the universal negative, the particular affirmative and the particular negative judgment can be intertwined. These 16 cases, which result from such an external enumeration of the possibilities, he treats in the ⟦437⟧ first figure all of them and with particular diligence.¹ The valid cases of the first figure he proves directly, and directly for the most part also the valid cases of the other figures, by reduction to the first. The invalid ones he removes by an indirect proof. Thus here the indirect proofs delimit, while the direct ones generate. As the two unite, the strictly closed ground-plan of syllogistic arises. Where development cannot yet proceed genetically, such an indirect procedure in the service of a universal division of the possibilities often leads to the goal. Here belongs in particular the so-called method of exhaustion of the ancient geometers. It is proved indirectly that a magnitude is neither smaller nor larger than another. Consequently, one concludes, it must be equal to it, since only this third possibility of a relation remains. The simple principle of division of the disjunctive judgment which is present in these cases here gives a perspicuous clarity. One contents oneself with such a necessity of delimitation where an inner development is not yet possible. So, when we deliberate about the ⟦438⟧ possibilities and ends of the future, we are accustomed to take such a course.¹ And even in the judgments of ends by which nature seems to be guided in the organic, we might recognise a similar excluding procedure. Where we wish to guess a hidden inner ground, we seek such universal points of view of possibilities, in order to experiment with them and thereby to find indirectly what is sought. If now in this way the knowledge of the impossible forms the insuperable boundary of the actual, then for the matter a ground of knowing has indeed been found, but not yet the inner ground of origination.

[[1293]] 3. In the procedure just described, knowledge is directed to a determinate place and fixed in it through the union of the complete disjunction and the indirect proof. In this no disjunctive judgment suffices that is divided only contradictorily into an affirmation and its pure negation (A and not-A). For pure negation (not-A) as such cannot be the basis of a development. It is wholly indeterminate and contains a wide possibility which must first be translated into the positive cases.

[[1294]] If the disjunctive judgment arisen from universal insight, which subordinates the indirect proofs to itself as members, is lacking, then the procedure stands halfway. Then the indirect proof yields only negative results. With every negation we gain, we are indeed brought nearer to the affirmation. But whether we have exhausted all possibilities, whether we have now grasped the actual, is not guaranteed to us.

[[1295]] The empirical theories necessarily stand at such a standpoint. They get as far as the negation of a ⟦439⟧ view by means of the indirect proof flowing from the consequences; but in letting go of this old possibility they merely seize a new one. Whether there are not yet others is not established; for the closed circle of the disjunctive judgment proceeding from the higher universal is lacking. Since, by the nature of experience, the genetic proof of what is correct is also lacking, it is again only the consistent elaboration of the theory and its agreement with the fixed points of knowledge that stand in for the positive proof. But what is agreement with the fixed points? These points are relatively few, and agreement means only that these few do not refute the theory and that no indirect proof speaks against it. Thus the hypothesis is confirmed in its consequences; but the confirmation is always only conditional. For those hypotheses are only chance grasps, since the ordering universal is lacking. The struggle of theories is nothing but an indirect proof, but still without that comprehensive whole which excludes the contingency of the possibilities. The less, therefore, an empirical science has yet been worked through, the less it has yet succeeded in leaning upon a higher universal, the more are the hypotheses of explanation still determined by mere hitting the mark and groping about. Nevertheless, in contradiction with what is fixed and certain, what is false and uncertain annihilates itself. Contradiction appears here as the goad that drives the knowing mind out of what is nearest and superficial into the depth of truth. Therein lies its great significance.

[[1298]] If we look at the form of this process, it goes no otherwise with the mature mind of science on the paths of its research than with every child whose senses and representations are educated by the surrounding objects. When, for example, the child, prompted by the image of sight, reaches out with its hand but misses the object, when it forms a speech-sound after hearing but is not understood, ⟦440⟧ or when in some other way things do not answer to its representations, it finds itself refuted by this indirect proof; it gives up, as it were, its hypothesis and tries a new one, until it knows itself in harmony with life.

[[1300]] 4. The indirect proof, as Aristotle already shows, has less scientific value than the direct. If it is to demonstrate something positive, it passes through a double negation and comes about through the negation of the negation. For since the contradictory opposite is determined by negation, this negation is cancelled by the consequences. The provisionally assumed not-A, even if it should divide into the cases α, β, γ, is cancelled in the consequence, which shows itself to be impossible, and thereby the positive A is restored. The impossible results from the collision with propositions already known. The indirect proof therefore opens no insight into the inner grounds of the matter and is properly possible only where propositions already stand as proved. Its force lies in the repelling power (in the repulsion) of these propositions, thus outside the matter to be proved, outside its creative process.

[[1301]] Such fixed points, which are the condition of the indirect proof, form only within the system. How does it happen, nevertheless, that precisely the principles of systems, on which all stability depends, mostly fall to an indirect proof?

[[1302]] That this really happens can easily be observed. Aristotle already remarks that the logical principle of identity and of contradiction can be proved only indirectly.¹ ⟦441⟧ The many proofs which, in the principles in particular, from Aristotle¹ to Hegel, turn on the impossibility of a regress into infinity are indirect. In Spinoza the proofs of the first propositions governing the system are indirect, if they are not tacitly presupposed in the definitions. The foundation of the Leibnizian monadology is grounded indirectly. When Kant sets matter, according to its fundamental concept of the filling of space, into an equilibrium of attraction and repulsion, the proof is indirect; for the repulsive force alone would disperse matter into infinity, the attractive alone would contract it into a mathematical point. In both cases matter would be annihilated and no space filled. Whoever examines the establishment of principles will easily multiply these examples for himself.⁶
