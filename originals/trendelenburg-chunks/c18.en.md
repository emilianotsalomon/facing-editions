[[1310]] # ⟦443⟧ XXI. THE SYSTEM.

[[1311]] 1. The various modes of grounding have been set out. We have drawn attention to how they demand one another and work together in the living act of knowing. An example may illustrate this unity, which at the same time leads over to a larger logical form.

[[1312]] All understanding is interpretation, whether of the spoken word or of meaningful phenomena themselves. The inner process has in both cases a great affinity. We therefore call to mind the course of the mind in philological explanation, in order to rediscover in this more easily observed activity the more intricate one; and we shall recognise the unity of the methods if, for example, we observe in ⟦444⟧ detail what turns our thinking tacitly makes in order to understand a difficult and obscure passage of an ancient classic.

[[1313]] > (1) In the "Erläuterungen zu den Elementen der aristotelischen Logik," 2nd ed. 1861, the author has given examples from the most diverse disciplines, and in the "Naturrecht auf dem Grunde der Ethik" he has devoted a whole section (§§. 71-82) to presenting the logic of law, in its origin and application, according to the various methods that intertwine in it. It is important not to keep abstract logic in the abstract or to enfeeble it in contrived examples, but to follow it into the actual sciences and to behold it there in its full significance. To this the writings named may serve as a guide, in agreement with the "Logische Untersuchungen."

[[1314]] The procedure here is, in its whole direction, analytic. From the written word as the visible phenomenon, the producing ground, the thought, is to be found. But in solving this task we at once proceed synthetically. For we understand the individual passage, as we read on, through the living reconstruction of the whole. Hence, when a passage strikes us as difficult, we already stand in the midst of the propelling ground of the thought. We stumble precisely because the analytic procedure, which as it were gathers the sense from the signs, comes into contradiction with the synthetic, which from the whole illuminates every part that arises through analysis. The new part will not fit into the image of the whole already gained, and the power of the unity in which all understanding takes place rejects it as foreign. At once the previous synthesis becomes problematic, and the question arises: is the new part or the old whole, or are both, wrongly taken, and how can they be reconciled? The means we apply to such a question are in the first instance analytic. We construe the passage, say, according to the word-forms, which, like grounds of knowledge, permit an inference back. Now a sense is brought out. Is it the right one? The connection of the whole passage, that is, the synthesis, is the test of this analytic result. The attempted explanation is perhaps false. The refutation then appears in an indirect proof. For if that view gave the correct sense, we infer, then this and that in the whole or in the particulars would be absurd. The context offers that resistance from which an indirect proof in general starts. The explanation is given up; a new one is tried, until the analytic procedure, which rests on the grammatical relations, and the synthetic, which from out of the whole recreates the inner ⟦445⟧ thought, mutually confirm each other. The inner genesis of the thought, which clothes itself with necessity in the given form, is the direct proof. In the whole process the gaze is directed to the individual, and hence the syllogism, which derives the individual from the factual universal, easily vanishes from observation. But it is tacitly present. If, for example, a universal grammatical rule is applied in the course of it, or if in the indirect proof one argues from a universal, this happens through the rapid linking of a syllogism of the first figure. The exclusive refutation usually ends in a syllogism of the second figure. Induction is active as an auxiliary power, for instance in establishing a lexical meaning which is tried out for the understanding.

[[1315]] In the rapid interchange of thoughts we do not distinguish these different directions of method. But if we attend to it, we admire our own weaver's masterpiece:

[[1316]] "Where *one* tread stirs a thousand threads,

[[1317]] The shuttles shoot across and back,

[[1318]] The threads flow on unseen,

[[1319]] *One* stroke strikes a thousand connections."

[[1320]] We think in a manner similar to the way we move. In an instant we set the hand in free play. How many muscles work together in *one* unity to do so! When the physiologist shows us their intertwined activity, we admire the organism. The forms of thinking work mentally as the muscles do bodily. We exercise both without seeing or knowing them.

[[1321]] The understanding of a difficult passage, as we have just analysed it, is as it were a model of all knowing. If in general the reconstruction of the thing from the whole (the synthesis) grows into the forms of the phenomena (the grounds of knowledge of analysis) in such a way that the two affirm and attest each other, then what ⟦446⟧ can be attained is attained. It is only the task of the human mind to understand the world as a whole in the same way.

[[1322]] 2. In the preceding example, which presents the knot in which the methods are tied together, it emerges anew that the mind is directed towards a unity of the whole of knowledge. This unity of the whole is everywhere the silent presupposition. All cognitions seek to gravitate about *one* centre. What is remote is not to fall apart, and what is near is not to shrink together. Unity is not merely absence of contradiction, which is demanded in the first instance in the indirect proof, but community of thinking and being, out of which alone mental necessity weaves its eternal bond.

[[1323]] The system presents this great unity and is, as it were, only *one* expanded judgment.

[[1324]] Thinking and being correspond here too. The concept became living in the judgment, as substance in activity. The ground poured itself out into its consequences, as the cause into its effect. The connection of concepts and judgments forms the system, as the connection of substances and activities forms the world.

[[1325]] The logical unity that corresponds to the metaphysical has been treated above. The reproduction shows itself here only on a larger scale.

[[1326]] We distinguish a system of arrangement and a system of development. Both master a multiplicity of cognitions through unity. In the one the survey of division prevails, in the other the living generation from a principle. In the former, finished substances are put together according to their affinity; in the latter, they arise from their grounds.

[[1327]] The rule of a principle of division determines the system of arrangement; the genetic method, when it completes itself, produces the system of development. ⟦447⟧ The former is meant to be a preliminary stage of the latter, and only the latter is system in the eminent sense.

[[1328]] When first, through a view from the standpoint of the onlooker, a flash of light falls upon a mass of representations, and these now link themselves in a fundamental thought, even if still a subjective one, and when the theory then presses further into the explanation of the thing, these attempts complete themselves in the system.

[[1329]] The system seeks in its development to cover a developing domain of phenomena, and seeks the independent whole.

[[1330]] The individual systems of the sciences are themselves only members of *one* great system. They grow into one another by drawing nourishment from one another. When these dependent members close together into *one* organism that realises itself, there arises the image of the great system, which seeks to be the mental counter-image of the world.

[[1331]] 3. It lies in the nature of that fundamental science which we designated at the outset and have pursued in our investigations that, in seeking out the logical and metaphysical principles, it should obtain the basic outlines for the articulation of the system of the sciences. We therefore attempt, in a glance at the results, to mark the lines that form the ground plan.

[[1332]] We laid weight on the concept of stages, on such a progression that not only does the earlier prepare the later methodically and really, as the simple prepares the compound, but also the earlier, measured by the end of the whole, appears as the lower, without which, however, we do not reach the higher. Such a relation we saw in particular in those two groups of principles which were distinguished as efficient cause and end. The stages rise, and in ⟦448⟧ development we see the lower striving toward the higher, and the higher, even at a time when it is not yet outwardly there, drawing the lower to itself or preparing it as its organ. In the same sense the sciences form stages of knowledge. In order to present them, we attach ourselves to the questions in which at the beginning we saw the motives for logic and metaphysics; and it will thereby become clear whether, and how far, we have kept them in view. These questions could be put in two expressions which at bottom intend the same thing. Taken universally, they ran thus: how is science possible at all, and how does the mind produce necessity? These universal questions articulated themselves of their own accord, through the principles that set themselves off from and graduate one another, into the particular questions comprised under them.

[[1334]] Through the elementary mediation of thinking and being that was demanded, which turned out to be constructive motion, the foundation was gained. With this an unconscious causal activity, motion in space, was connected with a conscious one, the constructive, the two were regarded as corresponding to each other, and the unconscious one, by entering under identity in consciousness, was made knowable. Since with the figures of designing motion there arose the possibility of intuiting a priori, i.e. before experience and as conditioning experience, the question was answered: how is mathematical knowledge possible? The logical deed, growing consistently in this domain within the human race, was explained by the possession of a real principle; for constructive motion, generating figures and numbers, must be designated as such a principle. Without a real principle at its origin, pure knowledge would remain empty. In the same act we see mathematical necessity arise. If in all necessity, as emerged in the investigation of the modal categories, ¹ subjective and objective ⟦449⟧ agree, then in pure mathematics this relation takes the form that the law of the thing flows from our own activity and its inner determination. Mathematical knowledge is the seen-through consequence of a generating deed of our own. From construction and determination there springs the manifold in unity; and because this origin is known, it is possible to lead the given manifold back to the law of its origin and to follow out the consequence in the interaction of the figures that have arisen. The point is only to find out what is posited along with the generating deed; and to this the mathematical acumen is directed in the knowledge of laws. In Kant's example, 7 + 5 = 12, we count together, we set off, we have instituted the decimal order. The example of mathematical necessity, 2 × 2 = 4 (we say that something is as certain as that twice 2 is 4), is evident to everyone because it is our own deed. Once posited, it yields, through relations which it takes up, other necessary things, e.g. 4 : 2 = 2, 3 + 1 = 4, and so on. It is the same with the triangle that we construct, with the parallels that we draw. The trigonometric laws, which no one suspects in the triangle at first glance, are nevertheless in it; when the circle and its relations are combined with the triangle, they come forth. For the progress of mathematical necessity everything depends only on finding the means to follow out the consequence of the essence in its interaction with something else. Mathematical necessity proverbially counts as the strict one. It is alloyed with nothing foreign that might come from outside, and therefore with nothing contingent.

[[1336]] In the domain of experience, which appeared as the second stage, the given rules. The knower stands within it in real interaction with the real, and perception, which in the end becomes sensible to him in pleasure and pain, vouches to him for this actuality. From this arises, in this domain, the concept of fact. However ⟦450⟧ far the inference back may move away from the first impression, the effect of the real underlies it. In opposition to plays of imagination, to detached representations which carry on their doings within us, experience bound by fact instructs through the things themselves and creates power over things. In the new that this stage presents, the old is at work. Appropriation through the senses takes place with the help of constructive motion; investigation goes back into mathematical laws; matter is in the end intelligible only through motion. Thus the question was answered how experience of material forces (physics in the narrower sense) is possible. In it the understanding once more produces necessity — let us call it material (physical) necessity — whose peculiarity within the efficient cause is the interweaving of fact and ground. The compelling fact, the basis of this necessity, exercises indeed only an external constraint, but the mathematical consideration of the ground transforms it into mental necessity. Let one but think away from physics and technology the mathematical element, all constructions and calculations, and one sees that no necessity remains in them. The proposition is therefore correct that there is only as much necessity in physics as there is mathematics in it. The material fact is permeated by mathematical necessity.

[[1337]] A third stage appears where organic nature reveals a new fundamental concept, to which the earlier principles serve as the condition of its existence. In the end, which the inventing mind designs and the contemplating mind recognises again where it is realised, in the end, which is intelligible only from the prefiguring thought that anticipates the effect to the cause, the question is answered how a knowledge of organic nature is possible. It yields organic necessity. Resting on the two preceding stages, for these become its organ, ⟦451⟧ it becomes peculiar through the thought as the designing unity that subordinates the many to itself; it is necessity from the determining thought of the whole. As in a geometrical problem the known laws become the means of solution, so for the end from which organic necessity springs, mathematical and physical necessity become means. The thought of a whole becomes the soul of a physical necessity. Constructive motion makes possible the saying attributed to Plato, that God is in the world a geometer; and if he is so, in the physical as in the organic, then the principle of this divine geometry had to form the beginning. Since necessity, as it were acknowledged being, arises only from a community of thinking with what is, from a contact of the subjective and the objective, the subjective element has now risen. Where in the physical the thought still empties itself out into material multiplicity, in the organic it finds its ownmost concept again as a formative one.

[[1338]] Out of the organic stage, finally, the ethical stage rises. It masters the earlier ones and at the same time liberates them. If one asks how a knowledge of the ethical is possible, the answer lies in this, that the ultimate end of the human essence, and human nature as means or organ for this end, can be known. Now, as the law enters into the will, ethical necessity appears, and as the will satisfies the law of its essence, the same necessity appears as freedom. In ethical necessity organic necessity, which determines multiplicity from unity, is presupposed, and with the organic, physical and mathematical necessity. The forces which in the organic are means rise in the ethical to persons, who are means and at the same time ends in themselves.

[[1339]] From stage to stage the principles become more concrete, more intergrown, more bound, but also, through the known conditions ⟦452⟧ of the preceding ones, clearer and freer. In that element in which at each stage the thinking mind has community with them, the possibility is given it of so fitting itself to the objects determined by these principles that it grasps them. Its logical forms therefore belong to all the sciences, but are determined in them specifically according to the object, so that through them the knowledge of necessity may ripen.

[[1340]] It cannot be denied that these stages, which must form the division of the system advancing according to inner principles, rise towards us, and it is as if on the last we crowned ourselves. Do we really do so? We confess that what we men call system stems only from a little piece of the world and is thought only on the earth, that is, perhaps only on a splinter of the glowing and shining ore of the sun flung out into great space; but we feel that already in all necessity a trait announces itself which is mightier than man and points beyond man, who is everywhere conditioned.

[[1341]] 4. Already in the thought of the world we overfly the circle of experience. For wherever we look, there is fragmentary work. But driven by the impulse of the mind, we seize the whole.

[[1342]] The idea of science here goes further than its realisation. Not even the whole of the phenomena, infinite in great and in small space, is accessible; much less the depth of the whole ground. Only the Promethean defiance of human knowing points to the earth as the sole dwelling-place of mind and says presumptuously: hic Rhodus, hic salta; as if there were nothing else. Does not experience itself already show us the worlds that we do not know? But to be sure, enough is given us, and it is our task to understand from the fragments the spirit of the whole; for the phenomena are its revelations.

[[1343]] ⟦453⟧ Herein a new concept announces itself, a concept of the mind boldly surpassing conditioned experience, the unconditioned, the absolute, which is regarded as the proper object of metaphysics. We are led to the same concept when we follow the grounds of things backwards into their conditions, proceeding from conditions to the conditions of the condition. The first ground, which would condition all, will itself be the unconditioned. There remains for the next chapter the question whether, and how far, a knowledge of the unconditioned is possible. The answer must stand in close connection with what is necessary, as the preceding investigations have yielded it.

[[1344]] Before we pass on to this last question, only one more point may be discussed, so that no gap may remain in the articulation of the sciences just indicated.

[[1345]] If we saw the sciences graduate themselves with the principles and rise as mathematical, physical, organic and ethical, the question arises: where, then, do logic and metaphysics belong, whose unity we have held fast? We designated them above as the fundamental discipline, and we remark the following in justification.

[[1346]] In the division and sequence of the sciences two guiding points of view easily cross: the order that follows the origin of the thing, and the order that the course of teaching and learning makes necessary. The methodical consideration cuts across the genetic rigour. For the genetic consideration draws from the ground of the thing, whereas the methodical arrangement adapts itself to the needs of the developing, learning mind. The position of logic in the systems therefore not seldom appears like a hysteron proteron. As theory of science it must enter into principles which belong to the other sciences and which it first receives from them; and yet in the philosophical system of the disciplines it can hardly come after them; for it is meant to secure their ground ⟦454⟧ and to sketch out their structure. As the investigation of subjective thinking, logic becomes in the genetic system a part of psychology; but as theory of knowledge, as theory of science, it must serve as guide not only to psychology but also to the sciences that precede it. This double relation brings a wavering into the position of logic.

[[1347]] If one places oneself at the point at which philosophy first arises at all in its difference from the individual sciences, the circle will be resolved in which such a science seems to ground the following philosophical disciplines and yet to stand on their ground.

[[1348]] Although philosophy, if we ask history, arose in a unity with the other sciences, this bond has nevertheless long since been dissolved through the division of labour, and philosophy now finds the individual sciences before it in their dispersion and in the form they have given themselves on their own account. Logic and metaphysics have in them their material for consideration; they find in them methods and presupposed principles, and have the task of seeking out their origin and their unity. Through this apprehension of the common source, through this mutual regulation and animation, philosophical content is generated, and those germs arise which, in the development of the system, become the principles of the philosophical real disciplines. In this way, the isolated sciences in their historical forms are indeed presupposed by the fundamental science of logic and metaphysics, but the philosophical disciplines proceed in their articulation from the latter. Logic and metaphysics thus do not reach forward into the philosophical disciplines, but back into the empirical ones.

[[1349]] In this sense philosophy is neither an idle repetition of the particular sciences nor an ency⟦455⟧clopaedic digest of them; rather, on the ground of logic and metaphysics, of fundamental philosophy, it completes the knowledge of the human race at any given time, in that, intent on the idea of the whole, it brings the philosophical principles to bear in the articulation of the particular, and generates or conditions the principles for the subordinate particular. How far it advances thereby into the individual sciences is left to the art with which it handles the principle formatively. Thus, on the soil of the fundamental science, logic and metaphysics, it designs those four graduated real disciplines and attaches them to the knowledge of the absolute as to the ultimate point of fastening.

[[1350]] 5. The proposed division of philosophy is taken from the principles of the matter, from the inner relation of the objects, and only such a division will turn out sharp and determinate. According to a remark of Sextus Empiricus¹ the division of philosophy into dialectic, physics and ethics, which with the Stoics became the norm of the system, already lies in germ in Plato. In Plato dialectic is that fundamental science, demonstrating the idea, which unites logic and metaphysics, and physics and ethics are borne by it. According to the result of our investigations, physics must be differentiated into the knowledge of the mathematical, the physical and the organic stage. What is found in Cartesius as the indication of a division, and in Spinoza as a plan in the sequence of the books of his Ethics, corresponds on the whole to the original simple design of the Platonic division.

[[1351]] In Aristotle a subjective division early confronts it, which articulated philosophy according to the three modes of human activity, contemplating, acting and making, as theore⟦456⟧tical, practical and poietic, as knowledge of contemplation, of active life and of formative art.¹ It was a falling away from the first point of view when, on a new, material principle of division, theoretical philosophy was divided into first philosophy, physics and mathematics, practical philosophy into ethics, economics and politics, and logic was then placed before all as the instrument of the disciplines; and this turn towards the objective may be left aside. The question is how far that first and most universal principle of division suffices. It is not to be overlooked that the three activities, contemplating, acting and making, are distinguished according to the directions of their end. Contemplating seeks to know for the sake of knowing; making seeks to produce in order to intuit a thought or to fix a feeling; acting, on the other hand, seeks an effect as such. But these different ends carry the others within themselves reciprocally as means, and are therefore unsuited to serve as specific difference. Contemplating is contained as a requirement in acting as in making. For acting must be permeated by reason, and making is meant to present an idea and bring it to intuition. Likewise making is contained in acting as in contemplating; for acting completes itself only in ethical beauty, in a presentation which, like the work of art, corresponds to its idea. Contemplating requires productions in order to reach its goal. In the disciplines one can set theorems and problems, propositions and tasks, against each other as science and art. Now whoever perceives how the solution of the tasks is conditioned by knowledge, and the propositions and the proof of the propositions by the carrying out of tasks, and how further in the natural sciences observation and experiment accompany each other, sees easily how science and art, contemplating and making, ⟦457⟧ advance together, and that these concepts are therefore not suited to draw a boundary line between two domains of philosophy. Finally, acting fulfils itself in a peculiar way in the scientific calling through contemplating and in the artistic through making. If, therefore, a division of philosophy on the ground of these concepts is strictly carried out, repetitions are unavoidable. Already in Aristotle, the originator of this tripartition, into whose peculiar determinations we have refrained from entering, it becomes doubtful where individual disciplines, e.g. rhetoric, are to be reckoned.

[[1356]] The Aristotelian division reaches into recent times. Christian Wolf divided philosophy into theoretical and practical. Baumgarten added aesthetics and to that extent restored poietic philosophy as a third part. Kant, as regards the division of philosophy, is dependent on Chr. Wolf. One sees this clearly when one compares Kant's architectonic of pure reason with the introduction to Wolf's logic. When Kant, like Wolf, divides philosophy first into theoretical and practical, the separation of mental activity into the faculty of knowledge, the faculty of desire and the ⟦458⟧ faculty of feeling has an essential influence on this in Kant as in Wolf.¹ But the results in Kant testify against the correctness of the division. Practical reason in his case reaches back into the domain of theoretical reason, in that it produces postulates, that is, theoretical presuppositions which were doubtful to the *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*.

[[1359]] Herbart belongs here insofar as he too does not divide philosophy according to objects. When he defines philosophy as the elaboration of concepts, his principle of division is the logical activity that they require. From the chief ways in which concepts are elaborated the chief parts of philosophy result. Insofar as the end is to make concepts clear and distinct, logic arises for him. Insofar as given concepts of experience carry contradictions within themselves and must therefore be altered and supplemented according to their particular constitution in order that they may become thinkable, there results for him the science of metaphysics, which, in a similar way as with Wolf and Kant, finds its application in psychology, philosophy of nature and natural theology. Finally, concepts are distinguished which in our representing necessarily bring about a judgment of approval or disapproval, and the science of such concepts is for him aesthetics. Applied to the given, it passes over into a series of technical doctrines, all of which may be called practical sciences; practical philosophy in the narrower sense is for him that one among the technical doctrines whose precepts bear the character of necessary compliance because we involuntarily and unceasingly present its object. This division is rooted wholly in Herbart's peculiar philosophical intuition and can be judged only together with it. Meanwhile the rigour of the division is doubtful already for the following ⟦459⟧ reasons. First, according to the principle of division indicated, logic and aesthetics do not stand sharply apart. For the clearness and distinctness of concepts also pleases, and a technical doctrine can be directed to this too. In Herbart's school this consequence has in fact been drawn. Bobrik's logic¹ transfers the analogy of practical philosophy to the theory of knowledge and designs five original and five derived logical ideas, as Herbart presents five original and five derived practical ideas. The specific difference between logic and aesthetics thus does not hold through. Further, it was called into doubt above whether, with regard to the concepts of experience, there exists such a task as the metaphysical correction and supplementation demanded by Herbart. Partly the contradiction in the concepts of experience appears only by the falsely applied standard of the law of identity; partly, if it is assumed, it is resolved by Herbart only in appearance. Hence this kind of elaboration of concepts is unable to ground a metaphysics; and were it able to, the specific difference would again not hold through. For if one determines contradiction in Herbart's sense, the aesthetic concepts, namely the practical ideas, contain the same contradiction within themselves, as, for example, the idea of equity, according to Herbart's conception, is not thought without the change brought about by an action, a concept which according to Herbart's metaphysics contradicts itself. For these reasons Herbart's foundation of the division will not be able to hold even under his own presuppositions, and much less as a universal one outside his system. In particular, the unity of the system and of the world-view is torn apart by the fact that practical philosophy is deliberately detached from the foundation of metaphysics and ⟦460⟧ the ethical concepts are made self-standing through the character of necessary approval. Thereby the community is abolished in which the sciences, without prejudice to their difference, flourish.
