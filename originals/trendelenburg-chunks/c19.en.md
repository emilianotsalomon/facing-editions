[[1364]] # ⟦461⟧ XXII. THE UNCONDITIONED AND THE IDEA.

[[1365]] 1. Only in the concept of the whole does the restless motion of the mind come to rest. Unconditioned unity is the tacit presupposition in the process of knowing, if that process does not arbitrarily check itself on its way. We carry this result over from the last consideration. This unconditioned, which bears the unity of the whole, philosophical abstraction calls the absolute; livelier faith calls it God. In the absolute alone is the relative made fast; in the unconditioned the conditioned gains support and significance; in God creation gains unity and end.

[[1366]] In this proposition we combine two things which, psychologically taken, are not of the same origin: the unconditioned and the concept of God. The representation of God is there, in some shape or other, before science; the concept of the unconditioned, by contrast, is produced by science.

[[1367]] The representation of God has its own phases in the history of human consciousness, from that lowest shape in which it is connected with the same affects that produce superstition, up to that in which the rational concept of ⟦462⟧ unity has already cast multiplicity to the ground, ethical concepts have purified and exalted the God of fear, and reflections akin to science have formed a God of thought. This tendency of human consciousness sets out from another side than science; it sets out from what is first for man; the concept of causality appears in the mirroring of human affects or in the certainty of ethical need. Science, by contrast, pursues what, as the essence of the matter, is first by nature. Where it thinks the ultimate ground, it meets with that tendency which immediately seizes upon the ruling power and shapes it into that in which man shelters and makes fast the destiny of his transient existence.

[[1368]] When Parmenides set over against the existent, which is here or there, is today and tomorrow is not, and which announces its limitation, and so its not-being, on every side, what is, simply and absolutely, the unbecome One; and when Plato's dialectic, perceiving the conditioned basis of all the particular sciences (the ὑποθέσεις) in the presuppositions of the object and of the principles, pointed to the concept of the unconditioned (the ἀνυπόθετον): then the absolute appeared as the necessary consequence of scientific thought.

[[1369]] Plato already carried the unconditioned over, in the Idea of the Good, to God and the divine; and Platonism, taking the word in the widest sense, has always posited the concept of the unconditioned, which is produced by the logical path, and the concept of God, which originally belongs to religion, as one and the same. Only Democritism, with Epicurus at its head, has separated the two domains. Even Spinoza, whose concept of God is the necessary, the aeternum, always calls the unconditioned God.

[[1370]] It would perhaps be more correct to keep the two concepts apart here. Now and then we have already anticipated above in the sense ⟦463⟧ of this unification, and, for example, in the end of our existence have called the inner destination, as the unconditioned within this sphere, the divine. But at bottom, in all those considerations which do not make matter alone the unconditioned, the two coincide in one.

[[1371]] Philosophical theology inherited the concept of God as a given one, and in the face of doubt it had the task of grounding the reality of this concept. Hence comes the peculiar form in which metaphysics treats the unconditioned. As the jurist demands proofs of a fact that has been called into doubt, so metaphysics demands proofs of the existence of God and examines the proofs, as Kant does. We shall follow them up after we have sent ahead some general remarks on the concept of the unconditioned and the knowledge of it.

[[1372]] The unconditioned is no negative concept. The negating expression refers to the path by which we arrive at the concept; it negates the negation which is proper to the conditioned as something limited. The concept itself is positive and, if it has truth, the most affirmative of all; for the unconditioned, borne by no other but bearing all else, sufficient to itself and grounded in itself, affirms itself and everything conditioned. Given nowhere, for what is given is what is limited, the concept, which in metaphysical consideration first appeared in the "what is" of Parmenides, is the highest divination of the mind.

[[1373]] Is this unconditioned, then, in truth? or is it only the necessary but deceptive ideal of the mind? And if the unconditioned is in truth, what is its life, and how is it to be known?

[[1374]] Kant dissolved the unconditioned into a manufactured ideal, into the illusion of an inner phantasm. When we have the coloured rainbow before us, we have the sunlight, ⟦464⟧ the changeless One, behind us, and we need only turn round to it. So too in that archetype of the human mind the eternal light will be mirrored. Nowhere in nature is there an appearance that would not have a mightier being behind it and stream forth from it. Should there, then, for the first time in the human mind, be such an appearance without a being that produces it? Let us only turn towards that being.

[[1375]] It might be said that the unconditioned which we posit arises for us only through the determinateness which is, once and for all, the character of our knowledge; that it is only an analogy which we transfer from the individual things we survey to the whole. This sceptical possibility has at least in part already been refuted in the history of the sciences, and it refutes itself by an indirect path, unless knowledge is to fall to pieces.

[[1376]] It has already been shown above that principles as principles admit of no direct proof but only of an indirect grounding. This case occurs here with doubled force. For the unconditioned is what is original; it has nothing before it from which it could be known, as, for instance, the circle has motion and the radius before it, from which, as from its grounds, it is known. But the fixed point which, in indirect grounding, has the power to annihilate the thought of the opposite, is in this case not an individual thing but the whole of knowledge and whatever has any hold for man.

[[1377]] But if we now wish to think the absolute, with what determinations are we to think it? The categories were derived from motion, the first act of finite thought and finite being, and the end, which gave the categories a new design, was understood from the community of the two. They can therefore hold for us only of the ⟦465⟧ finite. We have no right to extend space and time, quantity and quality, substance and accident, effect and reciprocal action, as they flowed for us from generating motion, beyond this finite domain. We have no right to grasp the infinite in these categories, won and tested only in the finite, and thereby to determine its innermost essence. The critical consciousness of the conditioned origin of the categories would be lost to us if we wished to grant them, in and for themselves, the right to present the innermost essence of the unconditioned. At this boundary we lay down the arms of our finite knowing.

[[1379]] In this respect there is no proof of the existence of God, if by proof one means the genetic one; in this respect also no constructive knowledge of his essence, provided that all construction is possible only through the intuitive categories which we derived.

[[1380]] 2. The so-called proofs of the existence of God therefore have value only as points of view which cannot be understood without the absolute. They are indirect groundings which work out, each in its own way, the fundamental theme of the unconditioned. How little they prove outright with strict necessity, Kant has demonstrated. Nevertheless they indicate what discord would arise if God were not posited. In this thought lies their compelling power. What is true in the finite by its necessity cannot be untrue in the infinite. Rather, what is necessary in conditioned knowledge becomes the reliable point to which the presupposition of the unconditioned is made fast. But let no one believe that the proofs alone could give the concept of God the life which it has through tradition from generation to generation.

[[1381]] It is customary to enumerate the ontological, cosmological, teleological and moral proofs without inner order ⟦466⟧ or without the guarantee of completeness. They appear like torn-off parts of a view of the world.

[[1382]] One could order them logically according to the thought that underlies the task of knowing. First, God appears as a presupposition of thinking, and we could compare this grounding, even if in a different sense, with the ontological proof. If, further, God is known as the presupposition of all being, the cosmological proof arises. If, finally, God is regarded as the presupposition of that mediation of knowing and being which we grasp as realised in the world permeated by thought, then the teleological proof results, and, in the particular sphere of human action, the moral proof.

[[1383]] The teleological and the moral proofs are mostly separated from one another, and in this separation one still recognises the preponderance which Kant gave to the practical proof. From a higher point of view the two come together into a unity. Both have their force in the harmony of the end, which God posits and upholds; in the one this appears in the instrument of nature, which is strange to itself, in the other, by contrast, in the self-determining and self-surrendering organ of free man. This difference shapes the different course, but it does not blur, rather it realises, the one fundamental thought of the divine end.

[[1384]] 3. The ontological proof, as it has occupied metaphysics and the philosophy of religion since Anselm, seeks to demonstrate the existence of God from the concept of God. Sometimes this concept is taken, as by Anselm, as the concept of the highest being, which, just as the highest, could not be imagined merely, since the actual is higher than what is merely thought; sometimes as the concept of the being that includes in itself all perfection and therefore also the perfection of existence, as Cartesius took it; sometimes as the concept of the being that contains only ⟦467⟧ affirmations and therefore no obstructing contradiction, as Leibniz determined it. However these concepts are taken in detail, and whatever particular defects each may suffer from by itself, all have one and the same infirmity. We have only thought these concepts, and therefore have also only thought the existence that may lie in them. Everything remains enclosed in thought. What compels us to posit this thing thought and to determine it as an actual one? This compulsion does not stem from the proof itself and can be brought in only by considerations from elsewhere. The proof, then, is no proof. If, within formal thinking, one wishes to reach God from thinking, one arrives at no being, because one looks away from being. Kant is therefore right with his well-known critique against these shapes of the ontological proof.¹

[[1385]] When we showed above that it is the highest stage of knowledge that what is dependent is developed from the concept of the thing, the ontological grounding is not to be confused with this. There either the intuition of existence or the construction of the coming-to-be was to be presupposed; here this basis is lacking.

[[1386]] The ontological proof is the boldest attempt a priori. Hegel brought it into honour anew. With him, however, it means something quite different. With him it is not, as with his predecessors, compressed into the force of a single syllogism. Rather, for him the whole of logic is the one ontological proof. "The pure concept is the absolutely divine concept itself, and the logical course is the immediate presentation of God's self-determination to being."² Accordingly the ontological proof is the dialectical development in which the absolute concept gives itself objectivity. But the dialectical development has been refuted above, and with it this shape of the ontological proof as well.

[[1390]] ⟦468⟧ Accordingly there is no ontological proof in the previous sense. In its place one could set a logical proof, parallel to the physical proofs (the cosmological and the teleological) and to the moral proof, by starting from the nature of human thought. Its moments would be roughly the following.

[[1391]] Human thought knows itself as finite thought, and yet it strives beyond every barrier. It knows itself as dependent on the nature of things and the nature of things as independent of itself, and yet from the very outset it proceeds as if they were determinable by it, and it rests only when it has subdued them. This confidence would be a contradiction if something thinkable were not presupposed in things, truth in the actual. All thinking would be a game of chance or a boldness of despair, if God, the truth, did not underlie thought and things as their common origin and as their common bond. Without this the right of thought would be presumption.

[[1392]] This proof, if one wishes to give it this mathematical and juristic name, is indirect. Such a proof is the more compelling, the more firmly the proposition stands against which the assumption of the opposite is to break and be annihilated. Here thought itself, and so what is in itself most certain, is this secure point. Were there no truth in things, thought would contradict itself. The intelligible is its postulate.

[[1393]] No more is to be drawn from the consideration than lies in it: truth in thought and truth in things through a higher mediation, the intelligent in thought and the intelligible in things. Fichte once showed how from ethical action, if it was not to contradict itself, there follows faith in an ethical world-order, in the world as the material of duty. In the same way there follows from knowing thought, if it is not to contradict itself, faith in an intelligible world-order, in the world as the material of thought.

[[1394]] ⟦469⟧ 4. The cosmological proof infers from the contingency of the world to an absolutely necessary existence as the ground of itself and of all things. So Aristotle already inferred from motion to an unmoved that moves. Finite things are rooted in others, and these again in others. This series of effects and of the causes to be found for them runs on into infinity. This indeterminateness is cancelled only by breaking off the series and placing at its head a self-creating cause (causa sui). This all-generating unity can be further confirmed by the fact that the explanations which penetrate into the grounds from the most diverse phenomena form a converging series which seems to point to one ultimate common point. This essential consideration, which is forgotten in the abstract proof, must support the cosmological inferences, which might otherwise lead to a plurality or multiplicity of the unconditioned.

[[1395]] The contingency of the individual, which drives towards the necessary and, like the transient, awakens a longing for the eternal, can easily be worked out further. It meets us everywhere; but the kernel of the proof lies in that simple view.

[[1396]] The grounding is only indirect, inasmuch as the assumption of the opposite is refuted on that impossible course into infinity. It has as much power in itself as that indeterminate progress is unbearable to thought. Examined more deeply, it is necessity itself, which is one with thought, that thrusts from itself the indeterminateness of the infinite course.

[[1397]] The difficulties do not hide themselves here. Since the individual is always only contingent, the sum of all these contingencies is supposed to make up the necessary. To escape this contradiction, thought bends the series of causes and effects back into itself and posits the unconditioned as cause of itself. The concept is consistent; but the ⟦470⟧ intuition is lacking. One may illustrate it by the analogy of the living being which moves itself, or of the I which has self-consciousness only from itself. In these examples, nevertheless, the cause of itself (causa sui) rests on foreign conditions and a foreign foundation. The analogy of everything conditioned does not help in the unconditioned. Rather, at this point too the absolute withdraws from the finite categories that have been developed. Where the cause presents itself in the finite, it is in itself something manifold, a sum of several conditions; otherwise it would remain unchangeably shut up in itself. In the unconditioned it is supposed to point to an ultimate unity.

[[1398]] Here too no more may be taken from the premises than really lies in them. The unconditioned appears here as the cause sufficient for the world, and consequently as the absolute power. Further, nothing in the cosmological proof drives out of the world to an unconditioned being beyond it. The series of causes and effects runs on within being. In being closed together into a necessary whole, they nevertheless remain within themselves. Hence the most consistent expression of the cosmological view of the world is the system of Spinoza, in which substance is the cause of itself and of the accidents. The finite, selfless in itself, is surrendered to the infinite because it is contingent. In the cosmological proof, in its old shape, only the efficient cause is grasped, which is the character of being when it has not yet been determined by thought. The yield is therefore the unity of efficient substance.

[[1399]] 5. The teleological proof does not stop at the general dependence of conditioned being on the unconditioned, but shows the harmony of the conditioned through the end that rules the world. The end can only be understood through the anticipating ⟦471⟧ thought which determines the present out of the future. So far, therefore, as the end rules, so far thought rules. The blind power of substance, the yield of the cosmological proof, rises to creative wisdom.

[[1401]] In this consideration the intelligible world-order tacitly presupposed by subjective thought, and the view of real substance that arises in following up the efficient cause, are gathered together into an unconditioned realisation of reason. The world is rational, and reason is actual.

[[1402]] What has been objected against the teleological proof, both by Spinoza, who denied the end, and by Kant, who blunted it into a principle of reason that is regulative indeed but not constitutive, and flattened it into a subjective maxim of the knowing mind, has been refuted above in the consideration of the end.¹ In that the objective significance of the end has been demonstrated, the foundation of the teleological consideration is established.

[[1403]] If in recent times the intelligence that determines ends is circumvented by putting an unconscious formative drive or a plastic principle of life, as the ground of harmonious purposiveness, in the place of waking reason, then the universe is conceived after the analogy of the sleeping plant or of dreaming animal life. What is possible in such individual phenomena precisely only through the unconditioned cannot be the form of the unconditioned itself. The analogy is therefore absurd. It has also been shown above that the concept of the formative drive, when it is analysed, becomes intelligible only through the free thought that gives it its direction.

[[1404]] The realised end can be comprehended only through the prius of thought, into whose hand power over being has been given. Hence the end-governed ⟦472⟧ world guarantees the unconditioned, almighty thought. Deus cogitat; ergo est.

[[1406]] But we must not conceal the difficulty from ourselves. Only the completed view of the world, which has carried the end victoriously through all shapes, will give this full certainty. Has the world, then, already been so transfigured in science? At bottom matters stand no differently than they stood in the time of Plato, who complains that Anaxagoras calls in understanding only when the physical causes do not suffice for explanation. The sciences almost without exception take the direction of comprehending the nature of things from the necessity of the efficient cause, and only under compulsion do they accommodate themselves to ends. They do well in this, so far as they can get through with it; for the proper right of the matter must not be curtailed, nor anything foreign forced upon it. But until this discord of directions is settled, until knowledge of the end governs the whole world with thought, so that nothing withdraws from it and all efficient cause has become means, the foundation of the teleological proof still wavers. We are still far from this goal, not to mention that every malformation seems to betray an impotence of the inner end.

[[1407]] The settlement of the dispute must take place still more methodically than hitherto. It must be investigated exactly how the explanation from the efficient cause stands in relation to each phenomenon, and whether such an explanation does not, in the great connection, after all have before it an end from which it proceeds. All means which are not recognised as such appear as efficient causes and contend for the mechanical view until, seen through as bearers of an end, they rather confirm the organic view.¹

[[1410]] The end has yet *one* difficulty in the unconditioned. Only in division, in opposition, and so in the relative, does it come into activity. Whence this opposition in the original unity? We have reason to answer this question with an ethical motive in God, with the motive of love.

[[1411]] 6. The moral proof has been worked out especially by Kant and Fichte. If one looks away from the peculiar form which it has received from both, it rests on a teleological view of the world. Its basis is the end; but not as the end rules in nature, in a foreign element that obeys selflessly, so that organic life appears only as a wonderful play of a foreign intelligent power. In the ethical sphere too, it is true, the universal end is given, not arbitrarily made; but the given end is freely received, shaped in an individual way, and consciously carried out. The end is surrendered into free action; and freedom, determining itself to be the organ of the end, becomes wisdom and love, the one knowing, the other forming and creating. The disposition that surrenders itself to the end is the centre of the so-called moral proof. Its obedience to the unconditioned moral law, its following of the law for the law's sake, its self-sacrificing deed, would be senseless and would fall into irreconcilable contradiction with other legitimate tendencies in man, namely with happiness, if there were not a settlement which has its guarantee in faith in immortality and in God. Thus, roughly, did Kant conceive this ⟦474⟧ postulate of practical reason. Fichte did not reach so far. Our duty is what is most certain. Our world is the material of our duty made sensible; this is the true stuff underlying all appearance. To accomplish joyfully and without constraint what duty bids on each occasion, without doubting and subtle reasoning about the consequences, is the real confession of faith. Every one of our actions is carried out in the presupposition of the divine, and all its consequences are preserved only in the divine. The living and actual moral order is itself God. Thus Fichte shows that the individual (conditioned) action, if it is not to contradict itself, presupposes the unconditioned.

[[1413]] This grounding is indirect and runs parallel to that consideration which inferred from the task of thought to the presupposed truth of things. We can neither think nor act unless with our thinking or acting we rest in the unconditioned, unless we were willing to think or act blindly and give ourselves over to contradiction.

[[1414]] If the inner end in the essence of man, becoming free in consciousness and will, is recognised as the ethical destination, then the relation to the divine lies still nearer to the man who thinks it. For in that end, in which he thinks the unconditioned ground of his existence, he thinks the divine will.

[[1415]] This consideration forms the summit. Since it proceeds from comprehending thought and from free action, it posits the unconditioned as spiritual and free, as the source of truth and of salvation.

[[1416]] 7. In the proofs of God a graded sequence presents itself throughout. The cosmological proof grasps bare existence, and that solely in the determination of its dependence, and finds the unconditioned power. The teleological proof brings out the relation of end which expresses itself in individual existence, and finds the unconditioned thought that pervades the world. The ⟦475⟧ moral proof seizes the end-determined law of freedom and finds as its ground the unconditioned free love. The logical proof, finally, examines thought in its own certainty and finds the unconditioned power, the end that rules the world, and free love grounded in the thinking primal spirit.

[[1417]] Each of these considerations, which are directed from the conditioned to the unconditioned, is by itself a torn-off part; each presents *one* side. Other groundings could easily be formed as well, such as an aesthetic or a psychological proof, if it were a matter of multiplying the number. For every point of the world must lead to God, as every point of the periphery leads to the centre. Grasped in living relation, the conditioned points beyond itself and rests only in the unconditioned. But all such considerations will fall into place under those given above.

[[1418]] As regards the aesthetic, we recall that philosophers such as Fries, who see in space and time and the categories only something subjective, who then treat the organic only mathematically, without real purposiveness, and who in general allow the laws of nature to hold only for man, as laws of the sensuous apprehension and combination of the appearances of things, nevertheless divine the eternal truth, even for natural appearance, in the feeling of the beautiful and the sublime, and claim for this divination the same degree of certainty as for knowledge. If knowledge is declared to be merely human and is sacrificed to the subjective, if in the realm of the living, with the organic apprehended only mathematically, the beautiful is robbed of the kernel of its essence, then it is not conceivable, and it is a vain undertaking, that in the weaving and hovering of feeling, which is after all really only a reflex of appearance upon one's own life, the eternal truth should be regained and rescued. Divination is no adequate form for grasping truth, and, in conscious contradiction with knowledge, it is scarcely even a dim, wavering guarantee. The consideration takes another shape if ⟦476⟧ our investigations should have succeeded in demonstrating the inner end as objective, and with it an objective side in the beautiful. Then there is no conflict; rather the one is grounded in the other; and the feeling of the beautiful can now be an immediate expression, an expression of sensation for the knowable harmony of the thing. From remote sides the elements fused into harmony in the beautiful point to an underlying unity of the whole. If, for example, the colours and forms of vegetation, produced from the plants' own ends and sufficient to their own essence, at the same time do good to the human eye, which scarcely stands in any causal connection with vegetation, and accord with the organ of intuition, then harmony is knit to harmony therein from the remotest ends of life; and the inner destination of the whole, which makes itself known in such testimonies of feeling, may find a place in the teleological proof.

[[1419]] The questions of the philosophy of religion have metaphysical support only in the considerations indicated in the proofs of the existence of God: for example, the question of the transcendence or immanence of God, whether God is to be thought as transcendent, an extramundane being whose throne is heaven and whose footstool is the earth, or whether God is to be thought as exclusively immanent, comprising himself in the world, or whether and how both, for in him we live and move and have our being. Aristotle could still keep his first mover, the unmoved that moves, beyond the sphere of the fixed stars that closes the world, and so extramundane; but since Copernicus opened the heavens and disclosed the view into infinite cosmic space, this is no longer possible in that sense. If the question of transcendence and immanence is not to turn into a sensuous dialectic of the prepositions trans and in, beyond and within; if the self-contradictory attempt is not to be made to grasp the transcendent spatially, and so to think a beyond beyond infinite space; if the question ⟦477⟧ is to have a meaning for the concept: then it goes back to the world-determining end; for therein thought is the prius, and in this respect the transcendent.

[[1420]] Every proof of the existence of God contains a pointing of the conditioned to the unconditioned by which it is conditioned; but each mirrors only *one* side of the unconditioned; whoever draws them together and permeates them grasps the *one* God as he reveals himself in this world.

[[1421]] 8. Does he really grasp him? If God is known only through the conditioned and yet is not the conditioned, if all our determinations of thought move in the first instance only in the finite and only confess the insufficiency of the finite in order to point to the infinite, then a contradiction must arise as often as we think God. We give up finite thoughts in order to reach the infinite, and what we reach is, if we wish to be honest, after all only something finite. We annihilate the categories, and what rises upon their ruins is once again only through the categories. In this contradiction between the eternal Idea and its finite organ there lies a sublimity which already stamps itself upon the words of Augustine, when he rejects all the Aristotelian categories in order to think God, and yet, with clear consciousness, expresses what he thinks within these categories. Augustine writes:¹ Deus sine qualitate bonus, sine quantitate magnus, sine indigentia creator, sine situ praesens, sine habitu omnia continens, sine loco ubique totus, sine tempore sempiternus, sine ulla sui mutatione mutabilia faciens nihilque patiens. Never, perhaps, has the pale colour of logical abstractions presented a more sublime picture.

[[1422]] 9. It is, to be sure, conceivable that the categories, which, as we saw, rise in stages along with the principles and deepen their content especially through the end, should now take the unconditioned into themselves and thereby undergo their final ⟦478⟧ intensification. In this sense one speaks of absolute causality, absolute end, absolute personality (absolute subjectivity), or, since the Neoplatonists, designates this elevation by a "super-" prefixed to the categories, as for example in super-essence, super-being, super-substantiality. The sign of such a raising to a higher power is easily invented; but the question is how matters stand with the concept behind the sign.

[[1424]] If causality and substance (activity and thing) are those fundamental concepts on which the others (quantum, quale, measure) in turn depend, then it is with them that we are first concerned.

[[1425]] The causa sui, as was shown, no longer has what distinguished causality in the finite. In the finite, one thing is the cause of another, and the causa sui, measured against this form of causality, the only one evident to us, appears as a contradiction in the adjunct. Properly speaking, it even states that the concept of causality is not to be applied any further. We posit something as the first and last, beyond which, asking after the cause, we cannot go. For one, matter is this eternal that is from itself, this causa sui; for another, it is mighty thought as what is original; for a third, the indifference of both. But no one truly sees how something absolutely generates itself, is causa sui.

[[1426]] If the end is now joined with the causa sui, we obtain the unconditioned as an end to itself. But the end, now, has sense only in the relative. The division in the original unity, the opposition without which there is no end, follows in and for itself neither from the causa sui nor from the absolute that is combined with the concept of the end.

[[1427]] The category of substance seems at first sight to come into its own through elevation into the infinite, since in the finite no substance is absolutely grounded in ⟦479⟧ itself and each is substance only relatively. But the difficulty returns in the content. Substance has risen in the ethical sphere to the person, and the unconditioned will accordingly have to be conceived as absolute personality. In opposition to the impersonal world-reason, which would properly be reason without consciousness and will, reason blind and lame, that view of the world which seeks the end as the inner power of things will conceive the unconditioned only as thinking and willing, and indeed both in unity. What the inner end, as the essence of things, expresses as the ought in the conditioned is, in its content, the will in the unconditioned.

[[1428]] With this the kernel in the concept of the personal God is reached. But philosophical knowledge, which wishes neither to overrate itself nor to overreach itself, must not forget its limits.

[[1429]] If the finite categories are raised into the infinite, for example the person into absolute personality, one follows a method similar to that which is used in mathematics when finite relations are carried over into the infinite, as for instance when in the ellipse the foci are moved further and further apart until their distance becomes infinite, and one thereby arrives at the parabola. So in the concept of the person one sets the finite intelligence, which is bound to a narrow circle of few objects and limited in its fathoming, as infinite in breadth and depth, in order to ascribe it to absolute personality. But this path nevertheless has its limit. Already in the will this infinite extension is impossible; for the will has its force in determinateness, which is limitation; and it is bound by the necessity of consequence, beyond which the will cannot go. In the finite the person appears to us only in opposition to other persons, through which it becomes conscious of itself, the I over against the Thou and He. It has its essence in self-limitation; it gathers itself together and shuts itself off from ⟦480⟧ others. Absolute personality, thought as absolute, unlimited and all-embracing, does not bear this limitation. Thought as a person before all persons, it has a sublime solitude which, so it seems, became one of the impulses to think persons in God. In that the specific character falls away which the concept of the person has in the finite, the difficulty makes itself known of so transforming the finite concept of the person that it becomes adequate to the absolute.

[[1430]] When we saw above how the end drew the categories of the efficient cause up to itself, their content remained untouched and unimpaired. But in this elevation of the fundamental concepts to the unconditioned the previous content breaks off. When, therefore, philosophy, in correct self-knowledge, reflects on the means of knowing, it no longer dreams the gigantic dream of an adequate knowledge of God, in which spun-out metaphors are passed off as proven science. It is said of God in an old phrase: nesciendo scitur.

[[1431]] If, accordingly, knowledge renounces the attempt to construct the essence of the unconditioned from the concept, it is directed to sketch its representation in the direction in which what is given and conditioned offers guidance. This is the real sense of the so-called proofs of the existence of God which we have run through. The pointings of the finite to the infinite have doubled significance in a view of the world in which, unlike the Kantian, purely subjective forms, time and space and the categories, do not veil the thing in itself and frustrate every penetrating and certain look. Where it is denied to us, beings conditioned on every side, to know the essence and, as it were, the life of the unconditioned as it is in itself, they teach us its relations to us.

[[1432]] 10. In these pointings to the unconditioned, the ⟦481⟧ necessary that has been known forms the secure foundation; for it is impossible for us to think that the necessary, which cannot be otherwise, should nevertheless become otherwise through the unconditioned, and the unchangeable change. The necessary springs up for the human mind within what is limited; and it can therefore, seen from the unconditioned, enter into a greater connection so as itself to become greater; but in and for itself it remains what it is, necessary.

[[1434]] Since, now, the end in the conditioned, as was remarked above, points to knowledge and will in the unconditioned, it is an old question how the necessary, called veritates aeternae, is related to God's knowledge and will. By the eternal truths have been understood in particular the mathematical laws, in which necessity appears most purely: for example, that twice two is four, or that the radii in a circle are equal, or that the angles in a plane triangle are together equal to two right angles. Independent of God's will, for it appears absurd to make a consequence that is of a logical nature dependent on the decree of a will, as Cartesius did, they appear to some even as independent of God's knowledge; they are true, said some Scotists, even if there were no understanding, not even the understanding of God. Mathematical necessity is only the simplest example of such eternal truths; for the suggestion that they could be otherwise at once proclaims its own folly. With mathematics, which spreads through physics into the spiritual world, the realm of these eternal truths spreads; and Bayle already made the eternal truths in the ethical sphere clear by the contrast of the unchangeable Decalogue with the Jewish ceremonial law, once given but changeable. With the necessary advancing in all science, the boundaries of the eternal truths will advance. Where they carry the end ⟦482⟧ in themselves, or are means in connection with the ultimate end of the whole, they appear, traced back to the absolute, as an outflow of the will; but considered prior to the end and in and for themselves, like the mathematical truths, as the consequence of something original, as for example of the constructive elements in mathematics. Whoever wills and posits what is original, for example the decimal number system or the design of parallels, posits and wills its consequences. The will that posits and wills the nature of the human being posits and wills at the same stroke the essence of justice, and thus, for example, the necessity of the Decalogue. Only when we wish to make God's knowing will free of its own consequences and detach it from them does that conflict arise in the thought of the unconditioned. Whoever, once and for all, wishes to descend with the lamp of human analogy into God's understanding and will as into the deepest depths must think what is original and what is foreseen in the original for all eternity. Few can bear this thought. But as before God a thousand years are as *one* day, so before him thousands upon thousands of syllogisms are as *one* concept, and the intricate world as *one* simple fact.

[[1437]] 11. In the history of philosophy *one* expression in particular maintains itself for determining the absolute in itself. It will be worth while to examine it in this connection. If the opposite ends of the world point back to the unconditioned, and if the whole presents itself intuitively in oppositions, then it is natural to bend what is opposed together and make it the expression of the absolute. And indeed dialectical thoughts and grand images have vied with one another in designating, deeply and comprehensively, the might and glory of the unconditioned in the unity of opposites.

[[1438]] We pass over the Neoplatonists, Cusanus and Giordano Bruno, and mention only those conceptions of this kind which have won great applause in the most recent philosophy.

[[1439]] ⟦483⟧ No one has given a mightier impulse than Spinoza to the doctrine that the absolute is the identity of opposites, the indifference of the ideal and the real, of the subjective and the objective.

[[1440]] If until then either blind being (matter) had been placed as what is original before and above thought, and thought regarded only as the fortunate effect of blind force, as in Democritism, or thought had been placed before and above matter and matter regarded as determined by it, as in Platonism, then Spinoza grasped the fundamental problem of metaphysics, the real relation of thought to being, anew and in his own way; Spinozism, born of itself, joined those two as the representative of the third possibility, the only one that could still exist besides them.¹

[[1441]] Spinoza expresses God, the *one* substance, through two attributes, infinite thought and infinite extension, through which the understanding apprehends its identical essence in different ways. Since the two attributes, for the understanding the different expressions of the same substance, stand in no causal connection with each other, neither has precedence over the other, neither preponderates over the other, and neither explains what goes on in the other; what happens in extension happens also in thought; the two run parallel and are the same thing, only regarded under the other attribute. So the fundamental thought is conceived. It demands that there be no end, or that the end be at most a human invention; it properly demands also that there be no sensuous knowledge, for in that the body (extension) would otherwise encroach upon the mind (thought). It has been shown above that the denial of the end speaks against Spinoza. How Spinoza helps himself and yet falls away from himself, how it becomes impossible for him to carry through the ⟦484⟧ fundamental thought, and how, in contradiction with it, he in the end nevertheless gives thought power over extension, has been shown in another place and must not be repeated here. Since Spinoza, keen and strict as he is, misses the goal, he leaves no hope that metaphysical truth lies along his path.

[[1444]] More recent philosophy, perhaps misled by Jacobi's indefinite exposition, dropped Spinoza's proviso that thought and extension, though distinguished in the understanding, are the expression of *one* and the same thing and therefore stand in no causal connection with each other. Without this caution it determined the absolute as the identity of the subjective and the objective, of the ideal and the real. Schelling, in his first epoch, brought these formulas into vogue. People then trusted the bold intuition and overlooked the weak grounding.

[[1445]] Let us compare, for example, an exposition such as the following.³ Being and knowing, says Schelling, are immediately, without a higher bond, and in themselves *one*. The being which we know as the absolute is, as surely as it is true being, so surely its own affirmation; were it not essentially self-affirmation, it would not be absolute, not wholly and entirely of and from itself. Conversely, this affirmation of being is nothing other than just being itself. Were it not this, it would be outside being and could not itself be. As surely, therefore, as it is really affirmation of being, i.e. ⟦485⟧ is itself positive, so surely is it not distinct from being and is itself being. Affirmation of being is knowledge of being, and conversely. The eternal, therefore, since it is essentially a self-affirming, is in being also a self-knowing, and conversely. The unity between being and knowing in general is accordingly a direct unity, that is, one with which no opposition is mixed. Existence is self-affirmation and self-affirmation is existence. The one is entirely synonymous with the other; the relation of the two is a mere relation of indifference. It follows that no part of nature can be mere being or something merely affirmed, but rather each is in itself just as much self-affirmation as consciousness or the I is; it follows that every thing, grasped in its true essence, can be regarded with entirely equal validity as a mode of being and as a mode of self-knowing and self-revealing. Accordingly, neither is knowledge dependent on being nor being on knowledge; rather, knowledge is just being itself, and being is knowledge (in that higher meaning of self-affirmation).

[[1451]] In the word identity, or indifference, of the ideal and the real, unclarity and indeterminateness take refuge. As logical identity it cannot be thought, and as actual equilibrium it cannot be clearly grasped. Schelling, striving from the ready-made formula towards the living God, himself abandoned this standpoint and sought to show how God makes a part (a potency) of himself into ground, so that the creature may be possible, and subordinates this part of his essence (the non-intelligent part) to the higher. With this the identity is given up and the indifference converted into a superordination of the ideal over the real.

[[1452]] Schleiermacher arrives at the same formula of the absolute, at the identity of thought and being, of the ideal and the real. To be sure, by a path peculiar to him. He does not seize the absolute as this indifference of opposites in intellectual intuition, but only presupposes it to all knowledge as to all willing. The absolute, Schleiermacher teaches, can be neither known nor willed. A willing directed at the absolute would be pure nullity; for it would let man come to no determinate deed. A knowledge of God in and for himself would have to be nothing other than a concept which nevertheless still persists in opposition. Every particular knowledge exists as particular only in oppositions and through them; for it is only a particular one inasmuch as something in it is not posited or is negated, which, however, must be posited elsewhere. The highest knowledge, on the other hand, is not determined by oppositions, but is the absolutely simple expression ⟦487⟧ of the highest being equal to it, and contains the oppositions bound within itself. Absolute knowledge is the expression of no opposition at all, but of absolute being identical with itself. Hence the absolute is subject-object, because neither as knowledge does it have being outside itself, nor as being knowledge. Absolute knowledge is, in actual consciousness, no determinate knowledge, that is, none that could be expressed in an adequate way in a plurality of concepts and propositions, but only the ground and source of all particular knowledge. The highest opposition, under which all others hover before us, is that of material and spiritual being. Being is material (real) as what is known, spiritual (ideal) as what knows. Each member of this opposition, taken separately by itself, is nothing in being and knowing, but remains only a dead sign. Hence the highest Idea is the Idea of the absolute unity of being, in so far as the opposition of thought and object is cancelled; and the ground of all and everything that is to be presupposed is the unconditioned interpenetration of knowledge and being, and that absolutely, so that they are not, say, still a twofold, as in human knowledge, merely held in correspondence, but pure and absolute identity of knowledge and being.

[[1455]] We cannot know the absolute, Schleiermacher teaches, since he raises the absolute above the oppositions within which knowledge moves. But even if the absolute is no object of knowledge, we must nevertheless be able to think the formula that has been found, identity of the ideal and the real, or to represent to ourselves the schema, as Schleiermacher calls it. The more insistence is laid on pure and absolute identity, the less do we consider this possible.

[[1456]] The formula to which Spinoza led the way is not, however, to be understood as with Spinoza, who regards thought and extension, distinguished in the intellect, as expressions of the same *one* substance. Rather, with Schleiermacher, knowledge and being, ideal and real, are taken as actual oppositions ⟦488⟧ in the thing itself, and the unconditioned interpenetration of the two states something more real than a reunion of what, as attribute, was separated only in the intellect.

[[1457]] The absolute identity of knowledge and being negates both the dualism of the two elements and the subordination of the one and superordination of the other; but what it is and how it is to be thought, it does not state. No image reaches it. We may neither think it mechanically, like a mixture, nor chemically, like penetration and saturation; for otherwise the two elements, as separate, would be the more original. Is every point that is, also to think? and every combination of the possible also to be? It is proper to thought, so far as we know it, to sketch and compare what is possible; but the possible, wide and manifold, always shoots out beyond the actual. Thus in fact the formula cannot be thought, for indeterminateness.

[[1458]] The absolute identity of knowledge and being is neither a point from which one can set out in derivation, nor a goal towards which one can strive in action. One cannot set out from it; for absolute equilibrium persists in itself, and nothing therefore follows from it; and one cannot strive towards it; for a norm for action can lie only in the shaping of difference.

[[1459]] Has Schleiermacher, then, demonstrated the identity of the ideal and the real as a necessary expression of the presupposed absolute? For the answer to this question we go back to his exposition.

[[1460]] The highest knowledge, he says, is not determined by oppositions, but is the absolutely simple expression of the highest being equal to it. For if in ascending the oppositions diminish, one can only have ascended to the highest when they have entirely vanished. This ascent is only an abstraction. The pregnant unity of knowledge and being, which, binding the oppositions, is to express the absolute, cannot be an abstractum; otherwise it would ⟦489⟧ after all be only the ens universalissimum, which Schleiermacher decidedly rejects.¹

[[1461]] The opposition of the ideal and the real is, as opposition, in the first instance only in our concept. It is correct that each member by itself is in the first instance only a dead sign. But does an absolute interpenetration of the two follow from this? The union can come about otherwise than through identity and indifference. The sign ceases to be dead when, for example, the subordination of the real to the ideal is intuited. The proof, then, falls short of the assertion.

[[1462]] The conditioned, in the high points of its appearance and in the question about its inner possibility, points to another presupposition in the unconditioned: to the determining power of the ideal in the real.

[[1463]] Schleiermacher cannot do without this same presupposition, and a teleological principle lies hidden in him. We shall try to draw it into the light at a few points.

[[1464]] Knowledge and being, says Schleiermacher, exist for us only in relation to each other, and each is the measure of the other. When we call a thing imperfect in its kind, this happens because it does not correspond to the concept, and likewise conversely.
