[[1463]] This proposition carries a teleological relation within it. For the concept has a right to be the measure of perfection or of defect only if it carries the determining end within itself. Where being is blind force, the concept must conform to being, but not the reverse.

[[1464]] It is peculiar to Schleiermacher's ethics that it presents the action of reason upon nature, and indeed as organising and as symbolising action; the former makes nature into an instrument, the latter brings it to man's conscious knowledge. Both ethical processes are for him only the extension and heightening of an original union of ⟦490⟧ reason and nature, partly of one that is given to us in our organs, partly of one that is given to us in our consciousness. Thus the rationality of the organs and of consciousness is presupposed, because otherwise, says Schleiermacher, the delimitation of science as well as the certainty of immediate consciousness would cease. This proof demands a concession to human limitation, but it is no ground drawn from the matter itself. That ground lies elsewhere than in a consideration of what would otherwise befall us in science and in immediate consciousness. If we ask what the ground in the matter itself is for the rationality of the organs and of consciousness, it leads immediately into the inner end; and if we ask further whence we are immediately conscious of this rationality, then the human trust in the conditions of spiritual life in which we find ourselves, this faith in an original destination, leads into the same intuition.

[[1467]] The ethical process, according to Schleiermacher's exposition, realises the progressive union of reason with nature, in such a way that nature is permeated with rational content. In the organising and in the symbolising process it becomes more and more the instrument of reason, whether of action or of knowledge.

[[1468]] This goal is properly the opposite of the principle, the opposite of the pure identity of knowing and being; for permeation with rational content is the superordination of the ideal over the real and the subordination of the real as a means. From an indifference of knowing and being no ethical direction, no measure of the ethical process can proceed, and this for the simple reason that it is pure identity.

[[1469]] This incongruity could, were this the place, be traced further into Schleiermacher's Christian ethics, whose presupposed ⟦491⟧ concept of God scarcely agrees with the pure identity of knowing and being. The feeling filled with this indifference of the ideal and the real, which in the sense of his philosophical ethics would become religion, cannot have the significance of the feeling that resounds in the words: "In the beginning was the Logos."

[[1471]] Thus neither in Spinoza nor in Schelling and Schleiermacher has the endeavour to express the absolute through the unity of opposites reached its goal. The formula sounds deep; the intuition is bold. But its inner indeterminacy and its results bear witness against it.

[[1472]] 12. We turn back into our former path. The last investigation set out to examine what the expression of the absolute as a unity of opposites accomplishes in modern philosophy, and since it proved insufficient, the general view was confirmed that the means for a direct and adequate knowledge are lacking.

[[1473]] This reserve does not answer to the longing that drives us towards the concept of God, towards the concept which has all worth in itself and gives all things their worth. Practically a power in the soul, it becomes theoretically a limiting concept, which we only approach. We want more; we want to go further. As we think ourselves into the finite and, comprehending it, create it anew, so the same impulse drives us to transport ourselves, with the life of our formative thought, into the infinite essence of God.

[[1474]] But criticism can be neither undone nor disdained; it remains at the side of philosophy on its path as a warning. Whoever, then, is not conscious of that contradiction between the finite means and the infinite object, whoever imagines that he reproduces God within himself as a process of nature, deceives himself, like the profound theosophist. For here no insight into a becoming is opened; all knowledge is only indirect. "God alone can comprehend God." Theosophy imitates him in this. It seeks to open unfathomable depths, ⟦492⟧ to behold God's essence in becoming and to transform his being into a history. The share of extravagant fancy, without which this does not come off, lifts theosophy on high, but also beyond sober metaphysics.

[[1475]] No one holds it against the eye when it is quietly conscious that not the changeless pure light, but only the subdued and reflected light, or light broken in the play of colours, that not the sun of heaven but the brightness of the earth has been assigned to it as its domain of activity. But in human thought it is censured as unbelief or sloth when, like the eye, it knows that the circle of the finite and conditioned, which is after all wide enough, is its free and joyful field of play. When the eye is delighted by the harmony of colours, it does not deny the sun; rather, it knows, as it were, that the colours are born of light. When thought happily exercises itself upon things, it does not deny God, but sees him in the reason of the world and knows that this reason comes from God. But by the sight of the sun itself the eye is dazzled and then sees only its own phantasms; and by the intuition of God finite thought is swallowed up and yet produces only a mirror-image of the finite.

[[1476]] The unconditioned becomes the transfigured analogy of the conditioned, and yet, considered logically, all analogy from the conditioned to the unconditioned is lacking; for the specific difference between the two has, as it were, become infinite.

[[1477]] All proofs of God resemble the attempt to find pure light from the colour in which the light is dimmed, as though one could simply subtract the dimming. They are nothing but a faint glimmer and a cold semblance. Compared with the living idea, they remain at a great distance. But whence the idea of God before the proof and apart from the proof? Sceptical criticism has a wide field here, but it does not explain what it would like to explain away. The profound intuition of faith and the powerfully unifying spirit answer de⟦493⟧cisively. Taken logically, the conditioned would fall apart for us if there were no unconditioned, and the unconditioned, by its concept, towers above the little fragments of the conditioned which human thinking interprets as a reduced image of the unconditioned. So the sun, which illumines planets and moons, towers above the colours that shine for us, above the day that gives us light.

[[1478]] Accordingly it is not given to us to develop the essence of God with that logical necessity with which the mind is able to penetrate finite things. All construction is only an image of God drawn from the world. How, it is asked, must the unconditioned being be constituted that reveals itself thus and not otherwise in the world? All grounding is here indirect. Whoever goes beyond this composes a theosophical poem, whether with Jacob Böhme he grasps the groundless [Ungrund] in ground and takes adversity as the revelation of the hidden life, or with the later Schelling, pointing his phrases dialectically, he sketches a process in which immemorial blind being is raised into that which can be, the unity of both forms the necessary spirit, that which possesses itself, and in the tension of the divine potencies the world becomes the suspended act of necessary divine being; or whether with Hegel he posits God as the syllogism of reason in which all three terms permeate one another; for the type of "being in itself," of "coming out of itself" and of "returning to itself," which always underlies the scheme of Father, Son and Spirit, is only a human likeness, by means of which the ⟦494⟧ concept does indeed imagine that it immerses itself in the rich life of God, but in which it has only a barren formula.

[[1480]] Let us be permitted a parallel, in which, taking life and art together as one, we compare the world with an artistic whole.

[[1481]] When we read a poem, we gather, as it were, little by little from the parts the thought of the whole and combine it into an image, which then in turn illumines the sense of the parts. Only from the parts do we understand the whole, and again only from the whole the parts.

[[1482]] We read the world no otherwise than such a poem. When we strive from individual appearances to the ground, from the parts to the whole, we go the way of experience. And when the parts receive new light from the provisionally grasped whole, the idea leads us.

[[1483]] Experience and idea accordingly demand each other; and the greatness of knowledge lies in their permeating each other.

[[1484]] When the idea of the poem stands before us, clear and significant in itself, and shaping and animating every word, then an image of the creative spirit of the poet stands before us. He does not, indeed, appear to us wholly as he is in himself, but only so far as his soul and his genius poured itself into this one work and sought its likeness in it.

[[1485]] As in this way the spirit of the poet speaks from the poem, so God speaks from the world. The poem is a single play, and hence the spirit of the poet appears in it only in the form of an isolated metamorphosis. The world that we read is also only a fragment, but, like the single drama of an ancient tetralogy, whole in itself. Enough is given us in it to recognise the glory of the creative spirit. The world is the counter-image of his essence. The further we embrace this counter-image, the deeper we look into it, the more is it his revelation. Nature and history are only two different leaves of *one* whole, and history becomes ⟦495⟧ itself, if the whole is to come together in *one* idea, a living member, indeed the most significant member, of a great view of nature, or more correctly of our whole intuition of the world.

[[1486]] It may happen that we read a poem only carelessly, and then we lack the necessary points for sketching the thought of the whole. Or we may read a poem carefully indeed, but nevertheless without spirit, and then the clearly recognised parts do not come together into any whole; they remain parts and at most struggle with one another, instead of mutually animating one another into members of *one* thought. Neither to him who reads carelessly nor to him who reads without spirit does the idea appear. The sciences that fail to recognise the unconditioned behave like careless or spiritless readers.

[[1487]] We read even the first verse of the poem on the presupposition that it serves, along with the rest, to reveal to us a greater thought. On the same presupposition we give ourselves up reflectively to all that follows. So too, in the first step of knowing that the mind takes in the world, the idea of the divine thought realised in the world is its tacit, if often uncomprehended, presupposition. In it all thinking and willing is transfigured. Without it thinking has at most the charm of an idle riddle, and willing at most the worth of a sounding string which, instead of joining in a great harmony, vibrates senselessly and purposelessly.

[[1488]] The knowledge of the finite mind, however far it may advance, is nonetheless piecework for each individual; and whether someone has come to know a particle of the world or a part, the thought of God is always the completion of this piecework.

[[1489]] We are still always reading that beginning, but on the presupposition that the divine thought from which it springs is mirrored in it.

[[1490]] Accordingly the peculiar intuition of the unconditioned rests upon the view of the world which strives to master the various cognitions by a unity.

[[1491]] ⟦496⟧ 13. Every system has its own view of the world and is a system of its own only in this view. In agreement with the preceding investigations, however, essentially two intuitions stand opposed to each other, which are only determined and carried out differently in the individual systems. The one recognises only the efficient cause as the power of the world; the other founds the dominion of the end. The former may be called the physical (or mechanical) view of the world, since it rests solely upon physical causes; the latter the organic, since in it things become organs of a purposive thought. The former was developed consistently and in its own peculiar way in antiquity by the atomists, and in modern times boldly and unsparingly by the *système de la nature*; the latter is the essence of Platonism and of all the tendencies akin to it. If in Spinoza's substance thinking and extension really permeated each other, and did not merely stand side by side like two expressions of one and the same thing, then an organic view would be possible there too. But for Spinoza this is only an alien consequence. Since he abolishes the end, he abolishes thought in the ground of things. Thereby, although differently disposed, he falls to the physical view of the world and to that extent forms an opposition to Plato.

[[1492]] These contending views of the world do not first appear in the philosophy that completes them. They wrestle with one another in the individual sciences and are a question of fact. Mathematics and the physics of natural forces widen their circles and thereby push forward the boundaries of the physical view of the world. Ethics holds fast to the end, but the advancing study of nature wrests concessions from it, and already a tendency shows itself to abolish the distinction between natural and moral law. Physiology stands in the middle between the dominion of the efficient cause and that of the concept of end. The end confronts it irresistibly in organic life, ⟦497⟧ but in particulars it wavers between the two views and believes it gains as much in necessity and reason as it pushes back teleology through deeper investigation of the co-operating forces of nature. But in the great fundamental features the governing end nevertheless remains, and ethics may appropriate it for itself from nature itself.

[[1495]] The physical view sees the world from the standpoint of driving causes and effects, like a sea moved by the wind. Nothing has a ground in itself, as it may well seem to have in the domain of life. The individual is only a fragment torn loose from the whole, in that what seems to be its own is only a continuation of what is alien. Whatever great thing arises is not properly produced, but only knocked into shape in fortunate co-operation. The force of past states determines the present. The motion of causes goes forward like a river, forward and ever forward. Matter and motion are the factors of all appearances. They are the first and the last. The end is only semblance, and life nothing but the overweening force that tore itself loose from substance in order to fall back to it again. Thinking is a product of physical cause; it is not the ground of creation, but its consummated effect. Hence God first comes to consciousness in man. Things have no truth; for no thought underlies them. Truth is only in human thinking, and there is no other truth than the sum of ⟦498⟧ erring understandings. Necessity rules all things, but it is only the inevitable compulsion of the efficient cause, recognised indeed by thought, but as something alien that does not spring from it. For the mind that asks after mind, this necessity is after all only chance. The view, carried through consistently, yields in the ethical sphere nothing higher than brute force or subtle cunning; for might alone has right; the efficient cause is might; therefore wrest the victory (the effect) from it, by either making it powerless through your force or felling it through its own weakness. Naked pragmatism in history is only an expression of this view of the world in the ethical sphere. Success alone decides; for the unconditioned is might.

Words are then replaced by facts, hypotheses by analyses. The laws of organic bodies then coincide with those of inorganic ones and, like these, become capable of explanation and simplification." Thus it is called a simplification when explanation explains thought away.

[[1497]] This view of the world rests in the first place upon the power of the mathematical, which pours itself with motion through the whole world. But if only motion belongs in equal measure to the formative mind, it does not follow that the physical force of the mathematical element holds sway originally free and unbound from thought and its ends. To a mathematician the saying is ascribed that he had searched the heavens and nowhere found the finger of God. In the mechanics of the heavens one does indeed find only the efficient cause, which holds the masses together and relates them to one another. But the masses become, at least according to experience on our earth, means for the existence of life and of mind. To that extent they are demanded by thought, and gravitation becomes the first condition of the world-whole. The outermost and last force, which passes through everything and in and for itself does not make thought known, reveals it by serving it. The physical view of the world grows, further, because the ends fancifully thought into the world suffer defeats at the hands of sober science. The childlike representation animates the forms of the world, which are necessary in strict connection, with contingent ends homogeneous with its own mind. When ⟦499⟧ this illusion retreats before more manly thought, it easily takes more with it than it should; and with faith in the invented symbols of particular ends there also falls, as like as not, faith in the divine end altogether. Finally, the advance of the physical view of the world proceeds from the isolation of the sciences. The end springs from the whole and is the thought of the whole in the midst of the parts that produce the whole. If now the parts are set up on their own, as though they were independent and existed of themselves, they must thereby forfeit the thought of the whole that encompasses them and realises itself in the parts. Consider the hand by itself, and you see only the extensors and flexors that move the little levers of the bones in manifold play. But consider together with it the eye, which directs and guides the hand, and mind and end enter into this instrument of instruments; yet eye and hand agree only under the great presupposition of the living body that embraces both. As in this example, so it goes with the sciences in general. The one considers the matter of the earth, the other the light of the heavens. In both the efficient causes are sought. They are the ultimate opposition in the knowledge of nature. But in the whole they exist for each other, and, separated by an infinite distance, a common end binds the two. Matter is dead without the animating light, and light is blind without the matter against which it strikes. If, therefore, philosophy at every time fulfils its calling to sketch from the isolated sciences, as parts, an image of the whole, so that in it the sciences have communion with the whole of knowledge, then it will always mediate the organic view of the world. And from the side of the spiritual, which in the physical view becomes a play of chance, like a big prize in the lottery, and comes to ruin before the superior power of the efficient cause, another light then pours also over the view of the efficient forces and of matter in motion.

[[1498]] ⟦500⟧ The organic view sees the world from the standpoint of the end and of the forces permeated by the end, like a living body. One must not let oneself be misled by the name of the organic view of the world, as though the organic consideration were merely a more "physical" one, as has been objected, for example, against the organic view of language. Only thought is able to form an organon (instrument) for itself, and only thought is able to direct it. Hence the organic view is precisely the spiritual one, the view of mind realising itself. The individual now receives in the end which it realises a centre of its own and has from there a life of its own. All the categories which, determined by the mere efficient cause, remained alien and blind in themselves, are illuminated by thought, as was shown above. Thought is not born afterwards, as in the physical view, but is the creator himself, almighty from the beginning. The truth of every thing is a ray of this thought; as a concept underlies things, so they are to satisfy this concept. Truth thus traces itself in the forms of creation, and we contemplate it in them devoutly and piously. As in the wonderful structure of the limbs and organs a thought reveals itself "before which all the problems of physics are solved from the very beginning," the problems of light and sound, of chemism and of motion, so this thought becomes the absolute prius of the natural and the moral world. If one succeeds in carrying the end through the world, the merely mechanical cause appears only as a side-effect. One can then indeed consider the efficient cause by itself; but only by lifting it out of its connection with the end and persisting in the consideration of the part. The necessity of the world is now no longer blind, like chance, but conscious, like reason; ⟦501⟧ and human reason is now no longer in the world like a stranger, but like the first-born son in his father's house; it is now no longer like a feeble consonance that infallibly perishes in the roar of sea and wind, but like a unison within a greater harmony. All knowing is now the trustful deed that creates after thought, all perceiving a listening for its revelation, all thinking a thinking-after. The organic view is heightened in the ethical domain when it is able to take freedom up into itself. Things and men now confront the agent as organs out of which an end speaks, and in this they bear their significance and their worth. Hence the task appears of knowing this thought of things, this end of the individual in the whole, and of treating men and things according to this divine element that is in them. Love, in contemplation and in action, gives itself freely to this thought, which goes beyond the self-contained life of the part. Hence we might take Plato's words more deeply and call love the bond by which the universe binds itself together with itself. Thought is before all things, and all things consist in it; all things are created through it and for it. Therefore the love that is grounded in this view is the "bond of perfection." The beautiful is now no longer a contingent charm of force, but an expression of inner harmony. The organ of the body, the eye for example, is the more a microcosm of the whole the higher it stands. So the moral man appears as a microcosm of the free thought realised in the world.

[[1502]] No one has characterised the organic view within the Christian sphere more beautifully than the apostle Paul. The organic view of the world would be only a generalisation ⟦502⟧ of what appears in the Christian sphere as at its highest summit. Paul characterises the moral forces released from the end, when they govern the world as in the physical view, with the striking words:¹ "But if ye bite and devour one another, take heed that ye be not consumed one of another."

[[1503]] In this way the organic view of the world takes shape when it is carried through. Without it a dualism is unavoidable. For the end is a fact of the world, and the only question is whether wholly or in part. If it is so only in part, then it is in the world like an inconsistency. From this indirect grounding arises the endeavour to extend the analogy of the end from the most significant members over the whole. Once it has permeated the whole, that external teleology ceases which subjects nature to alien ends. For nothing is outside the all-embracing whole. The ideal sketch is easy, but the real demonstration lags far behind it. The fact is to be investigated from itself and not reinterpreted. The tendencies of the sciences waver to and fro. Deeper investigation brings now a deeper end, but now, instead of any end, an efficient cause. The mediations between the members of the world will not appear so visibly that they point, as it were spatially, to the centre. The sciences quarrel over their queen, and they cannot be released from striving to fathom particulars solely from the matter itself. But this cannot lead the mind astray. According to the most significant appearances and according to its own nature it decides and supplies what is lacking.

[[1504]] 14. In the organic view of the world the thought which Plato once placed at the head in the *Timaeus* will remain the theme of metaphysics: "God was good, and because he was good he was without envy and willed that the world should become as like him as ⟦503⟧ possible." As the knowledge of the world grows in fullness and depth, it at last completes itself through this thought of unity. As the human mind always represents the absolute to itself only relatively, so too, where the content of the relative is recognised as richer and greater, the representation of the absolute will receive new and greater impulses. Religious representation often believes it is losing where it receives back more than it loses. When the mathematical understanding did away with the heaven of fixed stars enclosing the world, that last celestial sphere modelled on what the eye beholds, opened up infinite space and recognised the countless lights of heaven as distant worlds, the mightily stirred imagination of mankind expanded, and it was in danger of losing the transcendent unity over the unmeasured many. But at bottom the chords that introduce the thought of the unconditioned have also grown all the mightier, as when, for example, Klopstock, enlarging the old restricted representation of heaven, begins his psalm: "Around earths wander moons, earths around suns; the hosts of all the suns wander around one great sun: Our Father, which art." When in recent times life is discovered in the smallest space, when the history of the body of the earth opens a view into unmeasured ages and brings to light from the remotest periods traces of life, and of life ever arising anew in struggle with the elemental powers, then our representations grow at that expression with which already the Book of Wisdom addresses God, when it says: "thou lover of life, thine incorruptible spirit is in all."

[[1506]] 15. The passage cited from Plato's *Timaeus* leads to the optimism that found its expression in Leibniz's *Theodicee*, to the doctrine that the world is the best of the worlds that were possible. Today optimism is widely held to be a pious wish, and it is deemed a task of progressive metaphysics to teach pessimism; indeed Schopenhauer took the word in the strict sense ⟦504⟧ and even undertook the philosophical proof of the superlative that lies in the word pessimism. Our world, he holds, is the worst of all worlds possible at all; for it is so arranged as it had to be in order to be able to subsist by the narrowest margin; were it but a little worse, it could no longer subsist at all; it itself is therefore the worst among the possible ones.¹ Much is lacking for an actual grounding of this presumptuous thought. Does philosophy know what the conditions are under which a world can subsist and under which it cannot? As in a ledger of existence, the sensations of pleasure which the world offers are set down on the one side of the page, and on the other side the sensations of displeasure to which we are subject, and in the reckoning of plus and minus the pessimum, namely an enormous surplus of the sensations of displeasure over pleasure, is found as the sum. Thus, in contrast with the earlier optimism, pessimism is to become the doctrine of metaphysics.

[[1507]] Meanwhile the question arises whether pessimism can at all become the consistent doctrine of any metaphysics, i.e. of the science of principles. In principle, namely, as has been shown, two fundamental metaphysical views stand opposed to each other in the systems: the one the system that asserts the sole dominion of the efficient cause, the other the system of the inner end, which sees reason in things and presupposes thought and will for the world. The systems of the latter genus, which we designate as Platonism in the wider sense, necessarily arrive, with the providence they teach or with the reason inborn in the world, at the faith of optimism; the opposite genus ⟦505⟧ of systems, which seeks the naked necessity of blind forces, takes as the ultimate the strict concatenation of physical causes, to which the ancients gave the name of fate. It would be foolish to wish the necessary, which as such cannot be otherwise, to be otherwise, and these systems therefore seek either to win from the necessary what is useful to man, by laying their own striving into the necessary as well, or, where this is not possible, to rid themselves of foolish wishes, which are only illusions of something impossible, and to resign themselves calmly to the necessary. From such a tendency fatalism, or something similar, may follow, but no pessimism. Such a metaphysics must rather regard pessimism as an inconsistency of empty longing. In the systems of the end it can appear only where the ends are taken blindly and singly, like individual forces, without an ultimate all-embracing thought of unity to which they are subordinated. Such a view will have in the ends of the unconscious little more than another form of blindly efficient causes. That faith in the good God, and therefore in a world that is as perfect as possible, belongs to Platonism and to the kindred Christianity; it produces resignation to the will of the good God.

[[1510]] A critique of pessimism will go back partly into psychology, partly into ethics. Psychology has to know the nature of pleasure, which pessimism makes its measure of worth, in its distinctions, and in particular to bring out the eminent significance of the peculiar sensations of pleasure that stand in connection with the inner destination of man. Ethics will have to examine the measure of worth itself. In so far as the portrayal of joyless, frail existence which is supposed to ground pessimism can at the same time serve to spur men on to united help, an ethical motive cannot be mistaken, and the picture can act ethically in the sense of compassion, which Schopenhauer holds to be the proper ⟦506⟧ principle of the good. For everything that excites and enlivens fellow-feeling will further the good, if at the same time it takes the affect, blind in itself, into the service of reason. If, however, pessimism seeks to become a general way of thinking, it comes into contradiction with itself in so far as it, for its part, increases the sensations of displeasure which for it are the evil of the world, and has as its necessary consequence a discontent that embitters every stirring of life. The healthy force of life sickens in a pessimistic mood.

[[1511]] This contradiction becomes still more palpable when, as in v. Hartmann's *Philosophie des Unbewussten*, pessimism is taught theoretically (for, good as the world is, it is nonetheless thoroughly wretched and worse than none at all), but practically the affirmation of the will to live is upheld, and the human task is placed in full devotion to life and its pains and not in cowardly personal renunciation and withdrawal.¹ The deeper the roots this practical maxim strikes, the more vigorously will it defend itself against the theoretical doctrine of pessimism and seek to overcome it. That pessimism cannot find a soil in every ethics is evident. Whoever, for example, with Kant in the *Metaphysik der Sitten*, ascribes to the human being, as the being destined for reason, a worth absolutely, the worth of the person, that dignity of humanity by virtue of which man is an end in himself and can never be merely a means, disturbs the plus-and-minus reckoning of pleasure and displeasure on whose outcome pessimism raises its gloomy banner. For this worth in itself, this worth above all worths, does not fit into this addition of finite, changeable worths.

[[1512]] Accordingly we may exclude the so-called new problem of pessimism from our metaphysical consideration.

[[1515]] 17. With the organic view of the world, which lets the world and what is therein take root in the thought of the whole as what is original, the concept is transfigured in the idea. The naked view of the efficient cause knows no idea, but as the ultimate only the concept, in so far as it is the representation that has taken up into itself the producing, efficient ground of the thing. There is a concept of the circle, of falling, of magnetism, but no idea of them, unless they are related organically to the prefiguring thought of a whole. Language, however, speaks of the idea of an organ when it is traced back in its function to the whole of the living body and when its appropriate formation is comprehended from this.

[[1516]] In the history of philosophy the idea arises with a teleological and ethical consideration; and it is incorrect to derive it, in Plato, its originator, from a merely aesthetic intuition or merely from a dialectical reconciliation of opposites. For the idea of the good stands for him at the summit as the all-determining one.

[[1517]] The idea is the concept of the thing, known in the organic determination of a conditioning whole. Thus we speak of the idea of right when we grasp it not as an efficient appearance, and accordingly, for example, with Kant as the sum of the conditions under which the freedom of one can coexist with the freedom of another, but in a higher connection, say as the organ through which the ethical realised in common life main⟦508⟧tains and further develops itself. The concept becomes idea when it appears first in the determination of the higher end or finally in the light of the unconditioned. Language pursues this point of view in its use of the word. It acknowledges, indeed, that there is a concept of a disease, of a defect, but it will hardly speak of the idea of disease, of the idea of a defect; for these are not what is willed and organically determined in the teleological view, but rather its opposite. French philosophy, in which the view of the world in terms of natural causes always predominated, has consistently lost the deep significance of the idea and flattened the word down to the contingency of an arbitrary representation. German science has always held it in honour.

[[1518]] When the organic view of the world runs out into the idea, it grasps the idea in God; and the ethical idea has therein its power over the soul and the will. The fully developed end presupposes the eternal power of mind; and the centre to which the radii point is the deed at the origin of things.

[[1519]] The tendency of theology that seeks to find God in purposive formations has often been wittily attacked.¹ Yet over the abuse of a shallow teleology one must not forget the great content of the concept. In the first place the question is only one of fact. Is nature without the end, and does one understand it without the end? Let whoever would deny it prove it. But if it cannot be denied, the second question arises: how can one comprehend the end? The matter stands in part thus. One recognises the divine in nature, but calls it a limitation to think the divine through God. Speak honestly, you who speak thus: ⟦509⟧ can you understand the divine without God, the end that permeates the world without the spirit of the creator? To be sure, one need not reach so high. It is a free elevation, and let no one suppose that faith is anything other than a free elevation of the spirit. One can appropriate the world as one can eat bread without asking where it comes from. One need not look up to the stars and can still live. You understand a poem without knowing the poet; you can perhaps understand the world without knowing God; so plastic is the poem, so plastic the world. But will you limit yourself to that? It is precisely this perfection that both have received only from the spirit that created them. The poem gives you an image of the poet's spirit, the world an image of God.

[[1522]] And it is otherwise with the world than with the poem. What we know of it is always a fragment. The artistic deed which from this fragment sketches the formative spirit illumines the particles of human knowledge with a bright torch. We now behold nature with a more attentive eye and listen to revealing history with a more receptive ear. Being and every development of being is now a glance of the spirit. Things or beings are now the stages of development, beheld in their products, of the *one* infinite activity, the idea held fast, as it were, or lingering (eternal). It is the task of the philosophy of the real to seek out and set forth this thought in particulars; it begins here, where logic ends.

[[1523]] The infinite now appears to us in the finite as in a mirror. In man everything thereby receives a new significance. We already divine an infinite destination in the eye opened to infinity, for the animals have an eye only for the light of the earth; in the transfiguring imagination, for it carries reality away to the ⟦510⟧ truth of the ideal; in harmoniously moved feeling, for pleasure is exultation over the victory of the divine end in reality; in the self-sacrificing will, for, believing in something higher, it soars beyond its own self; finally in the concluding understanding, for whence would it get the bold right to complete the piecework of experience? Where the human mind hastens ahead of itself or of reality, there the idea of God stirs within it.
