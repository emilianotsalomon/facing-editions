[[1524]] # ⟦511⟧ XXIII. IDEALISM AND REALISM.

[[1525]] 1. From yet another side the whole of our view of the world may be characterised.

[[1526]] There are categories current in common life under which philosophical considerations are filed away, in order to be done with them, often before they have been understood; and in this people do not always understand even these categories themselves. Such titles, since Kant, are idealism and realism.

[[1527]] Even in Leibniz's first writings the names play differently, in that with him the opposition of realism and nominalism still echoes from the Middle Ages. The names are different, but the view is related. The realism of that time, which means the reality of the universal after the manner of the Platonic Ideas, corresponds to what, at least in *one* meaning of the name, is today called idealism; and the nominalism which insists stubbornly on the exclusive actuality of the individual corresponds in many ways to the realism of today.¹ But the motives, which start in the first instance from the investigation into the possibility of knowledge, are in part different.

[[1528]] We put first a remark on the name of ⟦512⟧ idealism. For it is useful to separate the meanings and thereby to spoil the game of indeterminate names.

[[1529]] > (1) Compare what even Herbart, from the standpoint of his realism, sets out on the universal in the Metaphysik §. 329.

[[1530]] Kant preserved the Idea in a sense which links up with Plato. For to him the Idea is a necessary concept of reason, to which no congruent object can be given in the senses. Since reason is directed to the unconditioned, the Idea as a concept of reason is directed precisely thither. Ever since, soon after Kant, the study of Plato reawakened in German philosophy, the concept of the Idea has preserved in Germany this dignity that towers above experience; and not seldom one today understands by idealism that conception of things which seeks the origin of the actual in the prefiguring thoughts of God. The ideal and the Ideal [das Ideale], as against the merely ideational [das nur Ideelle], which the tact of language has gradually branched off for the sake of the distinction, govern this use of "idealism."

[[1531]] Kant says, in a passage of the Prolegomena in which he endeavours to separate the result of his doctrine from the doctrine of Berkeley: "The doubting of the existence of the thing properly constitutes idealism in the received meaning." In the sense of this idealism Berkeley had taught that man perceives no things, but nothing except his "ideas" (representations). The use of the English word idea, going back to the use of idea which we already find in Descartes and Spinoza, and long since fallen away from Plato's sense, who intuits the Idea as the archetypal fundamental form of things, determined this meaning which, as Kant says, is the received one. While Kant expressly links the Idea with Plato, he conversely holds fast to idealism in ⟦513⟧ a sense which attaches itself to this received usage. Thus Kant, in the *Kritik der Urtheilskraft*, speaks of the idealism of purposiveness, and understands by it those systems which abolish the end in nature by tracing all purposive form of the products of nature back to chance, like Epicurus, or to a fate. The systems contending for the idealism of the final cause deny "intentionality" in purposive natural things; they deny that these were intentionally determined to this their purposive production, or in other words, that an end is the cause. In the usage of the Greeks one cannot ascribe any idealism to an Epicurus. In Kant's usage, idealism here designates the opposition to the "realism of natural ends," the cancelling of their actuality, their vanishing into a thing of representation. It is an idealism void and bare of the Idea in the sense of its Platonic origin.

[[1533]] > (2) Werke in Rosenkranz's edition III. p. 51.

[[1534]] > (3) Already Chr. Wolf, in the "Gedanken von Gott, der Welt" etc. 1720. §. 787, has this meaning: "Idealisten, welche die wirkliche Welt ausser der Seele leugnen," and Reusch, systema metaphysicum 1735, cites (§. 790) Berkeley alongside Malebranche as idealists.

[[1535]] When, now, Kant coins for his mode of consideration the name of transcendental idealism, he is not thinking of Plato's Idea, but of the opposition to Berkeley's empirical idealism. "We have," says Kant, "sufficiently proved in the transcendental aesthetic that everything which is intuited in space or in time, and therefore all objects of an experience possible for us, are nothing but appearances, i.e. mere representations, which, in the way in which they are represented, as extended beings or series of alterations, have outside our thoughts no existence grounded in itself. This doctrine I call transcendental idealism." "I have," Kant says further, "at times also called it formal idealism, in order to distinguish it from the material, i.e. the common idealism, which itself doubts or denies the existence of external things." According to ⟦514⟧ Kant, the forms of space and time, in which things appear to us, are a priori, merely subjective; hence Kant calls his idealism formal or transcendental, the latter word designating for him the possibility and the use of knowledge a priori.

[[1538]] In essential considerations one may regard Kant as the unconscious continuer of Plato. What in Plato is knowledge as recollection, in which, as he himself says, the mind draws science out of itself, appears in Kant as knowledge a priori. As Plato, for example in the Meno, sets out the recollection vividly in mathematical knowledge, or in the Republic designates the latter as a pure knowledge in the domain of the intelligible, so Kant's transcendental aesthetic stands in the closest kinship with pure mathematical knowledge. Through the a priori of space and time Kant believes he has answered for the first time the question how it was possible for human reason to bring about a priori the synthetic, apodictic, boundlessly expanding knowledge of pure mathematics. When Plato in the Phaedo³ proves knowledge as recollection from the fact that we see things fall short of what they want to be or ought to be, and yet through the senses have things only as they are, we readily perceive in this the presupposed end, even though it is not expressed there, as the tacit measure. This same end Kant, as we saw above, conceived as an a priori. When, finally, Plato in the same passage of the Phaedo designates the equal as a concept which we nowhere find in the sensible and yet apply to the object of the senses, this recalls, in Kant, the identity of self-consciousness as the ultimate source of all a priori; for the equal is the identical in quantum.

[[1539]] Accordingly one might be inclined to attach Kant's ⟦515⟧ transcendental idealism more closely to Plato; but one may not, for one would blur the historical sense of the word, the relation to Berkeley. With Kant the name of idealism is not the affirmation of the Idea, but the negation of the real in representation. In the same Kantian sense the doctrine of Fichte and of Schopenhauer is called idealism, and this usage still prevails in Schleiermacher's Dialektik.¹

[[1543]] The designation takes another form, to be sure, when, as has already been remarked, in the sense of later German philosophers, it links idealism directly to Plato's Idea and refers it not to the thing of representation but to the thought in things.

[[1544]] In so-called absolute idealism, the dialectical doctrine of Hegel, the two coincide: the concept in the human mind and the concept in things.

[[1545]] The confusion that has set in would be resolved if what Kant called idealism were rather called eidolism, or, if one prefers, subjectivism. Yet there is already enough in the language of the "-ism" that teases minds, and we do not wish to increase this stock.

[[1546]] 2. The healthy practical man is a realist; the theoretical, the speculating man cannot be one in the same sense; for to him the immediate appears as mediated. But he must not cut off the relations which lead back into the real; otherwise man straightway becomes the measure of things, and together with the theory the practical consideration easily becomes egoistic.

[[1547]] Herbart asserted realism in particular in opposition to the subjectivism in Fichte's doctrine of the positing and counter-positing I. Herbart points everywhere to the given, to the given matter of sensation and to the form given in sensation. ⟦516⟧ "The sensations do not lie in us," he says,¹ "like a loose aggregate or like a chaos; rather, precisely in being given, they arrange themselves in determinate groups and series, and only in this determinateness can one appeal to them as to something given." Herbart's absolute position lies in sensation. From what is given in experience his whole speculation, and the inference back to the true happening, proceed. Herbart wants for his metaphysics a realistic regulator: what is given in sensation.

[[1548]] > (1) Dialektik §. 57. §. 169

[[1549]] This pointing to the given has had a salutary effect. But whither has the impulse led, since Herbart, like the Eleatics, applied metaphysically the law of identity, which has significance only when there are already necessary concepts, before all content of concepts, and drew out of it a being without its like?

[[1550]] The result became the opposite of that which has compelling force in the given. What is, as Herbart devised it according to those norms, is to be absolutely positive, simple, without negation and relation, rejecting every determination of magnitude and excluding motion as well. But such a thing is nowhere given to us; indeed everything given is given to us in precisely the opposite way. Where motion, through which and in which alone anything is given to us, becomes objective semblance; where in the investigation of metaphysics, and hence in scientific application, the end is lacking which comprehends multiplicity into unity, and with it the proper support for unity, both in the individual and in the whole: there the realism of the beginning has at the end turned into negation, and in objective semblance has developed further into a kind of idealism, the word taken in that received sense of Kant's. It is vain to cover motion as objective semblance with the objective semblance in astronomy. Copernicus reversed the apparent motions in the heavens ⟦517⟧ and taught us to convert them into actual ones. What becomes possible within motion and through the relation of motions no longer has any sense when it is turned as an example against motion as such, and the whole of motion is now to be objective semblance. The aesthetic judgments, the practical Ideas, which in a higher sense could be called ideal, have in Herbart, together with the necessary feeling of harmony which they carry with them in the beautiful and the ethical, their ultimate ground in the law of the psychical mechanism.

[[1553]] Thus Herbart's realism is idealistic in its consequences, but in its ground by no means ideal.

[[1554]] 3. Our senses count as the witnesses of realism; but already Locke's empirical investigation begins to convert their energies into idealistic presuppositions. With Locke, to be sure, this happens only from *one* side.

[[1555]] In distinguishing, in his considerations on the senses, the primary qualities of bodies from the secondary, and determining the former as those which are inseparable from the body in every state, and the latter as those which presuppose the original ones, he designates the representations of the original qualities as copies whose patterns are actually there in the bodies themselves. Such representations, corresponding to the given and resembling it, are the representations of the corporeal (solidity), of extension, figure, motion and rest, number. It is otherwise with the secondary qualities of bodies. As little, says Locke, as the pain which an external thing causes is in the things, so little can we say that the derived qualities (colour, smell, heat, taste, etc.), as they are produced in us, are likewise in the things. Empirical investigation here begins scepticism against the empirical and teaches us critique.

[[1556]] Against Locke's theory, which draws the ⟦518⟧ original qualities of bodies, solidity, extension, figure, motion and rest, number, immediately and as a given copy from the senses, it can easily be shown that the senses give only motives for these representations, out of which the mind forms or projects them. Number, arising from the distinguishing and combining of the individual, lies beyond the senses. Figure and extension are projected from the elements which the sense of touch collects or the sense of sight offers for construction. Judgment co-operates in this. Motion and rest, in order to be thought, presuppose a comparison of spatial relations; so far as they are found in the external world, they are inferred. The solidity which we think in the body resisting our sense of touch is after all something other than the peculiar sensation of pressure in the finger. When, at the prompting of the senses, we attribute to bodies extension and figure, motion or rest, solidity, number, the mind has already made out of what is given by the senses something which the senses do not have; and if we ask how it apprehends the elements and forms from them these necessary qualities of the body, without which it ceases to be a body, we see that constructive motion, which appeared to us as intellectual in all perception, working and forming in them all. Through motion rest is known, figure described, continuous extension projected; number goes back to time, the inner moment of motion. Even the impenetrability of the body is thought through resisting motion. These remarks follow from the investigations above.

[[1558]] While in this way, even in the original qualities of bodies, the opinion refutes itself that the process of sensible sensation is a copy of what goes on in bodies, so that sensible representation and the original qualities would correspond to one another like impression and imprint; while it rather shows itself that our ⟦519⟧ representations of these qualities are already conditioned by a projection of mental origin: modern physiology has pursued further the question how far the representations of the secondary qualities are empirically grounded in the senses.

[[1559]] 4. The physiological consideration here starts from a fact which is established by experiments. Stimuli of different kinds acting on the same sensory nerve always produce only a sensation of the same kind, a sensation within the same sphere; and conversely the same external stimuli excite different sensations in different senses, according to the nature of the sense. Thus, for example, a mechanical action, a blow, a jolt, a pressure in the eye, excites the sensation of light and colour, as fiery circles arise in the field of vision through a pressure or blow on the eyeball, and likewise through a blow the sensation of a bang in hearing. An English officer, as Bell relates,¹ received a musket-ball shot which passed through the bone of the face, and described his sensation at the moment of being wounded in these words: it had been to him like a flash of lightning before his eyes, accompanied by a sound as when the door of St. Paul's Cathedral slams shut. At the same galvanic or electrical stimuli the eye flickers, the ear rings, the tongue tastes. Chemical influences on the blood produce sensations of light in the eye, a rushing in the ear, a tingling in feeling. The same rays of the sun are sensed in the eye as light and in feeling as warmth. Physiological experience has shown that through stimulation of each single sensory nerve fibre only such sensations can arise as belong to the circle of a single determinate sense, and that every stimulus which is able to excite this nerve fibre at all calls forth only sensations of this particular circle. From ⟦520⟧ this one infers the significance of sensation. If specific qualities were perceived, the same external influences could not produce different effects on different organs. With the nerves of the different senses it is a matter of excitation as such, but the nerve does not concern itself with the nature of the exciting object. The sense senses only its energy and not the specific quality of the external world. What comes to our consciousness through the senses is in the first instance only qualities and states of our nerves, and no quality and no state of an external body. The retina sees only itself; it is to itself subject-object. Thus in our intercourse with the external world we sense only ourselves. Altering causes do indeed lie in things, but no sense shows them in their peculiar nature, but only in its own kind of sensation. Our language, to be sure, transfers what we sense to the object which stimulates the sensation. We call the body light, coloured; but in truth we do not sense the light, the coloured, but only the nerve as light, and in it a difference of energy which we call colour. Only through the figure of speech of metonymy is the body called light, coloured. We call a food which we taste salty, but the salty is only in our sensation. According to this doctrine of specific energies, each sense has its form of sensation, into which it takes up all stimuli and with which it clothes things.¹

[[1561]] It is tempting to go further and to regard this doctrine as an empirical working-out of Kant's transcendental idealism, and the two as agreeing and bearing witness to one another. As in Kant intuition is active in its forms, in space and time, the understanding in its forms, in the categories, and the power of judgment in its point of view ⟦521⟧ of unity, the end: so the senses become aware only of their energies, and everywhere the mind has only its own energies for object. If the strict Kantianism which declares causality to be merely subjective combines with this doctrine of specific sense-energy, then no acting object either, in which the thing in itself would be causal, may be assumed; and then man is cut off and retains only his own little world for enjoyment or for torment.

[[1563]] 5. We enter into what these physiological inferences have taught us.

[[1564]] Without question the sense-impression, the sensible sensation, in which we stand in the midst of an interaction, is nothing purely objective, no unmixed report of qualities of things. A piece of our own life is comprehended in it. Without question it is rash to lend to things what happens in us.

[[1565]] It is likewise important to recognise that each of the senses, whatever the kind of action upon it, reacts only in its own way, speaks only in its own language. In the experiment with mechanical, electrical, chemical actions, which in each sense yields a sensation identical in kind, we learn to attend to the subjective share in sensation.

[[1566]] But the difference between such a violent impression and the natural and proper excitation by the corresponding force of nature stands out clearly.

[[1567]] Those violent actions yield a pathological fact, these natural ones a physiological fact, and one will neither confuse the two nor set their value equal. To the former pain and the feeling of threatened life lie near; the latter make themselves known through harmonious sensation.

[[1568]] It is a great difference whether a sense is stimulated by an adequate object or irritated by an inadequate force, just as it is a great difference whether by pressure on the eye we produce a fiery ring in the field of vision or ⟦522⟧ whether we see a coloured circle. In interaction with the objects of their proper sphere the senses involuntarily guide us to go outward with representation, or give us elements for the construction of an object, whereas on the other path they have only a confused sensation without hold or support. Only in interaction with the objects of their proper sphere do the senses learn and teach to distinguish and to fix.

[[1569]] Undeveloped sensation does not become conscious of the differences, and only in intercourse with adequate objects does chaotic sensation develop into the differences of the energies of which it is capable; for example, the sensation of light, the indeterminate sensation of brightness, into the distinction of the manifold colours, themselves again shaded within. Those born blind do not dream in visual images at all.

[[1570]] Where in the activities of the senses we feel a compulsion to distinguish and the impossibility of distinguishing otherwise, where, on reflecting upon ourselves, we become aware of our inability to have produced the differences out of ourselves: there we know the given; and this felt compulsion marks the given, which in the domains of all the senses makes experience into experience, into the great teacher of mankind.

[[1571]] The given compels the mind, through the senses, to posit it in differences. The objects carry out this compulsion by touching the senses with their effect, and the mind answers this compulsion by following up the given, or by projecting the sensible image out of the given motives.

[[1572]] Thus the mind, through its constructive motion, grasps the given in space and time and projects it as an object.

[[1573]] Is the mind wrong in this objectification?

[[1574]] An object in general, an acting force, is assumed. If one really wished to transfer the subjectivity which one derives from the specific sense-energies to space and time as well; if one wished to see the truth ⟦523⟧ of the Kantian doctrine in its continuing the view of the subjective specific sense-energies into subjective specific energies of the mind; if, therefore, on this analogy, space and time, causality and end were to be merely subjective forms: then the end of this view grounded on experience would be the cancelling of all experience. Each would sense only in his own sense-energies; each would set them, only through his own causality, in his own space and his own time. Idealism, in that meaning which Kant called the received one, would be completed by realism.

[[1575]] Action, causality, is acknowledged. If, now, the effects take place in the subject, they will necessarily have a subjective side; but this subjective side, which is conditioned by causality, cannot now be extended to causality itself. It would be a false analogy from the conditioned part to that which conditions.

[[1576]] The so-called specific sense-energies, taken by themselves, without a causality supervening, are properly no energy, but rather only potencies, faculties, which first await and need an excitation in order to become energy. This presupposition of their own leads out of the merely subjective.

[[1577]] What is given in the sensations of sense are effects, indeed an effect of effects. The exciting causality which strikes the sense is, in the great connection of nature, effect; and the sentient being, conditioned in its being and existence by manifold presuppositions, is effect. The appearance in sensation is the effect of both. Thereby the question, which proceeds backwards, what both are in themselves, becomes entangled. At the point of contact where the appearances are born in the sensations of sense, subject and object enter into a relation, but on both sides stands something unknown. Our known, one may therefore say,¹ copies relations without our ⟦524⟧ knowledge knowing the terms of the relation singly, because it proceeds only from such a given (the sensible) in which there is copied not the constitution of things, but only their being together and not being together. How, from such a situation, from the mere relation of x to y, is a value to be found for x and y, a content of the unknown things to be procured from the mere relation of two unknowns?

[[1579]] So desperate is the situation only as long as one keeps causality abstract, as a mere category of the understanding which states only a relation of succession, without determining this relation in its essence. The matter itself leads further. That contact of the two designated effects, which in their meeting produces the appearances in the sensations of sense, is possible only through something common which passes through both. Without continuous motion a contact of object and subject, the names by which we are accustomed to call those effects that meet, is impossible. In motion alone is the possibility given that there can be any relation at all of those unknown quantities introduced as x and y. Apart from our earlier investigations into causality, motion in its most universal form announces itself of its own accord at this point, where realism and idealism undergo their crisis. It still leaves open the particular modes in which it appears; but even its universal acknowledgment draws the greatest consequence after it, the acknowledgment of the mathematical element, which, as has been shown, stems together with space and time from motion. Through this common motion, through the common mathematical element, the mind has, as it were, a handle on things; it can grasp them and react upon them.

[[1580]] There is one thing more. Within the senses, be they duller or sharper in individuals, the effects appear constant in interaction with things, steady in an abiding relation. This constant gives the individual its point of purchase on the ⟦525⟧ objective. Were the appearance merely subjective, so that it had no cause behind it, it would be mere semblance, and semblance as such would admit of no action upon it. One can alter semblance only by acting on its cause; but if one acknowledges a cause of it, it has properly already ceased to be semblance; one then already grasps the being that lies behind it. Semblance as such, the false messenger of something actual, will never be able to guide the mind to such an action that things answer it as it wills; for that, it must call them forth out of their nature. What is steady in the appearances, which are effects, makes such an adequate action possible, on the presupposition of continuous motion, which is the essence of the acting cause. If the laws of motion are at the same time the laws of mind, as is evident in mathematics, which rests on constructive motion, then the constant effects can thereby be compared and analysed, and the things behind the appearances open up. The terms of the relation, subject and object, which interpenetrate in the appearance, now no longer stand over against one another wholly alien and unknown; they have in what is common (motion) a possibility of entering into one another. As bound material motion becomes free in the mind, it becomes universal, and in this universality is at once so creative out of itself and so pliant in fitting itself to the given that, going far beyond the senses, it teaches us to infer from the constant effect to the hidden causal process. It accords with the history of the sciences that they gain power over the efficient cause above all through the mathematical element, and this real power confirms the realism grounded in the given.

[[1581]] What Locke called original qualities of bodies, ⟦526⟧ namely extension, figure, motion and rest, number, rests throughout on motion and its products. In sensation these qualities do not imprint themselves like letters of type; the representations are not copies of them. But the mind projects them through the activity homogeneous with their origin, and in holding in this to what is constant in the given, that agreement comes about which occasions the comparison of a likeness or a copy.

[[1582]] > (1) See above vol. I. p. 161 f.

[[1583]] By the same path we enter into Locke's so-called secondary qualities, those properties which ordinary sensible representation throws, from the appearance which is in us, immediately into things, the one factor of the appearance. The physiological experiments which underlie the doctrine of specific sense-energies all presuppose motion as causality, and those excitations adequate to the individual senses, from which the so-called sensible qualities, Locke's secondary qualities, arise, are traced back by strict science to modes of motion.¹ Thus, for example, the deep bass note which the ear hears represents 32 vibrations in a second, and colours represent the largest numbers of undulations; and our sensations of these qualities are for us, so we may regard them, the abbreviated expressions of such modes of motion.

[[1584]] 6. We are ungrateful if, in the theoretical interest, we exaggerate and overstrain the demand and require to learn immediately from the senses what the nature of things is.

[[1585]] It will never be possible to devise for a living being a purely objective organ, i.e. one which, in conveying something outer to the inner, would eliminate the receiving inner, the active consciousness.

[[1586]] One must picture to oneself what would actually come of it if our senses were so objective that they indicated to us ⟦527⟧ immediately in things what, according to physical theory, is the true share of things in the appearances of our senses. If we imagined for a few moments that instead of the impression we immediately perceived what goes on in the object, for example, instead of the sensation of that bass note which appeals to our feeling of life, distinguished the 32 vibrations in a second, or instead of the impression of middle red light the 456 billion vibrations in a second, of a wave-length of about 24 millionths of an inch: then the apprehension of the world would be for our mind a perpetual, monotonous arithmetical exercise, or an uninterrupted problem of geometrical construction. What would we have gained thereby? We would perceive the qualities according to the truth of physical theory, but our intuition would be infinitely more monotonous than the intuition of those who distinguish only light and shade, but no colours, and see the world only "as in a copper engraving." This truth we would gain, but we would lose the harmony of sensation, which, in the language of our own life, grasps constant effects for us in abbreviated expression.

[[1587]] > (1) Vol. I. p. 253 f.

[[1588]] We have dealings with our surroundings in these abbreviated expressions, and since they present constant effects to us, they suffice for the end of self-preservation, for which the senses are there in the first instance. The constant effects which are represented in the sensations of sense give man the possibility of orienting himself about the object as acting cause so far that he can conduct himself rightly towards it and can, purposively, place himself in a friendly or hostile attitude toward it; and they give science a still greater possibility of inferences back.

[[1589]] In mediating self-preservation rightly, the senses guarantee a right revelation of objective relations. Those abbreviated expressions which we receive, in constant effects, upon our own life present to us, rightly interpreted, what is right. They further, moreover, the capacity of ⟦528⟧ comprehensive thinking, which without them would have to lose itself in infinite numbers, for example of vibrations; for, regarded as abbreviated expression, every sensation of sense is already a comprehending unity.

[[1590]] The senses help to employ the universal forces of nature, the undulations of light and of heat, the elastic vibrations of the air, chemical processes, for individual life and to transform them into an individual life. If, now, the senses, mediated through many real presuppositions, are attuned to the simple elementary potencies of nature, then *one* thought passes through what lies far apart, and through it the senses fulfil their vocation of supplementing, on the real side, the life of divided existence. While what enters into the senses from without is refracted and damped against subjective life and reaches our representation only in universal effects, our organs of motion go with their demand immediately outward. If we are to be able to move, there must be an abutment against which the body springs forward; these instruments of change of place demand a firm basis of ground. Nothing intervenes here, and this demand for the objective is satisfied. In general, in the need of sentient, self-moving beings and in the fulfilment which they find, there lies a guarantee of realism.

[[1591]] 7. In this way the given leads to the real; the same given remains the mind's direction for the application of its ideal category, the end. Where the given compels a conception through the inner end, where things are treated in the sense of a necessary concept of end and answer in its sense, the effect in things which corresponds to the anticipated representation confirms the correctness of the ideal presupposition. This principle, acknowledged in the given, leads beyond the given in its contingent shape; it becomes its measure and draws it upward, as in the ethical. The ⟦529⟧ moving, directing end points into the unconditioned and gives thought, in the origin of things, power over the efficient cause.

[[1592]] The compulsion of the given guides the mind in the application of the projecting motion which opens up the real, and the same compulsion of the given leads it to the application of its ideal category, the end. Thus this outer compulsion is the sign of the inner necessity which the mind seeks.

[[1593]] On this path a realism is grounded which cannot turn into materialism, for its determinations proceed, through the inner end, from the thought in the ground of things; and an idealism which cannot become subjectivism, for it grounds itself through an activity common to thinking and being, which in the conception of the appearance follows the compelling directions of the given.

[[1594]] Such a realism, which presupposes the a priori, becomes transcendental in its ground, if we use the word in Kant's sense; and the idealism which grounds itself in the given has its soil in the empirical. Thus the transcendental and the empirical exchange with one another; thought and actuality seek and bear witness to one another.

[[1595]] Realism without the Idea becomes materialism, and idealism without access to the real becomes a dream of representation, a world of eidola. In both directions it becomes difficult, indeed impossible, to preserve faith in the unconditioned, the will of God in the world, and the mind turns away, mourning and disheartened, from the dried-up spring of thought. Hence it is necessary to strive for the right unification and not to desist until it is attained.

[[1596]] In this unification human science has its dignity, and philosophy its noble task in the projecting of this goal, in the grounding of the path, and in the labour of carrying it through all domains.
